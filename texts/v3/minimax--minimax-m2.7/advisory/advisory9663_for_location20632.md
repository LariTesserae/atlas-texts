# Preparation Guide: The Weeping Plateau

You've arrived in a place of patience. Everything here moves slowly—the water climbing through stone, the erosion rounding the ridges, the seasons settling in and out like breath held too long. The people who live here have built their lives around that patience, and you'll need to do the same.

---

## Understanding Where You've Landed

This is a high plateau, vast beyond easy comprehension. The city—though calling it a city barely captures it—has grown across this limestone expanse for so long that distinguishing between the built environment and the stone beneath has become impossible. Both feel equally ancient. Both feel equally inevitable.

The water is the first thing you must understand. It rises from deep below, seeping upward through porous rock, emerging wherever pressure finds a seam. It is not rain. It is not a river. It is a slow, persistent weeping through the stone that has been going on longer than the city has existed and will continue long after. The water table sits close enough to the surface that moisture gathers on every surface that doesn't drain. Your clothes will be damp. Your bedding will be damp. Your food will absorb moisture from the air. You cannot fight this. You can only prepare for it.

The temperature is the second thing. Elevation has thinned the air and pulled the weather across this land in slow systems for long enough that the plateau has become a cool place even in what passes for summer. In the higher districts, the cold bites. In the lower basins, where the water gathers, it is not cold but it is perpetually damp, the moisture drawing heat from everything it touches. The stone holds that chill and conducts it upward—through your feet, through the soles of your shoes, through the floor of every building you enter. Even in buildings with fires, the walls remain cool to the touch, and warmth vanishes the moment you step outside.

The wind is the third. It comes from the interior of the plateau, drawn across the vast land by elevation and pressure, and it finds its way everywhere—through the streets, over the walls, down into the courtyards and up onto the roof terraces. It is not violent, but it is constant, and it carries that mineral coolness from the high stone surfaces and the wet cliff faces.

The people number in the hundreds of thousands, perhaps more. They cluster in the dense districts where walls share walls and streets are more bodies than space during the hours of movement, and they thin out toward the edges where the bare rock ridges break through the soil. Every surface of the plateau has been touched by human presence—carved, terraced, fitted with stone channels that direct the water, fitted with stairs and ramps and terraced platforms that follow the terrain's settled contours. This is a place that learned long ago how to fit its inhabitants into a finite space, and the organization is there if you look for it, but so is the density, and you will feel it constantly: the proximity of other lives, the sound of them through walls, the narrow passages between buildings, the way the built environment crowds the sky to a narrow strip between rooftops.

## Physical Preparation

### Acclimatization

You will arrive at altitude. The air is thinner here than what you are used to, and you will feel it most in the higher districts, where the steps are steeper and the wind is sharper. Altitude sickness is real, and it does not negotiate. Plan to arrive in the lower districts first, where the elevation is gentler and the air is still thin but less so, and spend at least two or three days there before climbing higher. Rest often. Breathe deliberately. Drink more water than you think you need—the moisture in the air tricks your body into thinking it has enough when it doesn't.

### Clothing

You will be damp constantly. There is no escape from this. The trick is not to fight the moisture but to work with it.

Bring clothing made of materials that handle damp gracefully. Wool holds warmth even when wet and resists the growth of odor-causing bacteria that will plague you otherwise. Linen breathes and dries quickly. Cotton is dangerous—it holds moisture against your skin and loses its insulation when you need it most. If you can find linen or wool blends, bring those. If you have access to modern synthetic moisture-wicking fabrics, bring them too, but understand that they will eventually fail in this environment where "drying" is a relative term at best.

Layer everything. The temperature swings between the early morning chill, the midday cool, and the night cold in ways that feel more pronounced than they are because of the moisture and the wind. You will need to add and remove layers constantly, and the ability to adjust will be the difference between comfort and misery.

Your outermost layer must be wind-resistant. The plateau wind is not dramatic, but it is persistent, and it will leech heat from you faster than you realize. A tight-weave wool coat or a waxed canvas jacket will serve you better than anything made of synthetic fleece or down, because those materials lose their loft when damp and never recover it in this climate.

Bring more clothing than you think you need. You will not be able to dry anything thoroughly. Items you wear will be damp by the time you take them off, and items you wash will take days to reach the state you would call "dry." Build a rotation that allows you to have dry-enough clothing while the other items slowly lose their moisture in the ambient air.

### Footwear

The streets are stone—wet stone, smooth stone polished by centuries of feet and animals, with narrow channels cut into the surface to direct the water flow. When it rains, and it does rain, though not often, the streets become slick and the channels become small streams. When it does not rain, the water still seeps up from between the flagstones, and every surface is damp or wet.

Bring footwear with good traction that can handle wet stone. Leather soles with deep tread, or rubber-soled boots with non-slip patterns. You will be walking on surfaces that are often slick, and a fall on stone is not a trivial matter. Break your footwear in before you arrive. Blisters are manageable at home; they become infected wounds in this damp environment.

Waterproof boots are not the answer, because waterproof boots do not breathe, and boots that do not breathe will fill with your own perspiration and never dry out. You want water-resistant footwear that can handle damp surfaces but allows air circulation. Leather boots treated with wax or oil will serve you better than rubber galoshes.

Bring multiple pairs. Rotate them. Allow each pair to dry thoroughly between wearings—not dry, just thoroughly aired, and understand that "thoroughly dried" may require leaving them in a dry district or near a fire for days.

### Skin Care

The moisture will do things to your skin. Your face will chap. Your hands will crack. The skin between your toes will soften and break down. Your inner thighs and groin will chafe. Your armpits will become raw.

Bring salve—thick, moisturizing salve, the kind you would use for severe dryness. Bring it in large quantities. Bring zinc oxide or similar barrier creams for the places where skin meets skin or skin meets wet fabric. Bring powder to help keep surfaces dry where powder can work, understanding that in true damp conditions it will cake and become grit.

You will need to apply these things daily, multiple times daily. Budget your supplies accordingly.

### Respiratory Health

The air is damp and mineral-scented, carrying the smell of wet stone and the organic matter of a dense human population. For most people this is manageable, but for some it becomes a problem—mold sensitivity, mineral dust irritation, the constant low-level exposure to things that would not matter in a dry climate.

Bring a supply of any respiratory medications you use at home. Bring a backup supply. Bring more than you think you need, because you will be in this environment for weeks or months, and you do not know how your lungs will respond until you are already there.

Bring a scarf or cloth that you can use to filter dust on the windiest days, when the air carries grit from the bare rock ridges or the dry districts.

### Eyes

The light here is bright. The elevation has thinned the air, and the sun is more intense than you expect. The moisture in the air scatters light in a way that reduces glare compared to a dry high-altitude environment, but the overall brightness is higher, and the reflection off wet stone surfaces can be surprising.

Bring eye protection. Sunglasses or equivalents that block UV and reduce glare. If you use prescription glasses, bring spares. If you use contacts, bring your backup prescription glasses—contacts in a damp, dusty environment with this level of particulate in the air will become uncomfortable, and you may need to switch to glasses for days at a time.

## What to Bring

### Essentials

You are traveling to a pre-industrial world. Nothing mechanical exists here. No electricity, no powered tools, no modern medical equipment. You will need to bring everything that cannot be sourced locally, and the things that can be sourced locally will be different from what you know.

**Food and water:** Bring concentrated calories. Your body will be working harder than you expect—the altitude, the cold, the effort of walking on wet stone, the effort of carrying water because you cannot trust that every source is safe for your system. Bring energy bars or equivalent, dried fruit, jerky, nuts. Bring a supply sufficient to get you through at least a week of local food trials, because you do not know how your digestive system will respond to the local cuisine and you may need to fall back on familiar foods while you adjust.

Bring water treatment. You cannot drink the water here without treatment—your gut microbiome does not recognize the local bacteria, and the mineral content of the limestone seepage will disagree with you. Bring filtration, or chemical treatment, or UV purification, or whatever you have that makes unsafe water safe. Bring enough for your entire stay, because you cannot count on finding equivalents locally.

**Medical supplies:** This is the most critical category. You are traveling to a world without modern medicine. If you have a medical emergency—a serious infection, a broken bone, an allergic reaction, a cardiac event, anything beyond basic first aid—you will be dependent on local practitioners whose knowledge is ancient and whose materials are limited.

Bring a comprehensive medical kit. Antibiotics, if you have access to them—but understand that bringing antibiotics raises ethical questions about disrupting the local microbial ecosystem, and you will need to think carefully about whether and how to use them. Bring analgesics, antihistamines, antidiarrheals, rehydration salts. Bring wound care supplies—disinfectant, bandages, suture supplies if you have training. Bring any prescription medications you require for chronic conditions, with backups.

Bring treatments for the specific challenges of this environment: antifungals for the moisture-related infections that will otherwise develop, topical antibiotics for the cuts and abrasions that will otherwise become infected, cough suppressants for the respiratory irritation that many visitors develop.

Consider bringing a medical reference. A comprehensive guide to diagnosis and treatment, oriented toward someone who is not a trained medical professional but who needs to handle emergencies. Paper-based, because digital devices will fail.

**Navigation:** Bring maps if they exist for this world. If you are traveling between worlds, you may have access to local knowledge or records that can help you understand the layout before you arrive. If not, prepare to get lost. The city is vast, the streets are dense, and you will not have a GPS or any equivalent. Learn to navigate by local landmarks—the water channels, the major temples or administrative buildings, the distinctive ridge-lines and cliff faces. Find local guides who know their districts and can help you move between them.

Bring paper and writing implements. You will want to sketch maps of the districts you learn, note the locations of essential services—wells, markets, administrative centers, the few areas where foreigners are welcome. Paper is heavy, but in a world without printing presses, it is invaluable.

### Technology

Your multiverse travel technology is presumably beyond anything this world possesses. Think carefully about how to use it.

**Power management:** You have no way to recharge batteries here. Solar charging may work in this climate, though the frequent cloud cover and the persistent moisture will reduce efficiency. Bring more power than you think you need, and plan to use less. Turn off devices when not in use. Reduce screen brightness. Minimize wireless connectivity. Conserve power as a discipline, not an afterthought.

**Redundancy:** Bring backup devices. If your primary communication device fails, you need a fallback. If your primary navigation aid fails, you need a fallback. Redundancy is survival.

**Discretion:** Do not display your technology openly. You are traveling to a world without mechanical devices, and your possessions would be not just unusual but incomprehensible—objects that operate without visible power source, that contain knowledge beyond anything locally available, that could be interpreted as magical or demonic or royal, depending on local beliefs. Use them in private. Use them sparingly. If local people see you using a device, be prepared to deflect questions in ways that do not compromise your position or endanger local people who have seen too much.

**Data:** Bring local information. Historical records, cultural guides, linguistic resources. If you have the ability to access records from previous travelers or local archives, use them. Your preparation should include as much knowledge as you can gather about local customs, local laws, local dangers, local resources.

### Trade Goods

You will need to eat. You will need lodging. You will need local assistance. You will need to pay for these things, and you arrive with currency that does not exist here.

Bring goods that have value in a pre-industrial economy. Items that are small, durable, and useful. Metal tools—knives, awls, needles—are universally valuable. Cloth, especially wool or linen, has high value. Salt is precious in any pre-refrigeration society. Beads and small decorative items can serve as currency in markets where foreigners are novelty. If you can bring small amounts of precious metal—silver or gold in small denominations—you will have purchasing power, but be aware that flashing precious metals can attract unwanted attention.

Bring items that are clearly foreign but clearly valuable. You are a traveler from elsewhere; your belongings will be unusual regardless of what you bring. Lean into this. Bring goods that local people can recognize as trade items, and that they can use or resell.

Do not bring weapons. The weapon you bring is a weapon that does not exist here, and that makes it not just a tool but a political object. The local authorities will not be amused. The local criminals will be very interested. Leave weapons behind.

## What to Expect When You Arrive

### The First Days

You will arrive disoriented. The air is wrong—not wrong in a way you can easily describe, but wrong. The moisture, the altitude, the mineral smell, the way sound carries differently over wet stone. Your body will spend the first day or two in a low-grade stress state, and you will not perform at your best. Do not plan to accomplish anything critical in the first forty-eight hours. Rest. Observe. Learn.

The people will notice you. You are foreign, and foreigners are rare in a city this old and self-contained. You may be an object of curiosity, or suspicion, or cautious welcome, depending on local attitudes toward outsiders. Be patient with the stares. Be polite. Learn the local gestures for greeting and respect before you need them.

The infrastructure will be confusing. Water channels run through the streets, feeding basins and fountains and private cisterns. The channels are tended by people whose job is to manage the flow, opening and closing gates to direct water where it is needed. You will need to learn where to draw water that is safe for your system, and where the water is for other purposes—cleaning, irrigation, the waste channels that run through the lower districts.

The streets will be disorienting. The city grew organically, following the terrain rather than imposing a grid. Streets climb and descend with the stone, doubling back on themselves, narrowing into passages and then opening into courts. You will get lost repeatedly. This is normal. Carry a small amount of local currency so that you can pay for guidance when you need it.

### The Daily Rhythm

The plateau has its rhythms, and the city moves within them. During the day, the streets are dense with movement—people, animals, goods. The movement has patterns: market hours, work hours, the times when the gates open and close, the times when the water flow is adjusted. Watch these patterns before you try to participate in them.

In the deep night, the movement stops. The streets empty. The wind continues, and the water continues, and somewhere always someone is speaking or working, but the sound is so faint that it registers as silence. This is the time when the plateau's scale becomes undeniable—when you step out of a crowded street into a wide court and suddenly the sky fills your vision, the stars bright and the blue deeper than you have ever seen it because the altitude has thinned the air and brought the sky closer.

The weather systems move slowly, as everything here moves slowly. A system arrives and settles in for days or weeks, and then departs. The local people have names for these systems, and they plan around them. Learn the names. Learn what each system means for temperature and wind and the rate of water seepage. Your survival depends on understanding the climate as a mode the sky enters and inhabits, not as a sequence of events.

### The People

You will be a stranger, and the people here are not naive—they live in a dense urban environment that has been continuously occupied for centuries. They know what outsiders are, even if they have never seen one from your world. They will be watching you, and you should watch them in return.

Observe before you engage. Watch how people treat each other, how they handle conflicts, how they structure their interactions. The city is organized, and the organization is layered—social hierarchies, occupational guilds, neighborhood associations, the complex web of obligations that allows a dense population to coexist without constant chaos. You will not understand all of this immediately, and you should not pretend to.

Behave with humility. You are dependent on local goodwill, local knowledge, local resources. You have technology and knowledge that they lack, but they have generations of experience in this environment that you cannot replicate. Treat that experience with respect.

Expect kindness and expect suspicion, sometimes from the same people. A dense urban population develops a sophisticated sense of who belongs and who does not, and you do not belong. This is not a moral judgment; it is a practical one. You will need to earn trust, and earning it will take time.

### The Dangers

The environment is not dramatically dangerous, but it is persistently challenging. Hypothermia is a risk, even in summer, if you are caught without adequate clothing in a cold rain or a wind storm. The moisture makes wet cold a constant possibility, and wet cold kills.

Injury is a risk. The streets are slick, the crowds are dense, the animals are large draft animals moving through narrow spaces. Falls are common. Being stepped on by animals is possible. Cuts and abrasions in a damp environment become infected wounds if not treated promptly. You must be more careful than you would be at home, because a minor injury here is not minor—it is the beginning of a process that can become serious.

Illness is a risk. The local pathogens are foreign to your immune system. Food-borne illness, water-borne illness, respiratory infections—any of these can lay you low for days or weeks. Treat all local water, all local food with caution, and accept that you will probably get sick at some point regardless of your precautions.

Crime is a risk, as it is in any dense urban environment. A foreigner with foreign goods is a target, and you should take standard precautions against theft. Do not display wealth. Do not walk alone in empty districts at night. Do not trust strangers with your security.

## Adaptation

You will not remain a foreigner forever. If you stay—if your journey involves extended time in this world rather than a brief visit—you will slowly become something else. Your clothes will become local clothes, adapted to local conditions. Your gait will change, your posture will change, the way you hold your body in the wind and the cold will become local. Your language will improve. Your understanding of the water channels and the market rhythms and the neighborhood associations will deepen.

This adaptation is the point. You are visiting a place of patience, and the patience is not just environmental but human. The people who live here have made their lives in this damp, cold, dense environment for long enough that it no longer feels damp and cold and dense to them. They have found ways to be comfortable, ways to be happy, ways to build families and communities and art and meaning. The place is not hostile; it is simply different, and you are not used to it yet.

Give yourself time to become used to it. Do not rush. Do not assume that your way of being in the world is the right way or the better way—it is simply a different way, evolved in a different environment. Learn from the people who have lived here for generations. Let them teach you how to dress, how to eat, how to manage the moisture, how to navigate the wind. Their knowledge is more valuable than anything you have brought with you.

And remember the water. It is always there, climbing through the stone, weeping from the cliff faces, gathering in the basins and feeding the wells. It will outlast everything you build and everything you bring. It has always been here, and it will continue, and the city grew to match it because the people who built the city understood what you are only beginning to learn: that the water is not a problem to be solved but a condition to be accepted, and that everything grows better when it grows to match the place it is in.

---

## Final Thoughts

You are traveling to a place that is ancient, vast, damp, cold, dense, and patient. The people who live there have made it home, and you can make it home too, but not immediately and not without effort. Prepare carefully. Bring the essentials that cannot be sourced locally. Leave behind the assumptions that will not serve you. Open yourself to a different way of being in the world.

The plateau will accept you slowly, the way it accepts everything: gradually, permanently, as though you had always been here and it simply did not notice until now. When it finally does notice, you will be ready.
