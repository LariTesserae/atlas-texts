# Preparation Guide: The Breathing Peaks of Suth Marang

---

## A Note Before Descent

This guide assumes you are human. Not baseline human—the multiverse produces many configurations—but the essential facts hold: you have lungs that need air, skin that needs shelter, and a nervous system that will interpret everything it encounters whether you want it to or not. You will arrive. You will need to survive. And then, perhaps, you will begin to understand what you are surviving *in*.

Bring no assumptions. The Breathing Peaks have been dissolving for longer than your species has had language.

---

## I. Atmosphere and Breathing

### The Air That Should Not Be

At nine thousand meters, the air should thin to something your body would call insufficient. It does not. The humidity alone should make each breath feel like drowning in reverse, your lungs pulling moisture instead of oxygen. Somehow—this is not a gift, merely a fact of the place—the atmosphere retains density while remaining breathable. Your body will accept it. Your mind may not.

**Practical concerns:**

- Carry no altitude medication. The pressure here is deceptive; standard calculations will mislead you. Your pulse oximeter will read strangely low by sea-level standards and yet you will not feel hypoxic. Trust the sensation, not the number.
- Sulfur is present. The geothermal systems carry it upward through fissures, and on still mornings the smell will reach you before the steam. It is not toxic in the concentrations you will encounter, but it will coat your respiratory tract with a metallic tang that persists for hours. If you have sensitivities to volcanic gases, bring protection—not because the Peaks are hostile, but because the Peaks do not care about your sensitivities.
- Acclimate for two days before ascending above the valley floor. The humidity alone will tax systems unused to breathing air this thick with moisture. You will feel, for a time, that you are breathing through wet cloth. Your body will adjust. Most bodies adjust.

### The Breath of the Mountains

At dawn and dusk, the peaks exhale. This is not metaphor. The thermal systems pulse; humidity condenses; mist rises from every fissure simultaneously, and for a brief interval the entire massif seems to be inhaling and exhaling on a timescale that has nothing to do with human respiration but feels, unmistakably, like it anyway.

There is no danger in this. There is only the experience of standing inside a breath that is not yours.

## II. Water, Weather, and the Daily Rain

### The Cycles

Every afternoon, without exception, the clouds that formed from morning heat will release their water. The rain arrives suddenly—a wall of it moving up the valleys, preceded by a pressure drop you will feel in your ears before you feel it on your skin. It lasts two hours, sometimes three. It is not gentle.

**Prepare for:**

- Continuous daily soaking. Nothing stays dry here unless it is sealed. Choose gear accordingly.
- Flash conditions in drainage channels. The ancient engineers built channels that still function; they will be running fast during and after rain. Do not attempt to cross running water above knee height.
- Leech-like organisms in standing water. They are small, nearly transparent, and their bite is painless. They carry nothing transmissible to baseline humans, but the woundsite will swell and itch for days. Check yourself after any water exposure.

### The Water Itself

The streams and pools are warm in many places—thermal runoff mixing with precipitation—and cool in others. The chemistry is mineral-rich, alkaline in thermal pools, neutral in rain-fed streams. You can drink from any source you would trust at home. The mountain filters what the sea will eventually reclaim.

## III. The Terrain

### Rock and Erosion

The stone here is deceptive. In places, millennia of dissolution have left it soft—not weak, but *finished*, crumbling under pressure into iron-scented powder. In other places, it remains as hard as any basalt you've climbed.

**Rules of thumb:**

- Test before you trust. A firm push with your hand will tell you what a visual inspection cannot.
- Avoid overhangs during or after rain. The thermal cycling and moisture infiltration create conditions where sections can calve without warning.
- The rock is stratified. Each layer represents a different age of emergence. You can read time in the cross-section of a cliff face, if you have the patience. Do not read time while standing beneath a cliff face.

### Paths and Navigation

There are no marked trails. There are paths—the mycorrhizal bridges—but they are not made for human weight and will require caution. The ancient structures sometimes connect via these routes; sometimes the bridges span gaps that the structures' builders never intended human visitors to cross.

Navigation is difficult. The canopy blocks sky; the mist obscures landmarks; the valleys branch in three dimensions. Carry reference points you can feel rather than see. The bioluminescent networks pulse in patterns that some travelers learn to read; others find them disorienting. Know your own tolerances.

---

## IV. The Living Light

### The Mycorrhizal Network

The fungal-root system that spans the Peaks is not a single organism, but it functions as one. Its bioluminescence is not constant—it responds to stimuli, and one of those stimuli is human presence.

When you approach a dense section of root bridge or enter a structure that the network touches, you will see it brighten. Dim, then bright. Like recognition. Like attention.

**What this means:**

- It sees you. This is not metaphor; the network processes information, and you register in it.
- It does not mean harm. Nothing in the record of travelers here suggests that the network has injured anyone. But it knows you are there. It will remember.
- If you are traveling with someone who has died here (unburied), the network near them will dim. This is useful information.

### Night Light

At night, the roots glow faintly—enough to navigate by, not enough to read by. Viewed from below, the light pulses through the canopy in waves, occasionally synchronous, occasionally staggered. Travelers sometimes mistake this for stars. It is not stars. It is the ground, lit by what grows in it.

## V. The Structures and Their Machines

### What You'll Find

The builders of Suth Marang carved deep. Their structures cling to cliff faces, hang over valleys, tunnel into rock. Most are sealed by sliding panels that respond to pressure and heat—place your palm against the surface for ten seconds, and the panel may recognize the warmth and slide. Or it may not. The responsiveness varies by structure and seems to have nothing to do with age.

Inside, you will find:

- **Stone mechanisms** that still function: counterweights, channels, gates that rise and fall in slow cycles tied to thermal expansion. Do not assume they are empty. Do not assume they are safe. They were built for a purpose; that purpose may have changed.
- **Cisterns** that fill and drain on schedules governed by rainfall and evaporation. The water in them is clean by any measure.
- **Rooms** that are simply rooms, their original function unknowable. Some contain remnants—shapes in stone that might have been furniture, hearths that might have been altars, windows that open onto views that have not changed in centuries.
- **Deep chambers** where the thermal systems are hottest and the air is thickest. These are sealed from casual access. The seals are not mechanical. If you find one open, do not enter alone.

### What This Means

The tech here is not electronic. It is not mechanical in any familiar sense. It responds to pressure, heat, proximity, the weight of water, the rhythms of expansion and contraction. Someone built these systems for a landscape that was already ancient, and the systems have not stopped.

You will not understand how they work. You can learn to use them. A panel that responds to warmth will respond to your warmth. A gate that rises with thermal pressure will rise when you light a fire beneath it. The grammar is learnable, but it is not written down.

## VI. Fauna

### The Thermal Birds

In the columns of rising heat, flocks of creatures between bird and lizard wheel and glide without apparent effort. They are beautiful. They are not dangerous. They are uninterested in you. Their feathers catch light strangely; their calls layer into a sound that seems to come from the air itself. At evening, listen for them. At dawn, watch for their silhouettes against the steam.

### The Canopy Pack

In the dense forests below, creatures the size of dogs move in coordinated groups. Their eyes reflect light; their calls are low and resonant, traveling farther than they should. They are not predatory toward humans. They are, however, territorial, and they will let you know if you are perceived as encroaching.

**If you hear the hum while in the canopy:**

- Remain still. They are assessing.
- Do not climb higher. They have the advantage there.
- Wait. They will lose interest or reveal themselves. Either way, you will learn something.

### The Waters

The streams and pools teem with life you will not have names for. Fish glow faintly; invertebrates drift through thermal currents; things that might be amphibians bask on warm rocks. None of it is aggressive. None of it is edible by baseline human standards, though the fish do not seem to know this. Do not attempt to catch them. They are faster than they look, and the thermal systems make the water more complex than it appears.

## VII. The Sounds

### What You Will Hear

The Peaks do not fall silent. Not ever.

- **Water moving through channels**, constantly, a drip-and-rush that forms the baseline of the soundscape. The ancient engineers built for this; every structure channels water somewhere, and the channels have not silted.
- **The hum of the networks**, low and constant, a vibration you feel more than hear until you learn to separate it from wind noise.
- **Wind through the canopy**, carrying the smell of iron and growth, sometimes rising to something that resembles music on still evenings.
- **At night, the mountain singing**—a harmonic resonance that seems to come from the stone itself, low and sustained and unchanging. This is the oldest sound in Suth Marang. It does not vary. It does not care.

### What This Means for Sleep

You will sleep lightly. This is not a warning. It is a description. The sounds do not threaten, but they insist. Your nervous system will learn to filter them, eventually, but the first nights will be spent half-aware, cycling between sleep and the awareness of water moving through channels beneath your shelter.

This is the mountain's way. You are not its guest. You are simply present.

---

## VIII. The Presence

### The Feeling

Travelers report this differently. Some call it atmosphere. Some call it the weight of history. Some call it the attention of something vast and patient.

You will feel, after a day or two, that the mountain is aware of you. Not watching—watching implies eyes. Not thinking—thinking implies a mind that operates like yours. But *aware*, in a way that is not hostile and not welcoming and has nothing to do with you at all. You are a small warm thing on a vast patient body. The body does not care about you. The body does not not-care about you. You are simply there, and the mountain is also there, and for the duration of your visit these facts will coexist.

### The Deep Caves

The oldest systems are in the deepest caves, where the thermal systems run hottest and the air is thickest with breath that is not yours. Something may live there. Something may have always lived there. The travelers who have ventured deepest have not returned with consistent reports. Some have not returned at all.

Do not enter the deep caves without preparation, without company, without a reason that outweighs the risks. The mountain will not stop you. The machines will not stop you. Only your own judgment can do that.

## IX. Practical Preparations

### Gear

- **Sealed waterproofing for everything you cannot afford to lose wet.** Ziplock bags are not beneath you.
- **Breathable rain gear.** You will be wet constantly. Gear that repels water while allowing sweat to escape will keep you from hypothermia in the thermal swings.
- **Footwear with grip on wet stone.** The basalt is slick when wet, soft when dry, and you will encounter both in a single descent.
- **Headlamp.** The bioluminescence is not enough for technical work.
- **Water purification, even though the water is clean.** Old habits.
- **Heat protection for your hands.** The panels are warm. The stone in places is hot. Burns here are slow to heal in the humidity.

### Mental Preparation

- **Expect disorientation.** Altitude, humidity, density, and the constant sensory input will alter your perception. This is the mountain changing you. You will not notice it until you leave.
- **Do not plan to stay more than two weeks.** Travelers who have stayed longer report changes—dreams that feel like memories, an attachment to the sound of water moving through channels, a reluctance to leave. The mountain is not trapping them. They are simply beginning to match.
- **Bring something to remember your origin.** A photograph, a token, a voice recording. You will want, later, to be sure you came from somewhere else.

### What to Leave Behind

Nothing that cannot survive humidity. Nothing that broadcasts a signal—the machines here may not be electronic, but they respond to energy in ways you do not understand, and it is wise not to give them more than you must.

Nothing that belongs to the dead. The mycorrhizal network dims near the unburied dead. You do not want to be the reason it dims.

---

## X. Departure

When you leave, you will leave changed. This is not negotiable. The mountain will have breathed on you, and you will carry the exhale.

The structures will not miss you. The machines do not remember. The networks will dim where you stood and brighten again, and the mountain will continue its slow dissolution into the clouds that form from its own heat, and the water will continue its path to the sea, and somewhere in the patient systems something will continue learning what this place is for.

You will not have learned that. No one has.

But you will have visited, and survived, and woken at dawn to find water condensing on every surface, and the peaks breathing in the morning light, and for a moment—before the day warms and the clouds burn away—you will have seen Suth Marang as it is: not a landscape, but a vast organism, something old and patient and still very much alive.

That is worth the preparation.

---

*Safe travels. The mountain is waiting.*
