Come to the Weeping Plateau as you might come to an audience with something that does not know it is being observed. Do not bring expectations. Do not bring schedules. The plateau operates on durations that render your visit a single breath, and you must make peace with that breath being enough.

The moisture will be your first teacher. It does not fall as rain falls elsewhere—it emerges, it settles, it inhabits. Everything you carry must understand this. Your clothing will never dry. Your electronics, if you bring them, will fail or be failed by the damp that works its way into every seam and seal. Your skin will prune and stay pruned. Your hair will hang limp. This is not a hardship to be overcome; it is simply the condition of being here, and you will find that once you stop fighting it, once you let yourself become damp the way the stone is damp and the root-mass is damp and the air itself is damp, something loosens. You begin to fit.

Bring nothing that cannot get wet and emerge wetter. This sounds simple, but it changes everything. Leather will mold. Paper will pulp. Cotton will become a second skin in the worst possible way. Synthetics that claim quick-dry are lying or at least exaggerating—the air will not accept your moisture no matter how aggressively you wring. Choose materials that move with the damp rather than against it: wool that holds its structure when saturated, tightly woven synthetics, rubber or oiled canvas for the things that must stay dry. And accept that some things will get wet anyway, and that this is fine, and that the plateau does not care about your inconvenience.

The light here is a rumor. The canopy above canopies filters everything to a green-gold haze that never quite resolves into day or night. Your eyes will adjust if you give them time—days, perhaps a week—but until then, carry light that does not depend on your failing vision. A headlamp, dimmed, carried at the hip or in a pocket, ready. The bioluminescent fungi will guide you in the deepest stretches, pale blue-green pulses that seem almost to brighten as you approach and dim as you pass. Follow them if you must, but do not rely on them for direction. They are not signs. They are simply life, doing what life does here.

Your feet will need to learn a different language. The ground is not ground—it is root and decay and the slow exudation of a living mass that breathes beneath you. It gives. It shifts. A step that seems solid may sink to the ankle, and a step that seems uncertain may prove firmer than the stone it conceals. Move slowly. Test each placement. Do not trust the surface to be what it appears; trust instead the patience of your own weight settling, the feedback of your soles learning the hidden grammar of this place. Footwear should be low, flexible, capable of feeling what lies beneath—bare feet, if you can bear the leeches and the soft rot of the decomposing matter, are perhaps the most honest choice, but boots with flexible soles and good drainage will serve if you cannot go barefoot into this intimacy.

Breathe through your mouth at first, until you acclimate. The air has weight, has presence, has a taste that is not unpleasant but is insistent—the mineral dampness of water that has kissed ancient stone, the organic whisper of decay and growth intertwined, something almost sweet and almost sour that settles on the tongue like a question. Your lungs will darken with moisture, and this is normal, and your body will adapt, but in the early days the mouth-breathing helps you not to feel overwhelmed. Drink more water than you think you need, not because you are losing it visibly—you are not sweating as you would in a desert—but because your body is processing humidity in ways you do not feel, and dehydration will creep up on you disguised as fatigue.

Come without hurry. There are no clearings to cross, no points of interest to efficiently consume. There are densities, and densities are not traversed; they are entered, inhabited, moved through at the speed of the place itself. A mile here takes hours. A mile here might be nothing at all—a slight change in the quality of the weeping, a shift in the dominant fungi, a different texture in the mat of growth beneath you. Set no distances. Mark no time. The plateau will tell you when you have been somewhere long enough, and it will tell you when you should leave, and you will feel these messages as a kind of resonance, a settling in the chest, a loosening or tightening that has nothing to do with reason and everything to do with attention.

Do not look for meaning. The builders who were here—who were here so long ago that their stone walls have become indistinguishable from the living hill—left no record, and the plateau has absorbed their works the way it absorbs water. Whatever they came here for, whatever they believed they were doing, it has been rendered down into substrate, into history that the land no longer remembers because it never quite understood it in the first place. You are more temporary than they were. Your works will not outlast theirs. Your presence will not leave a mark that matters. This is not nihilism; it is clarity. You are a guest in a space that predates the concept of guest, and your job is not to leave your mark but to receive one.

The quiet will teach you what the sound cannot. There are sounds here—the weeping of the cliffs, the whisper of scaled bodies in the understory, the occasional call of creatures you will never see—but they are sparse, separated by long silences that feel chosen. When the silence comes, let it come. Do not fill it with music or chatter or the hum of a device. Sit with it. The plateau is not silent; it is listening. And if you can learn to listen with it, to be quiet in the same way that the stone is quiet and the roots are quiet and the centuries are quiet, you will find that the silence has texture, has depth, has something almost like communication. The land does not speak, but it hums, and if you are still enough, you can feel the vibration in your teeth, in your sternum, in the spaces between your thoughts.

Bring little. Take less. The plateau is already complete, already total, and your additions to it will not be welcome in the way you imagine welcome. What you bring, bring because you need it to survive. What you take, take only in the sense of attention, of witness, of the memories you will carry out of this place and into whatever comes after. The plateau does not want your offerings. It does not want your story. It wants only what any ancient thing wants from the brief creatures who pass through it: that you have been here, that you have seen it, that you have understood, even partially, even for a moment, what it means to be something that has endured past all comprehension.

You will not understand it. Not fully. But if you come prepared to fail at understanding, prepared to be small and slow and temporary in the presence of something immeasurably patient, you will find that the failure is enough. The smallness is enough. The plateau does not require your comprehension. It only requires your presence, held gently, released easily, offered without condition and withdrawn without regret.

This is how you prepare: by preparing to be unprepared. By accepting that the Weeping Plateau will do what it has always done—absorb you, in some small way, the way it absorbs light and water and meaning, making you part of its slow accumulation. By letting yourself become weeping, too.
