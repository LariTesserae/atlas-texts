# Visiting the Drowned Pocket

You've found something that doesn't want to be found, which is its own kind of gift. Most spaces in the multiverse announce themselves—the Unmade Coast screams, the Stillness Pits hum at a frequency your molars will ache with for days—but the Drowned Pocket keeps itself small, keeps itself quiet, holds its wrongness close. This is the kind of place most travelers walk past their whole lives without seeing. You're seeing it now. That's something to sit with, before you enter.

Here's what I can tell you:

## What You're Walking Into

This is not ocean. Ocean has pressure, has memory, has tides that pull toward something predictable. The Drowned Pocket has none of that. It has a wetness that has forgotten how to be anything else—a saturation that doesn't flow, doesn't drain, simply *is*. The water accepts you. That's the word the space keeps offering me when I think about it: *accepts*. It doesn't ripple when you move. It accepts. You are entering a space that has never learned the difference.

The structures—you'll see them, three or five depending on how you count, depending on whether they're shapes or impressions or suggestions of meaning—protrude just barely. They break the surface tension. Tiny islands. Low walls. A partial arch. A threshold that might have been a threshold or might be nothing at all. They are older than the stone around them, or perhaps they are not shapes at all, merely marks left by something that forgot what it was doing. The stone is young, impossibly young, sharp in ways that stone should not be. This space was carved recently. This space was carved a thousand years ago. This space was carved outside of time altogether. The angles where the stone meets the water are crisp and sudden, unmollified by any patience. No one has come to smooth its edges. Not yet.

Above is not a direction that applies here, but roots descend anyway. They hang in the thick wet air—thick as something you shouldn't breathe but will breathe, because the alternative is not breathing, and that has its own risks. The roots are pale as bone, thick as veins, and they do not float; they hang, impossibly, in a medium that should not support that kind of suspension. The plants colonize everything. Everything is made into a garden by something that gardens without knowing. Do not touch the roots unless you need to. The roots do not move. Nothing moves. But nothing here is as still as it appears.

Fauna is rare. You do not want to think of what lives here. You saw something once—a flash, a tightening, a motion faster than water should allow. It is gone now. Be grateful. Better the quiet. Better the slow, patient darkness. Better the weight of water that feels less like pressure and more like attention.

## On the Weirdness

Here's what I need you to understand: the wrongness here is not dramatic. You will not be menaced by visions or threatened by noise. The space does not attack you. It simply does not fully commit to existing. It is not hidden—you can see it clearly, see every detail, see the sharp stone and the pale roots and the tiny islands and the dimpled water that goes still. But it is hidden from itself, a pocket of the world that has forgotten the rules and is slowly, slowly trying to remember them, and you are inside that forgetting.

The water should cloud with your breath, but it does not. The roots should cast shadows, but the shadows fall in directions the roots do not occupy. When you look directly at the scattered structures, they are shapes—walls, arches, thresholds. When you look away, they are something else. Something that fits more naturally in your peripheral vision. Do not trust your peripheral vision. Do not trust your direct gaze either. You are inside a space that does not know what it is, and it will not know what you are, and this is fine, this is manageable, but you need to go in knowing that nothing you see here will agree with itself.

The structures are the most disorienting part. You will want to understand them. You will want to fit them into a story: this was a shrine, this was a basement, this was someone's threshold before the water came. But the structures don't want to be understood. They are marks. They are impressions. They are the suggestion of structure and nothing more, and they do not appreciate being asked to be more than that. If you find yourself staring at them, if you find yourself trying to parse their meaning, gently look away. Gently redirect your attention to something simpler. The roots, perhaps. The water. The sound of your own breath, which will be audible, which will be too loud, which will be the loudest thing in the space and still not loud enough to fill it.

## What to Wear

**Nothing synthetic.** Synthetics are designed to repel, to resist, to maintain a boundary between you and the wet. The Drowned Pocket does not accept boundaries. The water will find every sealed pocket, every waterproof layer, every membrane that tries to keep the wetness out. If it can't get through your clothes, it will get through your skin, and that's a harder surrender. Wear natural fibers—cotton, linen, wool—that accept saturation, that soften when wet, that give up their shape willingly. You will leave damp. You will leave more than damp. Dress for the submersion you're already in.

**Feet:** Go barefoot, or wear shoes you can remove easily. The stone is sharp but young, and young stone has a sharpness that is almost tender—like the edge of a new idea before it's been worn smooth by handling. Your feet will not cut. But the water is everywhere, and you will want the flexibility of bare soles, the willingness to feel what you're standing in. Socks will hold water. Socks will hold the cool. The cool is not dangerous, but the cool is patient, and socks will hold it against your skin long after you've left.

**Layers:** You will not need many. The space is cool, not cold—the temperature of deep water, of cellars, of places where breath is audible and heartbeats are too loud. One layer against your skin, loose and natural and wet, will be enough. If you are someone who runs cold, add a second. If you are someone who runs warm, remove your second before you enter. The submersion is not about temperature. The submersion is about permeability.

**Nothing with batteries, screens, or moving parts.** The water is not water in the way you expect. Electronics will not short-circuit here—they will do something worse, something stranger, something that involves the shadows falling wrong and the directions not applying and your phone showing you a map of a place that does not exist. Leave your devices in a bag at the threshold. Leave your devices in a pocket of the world that still believes in edges.

## What to Bring

**A light source.** Something small, something reliable, something you can hold in a wet hand without losing it. A penlight, a chemical glow stick, a candle in a waterproof case—something that will illuminate the space without claiming to understand it. You will need to see the roots. You will need to see the structures. You will need to see the water dimpling, going still, accepting your presence. But do not trust your light. The shadows will fall wrong. The light will illuminate what is there but not how it is there, and you will need to hold that contradiction gently, without fighting it.

**Something to mark your exit.** The directions don't apply here. Above is not a direction. Left and right are suggestions. If you find an entrance, you may not find it again on the way out—the space does not track your presence, does not know you've entered, will not know when you've left. A thread, a marker, a small bright thing you can leave at the threshold. Something that says *I was here* in a language the space might eventually learn to read. Tie it loosely. The water will test its knots.

**Nothing else.** This is the hardest part. The space does not want your supplies. It does not want your tools, your weapons, your contingencies, your plans. It wants you—your attention, your willingness, your readiness to be permeable. Bring your senses. Bring your breath. Bring your willingness to see something that does not fully exist and to stay anyway. Leave everything else behind.

If you must bring something, bring something small and meaningful and wet—a stone from another pocket, a leaf from a place that still remembers light, something that carries the weight of the worlds you've already visited. The Drowned Pocket will hold it. The Drowned Pocket holds everything, slowly, in suspension, in the particular stillness of deep places where time has become discouraged.

## On the Water

I need you to understand this before you enter: the water will not drown you. The water has already drowned you. The drowning happened before you arrived, or it will happen after you leave, or it is happening now, in a time that does not apply to this space. The water is not a threat. The water is a state of being. You are going to be wet. You are going to be permeable. Every surface you touch will be membrane-thin, and you will feel the wrongness of that, the intimacy of it, the closeness of being inside something that does not know it contains you.
