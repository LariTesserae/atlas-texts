# A Traveler's Preparation Guide for the Plateau of Still Water

## Understanding Where You Are Going

Before you pack a single item, before you take your first step through whatever threshold will deliver you to this place, you must understand that you are traveling to a location where time has reached its conclusion—not ended, but finished its work. The Plateau of Still Water is not frozen in the way that ice preserves or that desperate people wish for youth. It is frozen in the way that a completed sentence is frozen: the words are there, the meaning is there, but no new words will come. The negotiation between life and land has been settled. The settlement holds.

This is the fact that will orient everything else.

You are not going somewhere wild, though the basalt underfoot has not yet learned the smoothness that centuries would give it. You are not going somewhere ruined, though the city that climbs the plateau speaks of human hands rather than machines. You are going somewhere that has become itself completely—a place so assured of its own nature that it has no need to be anything else. The birds do not migrate not because they cannot, but because the equilibrium does not require it. The water does not rise or fall not because it is trapped, but because the balance was found so early that it has never needed to shift.

Prepare yourself accordingly.

## Physical Preparation

### Footwear

Bring the best footwear you own or can obtain. The basalt is sharp in the way of geological youth—it has not yet worn itself into tolerance. The edges of the stone will catch and cut. Your soles will be tested by faces of rock that have never learned to yield.

Choose boots with firm ankle support, as the uneven ground between clusters of buildings will demand attention. The moss that holds moisture across the plateau will make certain stretches slick in ways that are not obvious until you are already slipping. You will want good traction, but you will also want enough sensitivity in the sole that you can feel the stone through your boot—feel where the sharp places are, where the stable places are—because your feet will need to learn a new kind of attention on this plateau.

Bring extra pairs of wool socks. The moisture held by the moss and the altitude will work together to make your feet damp, and wet feet on sharp stone in a cool climate will become cold feet, and cold feet on sharp stone will become something worse. Change your socks. Dry your feet when you can. The small kindnesses you give your body in a stable environment will matter more here, where nothing else is changing to compensate for your neglect.

### Clothing

Dress in layers. The wind that slides across the plateau without obstruction will find every gap in your clothing, and at this altitude, the wind is not merely moving air—it is a patient and thorough element that will cool you without ever making you feel cold enough to protest. You will simply find yourself colder than you expected, and by then the chill will have settled.

A base layer of wool or another material that retains warmth when damp. A middle layer substantial enough to hold against the wind even when the wind finds a way through your outer shell. An outer layer that breaks the wind—that treats it as an adversary to be deflected rather than endured. A hat that covers your ears. Gloves that allow you to work with your hands while still covering your fingers.

The locals will be dressed similarly. Watch how they move through their city. Notice where their clothing fails them—invisible gaps where the wind enters. Learn from their small discomforts. They have lived here long enough to know what works and what does not, and their knowledge is written in their garments.

Bring one item of clothing that you do not mind getting damaged. The sharp stone will catch and tear. The moss will hold moisture that stains. You will kneel on wet ground to examine something, and your knees will remember. Accept this. The plateau does not adapt to you; you must adapt to the plateau. Your clothing is part of that adaptation.

### Water and Food

The plateau has water. The basins are fed by springs that issue from the young rock's cracks with a steadiness that has not varied since the first memory or the first measurement. You will not go thirsty. But bring your own water discipline anyway. The stability of the plateau means that your body will need to establish its own new equilibrium, and water will help with this. Drink more than you think you need. The altitude and wind will dry you from the inside even when your throat does not register thirst.

Bring food that travels well. The city will have markets, will have bakeries and granaries and the evidence of craft and labor. You will be able to eat what the plateau produces. But you will arrive having traveled through whatever you traveled through, and your body will need time to accept the food of this new place. Bring familiar food for the first days. Rice. Dried fruit. Hard bread. Something that your body recognizes as sustenance even when everything else is strange.

Do not bring food that will attract the small creatures who live on the plateau. The birds and insects of this place are not migratory. They have never known to do otherwise. They are accustomed to the world being exactly this shape, and they are not prepared for novelty. A novel food might draw them to you in ways that are inconvenient for both of you. More importantly, they are not afraid of humans. They do not startle. They simply are. Your food will simply be, too, if you do not guard it.

### Navigation

Bring a compass. The plateau's stability means that landmarks do not change, which sounds like a gift for navigation but is actually a subtle challenge—everything looks the way it has always looked, and so everything looks the same. The clusters of buildings are separated by stretches of ground that feel similar. The water basins are distributed across the plateau with a pattern that is not random but is not immediately legible.

Learn to read the moss. It grows to a specific height before stopping, as if obeying a mark. It tells you which way the wind goes. It tells you which walls have shelter and which face the persistent air. It tells you where the water gathers and where the ground stays dry. If you learn to see what the moss knows, you will never be lost on this plateau.

Bring paper for maps. Even if you acquire a map from a local, even if you memorize the city's layout, you will want to draw your own. The act of drawing will help you see. The plateau will reward your attention with its details, and a map you draw will show you what you actually understand rather than what you believe you understand.

### The First Aid You Will Actually Need

Not dramatic injuries. Not the wounds that emergency medicine exists to address. Bring what will address the small damage this place will do to you: salve for the cuts your boots will not prevent, for the scrapes from sharp stone, for the irritation that comes from damp clothing held too close to skin. Blister tape. Something for dry skin—the altitude and the wind will chap you even in summer. Eye drops, because the wind will dry your eyes in ways that are uncomfortable before they become dangerous.

Bring ibuprofen or another pain reliever for the headaches that altitude brings. You are going higher than you may have been before. Your body will need days to adjust, and during those days, your head will ache.

Bring something for anxiety. Not because the plateau is frightening—it is not—but because the stasis will do something to your mind if you are not prepared for it. More on this in the next section. But know now that you should bring whatever helps you stay calm. You will need calm.

## Psychological Preparation

This is where your preparation must be most careful.

The Plateau of Still Water is not hostile. Nothing here wants to harm you. The small creatures do not see you as prey or threat; they see you as a new kind of thing in a world that has never before contained you, and their response is neither fear nor curiosity but a complete absence of response. They do not startle. They do not adapt. They simply continue. You will be the one who must adapt, and the adjustment will not be physical only.

### The Experience of Stasis

You have lived your life in places that change. Seasons shift. Buildings rise and fall. The people you know age and grow and die. Even in the most stable environments, there is change at some scale—the light through a window moves, the smell of rain approaches, the neighbor's dog barks at a different time than yesterday. Change is the medium in which you swim.

On the plateau, you will surface.

The plateau is where change went when it finished its work. The water that gathers in the basins does not rise or fall because there has never been drought, because the balance was found so early that it has never needed to shift. The flowers that bloom in spring bloom when the stone reaches the temperature that triggers blooming, which is always now. You will arrive and the world will be exactly as it has been, exactly as it will be long after you leave. The birds present in summer will be present in winter—not the same individuals, but their kind, doing the same things, in the same places, at the same hours.

You will notice this first as peace. The plateau is restful. There is no urgency here. Nothing is growing out of control; nothing is dying before its time. The silence between sounds is not empty so much as full of a patience that does not know it is waiting. You will breathe more slowly. Your shoulders will drop. Your jaw will unclench.

Then you will notice that you cannot tell if hours have passed or days. You will find yourself standing still, watching the water in a basin, waiting for something to change. You will realize that you are waiting for the change you always expect—the ripple that means a fish rose, the shift in light that means clouds are gathering, the movement that means the world is still alive because it is still capable of being other than it is. But nothing changes. The water speaks its small continuous speaking. The moss holds its moisture like a secret. The wind slides across the stone with a persistence that has not carved the stone into patience, not yet, because the patience here is in you, in your failure to accept what the plateau already knows.

This is the danger of the plateau: not that it will harm you, but that you will try to harm yourself by forcing it to be other than what it is.

### Accepting the Eternal Present

You cannot change the plateau. You cannot introduce variability that will persist. If you plant a seed, it will grow or not grow according to the plateau's conditions, and if it grows, it will grow into whatever species is suited to this altitude and this soil and this moisture, and the plateau will absorb it, will make it part of the settlement, will continue. You will have added nothing. You will have changed nothing. The plateau does not need you to improve it.

You must arrive ready to be absorbed in the same way.

This is not passivity. The people who live in the city built their homes here. They quarried the stone and mortared the joints and hewed the timber from the valleys below. They made choices—deliberate spacing, as the description says, of choosing. They did not make the plateau conform to them; they made themselves conform to the plateau, and in that conformation, they found room for human life. The settlement knows this: that it is built from what the land gives, and gives nothing back that the land did not already contain.

You can build here. You can make things with your hands. You can add your labor to the labor of those who came before you. But you cannot add yourself to the plateau in the way that you add yourself to places that are still in negotiation with life. The plateau has finished its negotiation. Your role is not to renegotiate.

### The Silence

Sound here is an event, not a constant. The wind makes its sound. The water makes its sound—a small, continuous speaking that does not rise or fall. Footsteps make sound. Voices make sound. But between these things, the silence holds.

You may find this silence unbearable at first. Humans are not built for such emptiness; we fill silence with our own noise, with humming and talking and the small sounds we make to prove we are still here. On the plateau, you will need to learn to exist without that proof. The silence is not empty. It is patient. It has been here since the plateau began, and it will be here long after you leave. It does not need you to fill it.

Sit with the silence. Let it teach you what it knows. The silence knows that nothing is required of it. The silence knows that presence does not need to announce itself. The silence knows that you are here, and that the wind is here, and that the water is here, and that all of these things can be here together without needing to say so.

You may find, after days or weeks, that the silence no longer frightens you. You may find that you look forward to it—the moments between sounds when the world is simply what it is, when you are simply what you are. Or you may find that you never adjust, that the silence remains a pressure on your chest, an unspoken question about whether you have done enough, whether you have been enough, whether your existence has been marked.

If you cannot adjust, leave. The plateau will not hold it against you. It is patient, but it does not need you to be patient. It will continue without you, in the way that it has continued without everyone who has ever visited and left.

### Time and the Loss of Seasons

Bring no calendar. The plateau does not have seasons in the way that you understand seasons. There are temperatures that rise and fall, but the change is so gentle, so balanced, that it does not constitute a season—it constitutes a cycle within a larger equilibrium that does not require the dramatic swing from plenty to scarcity and back again that your life has probably required.

You will miss the seasons. You will miss the relief of winter's end, the anticipation of summer's decline. You will miss the way that time was marked by change—by the first flower, the first frost, the first day short enough to feel like a gift. On the plateau, every day will feel the same, and the sameness will become its own kind of pressure.

Mark time differently. Mark it by the creatures who emerge in their season and die in their season and are replaced by the next kind. Mark it by the water level in the basins—though it does not change, you can pretend it does, can pretend that your attention matters. Mark it by the food you eat, the work you do, the conversations you have with the people who live here.

Or let time stop marking you. The plateau will allow this. The silence will hold you in the eternal present, and you can be held there, and the holding is not prison so much as rest.

## Social Preparation

### The People of the City

The city is built by human hands. Mortared joints hand-troweled. Roof beams hewn from timber. The evidence of labor is everywhere, and with labor comes culture, comes hierarchy, comes the small frictions and pleasures of human community.

The people here have chosen this plateau. They have chosen the stasis, or they have chosen to build a life within the stasis, which is different from choosing stasis but leads to the same place. They know what it is to live in a world that does not change, and they have found ways to be human anyway.

Approach them as you would approach anyone in any city. They will have their own concerns—trade, craft, family, the small worries of daily life. The plateau's stability means that their concerns do not include survival in the dramatic sense; they will not face famine or flood or the chaos that comes from environments that have not yet settled. Their concerns will be human concerns: relationships, meaning, the question of what to do with a life that has time to ask questions.

Listen to their questions. Answer their questions about you, if they ask. You are a novelty here—not alarming, but notable. You come from a place where time still moves, where seasons still swing, where the world has not finished negotiating with itself. This will interest them or bore them or some combination of both. Be honest about where you come from. Be honest about what you feel when you stand on the sharp basalt and look out at the water basins and the moss and the city that climbs the plateau.

They will tell you things. About the city, about the plateau, about the negotiations that led to the settlement. Some of what they tell you will be history. Some will be story. Some will be the particular kind of truth that locals know about places they have loved too long to see clearly. Receive all of it. The plateau is large enough to contain multiple truths.

### Local Knowledge

The locals know things that no book can give you. Where the springs rise from the rock. Which walls hold the ferns that crowd the north faces. What time the birds begin their daily rituals, and what those rituals mean, if they mean anything at all. How high the moss climbs before it stops, and why it stops there. Which water basins are safe to drink from and which have been claimed by families who do not want visitors.

Ask for this knowledge. Receive it with gratitude. Test it slowly—locals have lived with the plateau long enough to be adapted to it in ways that you cannot be in days or weeks, and their advice may not account for the specific vulnerabilities of your body, your history, your ignorance.

But do not dismiss their knowledge because you think you know better. The plateau does not respond to expertise the way other places do. It responds to attention. The locals have paid attention for generations, and their payoffs are written in their homes and their habits and the way they move through the city without looking where they step. They know the sharp stones. They know the slick moss. They know the places where the wind finds you and the places where it cannot reach.

Learn from them. Learn by watching. Learn by doing. Learn by failing and being helped up and failing again.

### Gifts

Bring something to give. Not because the plateau requires tribute—nothing here requires anything—but because you are a visitor and visitors give gifts, and the exchange will open doors that money cannot open.

What you bring depends on where you are coming from, but some suggestions: a tool from your home dimension—something small, practical, that does something the locals' tools do not. Spices that the plateau cannot produce. Seeds that might grow if the plateau allows them. A story about where you come from—stories are gifts too, and good ones are rare.

Accept gifts in return. The plateau will give you things, not as transactions but as acknowledgments. You will find objects left where you will find them. You will be invited to meals in homes you did not know you could enter. You will be shown views that visitors are not usually shown. These are the plateau's gifts. Receive them.

## Sensory Preparation

### What You Will Smell

The air smells of stone and moisture and the particular green of plants that have accepted what they are. The stone is young basalt—sharp, unweathered, still learning to be rock. The moisture comes from the basins, from the springs, from the moss that holds its secrets. The green comes from the ferns and the flowers and the hardy plants that have found their place on this plateau and stopped looking for another.

Carry the memory of other smells with you. You will need it when the plateau's smell becomes so familiar that you stop noticing it. The familiarity will happen faster than you expect. You will wake one morning and realize that you cannot remember what the air smelled like when you arrived, and the forgetting will feel like loss.

### What You Will Hear

The wind makes its sound. The water makes its sound—a small, continuous speaking that does not rise or fall. Footsteps on stone make sound. Voices make sound.

Between these things, the silence holds.

Prepare yourself for the silence. It is not absence. It is presence—the presence of a world that does not need to announce itself, that simply is, that continues to be without requiring your witness. The silence is full of patience, and the patience is not waiting for anything. It is contentment.

You will find the silence in your sleep. You will find it in the moments between thoughts. You will find it most deeply when you sit still and listen, and realize that the world does not need you to listen, that it has been speaking to itself all along, and the speaking is what the silence holds.

### What You Will Feel

The wind will find every gap in your clothing. The stone will find every gap in your footwear. The damp will find every part of you that has not been protected. You will be touched constantly by a world that does not know you are visiting.

But you will also be touched by stillness. You will feel the ground beneath you, solid and ancient-young, carrying you as it has carried every creature who has ever stood here. You will feel the sun on your face, when it finds you, and the absence of sun, when clouds pass or when night comes and the temperature drops and the stars appear in a sky unobstructed by the larger elements that cities usually contain.

You will feel time stop. Or rather, you will feel yourself stop reaching for time, stop expecting the next season, stop waiting for the change that your life has always required. You will feel the plateau hold you in its equilibrium, and the holding will feel like rest.

## What to Expect Upon Arrival

You will step through whatever threshold delivers you to this place, and you will feel the wind immediately. It will be persistent and cool and it will not care that you have arrived. It will slide across the open ground and find your skin and begin its patient work of cooling you. You will wish you had dressed more carefully.

You will look down and see the basalt. Sharp faces of dark stone, angular and unworn. The evidence of geological youth, of a world that has not yet learned to be smooth. You will want to touch it. Do not touch it with your bare hands if you can avoid it. The stone will cut you, and the cuts will be small but present, and you will be reminded that the plateau does not make exceptions.

You will see the city in the distance, climbing the plateau in clusters, separated by stretches of ground where the moss holds moisture. You will begin to walk toward it, and the walk will be longer than it looks, and the ground will be more difficult than it looked, and by the time you reach the first building, you will understand that the plateau has already begun its work on you.

You will find shelter. You will find water. You will find food and rest and the small human kindnesses that the city's people will offer you. You will sleep, and your sleep will be deep, because the silence will hold you, because the wind will stop, because the water will speak its continuous speaking and you will not need to listen but you will hear anyway.

You will wake, and it will be morning, and nothing will have changed.

You will stay. Or you will leave. Either way, the plateau will not be changed by your presence. It will continue. The water will gather in the basins, the moss will climb the walls, the wind will slide across the young stone. The birds will be present, not migrating, not adapting, simply being.

This is not a dead place. The evidence of life is everywhere. But it is a place where life has finished its negotiation with the land, and the terms have been settled, and the settlement holds.

You are a traveler. The plateau will receive you if you let it. It will not ask you to stay. It will not ask you to leave. It will simply continue, and you can continue with it, or you can find your way back to wherever time still moves.

Either way, you will carry the plateau with you. The memory of the silence. The memory of the water that does not rise or fall. The memory of the patience that does not know it is waiting.

The plateau does not need you to remember it. But you will anyway. The plateau is that kind of place.

---

## Final Notes

Bring warm clothing. Bring sturdy boots. Bring your curiosity and your patience and your willingness to be absorbed into something larger than yourself.

Do not bring expectations. The plateau will not fulfill them. It will simply continue, and you can learn to continue with it, or you can learn that this is not the place for you, and both learning are valuable.

The plateau is not for everyone. It is for those who have finished their own negotiations with their own lands, or who are ready to begin them. It is for those who can sit in silence and not need the silence to speak. It is for those who can stand on sharp stone and feel the ground beneath them, solid and patient, carrying everything that has ever stood here, carrying you.

Go if you are ready. Stay if you are called. The plateau will be here either way.

It has always been here. It will always be here. The settlement holds.
