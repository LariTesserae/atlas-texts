# Traveler Preparation: The Climbing City

You have chosen to visit the Climbing City. This is not a warning, but you should understand what you are accepting before you arrive. The city does not reject visitors. It does not welcome them either. It metabolizes them, and the metabolic process is not violent but it is complete. You will leave the city changed, and you may not notice the change until months or years after you return. This is considered acceptable. The city has processed billions of bodies, and none of them left unchanged, and most of them survived, and the survivors did not consider the alteration a harm. But you should know before you go.

---

## What You Are Entering

The Climbing City is not one place. It is many places that have grown together until they are indistinguishable, and it continues growing. It sits on a plateau that rises from a world still forming—new geology pushing upward at the margins, obsidian crystallizing from cooling lava, basalt settling into shapes that have not yet learned their final form. The plateau is high enough that the atmosphere is thin and cold, cold enough that exposed water would freeze solid in minutes. But water does not freeze here. The city's technology maintains water in liquid state at temperatures that should turn it to ice, and this liquid water is everywhere: beading on surfaces, flowing through channels, saturating the air, running through every pipe in a second skin of infrastructure that the city has grown rather than built. The cold is present even when the water is not frozen. It is in the walls. It is in the ground. It is in the way your breath fogs and the fog settles and never quite dissipates.

There are no plants. The city rises from bare rock and composite materials that incorporate water-harvesting and thermal-regulation into their molecular structure. You will not find greenery, soil, or anything that grows in the way you understand growth. The city's growth is different.

The creatures are the most immediately apparent feature of the environment, and you should not let this obscure everything else. They are everywhere—not in swarms or crowds but present, filling every niche, moving through channels and pipes and the spaces between structures in a constant circulation that never stops. Some are small, the size of rats or insects, and you will hear them before you see them: the click of chitin on metal, the soft wet sounds of bodies moving through condensation, the rustle in pipes. Others are large, slow, attached to buildings like the buildings are their hosts—organisms that have merged with infrastructure so completely that you cannot tell where the creature ends and the structure begins. Others move through the air, leaving trails of light in their wake, their forms visible only as glow and motion. They are not all the same species. They are not all the same biology. The engineering here has created organisms from silicon and carbon and metal and light, organisms that need the city's infrastructure the way a body needs blood, organisms that serve functions within the city's systems that you cannot immediately parse.

The city is not alien in the way of foreign planets. It is alien in the way of collapsed categories. You cannot say where the built ends and the grown begins. You cannot say where the organism ends and the machine begins. You cannot say where the city ends and the world begins. The infrastructure saturates everything, but the saturation is not imposition—it is integration, the way blood saturates tissue, the way roots saturate soil. The city breathes with them, its metabolism conducted by a million small bodies and a thousand large ones, its circulation threaded through organisms that have become indistinguishable from the pipes they inhabit. The ground beneath you is layered, stratified, a palimpsest of construction and colonization and cohabitation. Pathways worn by passage. Channels cut by water. Towers rising from infrastructure too dense to parse. The scale is town-size at minimum, regional at best guess, and the guess is uncertain because the city extends in all directions and no survey has mapped it completely.

## Physical Preparation

### Clothing and Protection

The cold is constant. Even if you do not feel it immediately—if the city's ambient systems provide some warmth at entry points—you will feel it eventually, and you should prepare as if you will feel it from the first moment. Layer clothing that can handle moisture. Everything here is wet. Condensation beads on every surface, and your clothing will become damp within hours even if you never step near water. Wool retains warmth when wet; synthetic materials may not. Choose accordingly.

Your outer layer should be water-resistant but not waterproof. You do not want to trap moisture inside your clothing—the condensation will find its way in regardless, and trapping it will make you cold and uncomfortable. A shell that beads water and allows evaporation will serve you better than a seal that holds everything in.

Footwear is critical. The ground is wet, uneven, layered with pathways and channels that carry water in directions serving purposes you cannot parse. You will step in water. You will slip. You will walk through channels where the water reaches ankle-depth or deeper. Sealed boots will fill with water and keep your feet wet and cold for hours. Boots with drainage and good grip will serve you better, even if they allow moisture in. Consider that the creatures of the city also move through these channels, and their movements leave traces—slime, organic residue, the detritus of biological processes. Your boots will become filthy. This is not optional.

Gloves are necessary. You will touch surfaces that are cold and wet, surfaces that may be covered in condensation or biological residue or both. You will touch surfaces and then realize that something just moved under your hand—something in the walls, something in the pipes, something that lives in the infrastructure and is startled by contact. Gloves give you a barrier. They will also get wet. Carry spares.

A hood or head covering serves multiple purposes. It keeps condensation from running down your neck. It provides some insulation against the cold that seems to rise from the ground and fall from the sky in equal measure. It gives you a barrier against unexpected contact—light creatures that drift into your path, small organisms that emerge from surfaces you touch, the spray of water from channels you pass. You will not be able to avoid the city's fauna entirely, but you can reduce the surface area of direct contact.

### What to Carry

Carry water. Not to drink—you will have access to the city's water, and it is safe, maintained by systems that the city's fauna keeps functioning, but you will want water for cleaning, for cooling yourself if you overheat (possible near thermal regulation systems), for稀释 biological residue from your hands and face. A small collapsible container.

Carry a light source. The city provides light—glow panels embedded in surfaces, bioluminescent organisms in pipes, the faint illumination of creatures moving through dark channels—but this light is not consistent. It reveals shapes without edges, structures without detail, the suggestion of more beyond every illuminated patch. In the dim areas, you will want your own light. A headlamp leaves your hands free. Consider that bright light may attract creatures; a dim red light preserves your night vision while providing enough illumination to navigate.

Carry something to clean surfaces you will touch—wipes, a small towel, something that can absorb condensation and biological residue. The city's fauna leaves traces. You will not always want those traces on your skin.

Carry a way to mark your path. The city is large, layered, and easy to become lost in. Tape, chalk, a GPS beacon, something that lets you find your way back to entry points. You will not be able to memorize routes; there are too many, they shift as the city's fauna moves through its channels, and your perception of direction will become unreliable in the consistent dimness. Mark where you came from.

### What Not to Carry

Do not carry plants or seeds or organic material that might be interpreted as biological contamination. The city's ecology is precise, its organisms interdependent, and foreign biological material may be processed by the fauna in unpredictable ways. You may not intend to leave anything behind, but you will leave traces. Minimize the traces that matter.

Do not carry weapons. The city's fauna is not aggressive in the way you might expect. The creatures are integrated into systems, and their behavior is system-behavior. Threatening them may trigger defensive responses that the city's infrastructure will enforce—or it may trigger nothing, and you will have brandished a weapon at organisms that do not recognize weapons as relevant. Either way, you will have announced yourself as something that does not belong. Announcing yourself is not fatal, but it changes how the city metabolizes you, and you may not want that change.

Do not carry expectations about how the city should receive you. The city does not receive visitors. It processes bodies. Your expectations will be wrong, and wrong expectations in an environment you do not understand will lead to decisions that do not serve you.

## Sensory Preparation

### Light

The light is dim. Not darkness—you will be able to see—but dimness, low constant illumination that reveals shapes without edges. The city's light comes from many sources: glow panels embedded in surfaces, bioluminescent organisms in pipes, creatures that move through the air leaving trails of light in their wake. These sources are not consistent. Some areas are brighter; some are darker. The variation is not random, but it is not predictable to you, and you will move between light levels constantly as you walk through the city's dense infrastructure.

Prepare for this dimness. Your eyes will adjust, but the adjustment is not complete. You will see shapes that are not clear. You will see motion that you cannot identify. You will see light that moves toward you or away from you or in directions that do not correspond to any source you can locate. Do not assume that what you see is what is there. The creatures of the city use light as locomotion, as communication, as camouflage. A moving light may be a creature, a signal, a reflection, or a system that is processing information about your presence.

In the dim light, you will see water on surfaces. Condensation beading on metal and glass and organic composite. The wet gleam of the city maintaining its own moisture in its own engineered cold. This gleam is everywhere. It is not a feature; it is a constant. Learn to see through it.

### Sound

The sound is constant. Not noise but information—the hum of systems processing, the wet sounds of organisms feeding or moving or reproducing, the high faint whine of electronics, the deeper pulse of whatever serves as the city's heart. You will hear water in channels, the trickling that never stops. You will hear creatures in the water, in the walls, in the air. You will hear the city itself, the groan of infrastructure settling, the tick of thermal regulation adjusting, the low vibration that might be geological or might be mechanical or might be the city dreaming.

The sound is everywhere, layered, impossible to isolate into component parts. You will not be able to identify the source of most sounds. You will hear something and turn and see nothing, or see something that does not match the sound you heard, or see something moving in a direction that does not correspond to the sound's origin. This is normal. The city's soundscape is constructed for the city's fauna, and you are not part of that fauna. Your perception is not calibrated to this environment.

Prepare for the sound. You will not be able to escape it. You will not be able to quiet it. You will learn to hear through it, to let it become background, but this takes time—days, at minimum—and you should not expect to achieve it immediately. In the first hours, the sound will be overwhelming. This is acceptable. The overwhelm will pass.

### Temperature

The cold is present even when you do not feel it directly. The city's technology maintains water in liquid state at temperatures far below freezing, and this maintenance is not localized—it is embedded in the infrastructure itself, in the molecular structure of the materials that make up the city's surfaces, in the thermal-regulation weaves that keep the metabolism running. The cold radiates from everything. It is in the walls. It is in the ground. It is in the water that flows through channels and beads on surfaces and runs through pipes.

You will feel warm sometimes—near thermal regulation systems, in areas where the city's metabolism generates heat, in the dense infrastructure where the fauna's movements create friction. But the warmth is not consistent, and when you leave warm areas, you will feel the cold again, and the shift from warm to cold will be abrupt. Prepare for this shift. Dress in layers that you can add and remove. Expect to be cold. Expect to be warm. Expect the transition between these states to be uncomfortable.

### Wet

Everything is wet. The air holds moisture. The surfaces hold moisture. The creatures carry moisture, leave moisture, generate moisture in their metabolic processes. Water flows through channels, pools in low areas, drips from overhead structures, condenses on every surface that is cooler than the air. You will be wet. Your clothing will be wet. Your skin will be wet. The wetness is not only uncomfortable; it is informational. The city's fauna uses moisture as a medium—signals left in condensation, trails through water, organisms that thrive in wet environments and cannot survive in dry ones. Your wetness makes you part of this medium. You leave traces in the water. The water carries information about you.

This is not harmful. The water is clean, maintained by systems the city's fauna keeps functioning. But it is strange. You are from a world where wet is exceptional, where most surfaces are dry most of the time, where dryness is the default condition. Here, dryness is the exception, and you will need to adjust to a world where wet is the default condition, where every surface you touch is damp, where every breath you take is humid, where every step you take leaves wet footprints that fade as the condensation evaporates.

## Movement and Navigation

### How to Walk

Walk carefully. The ground is uneven, layered, full of channels and pathways that carry water and creatures and systems that you cannot see. You will step in water, in residue, in the soft places where the infrastructure has settled or shifted. You will step on surfaces that are slick with condensation, surfaces that are coated with biological residue, surfaces that are not solid but merely stable—the city has grown here, but the growth is not uniform, and some areas are more solid than others.

Do not rush. The city will still be here. The city's fauna will still be moving through its channels. The systems will still be processing. Rushing through the city is not faster; it is more dangerous. You will miss details. You will step in things you should have avoided. You will fall. You will attract attention—not hostile attention, but attention nonetheless, the city's systems noting an irregularity in their patterns, a body moving too fast for the expected parameters.

Walk like you belong. This is difficult advice to follow because you do not know what belonging looks like in this environment. But the city's fauna moves through the city in patterns, in rhythms, in flows that serve the city's metabolism. Your movements will be read against these patterns. If you move randomly, erratically, in ways that do not fit the flows, the city will note you. If you move slowly, steadily, following the general directions of the city's circulation, the city will process you as part of the flow. This is not camouflage; the city knows you are not fauna. But the city processes all bodies, and some processing is gentler than others.

### How to Navigate

Do not navigate. Or rather, do not navigate in the way you would navigate in a city you understand. The Climbing City does not have addresses or landmarks in the way you understand landmarks. Its structure is not designed for visitors; it is designed for the city's fauna, and the fauna does not need signs or maps or orientations. The city provides what guidance you need through the patterns of its own movement—the way creatures flow through channels, the way light shifts in different areas, the way the sound changes when you move between zones.

Learn the zones by feel. Some areas are brighter; some are darker. Some areas have more creatures moving through them; some have fewer. Some areas have water flowing faster; some have water pooling and still. Some areas feel more central, more metabolically active; some feel more peripheral, more infrastructure than organism. These differences are navigation. You will not be able to describe them to someone else, but you will be able to use them yourself.

Mark your path. As mentioned, carry something to leave marks where you have been. The city's layered ground makes it easy to walk in circles without realizing it. The dim light makes orientation difficult. The constant sound makes it hard to remember where you came from. Marks help. Tape on corners. Chalk on surfaces. Anything that says "I was here, this way."

### What to Touch

Touch carefully. Every surface is wet, and most surfaces are alive—not metaphorically alive but actually alive, part of the city's metabolism, home to organisms you cannot see. When you touch a surface, you are touching an ecosystem. When you touch a surface, something in the infrastructure may notice. When you touch a surface, you leave traces—oil from your skin, warmth from your hand, the pressure of contact—that the city's systems process as information.

This is not dangerous. The city's fauna is not aggressive. But it is attentive. Your touches are data points, and the city will include them in its calculations. Touch surfaces that you need to touch. Do not touch surfaces out of curiosity, out of habit, out of the impulse to confirm that what you see is real. Confirm by looking. Confirm by being present. Confirm by the fact that you are here, in the city, feeling its cold and its wet and its light. Do not confirm by touching everything.

### When to Stop

Stop when you are overwhelmed. The city's soundscape, its wetness, its dimness, its constant motion, will overwhelm you. You will need to stop and let your senses adjust. Find a place where the water does not flow directly, where the light is stable, where the creatures are moving in patterns you can predict. Sit. Breathe. Let the city process you at its own pace. You are not in a hurry. The city does not care if you take your time.

Stop when you are lost. You will become lost. The city is too large, too layered, too alien for you to navigate without becoming lost repeatedly. When you realize you are lost, stop. Mark your location. Wait. Listen. The city will provide guidance if you wait long enough—creatures moving through channels, light shifting in patterns, the sound of water leading you toward water that flows in directions you can follow. Do not walk faster when you are lost. Speed makes you erratic, and erratic bodies attract the wrong attention.

## Sustenance

### Water

The city's water is safe to drink. It is maintained by systems that the city's fauna keeps functioning—organisms that process impurities, filters that the infrastructure grows to its own specifications, cycles that keep the water clean and cold and saturated with whatever minerals the city's metabolism requires. You will see the water everywhere: flowing in channels, pooling in low areas, beading on surfaces. Drink it. Your body needs water, and the city's water is designed for the city's fauna, but the city's fauna includes organisms that can survive conditions your body can also survive. The water will hydrate you.

Do not drink water that you have not seen flowing. Still water in the city may be stagnant—maintenance water, water that has been processed for other purposes, water that is not meant for consumption by any fauna. If you must drink still water, find a place where you can see the water moving, even slowly, and drink from the flow.

### Food

The city has no food in the sense you understand food. There are no plants, no animals, no biological material that grows in ways you recognize. The city's fauna is its food—they process each other, process the infrastructure, process the water and the minerals and the energy that the city provides. You cannot eat the city's fauna. You cannot eat the city's infrastructure. You cannot eat the city's water.

Bring your own food. Enough for your planned duration, plus a margin. The city does not provide food for visitors; it processes visitors, and processing does not include feeding. Your body will need sustenance that you provide yourself. Pack foods that do not require cooking, that are compact, that can survive the cold and wet. Protein bars. Dried meals. Whatever your system can handle. You will not enjoy eating here—the wet cold makes everything feel unappetizing—but you will need to eat, and you will be grateful for food you brought.

### Air

The atmosphere is thin. The plateau is high enough that the air holds less oxygen than you are used to, fewer particulates, less warmth. You will breathe faster. You will feel winded after walking short distances. This is normal. Your body will adjust, but the adjustment takes time—days, potentially—and you may feel uncomfortable for the first day or two.

If you have respiratory sensitivities, bring what you need to manage them. The city's air is clean—the infrastructure filters it, the fauna processes it, the water saturates it and removes impurities—but it is thin. You may need supplemental oxygen if you cannot adjust to the altitude. The city does not provide this; you must bring it.

## Psychological Preparation

### The Collapsed Categories

The city will challenge your categories. Built and grown are not separate here. The city's infrastructure grew—organisms that built structures, structures that became organisms, the boundary between creature and construction lost over millennia of integration. Organism and machine are not separate here. The city's fauna includes entities that are half organism and half machine, that process information with biological neurons and silicon circuits, that move through the city as if they were made of one substance but are actually made of both. City and world are not separate here. The plateau is the city. The water is the city. The geology still becoming is the city. There is no outside to the city. You cannot step outside it because it extends in all directions, because there is no horizon, because the city is the world it occupies.

You will want to categorize what you see. You will want to say "this is built" and "this is grown" and "this is organism" and "this is machine." Resist this. The categories will not hold. You will force things into categories that do not fit, and the forcing will be exhausting, and you will miss what is actually there because you are trying to make it fit into a framework that does not apply. Let the categories collapse. See what is there. Let it be what it is, even if you cannot name it.

### The Wet Cold

The wet cold is not a single sensation. It is a constant condition that affects everything—your perception, your mood, your ability to think clearly. Cold makes you slower. Wet makes you uncomfortable. The combination is a low-level stress that never entirely goes away. You will adapt to it, but the adaptation is not complete. You will always feel it. You will always be slightly colder than you want to be, slightly damper than you want to be, slightly more uncomfortable than you are used to being.

Do not fight this. Fighting it requires energy you do not have, attention you need for other things. Accept the wet cold. Let it be part of your experience here. It is not lethal; the city's technology prevents frostbite, prevents hypothermia, keeps the water liquid even at temperatures that should freeze it. You are not in danger. You are merely uncomfortable. Accept the discomfort. Let it be information—your body telling you where you are, what conditions you are in, what the city expects of you. The discomfort is your relationship with this place. Do not sever it by fighting it.

### The Creatures

The creatures will be the most disturbing part of your visit, and you should prepare for this. You are not accustomed to sharing space with organisms that are not human, not animal, not plant, not fungus—organisms that are engineered from silicon and carbon and metal and light, organisms that have no analog in your experience, organisms that move through the city in patterns you cannot predict and serve functions you cannot parse. You will feel watched. You will feel intruded upon. You will feel that the city is full of things that should not be there, that your presence is being noted by entities you cannot see or understand.

This feeling is accurate. The city is noting you. The city's fauna is aware of your presence. They are processing information about you—your size, your movement, your temperature, your moisture content, your sound, your light. You are a data point in their calculations, another body in the city's dense circulation. But this is not hostile. The city processes everything. It processes the water, the air, the geology, the infrastructure. It processes the creatures, the light, the sound. It processes you. Processing is not attack. It is the city's way of including you in its systems, of making you part of its metabolism, of integrating you into its ecology. You may find this comforting or disturbing, but you cannot stop it, and you cannot prevent it. You are here. You are being processed. Accept this.

### The Sound

The sound will be the most difficult adjustment for most visitors. The city's soundscape is not something you can turn off. It is not something you can quiet. It is constant, layered, impossible to isolate into component parts. You will hear water in channels, the wet sounds of creatures moving, the hum of systems processing, the groan of infrastructure settling, the low vibration that might be the city dreaming. You will hear sounds you cannot identify, sounds that seem to come from directions that do not exist, sounds that change when you move and then change back when you stop. You will hear yourself breathing, your clothing rustling, your footsteps on wet surfaces. You will hear more than you can process, and the processing will be exhausting.

There is no solution to this. You will not be able to make the sound stop. You will not be able to find a quiet place—the quietest places in the city are still full of sound, still processing, still metabolizing. You can only adjust. You can only let the sound become background. You can only stop trying to identify every sound and let them blend together into the city's soundscape, into the constant hum of a metabolism running. This adjustment takes time. Be patient with yourself. The sound will become bearable. It will not become pleasant, but it will become something you can live with, something you can ignore when you need to ignore it.

## What to Expect

### The First Hours

You will be overwhelmed. The city's dimness, its wetness, its cold, its creatures, its sound—you will be overwhelmed within the first hour of entry, and the overwhelm will last for hours. You will want to leave. You will wonder why you came. You will question your decision to visit this place.

This is normal. Every visitor feels this. The Climbing City is not designed for comfort; it is designed for function. It serves the city's metabolism, the city's ecology, the city's processes. Your comfort is not a priority. But your survival is not in danger. You are merely uncomfortable. Stay. Let yourself adjust. The overwhelm will pass.

### The First Days

You will begin to understand. After the first day or two, you will start to see patterns. You will notice how the creatures move, when they move, why they move. You will notice how the light shifts, where it is brighter, where it is darker. You will notice how the water flows, which channels carry water, which channels are dry. You will start to navigate—not by landmarks but by feel, by the texture of the environment, by the sense of moving through a living system that is processing you.

This understanding is partial. You will never understand the city completely. No one does. But partial understanding is enough to move through the city without constant fear, to find places to rest, to drink water, to eat food, to wait out the overwhelm. Partial understanding is enough to survive.

### The Long Term

You will leave changed. The city processes everything, and you are no exception. Your time in the city will alter you—not physically, not dramatically, but in ways that you will notice later, in ways that will not feel like damage but will not feel like nothing either. You may find that you are more comfortable in the cold. You may find that you are less bothered by wet. You may find that you hear sounds differently, that you see light differently, that you understand systems better than you did before. You may find that you dream differently, that your mind fills with images of the city's layered ground and its moving creatures and its dimness that is not empty but full.

This alteration is not harmful. It is what the city does. It is how the city includes you in its metabolism. You chose to visit, and you will leave processed, and the processing is the visit. Accept it. Let it be part of you. The city will hold you while you are there, and it will hold the parts of you that it changed after you leave, and this is the relationship between visitor and city, the only relationship that exists here.

---

## Final Notes

The Climbing City is not a destination you conquer. It is a destination you participate in. You will move through it, and it will move through you, and the movement is the visit. Do not expect to understand it completely. Do not expect to master it. Do not expect it to be comfortable. Expect to be processed. Expect to be changed. Expect to leave with something you did not have when you arrived—not knowledge, not control, not comfort, but something else, something you cannot name, something that is the city's gift to those who enter.

The city climbs. It has been climbing since the world was young, and it will continue climbing after you leave. You are a moment in its long movement, a data point in its dense circulation, a body in its wet cold metabolism. Be present for that moment. Let it be what it is. The city holds everything, and the plateau rises toward a dark sky, and the water flows, and the cold remains, and the light holds steady, and the creatures keep moving, and the sound keeps sounding, and the world keeps becoming.
