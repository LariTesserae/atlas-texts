# A Traveler's Preparation Guide: The Drowned Terraces of Ahivatha

---

## On Arriving Alive

Ahivatha does not kill you quickly. That is perhaps the most important thing to understand before you step off whatever craft or translocation point has brought you here. The landscape is not hostile in any obvious, immediate way. There are no predators that chase, no poisons that act on contact, no weather events that sweep you away. The danger is subtler: it is the danger of a place that is *becoming*, that has not finished being what it is, and in that becoming, it is indifferent to the comfort or survival of anything perched on its shoulders.

You will arrive wet. Not because it rains — though it may, in the heavier seasons — but because the humidity here is not a condition of the air. It is the air. Your first breath will feel like swallowing something alive. Your second will feel like drowning by increments. Do not panic. Your lungs will, within a few days, begin to adapt. They will not adapt completely — you are not from here, and your physiology will never fully belong to this atmosphere — but they will adapt enough. Give them time. Do not overtax yourself in the first forty-eight hours. Walk slowly. Breathe deliberately. Let the damp settle into you rather than fighting it.

The first thing you should do upon arrival is find a dwelling that is not on the lowest inhabited terrace. The water does not stop rising. The people of Ahivatha have built their homes in tiers for a reason, and if you arrive during a period when the channels are widening — which you will have no way to know — the lower terraces flood. Not catastrophically, not violently, but completely. Rooms fill. Stored goods dissolve. Walls absorb water until they sag. The people here expect this. They have built their lives around a kind of cyclical surrender. You have not, so stay high.

## What to Wear

You will need to abandon any expectation of dryness, and more importantly, any expectation of comfort as you understand it. Your clothing choices here are not about aesthetics or modesty. They are about managing a single persistent condition: the complete saturation of everything you touch.

**Start with your skin.** Your outer layer should be a loose, breathable garment made from a fabric that can absorb moisture without losing integrity. Linen performs better than cotton. Wool, counterintuitively, holds up remarkably well — it retains some insulation even when wet, which matters when you consider that the warmth here is not consistent. The higher you climb through the terraces, the cooler the air becomes, and if you are sleeping on an upper platform, you may find yourself damp and cold in the small hours of the night. Wool addresses both states.

**Layering is your enemy and your friend.** Too many layers and you will sweat, and sweating in Ahivatha is a more serious problem than you might think. Sweat does not evaporate here. It sits on your skin and becomes part of the ambient moisture, which means it never cools you. You will feel progressively hotter and more lethargic as your body tries and fails to thermoregulate through evaporation. Too few layers and you will lose heat at night. The middle path: one inner layer of light, absorbent fabric, one outer layer of something that can get wet without coming apart. Nothing sealed, nothing rubber, nothing that will trap moisture against you.

**Footwear is critical.** The pathways between terraces are narrow and worn smooth by generations of feet and by the slow, constant presence of water. Stone becomes slick. Wood becomes slicker. Woven vine bridges — and you will cross many of these — flex under your weight and shift their texture as the humidity swells and shrinks the fibers. You need footwear with excellent grip and enough drainage that standing water inside your shoes does not become a blister factory. The locals go barefoot in many areas, and their feet have adapted accordingly: the skin on their soles is thick, almost leathery, resistant to the soft rot that claims poorly maintained wood. Do not try to emulate this on your first visit. Wear closed, drainable shoes with rubber soles and secure laces. If your shoes have any sealed compartment where water can pool and stay warm — and most shoes do — you will develop fungal infections within days, and those infections will compound every other discomfort you are already managing.

**A head covering is essential.** Not for warmth — the heat is steady — but for the light. The light in Ahivatha is relentless in its diffuseness. It comes from everywhere and nowhere, and it has no shadows in the way you are used to. It simply illuminates, flatly, from all angles, and after a few hours your eyes will ache from the absence of contrast. A wide-brimmed hat or a light hood will give your eyes something to rest against. It will also keep the worst of the mist from running down your face and into your collar, which brings us to the next concern.

**Carry a second set of everything.** You will not be able to dry anything here. This is not a matter of effort or resources — the humidity is absolute, and any fabric you lay out will simply sit there, damp, indefinitely. Your second set of clothing will also be damp, but it will be *fresh*damp, free of the oils and salts your body has deposited in the first set. Rotating between two sets will prevent the skin breakdown that comes from spending too long in clothing that has become a second skin of your own sweat and the place's moisture combined.

## What to Carry

Keep your load light. The terraces are steep, and the paths are not designed for wheeled transport or heavy packs. Every step upward is one you will feel on the way back down, and you will descend more often than you ascend, because the water is below and the markets and gathering places are on the middle terraces and the quiet contemplative spots are on the high ones but you will not be contemplative for long in this humidity.

**Water.** You might think, given that this place is *submerged*, that water would not be a concern. Think again. The water here is warm. It is not sterile. It carries the same organic complexity as the rest of the landscape — the faint sweetness, the breath-like quality — and if you drink it untreated, you will spend the next several days with a gastrointestinal experience that will make your lungs' adaptation period feel pleasant by comparison. Carry your own water, treat it with whatever method you trust, and treat it again.

**Preserved food.** The cuisine of Ahivatha is dense, starchy, and heavily fermented. It suits the climate and the metabolism of the people who have lived here for generations. It will not suit yours. Not immediately, and perhaps not ever. The fermented preparations — the sour porridges, the aged fish pastes, the vinegary greens — are nutritious, but they are also populated with microorganisms that your gut has no history of engaging with. Bring your own food for the first week. Eat theirs in small portions and watch how your body responds. Some travelers adapt. Some do not. The ones who do not wish they had brought more of their own supplies.

**Fire.** Not for warmth — you will not need warmth — but for light and for the treatment of water and food. Matches will not work here. The humidity will defeat them within hours, possibly within minutes if you are careless with their storage. Carry a lighter that is sealed against moisture, and a backup, and a third backup. Carry a small, waterproof torch — one of the chemical ones, not electrical, because you will not be able to charge anything here and the bioluminescence of the water below provides light at night but not the kind you can read or work by.

**A cutting tool.** Copper hammers and stone blades are the local tools, but you will not be working stone. What you will need is something that can cut through the dense vine growth that encroaches on every pathway, that can trim back the ferns that reach across the bridges at night, that can help you modify your shelter if the water level shifts and you need to re-route a door or shore up a wall. A good knife, well oiled and sheathed, will serve you better than any weapon here, because there is nothing here that requires a weapon and everything here that requires patience and the ability to cut.

**Salt.** This is not obvious, but it will matter. Salt is the one mineral that the bodies of people from dry environments crave most when they enter a humid one. You are losing salt constantly, through sweat you cannot see and through the moisture you exhale, and if you do not replace it, you will develop the dull, heavy fatigue that makes every terrace feel twice as steep as it is. Carry salt. Add it to your water. Add it to your food. Do not overdo it — excess salt in this humidity will make you retain water in uncomfortable places — but keep a supply and use it regularly.

**Something to sit on.** Not for comfort — comfort is not really achievable here — but for safety. The bridges and pathways are narrow, and the locals are accustomed to sitting on stone ledges or on the packed earth of terrace edges. You are not. The stone is slick with condensation. The earth is damp and gives no purchase. A simple foldable mat, made of something that does not absorb water, will give you a place to sit without worrying about slipping, to rest without absorbing more moisture from below, to set things down without watching them slowly damp through. A closed-cell foam pad is ideal. It weighs almost nothing, it dries quickly (relative to everything else here), and it will save you from the constant low-grade anxiety of trying to find a dry spot in a world that has never known one.

## How to Move

The terraces are organized vertically, not horizontally, and your relationship to this verticality will define your experience of Ahivatha. You will climb. You will descend. You will cross bridges that sway and bridges that don't, bridges made of stone that has been fitted by generations of hands into shapes that are almost organic, bridges made of vine that creaks with your weight and smells of the forest it came from. Some bridges are long. Some span channels where the water glows faintly green below, and if you look down — and you will look down, because something in you needs to see the depth — you will see the slow movement of things that have no names, the gentle displacement of water around shapes that are not quite fish and not quite anything else.

**Walk in the middle of pathways.** The edges of stone pathways are the most worn, the most slick, the most likely to crumble. The centers have been kept solid by generations of feet packing the same ground. Follow the middle. When a path narrows, flatten your feet and shift your weight deliberately, the way you would on ice, because that is what the stone becomes in the morning: a thin film of condensation over a surface that looks dry but isn't, and you will be on it before the sun has had a chance to evaporate the moisture, and you will slip, and you will learn.

**Do not step on vine bridges without testing them.** I cannot stress this enough. They hold. They hold well, because the people who built them understand the flex and the load and the way wet vine behaves under stress. But they hold in a way that is fundamentally different from how stone holds. They move. They respond. If you step onto one as if it were a stone bridge and take a long confident stride, it will sway, and the person behind you — and there will always be someone behind you, because the terraces are densely populated — will have to correct, and the whole structure will shimmy, and everyone on it will feel a moment of uncertainty that is, in this place, more stressful than it needs to be. Step onto vine bridges gently. Let your weight settle. Then walk with the sway rather than against it.

**Climb with your hands.** The steepest sections of the terraces are not paths but ladders — vertical climbs carved into the stone or built from lashed wood. Your legs will tire. Use your arms. Grip the stone where it has been worn into handholds by centuries of palms. The stone here is soft — softer than you expect — and it will give slightly under your fingers, and this is normal, and this is how the terraces have always been climbed. Do not grip too hard. Do not trust the softest sections, because the stone that gives most easily is also the stone that has been most altered by water, and it may not hold your weight if you load it badly.

**Descend with your heels.** Going down is harder than going up. Your quadriceps will burn on the steep sections. Descend heels-first, letting the impact travel through your strongest leg muscles rather than through your knees. If a step is too high to descend heel-first, lower yourself with your hands. Do not jump. The stone crumbles. A jump that lands on compromised ground could drop you into a channel, and while the channels are not deep, the water in them is warm and alive and you do not want to be in it any longer than you have to be.

## What to Expect From Your Body

Ahivatha will change you. Not dramatically, not dangerously, but noticeably, in the first week.

**Your skin will adjust.** The constant moisture will cause mild maceration between your toes, between your fingers, in the creases of your joints. This is normal. Treat it with the salt you are carrying — a paste of salt and a little water applied to the affected areas will dry them out enough to prevent the deeper maceration that leads to painful splitting. Zinc oxide powder, if you have it, works even better. The locals use something they make from the ash of certain burned woods, combined with rendered fat from the sluggish silver fish that live in the deep channels. It is effective. You will not want to use it, because it smells like the inside of a fire that has been underwater, but your feet will thank you.

**Your breathing will change.** Not because the air is thin — it is not — but because it is dense, and dense air behaves differently in your lungs. You will breathe more slowly. Your tidal volume will increase. You will feel, for the first few days, that you are not getting enough oxygen, and then you will realize, one afternoon, that you are breathing in a rhythm that feels almost meditative, and that the oxygen has been there all along, and that the feeling of breathlessness was just your body's confusion at working in a medium it was not designed for.

**Your sleep will be strange.** The mist never fully lifts, which means there is no sharp distinction between day and night in terms of light. The terraces are somewhat darker after the sun has passed its apex, but the green glow of the water provides a baseline illumination that never fully disappears. Your sleep cycle will shift. You will sleep in shorter bursts, or deeper at odd hours, or not at all on some nights and then for ten hours straight on others. This is not a disorder. It is adaptation. Roll with it. Do not fight your body's new relationship with time.

**Your balance will improve.** Not because the paths are well-maintained — some are, some are not — but because your inner ear will begin to account for the constant subtle sway of the environment. The mist, the humidity, the slow movement of water, the flex of bridges — all of these are in constant low-level motion, and your vestibular system will learn to factor them in. After the first week, you will feel clumsy on flat ground, because your body will have recalibrated to expect a world that moves. This is normal and it will pass when you leave, though the memory of moving through a world that breathes will stay with you.

## How to Behave

The people of Ahivatha are not alien. They are not strange in the way that other civilizations with different biologies or radically different social structures are strange. They are people. They build and argue and raise children and grow old on these terraces, and they do this with a patience that can only come from generations of living in a place that does not require them to fight it, only to accommodate it.

**Do not speak of the water as a problem.** The water rises. It widens the channels. It claims lower terraces and forces relocations. The people of Ahivatha do not speak of this as a crisis. They speak of it as weather speaks — as a condition, not an event. When you ask them about the rising water, they will look at you with a mild puzzlement, as if you have asked them about the color of the sky. Of course the water rises. Of course the channels widen. This is what water does. Why would you ask? Respect this. Do not impose your framework of urgency onto a landscape and a people who have resolved that tension generations ago.

**Ask before you cross.** Bridges here are often owned or maintained by specific households or associations, and while you will almost certainly be allowed to cross, asking is a matter of social courtesy that matters here more than it does in places where infrastructure is a public resource. Point at the bridge you want to cross, gesture to the nearest inhabited platform, and wait for someone to emerge. They will emerge. Someone is always watching. Nod. Cross. Nod again on the other side. This is the entire protocol, and it will serve you.

**Do not swim.** I mean this literally. The water is warm. It feels inviting. It is not inviting. It carries things — organisms, organic compounds, thermal currents — that your body will respond to in ways you cannot predict. The things that grow in the shallows, the iridescent membranous things with no names, are not dangerous in the sense of being aggressive, but they are aware, in some slow and diffuse way, and they respond to disturbance. The locals swim. They know how, and they know where, and they know what the water will do to an outsider's skin. You do not know these things. Do not swim.

**Do not eat the fish.** Even if you are very hungry. Even if they look harmless. They are sluggish and silver and slow enough that you might think they have been domesticated into safety. They have not. They are simply adapted to this environment, and you are not. There are preparations that render them safe — the fermentation methods the locals use have been refined over generations — but raw or quickly cooked, they carry a complexity that your digestive system will interpret as a full-scale invasion. Respect this. Bring your own food. Eat theirs when you are invited to, in small portions, with gratitude.

**Be quiet in the evening.** Not out of reverence or formality, but because the evening is when the water glows, and when the water glows, the sound of the terraces changes. The low hum that is always there becomes more present. The creak of wood and vine seems to synchronize with something that has no rhythm you can predict. The whistler birds return to the high canopy and their cries thin out into something almost like language, almost like a conversation that has been going on for so long that it has become structural. If you are quiet, you will hear it. If you are loud, you will drown it out, and you will miss the one thing that Ahivatha offers that no other place in any multiverse you have visited can offer: the sound of a world that has not yet finished learning how to exist, still practicing, still becoming, letting you listen.

## The Thing That Moves

You will see it. Probably on your third or fourth night, when you have been awake late enough that the glow of the water has become the most present thing in your field of vision. A slow displacement. A shadow passing through the glow. Too large to be a fish. Too deliberate to be a current. It moves through the deep channels below the city with a patience that suggests it has no reason to be anywhere else, no urgency, no destination. It simply passes.

Do not speak of it unless someone speaks to you first. The locals do not discuss it. They have lived with it for generations. They have chosen, collectively, not to give it a name, because naming it would make it something, and they have found, over the long time they have been building on this landscape, that it is better to let it be nothing — a shadow, a passage, a displacement in water that is, after all, mostly water. You are not required to share their restraint, but you are advised to. Some things become more real when you name them, and some things — this thing — becomes less bearable.

If you watch it long enough, you may notice that it moves with the water, but also against it. That it is not carried but chooses. That it passes the same channels at roughly the same intervals, as if it has routes, preferences, a geography of its own that intersects with the city's geography without ever touching it. Some travelers have described it as a kind of slow heartbeat. Others have called it the only true resident of Ahivatha, the one thing that was here before the terraces and will be here after. Both descriptions may be accurate. Neither may be complete.

Watch it if you want. It will not acknowledge you. It does not need to.

## On Leaving

You will leave. Everyone leaves, eventually. The terraces are not a place where outsiders settle permanently, not because they are unwelcoming but because the land itself has a way of making you aware of the ground you stand on — literally, constantly — and no human body is designed to stand on ground that is still learning what it wants to be. You will feel the terrain in your ankles, in your knees, in the base of your spine. You will feel the humidity in your lungs, in the soft skin between your toes, in the fine hair at your temples that never fully dried. You will carry these sensations for days or weeks after you leave, a ghost-pressure in places that have no names.

Do not resist this. It is the place saying goodbye in the only language it has. The dampness in your clothes, the chalk on your fingers, the low hum in your ears that is not quite tinnitus and not quite memory — these are Ahivatha pressing itself into you, marking you as someone who was here, who stood on the highest ridge and watched the mountain range break through the waterline like the spine of some slumbering leviathan and thought: *yes. This is what it looks like when a world is still being born.*
