# Preparation Guide: The Cistern Corridors

## Reading the Space Before You Enter

This is not a wilderness. Do not treat it like one. The Cistern Corridors are infrastructure — built space that has been slowly colonized by water, mineral, and life over decades. Understanding this will save you from the two failure modes most visitors fall into: treating it as hostile territory (it will wear you down with paranoia), or treating it as safe territory (it will hurt you through neglect).

You are walking into a world that runs on its own schedule. The water seeps at its own pace. The rats move in patterns that predate the infrastructure you see around them. The compressors cycle on rhythms set by engineers decades ago and never quite synchronized with anything else. There is no urgency here. The Cistern Corridors are patient. They will outlast your visit without effort.

Your job is to be present in that patience without being consumed by it.

---

## What Your Body Will Experience

### Heat and Humidity

The warmth is the first thing that will register. It rises from the machinery, from the compression of transit systems, from the city above pressing its thermal mass down through stone and concrete. You will not acclimate in a single visit — the heat is structural, and your body will treat it as a sustained low-level stressor.

The humidity compounds this. The air holds moisture that your skin cannot evaporate into. You will sweat without cooling. Your shirt will become a second skin within minutes. Your hair will flatten. Anything on your skin that can trap moisture will: watch faces, the backs of your hands, the inside of your elbows where fabric presses close. The condensation is not dramatic — you will not see water streaming down walls — but it is constant, a fine persistence that will dampen everything you wear over the course of an hour.

This combination is the primary physical challenge of the Corridors. You are performing light labor (walking, balancing, moving through uneven space) while your body struggles with thermoregulation. Dehydration will catch you before exhaustion. You will not feel thirsty until you are already behind.

**Preparation:**

- Water. More than you think you need. Plan for at least 500ml per hour of intended stay, and bring more than that. Electrolyte supplements are not necessary but will help if you are staying more than two hours.
- Moisture-wicking clothing. Synthetic fabrics that move sweat away from your skin rather than holding it. Cotton will become a liability — it holds water, stops breathing, and will stay wet. You will be cold in patches and hot in others as the wet fabric moves against you.
- Loose layers. You want airflow between your clothing and your skin. Tight clothing traps moisture against you and accelerates heat retention.
- A bandana or sweat towel. Something to wipe your face and neck. The condensation on your skin will mix with sweat and run into your eyes.
- Accept discomfort. You will not be comfortable here. You will be functional. That is the goal.

### The Wet

Everything is wet in the Cistern Corridors. Not soaking. Not dripping from above. But damp at every surface, condensation on metal, moisture in every crack, water finding its way through every seam in the concrete. If you set something on the ground, it will be wet when you pick it up. If you lean against a wall, you will feel the damp through your clothing.

This has three practical implications:

1. **Electronics**: Anything that cannot handle humidity is a liability.Phones will fog internally. Flashlights will have condensation under their lenses. Batteries will corrode faster in this environment. Bring protection for anything you care about, and accept that your phone may suffer.

2. **Feet**: You will be walking on surfaces that are damp, slick in patches, with water pooling in low spots. Grating may be slick. Concrete may be slick where water has brought fine sediment to the surface. Water will splash up from drainage channels. Plan for wet feet.

3. **Skin**: If you have any cuts, abrasions, or compromised skin, the water here is not clean. It has been through infrastructure, picked up mineral deposits, sat in cracks and channels, and accumulated whatever organic material the fauna has tracked through it. Cover any open wounds completely.

### Light and Dark

The corridor lighting is sparse and uneven. Industrial fixtures spaced far apart, each creating a pool of yellow-white light surrounded by a halo of condensation and steam. Between fixtures, the darkness is substantial — not absolute, but deep enough that you cannot see the far wall of the corridor, only the nearest edges of the space.

Your eyes will adapt. Give them time. The human visual system is capable of functioning in surprisingly low light, but it needs minutes, not seconds, and it will be fighting the heat-haze and moisture throughout. You will not see as well here as you do in open air. You will not see the texture of walls at a distance, the depth of drainage channels, the edges of grating. You will see shapes, movement, contrast.

The practical result: you will misjudge distances, step awkwardly, occasionally put your foot somewhere you did not intend. This is normal. Move carefully. Use your hands to feel surfaces ahead of you when you need precision.

If you bring a light source:

- A headlamp will free your hands and provide constant forward illumination. This is the best choice for navigating.
- A flashlight will light what you point it at but leave your peripheral vision in darkness. This is fine for inspection, less good for movement.
- LED lights will produce a harsh white that cuts through humidity poorly — the moisture diffuses the light and creates a washed-out effect. Warm light (tungsten or amber LED) will penetrate moisture better and give you better contrast.

The humidity will fog lights. Keep a dry cloth handy.

## The Fauna

### Rats

The rats are not your enemy. They are not interested in you. They have been here longer than the infrastructure you see around you, and they have organized their world with complete confidence. You are an anomaly — a large warm body that moves through their space — but not a threat. They have no reason to treat you as a threat.

This does not make them comfortable. They will move away from you. They will move away from any vibration, any footfall, any sound you make that suggests movement toward them. They are not fleeing; they are adjusting. The waves of movement you will see are not panic — they are reorganization. A moment ago this space was empty; now it is full; now it is empty again. The rats have simply relocated to wherever you are not.

You will see them. You will see many of them. They are visible in the light, crossing your path, occupying drainage channels, clustered in crevices and beneath grating. They do not freeze and stare at you. They do not hold their ground and challenge you. They move, and you will need to move through them. This is not a problem. Walk. They will clear.

The psychological effect of being surrounded by rats is the real challenge. You will be hyperaware of them. Every movement in your peripheral vision will register. Every rustle will make you flinch. This is a normal human response to a predator avoidance system that has not evolved for this scenario — you are not being hunted, but something in you feels like you are. You cannot eliminate this response, but you can manage it by understanding what is actually happening. The rats are going about their business. You are large and loud and they are giving you space. You are not in danger.

If you need to reduce your psychological load:

- Accept that you will see rats. Do not spend energy being surprised or unsettled by their presence. They are present. That is the fact. You will encounter them.
- Do not try to avoid them. Trying to step around rats, avoid drainage channels, navigate around clusters will make you tense, slow you down, and increase the total number of interactions you have with them. Walk through their space with confidence and let them move.
- Focus on your own movement, not theirs. Pay attention to your footing, your balance, the space ahead of you. The rats will handle themselves.
- Remember that they are the residents here. You are the visitor. They have a right to the space that you do not.

### Cockroaches

The cockroaches are smaller and faster. You will see them emerge when you are still, scatter when you move. They are sensitive to vibration — the footfall that rats ignore will send them diving into cracks and under grating. They are not a threat. They are not even an inconvenience. They are ambient life that you will stop noticing after the first twenty minutes.

You will see them on walls, under lights, in the gaps between pipes. They will freeze when you pass close. They will scatter in waves when you step toward a dark corner. This is fine.

### Sound

The soundscape is dense but not chaotic. You will hear:

- Dripping water. Constant. Multiple sources at different rhythms.
- The hum of pressure in pipes. Low, persistent, felt more than heard.
- The distant slosh of water moving through drainage channels. Sometimes rhythmic, sometimes irregular.
- Rat chatter. High-frequency, organized, a background conversation in a language you cannot parse.
- Metal settling. The infrastructure is alive — expanding, contracting, shifting under thermal stress and pressure changes. Occasional screeches, groans, clicks.
- The compressor heartbeat. A bass pulse, slow cycles, felt in your sternum as much as heard.

This sound will not change. It will not build to a climax or fade to silence. It will continue as long as you are here. You will stop hearing it consciously within an hour. You will notice when it stops (if a compressor cycles off) more than you notice it running.

If you are sensitive to sound or need quiet for psychological stability, bring earplugs. Otherwise, accept the soundscape as part of the experience. It is not hostile. It is just present.

## Navigating the Space

### Physical Layout

You are in a corridor. The corridor is roughly room-sized in width, with a walkway — grating or concrete — and drainage channels running parallel along the edges. The walls are concrete, raw and wet. Above you are pipes, cable trays, junction boxes, fixtures. The space is bounded — you can see the ends of the corridor, or nearly so. But in the darkness between fixtures, the corridor extends beyond your perception, and this will feel larger than it is.

There are structures: junction boxes where different infrastructure systems connect, inspection hatches set into walls or floors, valve assemblies, measurement equipment, cable runs. These are the accumulated additions of decades. They are maintained enough to function but not maintained enough to be clean. You will see dust, debris, staining, corrosion. The infrastructure has its own slow erosion happening, separate from the water and mineral accumulation.

### Movement

Move slowly. This is not a place where urgency serves you. The surfaces are damp and uneven. The light is poor. Your body is already under thermal stress. Moving fast increases your risk of a fall, a collision, a mistake. You have time. The Corridors are not going anywhere.

Watch your footing. Watch the grating — gaps can catch shoe edges. Watch the edge of the walkway where it meets the drainage channel. Watch for pooling water that may be deeper than it looks. Watch for debris that has accumulated over time — loose objects, fallen material, things that have worked their way into gaps.

Use your hands when you need to. Feel the wall ahead of you when you are uncertain. Feel the edge of a hatch before you step over it. Feel for pipes and cables that may cross your path at head height. The Corridors are navigable, but they require active attention.

### Hazards

The infrastructure here is not designed for visitors. You may encounter:

- Electrical panels: Keep your distance. Do not touch. Do not lean against them. Assume everything electrical is live and dangerous, especially in a wet environment.
- Unstable grating: Some sections of walkway may be loose, missing, or weakened. Test footing before committing weight. If you hear a grating section shift or flex under your weight, move carefully and report if possible.
- Sharp edges: Concrete edges, metal brackets, cable ends, pipe fittings. The humidity and mineral accumulation do not care about sharp edges. A edge that looks smooth may have a mineral deposit that creates a cutting edge. Move carefully.
- Low clearance: Pipes, junction boxes, and structural elements may be at head height. Watch your head. Watch your hands when reaching up.
- Flooding: In some areas, drainage channels may overflow onto the walkway. This is unpredictable and depends on the city's water management above. If you encounter standing water, assess whether it is shallow enough to pass through. If not, turn back or find an alternate route.

If you are not comfortable with a section, do not enter it. The Corridors reward caution.

## What to Bring

### Gear

- **Headlamp** with extra batteries, or a handheld flashlight with a way to attach it to your body if you need both hands free. Light is your primary tool for navigating this space.
- **Water** in a container that will not leak. More than you think you need. Plan for hydration, not for a comfortable margin.
- **A dry bag or waterproof container** for anything you cannot afford to get wet: phone, wallet, documents, medication. The humidity will get to everything over time.
- **A small towel or bandana** for wiping moisture from your face and hands.
- **Gloves** if you will be touching walls, pipes, or other surfaces. A light pair that will still let you feel textures. Wet concrete and corroded metal are not dangerous to touch, but they are not pleasant.
- **Footwear** that can handle wet surfaces and will grip on damp grating. Hiking boots with good tread are fine if you are confident in your footing. Trail runners with drainage will dry faster. Avoid anything with smooth soles or inadequate grip.

### Clothing

- **Moisture-wicking base layer**: A technical shirt that will move sweat away from your skin. Synthetic fabric, not cotton.
- **Loose outer layer**: A light jacket or vest if you tend to be cold in air conditioning (you will not be cold here, but the loose layer will protect your arms from walls and provide another barrier against moisture).
- **Long pants**: To protect your legs from wet surfaces and from brushing against walls.
- **Socks**: Moisture-wicking if possible. You will get wet feet regardless; synthetic or wool socks will handle it better than cotton.
- **Eye protection**: Not required, but glasses or sunglasses will protect your eyes from moisture if you are moving through a section with heavy condensation or splashing water.

### Optional but Helpful

- **Earplugs** if you are sensitive to sound or need psychological quiet.
- **Antiseptic wipes or spray** for cleaning any cuts or abrasions you acquire. The water here is not sterile.
- **A small tool** (leatherman or similar) for dealing with unexpected obstacles: a stuck hatch, a loose bolt, something that needs prying.
- **A marker or chalk** to mark your path if you are exploring and might need to find your way back.

---

## What Not to Bring

- **Cotton clothing**: You will regret it within minutes.
- **Electronics you cannot protect**: Assume they will get wet.
- **Anything you cannot afford to lose or damage**: The environment is corrosive, wet, and rough. Things will get damp. Things may get dropped. Things may get damaged.
- **Pets**: Leave them above. The Corridors are not hospitable to small animals, and the rats will not welcome competition.
- **Food with strong scent**: Rats can smell food. If you bring food, keep it sealed and do not eat in the Corridors. You will attract attention you do not want.
- **Heavy gear**: If it does not serve a purpose, leave it above. Every unnecessary item is weight you will be carrying in the heat.

## Psychological Preparation

### The Slow Time

The Cistern Corridors move slowly. Nothing here happens fast. The water seeps in geological time. The mineral deposits form over years. The rats go about their routines with patient efficiency. The compressors cycle on timers set decades ago.

You will be moving through a space that does not care about your pace. If you bring urgency into the Corridors, it will feel strange and wrong. If you slow down — not to a stop, but to a walking pace that matches the rhythm of the space — you will find that the Corridors are almost restful. There is no expectation here. There is no schedule. There is only the present moment of water seeping, rats moving, pipes breathing.

You do not need to fill the silence. You do not need to fill the time. The Corridors will hold you.

### The Darkness

The darkness is not hostile. You will be able to see. Your eyes will adjust. But the quality of darkness here is different from outdoor darkness or room darkness. It is humid darkness, moisture-filled darkness, darkness that has texture and depth. It will feel like you are moving through something rather than just through empty space.

Let this happen. Do not fight the darkness or try to penetrate it with your light beyond what you need. Walk in the dark when you can. Your eyes will serve you. The darkness is not a barrier; it is the Corridors accepting you into their rhythm.

### The Fauna as Landscape

After the first hour, you will stop seeing the rats as individuals. They will become part of the landscape — part of the texture of the space, as much a feature of the Corridors as the pipes or the dripping water. You will step over them without thinking. You will see them in your peripheral vision and not flinch.

This is the point at which you will be able to experience the Corridors as they are, rather than as you expected them to be. You will notice the mineral formations on the walls. You will hear the layered soundscape without parsing it. You will feel the humidity as just the condition of the air rather than as a force acting on you.

This shift may take an hour. It may take longer. It depends on the visitor. Do not force it. Just be present, and let your nervous system decide what to register as threat and what to register as environment.

## When Something Goes Wrong

### You Fall

The surfaces here are uneven. A fall is possible even if you are careful. If you fall:

- Assess damage. Can you move? Does anything hurt in a way that suggests injury?
- Get up slowly. The floor may be wet and slick. Move to a stable position before standing.
- If you are injured, you may need to call for assistance if you are not alone. If you are alone, you need to self-rescue. Plan for this before you enter: know your exit routes, know your limits, have a way to signal for help if you carry a phone.
- If you fall into water — a drainage channel, a pooling area — get out immediately and assess whether your equipment has been compromised.

### You Encounter a Hazard You Cannot Navigate

Electrical hazard, unstable structure, flooding, confined space that is smaller than you expected. Stop. Do not proceed. If you cannot find an alternate route, turn back. The Corridors will be here tomorrow. You will not if you push through something that is beyond your capability.

### You Feel Unwell

Heat and humidity can lead to heat exhaustion or heat stroke if you push too hard. Symptoms include:

- Headache
- Dizziness or lightheadedness
- Nausea
- Weakness
- Flushed skin
- Rapid heartbeat
- Confusion

If you experience these symptoms, stop. Rest in the coolest spot you can find. Drink water. Do not proceed until you feel normal. If symptoms persist or worsen, exit the Corridors and seek medical attention.

---

## Final Notes

The Cistern Corridors are not a place for conquest or for proof. You cannot master them; you can only visit them. Your goal is to enter, to move through, to experience, and to leave — not unchanged, but intact.

The Corridors will be here after you leave. They have been here before you arrived. The water will continue to seep, the rats will continue to organize, the mineral deposits will continue to grow. You are a temporary presence in a space that operates on its own terms.

Approach it with respect for that. Move through carefully. Observe. Listen. Feel the warmth and the wet and the slow rhythm of a world beneath the world.

That is the visit. That is enough.
