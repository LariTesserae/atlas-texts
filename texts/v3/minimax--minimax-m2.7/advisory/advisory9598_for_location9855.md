# Visiting the Reservoir: A Practical Guide

You've read the description. You've stood there, in the telling of it, on that slick steel walkway with cold pressing through your ribs and the orange light catching the drizzle. Now you want to go. Or maybe you need to go — sometimes these things aren't choices so much as thresholds you find yourself across.

The Reservoir will not care about your reasons. It does not judge. It simply is: vast, cold, still running the automated programs of a purpose it has outlived. Here is what you need to know before you go, and what you need to bring, and what you need to understand about the place that will hold you.

---

## Understanding What You're Walking Into

The Reservoir is not a cave. Caves are geology's work — patient, irregular, formed by processes that don't care whether anything ever visits. The Reservoir is industrial architecture that has been swallowed by water. Every surface was fabricated. Every measurement was deliberate. The scale is human-designed, not earth-made, and that changes something important: this place was built to be inhabited by workers, by crews, by people with flashlights and maintenance schedules and somewhere else to go home to. It is now inhabited by none of those things, and the emptiness that results is not the emptiness of wilderness. It is the emptiness of abandonment — of tools left out, of systems still running, of a space that was made to be full of purpose and now has none.

You need to understand this before you go. The Reservoir is not dangerous in the way that wilderness is dangerous. You will not get lost in the way you might lost in a forest. The space is gridded, columned, navigable in theory. But it is dark in ways that cities are not dark — the dark of spaces designed to be lit by systems now operating without intention — and it is cold in ways that will feel personal, will feel like the place is examining you, pressing its chill through whatever barriers you bring.

Bring what you need to not feel alone in that cold. Bring what you need to find your way back. Bring what you need to survive the pumps failing or the lights going out or the waterline rising on a night when the condensation runs heavier than expected.

The Reservoir will be here long after you're gone. It has that kind of patience. Make sure you do too.

## Clothing and Cold: The First Principle

The water is seven degrees. This is not metaphor. This is not the cool-but-manageable chill of a river in summer. Seven degrees Celsius is the temperature of meltwater, of deep storage, of liquid that has sat in the dark long enough to forget what warmth felt like. Your clothing will not protect you from this cold — not really, not against sustained exposure — but it will buy you time, and time is what you need.

**Layering is not optional. It is the only strategy.**

Start with a thermal base layer against your skin. Not cotton — cotton kills in the Reservoir, and everywhere like it. Wool or synthetic, something that will retain its insulation properties when wet. Your midlayer should be fleece or synthetic puffy, something that traps air and keeps trapping it even as your movement and the ambient moisture work against you. Your outer layer should be waterproof and windproof, but it should also be breathable, because you will sweat as you move through the place, and sweat that can't escape will find its way to cooling against your skin.

The walkway is slick with condensation. You will get wet. Not from falling in — though that is possible and would be catastrophic — but from the simple fact of moving through a space where water is always finding its way down. Dripping from high pipes. Condensing on railings. Running in rivulets down the curved walls to the waterline where you walk. Budget for getting damp. Budget for getting colder than you expect. Budget for the cold to feel like it is in your chest, like the Reservoir is examining you from the inside, pressing its patience against your sternum.

Gloves are non-negotiable. The steel railings will be as cold as the water. Your hands will lose dexterity quickly, and you will need dexterity here — for grip on wet surfaces, for steadying yourself, for the hundred small tasks that moving through a space like this requires. Liner gloves under waterproof shell gloves. Keep the shells dry when you can. You will not be able to keep them dry when you can't.

Footwear should be waterproof boots with good ankle support and grippy soles. The walkway is riveted steel, which means it has texture, but that texture is slick with condensation and algae in places, and the walkways cross the water at angles, and you will be tired, and tired feet make tired mistakes. Boots with insulation — the cold will find your feet through wet leather or rubber if you let it.

A hat. A proper hat, one that covers your ears and redirects water away from your face. The drizzle from high pipes will catch you when you least expect it. The ceiling is sixty, eighty, a hundred meters above in places, and the light doesn't show you where the water is falling until it's already on you.

Bring a change of clothes in a dry bag. Not for the Reservoir itself — you won't be changing in the Reservoir — but for the walk back. You will be damp and cold and tired, and the service tunnels will be just as cold as the main space, and the walk back is when hypothermia becomes a real risk. Dry clothes waiting at your exit point are not luxury. They are survival math.

## Lighting: What You'll Bring, and Why You Need More Than You Think

The sodium lights are orange and steady, yes. But they are also old, and they are also spaced for a maintenance purpose, and they are also illuminating a space that was designed to be lit from the walls and floors, not from a vaulted ceiling a hundred meters up. You will be walking in pools of light and corridors of shadow. The light will feel abundant in some places and absent in others, and the contrast will be disorienting, and your eyes will never fully adjust to the orange spectrum, and you will miss things — steps, edges, the dark shape that moved in your peripheral vision and might have been a rat or might have been a current-swell or might have been nothing.

**Bring more light than you think you need. Always.**

A headlamp is baseline. A good headlamp, with fresh batteries, with a strap that won't slide, with a beam that throws far enough to see where the walkway ends before it actually ends. The walkway extends in both directions until it disappears into distance, and distance in the Reservoir is not what your eyes expect. The perspective is wrong, or the scale is wrong, or something — things look closer than they are, and farther, and you will misjudge distances constantly until you learn to distrust your own depth perception.

Carry a handheld light as backup. Carry a second backup. Carry a chemical light stick in your pocket, not for illumination but for orientation — an orange glow in the dark helps you find your way back, helps you orient when the sodium lights blur into one continuous smear of false dawn.

The lights were meant to illuminate this space for maintenance crews. They do not illuminate it for you. Not fully. Not safely. Learn to light your own way, and learn to trust your light more than your eyes.

## Navigation: Reading a Space That Wasn't Made for Visitors

The Reservoir has grid lines. The walkway system was designed for systematic movement — for crews who had routes, who knew which junction led to which processing station, who could look at the hexagonal tiling on the ceiling and orient themselves by the pattern's logic. You don't have this knowledge. You will have to build it as you go, and the building will take longer than you expect.

**Start with a map if you can find one.** Service tunnel entrances sometimes have old signage — faded, rusted, half-readable. The Reservoir's designers were not subtle; the hexagonal tiles sometimes have numbering systems stenciled near the junctions. If you can read the numbers, you can orient. If you can find a map in the debris of someone's abandoned occupation — a maintenance schedule, a schematic, anything with dimensions — you will be better prepared than most visitors.

If you can't find a map, walk with intention. The Reservoir is vast, but it is not infinite. The walkways extend in both directions, yes, but they follow industrial logic: they go somewhere. They lead to equipment, to processing stations, to the drowned lower stories of towers that might once have been housing. Walk toward structure, toward the towers, toward the places where the scale is broken by something human-sized. When you can't see structure, walk along the waterline — the walkway always runs parallel to the reservoir's edge — until you find a junction or a stair or a railing with a number on it.

Mark your way if you can. Chalk, if you're not worried about leaving traces. A small piece of tape in an unobvious place, if you are. The Reservoir will not rearrange itself between visits, but your memory of it will blur, and the orange light will make everything look the same after a few hours, and the sound of drips will echo in ways that make you doubt which direction you came from. Mark your way.

The water is clear enough to see fifteen meters down. You can use it for orientation in a limited way — the submerged structures have shapes, and if you know which shape lies in which direction, you can triangulate your position. But this is an advanced technique, and it works best when you have a known landmark — a tower, a distinctive shadow, a place where the walkway turns — to start from. Don't rely on the water for primary navigation.

## The Water: What It Means, What It Can Do

The water is not your enemy. But it is not your friend, either. The water is simply the Reservoir's condition, the medium through which this place exists now, and it will do what water does: it will be cold, and deep, and still until something disturbs it, and then it will transmit that disturbance in ways that travel far and echo long.

**Do not fall in.**

This seems obvious. It is not obvious in the Reservoir, because the walkways are stable, and the water looks calm, and the fish move slowly, and the scale makes you feel like you're standing on solid ground in a well-designed space. But the walkways are slick with condensation, and the railings are cold and sometimes rusted, and you will be tired, and the edge of the walkway is closer than you think, and you will make one wrong step or lean too far or lose your footing on a rivet and then you will be in seven-degree water and your entire situation will change.

The shock will be the first thing. Your body will seize. Your breath will catch. You will have seconds — maybe thirty, maybe less — before the cold begins to compromise your coordination. You will need to get out immediately, and the walkway will be slick, and your hands will be numb, and the edge might be a meter away or might be ten, and in the orange light the waterline might be invisible until you're already at it.

If you fall in, move fast. Get horizontal, get your hands on the walkway edge, get your chest and then your hips and then your legs out in whatever order works. Don't waste time being shocked. Don't hesitate. The cold is not lethal yet, not in the first seconds, but it will be, and the longer you stay in, the more your muscles will stop listening to your brain.

If you're with someone, your companion's response time matters. Know where they are. Be close enough to reach them. Have a plan for extraction that doesn't require them to join you in the water. A rope, a stick, a folded jacket — something to extend reach without extending risk.

If you're alone, you do not fall in. You hold the railing. You test your footing. You walk with care. You do not take risks with your balance. You remember that the cold wants you in the water, that the Reservoir is patient, that the water has been waiting for centuries for something to slip.

## The Structures: What Lives Where

**The Upper Reaches (Above the Waterline)**

Pigeons. The cooing will be your first animal sound, bouncing off the vaulted ceiling, seeming to come from everywhere at once. The upper reaches are inaccessible by design — the walkway ends thirty, forty, fifty meters below the ceiling, and the hexagonal tiling is a maze of crossbeams and catwalks that were never meant for casual visiting. But you will hear the pigeons, and you will look up and see their silhouettes against the orange light, and they will look down and see you and not care. They have adapted to this space the way nothing else has: completely, and on their own terms.

Do not try to reach the pigeons. Do not try to reach the upper catwalks. The access points, if they exist, are not for you, and the structures up there are old and not maintained, and the pigeons will not thank you for disturbing them, and the ceiling is sixty meters up and the crossbeams are slick with condensation and you will fall and the water will be seven degrees.

**The Mid-Levels**

Rats. Quick, shy, more visible at the edges than in the center. They live in the drowned lower stories of the towers, in the debris of abandoned occupation, in the spaces between the walkway and the wall where wiring still hangs from junction boxes and someone's forgotten lunchbox has become a nest. They will not bother you if you don't bother them. They will scatter when they hear you coming. They are the Reservoir's cleaning crew, and they take their work seriously.

Be aware of them in the towers. The upper floors are dry and cluttered, and rats nest in the debris, and you will kick something or disturb something and they will scatter in ways that will make you jump. This is normal. This is fine. The rats are not dangerous in any meaningful sense — they are rats, they carry disease, you should wash your hands before you eat, but they are not aggressive, they are not pack hunters, they are just rats living in a vast engineered space that has become, in some ways, the world's largest urban colony.

**The Water**

Carp. Pale, slow, moving through water that is clear enough to see fifteen meters down but dark enough to hide what stirs deeper. You will see them near the surface, cupping for insects, basking in the thin warmth of the sodium light's reflection. You will see them deeper, as blunted shapes in the middle distance, drifting. You will not see what stirs deeper, if anything stirs deeper, because the water is dark and the light doesn't penetrate and the scale is vast and the carp are not the only things that live in it.

Fishermen fish here. They come through the old service tunnels and sit on the walkways and lower their lines into the cold. They have been doing this long enough that they know the Reservoir's rhythms — where the carp school, when they surface, how the light changes the water's color and the fish's behavior. If you see fishermen, they are a resource. They know this space. They know the service tunnels. They know which walkways are safe and which have collapsed and which towers are accessible and which are not. They will not always be friendly — fishermen have their own sense of territory, and the Reservoir is not large enough for everyone — but they will not leave you to die, not if they're the kind who fish here.

The fish taste of rust. This is a known fact. The water carries iron, the weld seams carry iron, the steel has been rusting since the day it was laid underwater. The carp taste of rust and nothing else, and some people say this is delicious, and some people say it is the taste of the place, and some people refuse to eat them on principle. You can decide your own relationship with the iron.

## Sound and Silence: What You'll Hear, What It Means

The Reservoir is loud. Not deafening — not the roar of a waterfall or the scream of a machine — but present, and constant, and layered in ways that will take hours to fully parse.

**The drip and echo of water finding its way down.** This is the dominant sound. Water condensing on the ceiling, running down the hexagonal tiles, finding its way to the edges, dropping to the walkway or the waterline or the lower structure with a sound that bounces and multiplies. In some places the drips are steady, metronomic. In others they are random, random enough that you will find yourself listening for patterns that aren't there. The sound will be in your ears long after you leave. You will hear dripping in your sleep.

**The hum of pumps in the distance.** Still running. Still cycling water through channels designed to handle far more than is coming in. The pumps are the Reservoir's heartbeat, mechanical and constant, and they will guide you if you're lost — the hum will orient you, give you a direction to walk toward or away from. But the pumps are also a reminder: this space is still running. Still serving a purpose. Still on autopilot in the dark below everything else.

**The silence of the water itself.** This is harder to describe. Water makes no sound when it is still, but the Reservoir's water is not truly still — it is being pumped, circulated, disturbed by the carp and the condensation and the slow mineral deposits running down every surface. But the scale is so vast that the disturbance is negligible, and the water sits at the bottom of a constructed space with a patience that feels geological. You will feel this silence more than you hear it. When the drips fade and the pumps hum and the echo bounces and fades and you are standing at the waterline looking down, you will hear the water not-making-sound, the pressure of a vast volume of liquid at rest, the sound of a thing that does not want to be where it is.

**The mechanical heartbeat of a system still running on nothing.** This is the summary of the soundscape. The pumps, the drips, the distant hum of machinery designed for an emergency that never came — they are all versions of the same story: purpose without reason, function without need, operation without observer. The Reservoir is an automated space, and you are not part of its automation, and the sounds it makes are not for you. But they are what you will hear, and they will become normal, and they will become the texture of your presence there.

## Safety: What Can Go Wrong, and What to Do About It

**Hypothermia**

This is the primary risk. Not from falling in — though falling in would be catastrophic — but from sustained exposure. The cold will get into you. The condensation will dampen your layers. The wind will find you in the spaces between walkways and towers where the air circulation creates drafts. You will be tired, and tired means cold, and cold means slow, and slow means more tired.

Dress for the cold you expect and the cold you don't. Carry emergency thermal protection — a space blanket, a dry suit if you have one, a change of base layers in a dry bag. Eat before you go; your body needs fuel to generate heat. Rest before you go; exhaustion is a cold risk factor. Monitor yourself and your companions for the signs: shivering, confusion, clumsy hands, a slowing pace. If you feel any of these, turn back. The Reservoir will be here tomorrow.

**Light Failure**

The lights are old. The pumps are old. The infrastructure is old, and it runs on nothing, and the automation that keeps it running is not being maintained by anyone. The lights could go out. One section, or all of them, or some middle configuration that leaves you in darkness deep enough to stumble.

Never rely on the lights. This is the principle. Your headlamp is your primary light. Your backup is your secondary. Your chemical stick is your tertiary. If the lights go out, you have yours. If yours fail, you have the next. If you have nothing, you stand still, you put your hands on the railing, you breathe, and you wait for your eyes to adjust. They will adjust, partly, to the orange glow of whatever sodium fixtures still work in the distance. They will not adjust fully. You will not see well. But you will see enough to find your way back if you know the route.

**Getting Lost**

The walkways extend in both directions. The perspective is wrong. The light pools and shadows. The sound echoes. You will lose your sense of direction, and you will doubt your memory of which way you came, and you will second-guess the junction you thought you recognized.

Mark your way. Follow your light. Trust your headlamp more than your eyes. If you're truly lost — if you can't find the walkway edge, if you can't find a junction, if the water is where you didn't expect it to be — stop. Stand still. Listen. The pumps hum in one direction. The drips echo from the ceiling above and the water below. Pigeons coo in the upper reaches, and rats scatter in the mid-levels, and the fishermen fish somewhere in the distance, and the water is still, and the orange light glows. Find a sound you recognize. Find a direction the hum comes from. Walk toward it. You will find structure. The Reservoir is too designed to not have structure.

**Structural Collapse**

The walkways are steel, riveted, solid in most places. But they are old, and the condensation corrodes, and the weld seams are sharp but not necessarily strong, and some sections have been underwater longer than others. The towers rise forty meters, their lower stories drowned, their upper floors dry and cluttered. Some floors are safe. Some floors are not. You will not always know which.

Test your footing. Test the railings. Walk on the walkway, not on the tower floors unless you know the tower. If you hear groaning, or feel vibration, or see rust that looks wet and fresh rather than dry and old — stop, assess, turn back. The Reservoir was built for maintenance crews, but the maintenance crews are long gone, and no one is inspecting the walkways for structural integrity. Assume that some sections are compromised. Find the sections that aren't.

## What to Bring: The Packing List

This is not an exhaustive list. This is not a gear guide. This is what your body and mind need to survive the Reservoir and return intact.

**Clothing**

- Thermal base layer (wool or synthetic, not cotton)
- Insulating midlayer (fleece or synthetic puffy)
- Waterproof outer layer (breathable, with hood)
- Insulated waterproof boots with grippy soles
- Liner gloves under waterproof shell gloves
- Hat that covers ears and redirects water
- Change of dry clothes in a dry bag (for after)

**Lighting**

- Headlamp with fresh batteries
- Handheld backup light with fresh batteries
- Second handheld backup light or chemical light sticks
- Spare batteries for all lights

**Safety and Emergency**

- First aid kit (basic, focused on cold exposure and minor injuries)
- Space blanket or emergency thermal bag
- Rope (short, light, for extraction if someone falls)
- Dry bag for your change of clothes
- Emergency whistle (for signaling if separated)
- Water and food (more than you think you need — the cold burns calories)

**Navigation and Documentation**

- Map if you have one, otherwise a notebook and pencil
- Compass (the pumps hum, but the compass is more reliable)
- Marking tape or chalk (for your route)

**Personal Items**

- Hand sanitizer (you will touch surfaces; the rats have been there)
- Snacks (high-calorie, portable)
- Camera if you want one (the light is extraordinary)
- Patience (you cannot rush the Reservoir, and it will not hurry for you)

## The Psychological Weight: What You'll Feel

The Reservoir will not try to harm you. It is not malicious, not haunted, not strange in the way that horror is strange. But it is too large for the life it contains. It was built for a purpose that never came, and now it serves that purpose by default — a cathedral of nothing running on autopilot, a storage space for an emergency that never arrived.

You will feel this. The scale will press on you. The orange light will make everything look the same, and then the same will blur into nothing, and the nothing will be the Reservoir's true face — vast, cold, patient, indifferent. The drip of water will become the sound of the place breathing. The hum of the pumps will become the sound of the place thinking. The silence of the water will become the sound of the place waiting.

This is not a horror you can survive by running. You survive it by accepting it. You are a visitor in a space that was not built for visitors. You are warm in a cold place. You are alive in a space that does not care whether you are alive. You are brief, and the Reservoir is not.

Bring your patience with you. Bring your willingness to stand still in the orange light and listen to the drip and echo of water finding its way down. Bring your ability to feel small in a space designed for purpose and find that smallness beautiful.

The Reservoir will be here after you leave. It has that kind of time. Make sure you have the kind that matches.
