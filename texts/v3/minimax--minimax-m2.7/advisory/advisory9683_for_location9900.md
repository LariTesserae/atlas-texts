# Visiting the Basin

The Basin is not hostile in the way that deserts are hostile, or frozen tundra, or places of active conflict. It does not kill you quickly or obviously. It kills you slowly, by inches, by degrees, by the slow breakdown of everything your body needs to function. It is a place that wants to dissolve you back into the moisture and the heat, and if you stay long enough, it will succeed. But on a short visit—if you can even think in those terms, if time in the Basin even works the way time works elsewhere—you have a chance of walking out whole.

Here is what you should know.

---

## The Water Is Not Your Friend

You will think, at first, that the water is the main problem. You see it everywhere: pooling in the cracks, beading on every surface, running in thin sheets down walls and across floors, pooling in every depression and low place. You will think you need to stay dry.

You do not need to stay dry. Staying dry in the Basin is not possible. It is not a goal you can achieve. The humidity is not something you can defeat—it is the medium you are moving through, as unavoidable as air. Your clothing will be damp from the moment you arrive. Your skin will never fully dry. Your eyes will water constantly from the moisture in the air. Accept this. Make peace with it. Your only task is to manage the water, not eliminate it.

What this means in practice:

Bring clothing that handles constant moisture without breaking down. Cotton is useless—it holds water, takes forever to dry, and will rot. Synthetics with good wicking properties will pull moisture away from your skin, which matters enormously for preventing the skin breakdown that prolonged dampness causes. You want things that dry quickly when conditions allow, because conditions do sometimes shift, and brief drier periods will occur.

Your outer layer should be breathable waterproof or water-resistant. Not impermeable—impermeable means your own sweat has nowhere to go, and in the heat of the Basin, you will be sweating constantly. You will cook inside your own gear. Breathable waterproof fabrics like certain synthetic membranes let moisture escape while keeping some rain off. But nothing will keep you dry in the Basin. Everything will be damp. Your goal is to stay *manageably* damp rather than soaked through and cold, which is a risk in the deeper shade, and to prevent the worst of the chafing and skin damage that constant moisture causes.

Footwear is critical. You need boots or shoes that drain well, that won't hold water against your feet, that have good traction on wet surfaces. Every surface in the Basin is slick—concrete, metal, wet brick, the thin films of water over stone. You will fall. Assume you will fall. Plan for it. Traction is life. Consider gaiters to keep the worst of the water out of your footwear, but understand that "out" is relative here—water will get in. Your feet will be wet. Choose footwear that functions when wet, that doesn't fall apart, that doesn't give you blisters, that you can move in without thinking about.

Bring eye protection. Goggles or wraparound glasses that handle fogging. Your eyes will be streaming constantly from the humidity, and the haze—whatever is in the air, the particulate, the residue—will irritate them. Clear vision matters in the Basin because of the fauna.

Gloves. Gripping wet surfaces, steadying yourself, handling anything in the environment. Your hands will be in the water constantly.

---

## The Heat Will Kill You

The Basin is warm. Not comfortable warm—oppressive warm. The heat rises from below, from the infrastructure still running in the deep places, from the trapped low elevation, from the water itself holding warmth in the air. On still days, the heat presses down like a physical weight. You will feel it on your skin. You will feel it in your lungs. Your body will struggle to cool itself.

Your body cools by sweating. The sweat evaporates from your skin, carrying heat away. But evaporation requires relatively dry air to work efficiently, and the air in the Basin is never dry. The humidity saturates the air, and when the air is already full of water vapor, sweat cannot evaporate. You will sweat, and sweat, and the sweat will just sit on your skin, and you will not cool down. This is the combination that makes the Basin dangerous: high heat plus high humidity equals constant heat stress.

You must drink water constantly. Not when you're thirsty—thirst is a lagging indicator of dehydration. Constantly, preemptively, continuously. If you wait until you're thirsty, you are already behind. You need more water than you think. Carry more than you think you need. Plan for the water you carry to be your primary water source. Do not attempt to source water locally unless you have excellent filtration and treatment—the water in the Basin is not safe. It stands in pools over old industrial surfaces, it runs through channels that carry god knows what, it condenses on materials that harbor bacteria and fungi. Assume all local water is contaminated.

Electrolytes matter. You will be sweating out salts. Replace them.

Watch yourself and anyone with you for signs of heat illness: headache, nausea, dizziness, confusion, decreased sweating despite the heat, hot dry skin or conversely clammy skin, rapid heartbeat. Heat exhaustion becomes heat stroke becomes death. In the Basin, this can happen faster than you expect.

Move slowly. Rest often. Do not exert yourself beyond what you can sustain. The heat will make everything harder. Your stamina will crater. Your judgment will suffer. Accept this. Plan for it.

---

## The Fauna Is Not Friendly

The Basin has no large predators, as far as anyone knows. What it has is rats, and cats, and insects, and birds, and all of them are adapted to this environment in ways you are not. They have been living here for a long time. You have not.

**Rats** live in the foundations, in the walls, in every gap and hollow that moisture has opened up. They are not aggressive, but they are not tame. They bite when cornered, when threatened, when you reach into a space without looking. They carry disease. They are always hungry. Do not leave food unsecured. Do not put your hands anywhere you cannot see. Do not sleep on the ground in rat territory without protection.

**Cats** are feral and thick-furred and wary. They hunt at dusk, moving through the drainage channels and along the walls. They will not approach you, but they will watch you. Do not corner one. Do not reach toward one. They are not pets and they do not want your contact.

**Insects** are everywhere—in the walls, in the floors, in the wet seams of concrete where things breed. Flies breed in the moisture. Moths come to the lights. Mosquitoes—if there are mosquitoes, and there probably are—breed in the standing water. Gnats and midges in clouds. They are a constant irritation and a disease vector. Cover your skin at dusk. Use netting. Accept that you will be bitten.

**Birds** watch from the rooftops. Crows, maybe, or something like crows. They are curious. They are intelligent. They will investigate you. Do not turn your back on a large bird, if there are large birds here. They may be harmless. They may not be.

The fauna is part of the ecosystem that has replaced human purpose in the Basin. They are not obstacles. They are residents. Move through their home with respect. Do not engage. Do not feed them. Do not leave anything behind that will habituate them to humans.

---

## The Air Is Not Clean

You will breathe the air of the Basin. It is warm, and wet, and it carries things you should not breathe.

The haze may be humidity. It may be residue from whatever processes once filled this atmosphere with smoke and steam. It may be mold spores, fungal particles, the products of decay. It may be all of these things. Assume it is harmful to breathe in quantity. Wear respiratory protection. A simple mask or cloth over your face will filter some of the particulate. A proper respirator with appropriate filters will filter more. This is not paranoia—this is precaution. You do not know what is in the air. The Basin has been breathing its own breath for a long time.

The condensation on surfaces is not clean. Do not drink it. Do not touch it to your skin if you have open wounds, which you probably will, because the Basin is hard on skin. The moisture everywhere carries biological material—the byproducts of decay, the waste of rats and insects and fungi. Your body can handle some of it, probably. It cannot handle all of it, especially if you are already weakened by heat and dehydration.

Fungal infections are a risk. Your lungs, your skin, everywhere moisture collects. The constant dampness is ideal for fungal growth, and you are walking through a world that is essentially a fungal garden in some respects. Watch for respiratory symptoms. Watch for skin changes. Keep your feet dry when possible—fungal foot infections are common in persistently wet conditions.

---

## The Light Is Deceptive

The sun in the Basin is a pale smear behind the haze. The streets glow with reflected warmth where water lies thin over stone. The light is diffuse, muted, directionless. It does not cast sharp shadows. It does not help you see clearly. It helps you see that there is light, and that is all.

Bring your own light. Flashlights, headlamps, whatever you prefer. Assume that you will need to navigate by artificial light. The pale daylight is not enough for detailed work—for reading signs, for inspecting surfaces, for checking your gear, for seeing what is in the shadows. The shadows in the Basin are deep and full of things that do not want to be seen.

The light sources in the Basin—whatever is running in the deep infrastructure—may provide illumination in places. The steam, the warmth, the glow of residual heat. But do not count on it. Carry light.

---

## The Buildings Are Not Safe

The city was built to last, and it does last, in its way. Brick and steel, the bones of it, still stand. But the organization has gone out of them. They are frameworks without purpose, shells without contents, teeth without a jaw to bite with.

They still contain hazards. Floors that have rotted through. Walls that are unstable. Foundations that have shifted with the water, with the heat, with the years. The moisture has worked its way into everything. Wood swells and warps. Metal corrodes. Concrete cracks and spalls. The buildings stand, but standing is not the same as safe.

Assume that any floor you cannot see is potentially unsafe. Assume that any room you enter has hazards you cannot see. Move carefully. Test surfaces before you commit weight to them. Do not trust stairs that have been wet for years. Do not trust railings. Do not trust anything that was not designed for the conditions it now experiences.

The interiors are warm—the residual heat from buried pipes and thermal exchangers keeps them from being cold, keeps the water from stagnating. This makes them survivable. It also makes them hospitable to everything that lives in the Basin. The walls hum with insects. The floors creak with rats. The dark corners are full of movement.

---

## The Infrastructure Still Functions (Poorly)

Something in the deep infrastructure is still running. You hear it as a distant mechanical pulse, a heartbeat at the threshold of hearing. Engines, maybe, or turbines, driving heat exchangers, pumping warmth through buried pipes. The city was designed to generate and distribute heat, and it still does, if barely, if wrongly, if in ways that serve no economy but keep the moisture and the heat alive.

This is a resource and a hazard. The heat exchangers provide warmth that makes the Basin survivable in the way that a constant-temperature greenhouse is survivable. Without them, the nights might be colder, the damp might stagnate. But they also mean that there are active systems, potentially pressurized systems, electrical systems, systems that were never meant to operate without tending. The steam you hear—hissing at pressure points—is evidence of these systems failing in small ways, releasing pressure, leaking heat. Do not assume that everything in the infrastructure is dead. Treat it as potentially active. Treat pressurized pipes as potentially dangerous. Treat electrical systems with extreme caution.

The drainage channels run through the city, carrying water toward the basin's center. They are clogged with debris, with organic matter, with the bodies of dead things. They are also the pathways that the cats use to hunt, and that rats travel, and that insects breed in. They are not safe to enter. They are not pleasant to be near. But they are part of the infrastructure, and understanding them may help you understand the water flow in the city—which paths flood, which stay passable, where the water pools deepest.

---

## The Sound Is Misleading

Sound in the Basin is present but subdued. The hiss of steam. The drip and trickle of condensation. The distant mechanical pulse. The fauna moves quietly: footsteps on wet concrete, the scrape of claws, the occasional call that carries brief and sharp through the heavy air.

The sound is deceptive because the air is thick. Sound does not travel the same way it does in dry air. Distances are hard to judge. Directions are hard to determine. What sounds close may be far. What sounds far may be very close, muffled by walls and moisture.

Listen to your own footsteps. They will tell you about the surface you are walking on—hollow sounds mean voids beneath, solid sounds mean stable ground. Learn to read the acoustics of the Basin.

The silence, when it comes, is not true silence. It is just the absence of obvious sound. There are always things moving. There are always things breathing. There are always things watching.

## What to Bring

The ordinary human multiverse traveler has certain advantages—you presumably have access to materials and knowledge from many contexts. Apply that.

**Water.** More than you think. At minimum two liters per person per day, and that assumes you are not exerting yourself. In the Basin, you will need more. Carry it in durable containers. Consider a water filter for emergencies, but do not rely on local water.

**Food.** Non-perishable. The humidity will ruin fresh food. The heat will spoil food faster. Bring what you need. Bring more than you think you need in case you cannot leave when you planned to.

**Clothing.** As described above. Extra layers for cooler areas. No cotton.

**Footwear.** As described above. Consider multiple pairs in case one fails.

**Eye protection.** Goggles or wraparound glasses with anti-fog coatings.

**Respiratory protection.** At minimum, a mask. Better, a proper respirator with particulate filters.

**Gloves.** Multiple pairs. Wet gloves are miserable gloves.

**Light.** Headlamp. Flashlight. Spare batteries. Red light mode if you have it, to preserve night vision and to not attract fauna.

**First aid.** Antifungal cream is essential. Antibiotic ointment for the inevitable cuts and scrapes. Bandages that hold in damp conditions. Pain relievers. Electrolytes.

**Communication.** Whatever works in this context. Radio, phone, signal mirror. You may need to call for help.

**Navigation.** Map, compass, GPS if it functions here. The Basin has no working signage, no functioning wayfinding. Know where you are going.

**Psychological preparation.** The Basin is not a pleasant place. The perpetual damp, the oppressive heat, the muted light, the sense of being in a place that has gone on living without purpose. This wears on the mind. Bring whatever you need to maintain morale—entertainment, comfort objects, reasons to keep going. The physical hazards are real, but the psychological hazards are also real. Do not underestimate them.

## How to Move

Walk slowly. The heat will make you slow anyway. Watch your footing—every surface is slick. Test the ground ahead before committing weight. Stay in the middle of the street, where the water runs thin and the surfaces are more likely to be visible. Stay out of the drainage channels. Stay out of the deep shade, where the air is stillest and the heat is worst.

Rest in the shade when you must, but remember that the shade in the Basin is warm, not cool. The heat rises from below. There is no escape from it, only temporary relief.

Dusk is when the cats hunt. Dawn may be when the birds are most active. Time your movements accordingly, or at least be alert at those times.

Follow the water if you can read it. The water flows toward the basin's center, toward the lowest point, toward where the pooling is deepest. This is useful information for navigation, and it is also a warning—deeper water means worse conditions, more hazards, more fauna.

Follow the sound of the mechanical pulse if you need to find the deep infrastructure. It comes from below, from the city's heart. Be careful near pressure points, near steam vents, near anything that hisses or whistles.

---

## How to Leave

The Basin is not a place to stay. It is a place to pass through.

Plan your exit before you enter. Know where you will emerge. Know how long you can afford to stay. Have a way to measure time—your body will lose track in the heat and the damp and the muted light.

Leave nothing behind that you do not want the Basin to keep. Everything you drop will join the metabolism of the place. Food will attract rats. Trash will join the detritus. Equipment will be consumed by corrosion or by creatures looking for nesting material.

When you leave, you will be damp. You will be hot. You will be tired. You will smell of the Basin—of moisture and rust and organic decay and the particular warmth of a place that breathes its own breath.

This is the price of entry. The Basin does not charge at the gate. It charges when you leave, in the cost of cleaning yourself, of drying out, of returning to a world that does not hold water in suspension, that does not press heat down like a weight, that does not hum with the distant pulse of things that run without purpose.

You can pay that price. The Basin is survivable. It is not pleasant, and it is not safe, and it is not a place you want to be for long. But it is survivable.

Go prepared. Go careful. Go with respect for the place that persists in the dark, wet, hot, and dense, fed by what remains and by what breeds in the remainder.
