# Field Preparation Guide: The Plateau of Drowned Summits

## Environmental Assessment

You've studied the entry carefully. This is a place of paradoxes — high altitude that should be cold but isn't, ancient erosion that should be stable but isn't, water that should sustain but doesn't quite. The combination of thin atmosphere, geothermal influence, and ceaseless wind creates something genuinely hostile to human physiology, not through a single overwhelming factor but through the sheer relentless pressure of multiple insults. This is the kind of place where ordinary problems compound: the thin air makes you tire faster, the wind makes you lose heat despite the warmth, the inconsistent water makes you ration and worry, the noise makes you lose sleep, and the unpredictable weather makes you always damp and always exposed. Preparation isn't about solving any one of these problems — it's about building systems robust enough to handle all of them, simultaneously, for as long as you're willing to stay.

The good news: this place will not kill you quickly. You have time to make mistakes, time to adapt, time to find your way. The bad news: it will wear you down through a thousand small difficulties if you're not prepared. Think of this as a place where preparation quality directly determines experience quality. A well-prepared traveler can find moments of genuine beauty here. A poorly prepared one will spend their whole time just surviving.

## Respiratory and Physiological Preparation

### The Thin Air Problem

Elevation effects are your most fundamental challenge. At extreme heights, your body struggles to extract sufficient oxygen from each breath, leading to reduced cognitive performance, impaired judgment, compromised immune function, and the suite of altitude sickness symptoms: headache, nausea, fatigue, and shortness of breath that can progress to pulmonary or cerebral edema in severe cases. The fact that geothermal warmth moderates the temperature actually makes this worse — you feel more capable than you are, more willing to exert, more inclined to push through that warning tightness in your chest.

**Preparation approach:** Spend time at elevation before you arrive. If you're coming from sea-level, ascend gradually through intermediate universes — spend a few days at 3,000 meters, then 4,000, then 5,000, if possible in your transit sequence. This allows your hemoglobin levels to increase, your lung capacity to adapt, your circulatory system to fine-tune. Even two or three days of pre-acclimatization dramatically reduces risk.

**On-site monitoring:** Watch for symptoms religiously. The headache that creeps on by afternoon, the slight nausea that makes food unappealing, the shortness of breath that feels normal but isn't — these are your body's warnings. Rest immediately when you notice them. Descend if they worsen. Altitude sickness doesn't negotiate.

**Medication:** Carry acetazolamide (Diamox) as a prophylactic if you're not acclimatized, or as treatment if symptoms develop. Nifedipine can help with pulmonary edema risk. Ibuprofen addresses headache and may help with acute symptoms. Consult a travel medicine professional before your trip for appropriate dosing regimens.

### Thermal Regulation

The geothermal warmth creates a deceptive situation. You'll feel warm when standing in the sun, during wind breaks, when sheltered. Then the wind shifts, the sun vanishes into cloud, and you'll be cold faster than seems possible. Your core temperature will fluctuate more than you're used to, and cold stress combined with altitude stress compounds both.

**Preparation approach:** Don't rely on the ambient warmth. Build your clothing system for cold, and shed layers as needed. The goal is never to be warm — it's to never be cold enough for your body to divert energy to warming.

**Layering principle:** Start with a thin, moisture-wicking base layer that pulls sweat away from your skin. Add an insulating middle layer — fleece or lightweight down — for static warmth. Top with a wind-resistant shell that blocks the cutting wind that penetrates everything here. The shell is critical; wind chill here is severe, and the wind is always finding channels to blow through.

**Carry a puffy jacket:** A compressible down or synthetic jacket that you can pull on the moment you stop moving. Your exertion keeps you warm while walking; the moment you pause to rest, eat, or observe, you'll start cooling. This is counterintuitive for people used to moderate climates — you don't need the jacket while exerting, you need it the instant you stop.

### Hydration and Electrolytes

The thin air accelerates moisture loss through respiration and evaporation. The wind desiccates you. The altitude makes you pee more (your body dumps fluid to acclimatize). The irregular water availability means you must carry more than you expect you'll need, and you must drink whether or not you're thirsty — thirst response is blunted at altitude.

**Preparation approach:** Calculate water needs based on exertion level, not comfort. At altitude, moderate activity, you'll lose 2-4 liters per day through various pathways. Carry at least that much, plus reserves for emergencies. Plan to treat any water you find, since pools are muddy and mineral content is unknown.

**Electrolyte management:** Replace sodium and potassium aggressively. Carry electrolyte tablets or powders. Add them to water regularly. If you feel muscle cramping, confusion, or profound fatigue beyond normal altitude tiredness, electrolyte imbalance is likely a contributing cause.

## Gear Systems

### Footwear

The stone here is worn smooth in places, rough and abrasive in others, coated with mineral deposits that can be slick when wet. Loose scree and shifting gravel appear without warning. Your footing will be tested constantly.

**Requirements:** Sturdy boots with ankle support — you will stumble, you will step wrong, you need protection. Vibram or similar aggressive soles for traction on varied stone. Waterproof or at least water-resistant, because the sudden rains will soak you and the damp will persist in creases and cracks. Break them in extensively before you arrive — blisters at altitude become serious infection risks.

**Gaiters:** Not optional. Gaiters keep debris out of your boots, provide a small measure of additional weather protection, and reduce the amount of gravel working into your boot seams. Choose durable, adjustable gaiters that can handle the rough stone.

### Waterproofing System

You will get wet. The rains come without warning, in sheets. Even in the periods between rain, the condensation on rocks, the moisture in the air, the morning damp — it all accumulates. Being damp in the wind is miserable; being damp at altitude is dangerous.

**Shell layer:** A fully waterproof and windproof outer layer. Taped seams, adjustable hood, long cut to protect your lower back. Gore-Tex or equivalent is standard. This is your weather armor.

**Backup dry layer:** Carry a complete change of base layers, including socks and underwear, in a waterproof compression sack. When you do get soaked — and you will — you need something dry to change into. Being able to dry out completely, even briefly, is a psychological and physical reset.

**Drying strategy:** Plan for wet gear. Your tent will be damp. Your clothes will be damp. You cannot prevent this; you can only manage it. A microfiber towel for wiping down, a line for hanging gear, patience. Consider synthetic fabrics that dry faster than cotton.

### Navigation

The landscape is disorienting. Mesas and buttes that look identical across vast distances. Gorges that cut the plateaus into irregular segments. Clouds that obscure landmarks. Paths that wind and dead-end. People have lived here — there are structures and remnants — which means paths existed, but those paths are ancient and eroded and not obvious.

**GPS:** Carry a GPS unit, fully charged, with downloaded maps or coordinate data. The magnetic field here may differ from your origin universe; bring a compass as backup but don't trust it absolutely. Electronic navigation is more reliable given the atmospheric distortion.

**Physical maps:** Print or hand-draw maps based on your observations and any information from previous travelers. The structures you see in the gorges — mark their locations if you can. This landscape rewards careful documentation.

**Trail marking:** Bring bright markers or tape. Mark your path at decision points, at water sources, at any feature worth returning to. In the wind and the noise and the visual sameness of eroded stone, it's easy to lose your way. A strip of orange tape tied to a rock formation is worth a thousand words of route description.

### Shelter

You need protection from the wind more than from rain, though rain protection is also critical. The wind here does not simply blow; it howls, threads through channels, gusts unpredictably. Sleeping in that is not restful. Sleeping wet in that is dangerous.

**Tent requirements:** A sturdy, wind-resistant shelter. Four-season tent at minimum — this landscape is harsher than most four-season contexts. The tent must handle wind loading without complaint, must have rainfly coverage that extends close to ground to block the howling drafts. Pitch it in sheltered spots — behind rock formations, in natural alcoves — not on exposed plateau centers.

**Anchor everything:** Stakes deep, lines secure, nothing loose. The wind will test every connection. Carry extra stakes; you may need to reposition anchors as wind patterns shift.

**Sleep system:** A sleeping bag rated for temperatures well below what you'd expect to encounter. Even with geothermal warmth, nights at altitude get cold, and the wind finds every gap. A sleeping pad with high R-value is non-negotiable — ground conduction will sap your heat faster than the air will.

### Water Procurement

You'll carry what you can, but extended stays require water sourcing. The pools are temporary and muddy. Rain is unpredictable. What looks like a reliable water source may be dry by afternoon.

**Filtration:** Carry a filter capable of handling turbid water — the muddy pools will clog simple filters. A backwashing or hollow-fiber filter rated for gross contamination. Alternatively, chemical treatment combined with settling time. Test your filtration method on dirty water before you arrive; you need to know it works.

**Scavenging strategy:** Learn to read the landscape for water. Watch where condensation forms. Notice which rock depressions retain moisture longest. Follow animal trails — even sparse life indicates water access. Be prepared to hike to water sources rather than finding them where you camp.

**Storage:** Carry water in multiple vessels. Bladders for convenience, hard bottles for durability. Never let one container be your only water; if it cracks or empties, you need options.

## Food and Sustenance

### Caloric Needs

Altitude increases metabolic rate. Cold stress increases metabolic rate. The effort of difficult terrain increases metabolic rate. You will burn more calories here than you expect, possibly 3,000-4,000 per day for moderate activity.

**Caloric density:** Carry high-calorie foods. Nuts, nut butters, cheese, dried meats, chocolate, energy bars — foods that pack substantial calories into small weight. You cannot carry unlimited supplies; every gram matters, but you also cannot under-eat.

**Ease of preparation:** Fuel for cooking may be limited if you can't carry much. Plan meals that require minimal heat, that can be eaten cold, that are ready to eat or require only rehydration. Hot food is morale-boosting but not essential.

### Micronutrients

Extended stays mean you'll develop deficiencies if your diet is monotonous. Vitamins, minerals, varied macronutrients — your body needs these for immune function, tissue repair, cognitive performance.

**Multivitamin:** Simple solution. Carry a daily multivitamin and take it regardless of how well you're eating.

**Variety:** Carry a range of foods, not just your favorite calorie-dense items. The monotony itself becomes a problem — eating the same thing day after day destroys appetite, and loss of appetite at altitude is dangerous.

## Physical Preparation

### Fitness Standards

This is not a casual destination. You need cardiovascular fitness for altitude, strength for carrying packs, balance and proprioception for difficult footing, endurance for days of sustained effort in harsh conditions.

**Cardiovascular base:** Build a base of sustained cardio work — hiking, running, cycling, anything that elevates your heart rate for extended periods. This isn't about speed; it's about efficiency and reserve.

**Load bearing:** Practice hiking with a loaded pack. Your pack will be heavy — water, food, gear, emergency supplies. Train with weights that approximate your planned load.

**Balance work:** Single-leg exercises, unstable surface training, anything that develops your ability to catch yourself when footing fails. This is more important than most people realize in rough terrain.

### Mental Preparation

The noise here is relentless. The wind never stops. The sudden rain, the sun, the clouds, the ever-present roaring and creaking and rushing — this is psychologically wearing. Add to this the isolation, the difficulty of terrain, the constant small decisions about water and route and shelter, and you have a context that demands mental resilience.

**Meditation practice:** Build a meditation habit before you go. Practice sitting with discomfort, with noise, with the inability to control your environment. This will translate directly into the mental endurance you'll need.

**Acceptance practice:** This landscape will not accommodate your preferences. Rain comes when it comes. Wind blows as it blows. Your plans will be disrupted. Mental flexibility — the ability to adjust, to accept, to continue without frustration consuming you — is essential.

**Expectation management:** This will not be comfortable. It will not be easy. It may be beautiful, but it will be demanding. Come prepared to work, to struggle, to be challenged. The most rewarding places are rarely the comfortable ones.

## Safety and Emergency Protocols

### Falls and Trauma

The gorges are described as bottomless, or nearly so. The stone is uneven, the scree loose. Falling is the most acute risk of this landscape.

**Never walk close to edges:** The edges look stable; they aren't. Erosion undercuts them. The stone breaks. Give yourself a wide margin from any drop.

**Travel together when possible:** If you're not alone, don't separate far. A fall into a gorge may be invisible from above; your partner may not know you need help.

**Trauma kit:** Carry a substantial first aid kit. Bandages, wound closure strips, antiseptic, pain management, splinting materials. Evacuation from this terrain may be impossible for days. You must be able to manage serious injuries yourself.

### Disorientation

The landscape is featureless in places, repetitive in others, and the weather and lighting can change everything about how it looks.

**Always know your exit direction:** Before you start hiking each day, confirm your bearing. Carry a compass. Mark your camp location visibly.

**Time limits:** If you're exploring away from camp, set a turn-back time. If you haven't reached your objective by that time, you return anyway, regardless of how close you seem. The light changes fast here; you don't want to be caught in a gorge at dusk with no shelter.

**Emergency signaling:** Carry a whistle, a mirror, a bright flag or panel. If you become disoriented or injured, signaling options matter. The acoustics of the gorges may carry sound farther than you'd expect — use that.

### Medical Emergencies

Beyond trauma and altitude sickness, the specific risks here are dehydration, hypothermia (despite the warmth), and the psychological effects of isolation and noise.

**Emergency communication:** If you have any means of contacting help across universes, test it here. Atmospheric conditions may interfere. Backup protocols matter.

**Evacuation plan:** Know how you'd get out if you had to. Which direction is nearest easier terrain? Are there structures that might provide shelter if you couldn't reach your base? Plan for the worst case.

## Environmental Considerations

### Water Contamination

You don't know what lived here before, what organisms persist, what mineral content affects human health. The iron taste suggests high mineral content; this may be benign or may indicate problematic dissolved metals.

**Never drink raw water:** Treat everything, always. Filter, chemical treatment, UV, whatever you prefer — apply it consistently. The temporary pools may look clean; they aren't necessarily.

**Monitor yourself:** If you develop gastrointestinal symptoms, unusual fatigue, or any signs of heavy metal exposure (cognitive changes, tremors, unusual weakness), consider water as a vector and switch to your reserve supply.

### Wildlife

The description mentions sparse fauna, but "almost no people" and "almost nothing" are not the same as none. Something eats the plants, drinks the pools, lives in the structures. That something may be rare, may be dangerous, may be protected.

**Observe before approaching:** Any structure, any pool, any thicket — look first. Watch for movement, for signs of occupation. You are a visitor; act like one.

**Do not interfere:** Don't collect plants, don't disturb structures, don't remove artifacts. The people who lived here left these remnants for reasons. Respect them.

## Psychological and Philosophical Preparation

### The Experience of Time

This landscape operates on geological time. The mountains are dying. The water is erasing them. What you see is a process so slow it exceeds human perception, except that here, where erosion is active, where the rock is always moving, you can almost feel it. You are a brief visitor in a place that measures existence in epochs.

**Sit with this:** Don't rush through. Find a moment when the wind dies, when the sun is warm, when you can simply be present. The scale of this place is humbling. The intelligence required to prepare for it is small and temporary, like the plants in the water-pockets, like the structures in the gorges. You are here for a moment. Let the moment matter.

**Contemplate impermanence:** The structures below, the trails long eroded, the people who built and lived and left — this place teaches impermanence. You too will leave. Your preparations, your caution, your care — all of it is temporary. This is not nihilism; it's perspective. You prepare not to conquer the landscape, but to be worthy of visiting it.

### Solitude

Almost no people. You may be alone for days. This is both the danger and the gift. The silence, when it comes, is full — of wind, of water, of the weight of stone. You will hear yourself here in ways you don't in busy places. You will confront your own thoughts, your own needs, your own patterns. This is useful.

**Bring an internal life:** A book, a journal, a practice. Something to do when you're not walking, not eating, not sleeping. The hours between activities can stretch long when you're alone in the wind.

**Welcome the solitude:** Fear of being alone is common. Here, you can learn to be alone without being lonely. The landscape is company. The stars, when the clouds part, are company. The work of preparation and travel is company. Being alone here is not a deficit to be compensated; it's a condition to be inhabited.

## Final Recommendations

### Packing Philosophy

**Multipurpose items:** Everything should work in multiple ways. A bandanna is a filter, a bandage, a face cloth, a paddle tie. A knife is a tool, a weapon, a signal device. Choose items that earn their weight.

**Redundancy with purpose:** Carry backup for what matters most. Two ways to treat water. Two ways to start fire. Two ways to navigate. Redundancy in critical systems; single items for everything else.

**Leave nothing:** Pack out everything you pack in. The people who lived here were careful — you see no trash, no remnants except the structures themselves. Be at least that careful. Carry out your waste, your broken gear, your packaging. This place deserves that respect.

### Departure Checklist

- [ ] Altitude acclimatization complete or prophylactic medication started
- [ ] Clothing system tested: base layers, insulation, shell, puffy jacket, gloves, hat
- [ ] Footwear broken in, gaiters fitted
- [ ] Shelter pitched and tested in wind
- [ ] Water procurement plan: filter tested, backup treatment ready, storage capacity confirmed
- [ ] Food calculated for duration plus 20% reserve
- [ ] Navigation prepared: GPS charged, maps downloaded, compass functional
- [ ] First aid kit assembled with altitude medications and trauma supplies
- [ ] Emergency signaling: whistle, mirror, flag panel
- [ ] Mental preparation: practice with discomfort, acceptance practice, realistic expectations set

## Closing Observations

This is not a destination for the casual visitor. The preparation required is substantial, the conditions are genuinely challenging, and the rewards — if you find them — come from engagement with a landscape that does not care about you at all, that is beautiful in its indifference, that offers glimpses of something older and stranger than human comfort.

You will labor for breath. You will curse the wind. You will feel, perhaps, the weight of the open sky pressing down. You will dry out and get wet again. You will miss easy water, easy terrain, easy conditions.

And then, on a morning when the clouds part for a moment, when the sun strikes the mineral-streaked stone and makes it glow, when the wind dies just long enough for you to hear water somewhere in the gorges below, trickling through channels that have been cut for eons — you will understand why people once lived here, why they built walls and roofs, why they endured what this place demanded. Not despite the difficulty, but because of something the difficulty made possible. Some contact with the old stone, the dying mountains, the vast indifferent sky. Some sense of being a small part of a process that will continue long after you're gone.

Prepare well. Travel safely. Stay humble. This place will teach you what it has to teach, if you're willing to learn.
