# Preparing for the Still Vale of Olorith

## On the Nature of the Journey

You will not find this place by accident. The valleys that approach it are raw and new enough that they resist passage—loose scree on those V-shaped cuts, stone that has never learned to be walked on. Whatever mechanism has brought you here, it has chosen this arrival deliberately, or you have chosen it without knowing. Either way, you are meant to reach the plateau, and reach it you will, and once you are there, you will need to understand what you are walking into.

The first thing to know is this: Olorith is not abandoned. Not exactly. Abandonment implies departure, and departure implies presence before absence. What you will find there is closer to potential—every element in perfect readiness, every system functional, every road clean and every canal flowing, but the readiness is not for you. It was never for anyone. The place holds itself in a state of anticipation so complete that it has become its own completion. Nothing arrives to fulfill it. Nothing needs to. The waiting is the thing itself.

This is important to understand before you go, because it will shape everything you experience there.

## Physical Preparation

### The Humidity

The air will be the first thing that claims you, and it will not let go. You will step from the sharp stone passages that lead onto the plateau and into a wall of warmth and wetness that has no edges—it simply is, the way water is water. Your lungs will respond. Your skin will respond. Your eyes will water and your clothes will cling and within an hour you will feel as though you have always been damp, as though dryness is the dream and this is the waking.

Prepare for this as you would prepare for altitude or extreme cold. The adjustment is physiological and it takes time, and until you adjust, you will be compromised. Move slowly. Breathe deliberately. Do not exert yourself in those first hours, because your body will be working to establish a new equilibrium, and the Vale will not help you find it.

Your clothing should be chosen for sustained moisture. Cotton will become heavy and cold as the hours pass. Synthetics will trap heat against skin that cannot cool itself through evaporation. Wool will hold moisture without the clammy weight of cotton, but it too will grow heavy. The ideal is layers of lightweight, loose-woven natural fibers—silk linings under linen under a breathable outer layer—clothing that can move with the dampness rather than fighting it. You will not stay dry. You will, however, stay functional.

Carry a change of clothes and keep it sealed against the ambient moisture. When you sleep, you will want something dry to wear, and you will not find the air drier at night. It never changes there.

### The Water

Do not drink from the canals.

I know how clear it looks. I know that stillness is not stagnation and that stillness is not污染. But the water that weeps from the eastern rock has come through mineral deposits that have been building for—how long? However long the place has existed, the water has been dissolving and redepositing and carrying whatever the stone contains into those channels. You cannot know what it carries. You cannot know what the clay lining has absorbed over those patient, silent years.

Bring your own water. Purify it again before you drink it. Treat it as though it has come from nowhere you understand, because it has.

There is also the matter of immersion. The canals are not deep—the water barely reaches your ankle at the center of the larger channels—but the clay has been wet for so long that it has become something more than clay. The edges are slick with biofilm. Your boots will grip and your feet will slip and if you fall, you will be lying in water that has never known anything but this valley, this stillness, this patient waiting. Wash yourself afterward. Carry what you need to wash yourself.

### The Grain

The fields are heavy with growth, and the growth is not grain as you know it. Broad-leafed, green, bending under the weight of water it cannot transpire away—that is what the text said, and you will see it and you will understand. It looks like food. It is grown in orderly rows, cultivated with evident care, and it has never been harvested.

Do not eat it.

I cannot tell you why. Perhaps it is toxic. Perhaps it is nutritionally inert, all bulk and no substance. Perhaps the plants that grow in Olorith have adapted to conditions that would not sustain you, have become something other than food despite their appearance. What I know is this: the fields have been growing since the place was made, and they have never been harvested, and no one has ever tried to eat from them, and you will not be the first to test what they offer. Whatever they offer, it is not for you.

Bring enough food for your entire stay. Bring more than you think you need, because you do not know how long you will stay, and you do not know what hunger in that place will feel like. The Vale does not encourage appetite. The air is warm and your body will be working in ways you do not understand, and you may find that you are less hungry than you expect—or you may find that the silence drives you to eat simply for something to do, something to taste, something to chew.

Either way, bring enough.

### The Silence

The birds do not call because there are no birds. The insects do not hum because there are no insects. The wind does not move because the air is too still to carry it, or because the Vale has no wind, or because the design of the place includes only the movements it shows you: water in channels, light through moisture, the slow bending of heavy leaves.

When there is no sound, your ears will begin to generate sound. You will hear your own heartbeat. You will hear the wet sounds of your breathing, the creak of your joints, the shift of your weight on stone. You will hear your clothing against your skin. You will become acutely, overwhelmingly aware of the sounds of your own body, and they will not be enough, and the silence beneath them will press in.

Bring ear protection. Not to block the silence—nothing blocks the silence—but to give yourself a layer of artificial sound, something rhythmic and predictable that your mind can hold onto. White noise, brown noise, whatever you can tolerate. The goal is not to eliminate awareness of the silence but to create a buffer, a space between yourself and the weight of it.

Some travelers recommend leaving a radio playing in your camp, tuned to static or to a station you cannot hear clearly. The point is not the content but the presence of a voice, a signal, something that suggests a world beyond the plateau. You do not need to listen to it. You need only to know that it is there.

## Psychological Preparation

### The Stillness Is Not Peace

You will want to call it peace. The light is soft, the air is warm, nothing moves except the water and the plants and you. It looks like peace. It feels, in the first hours, like a place where the body could rest, where the nerves could unclench, where the constant thrum of the world could finally, finally stop.

Do not mistake it.

The stillness of Olorith is not the stillness of rest. It is the stillness of suspension, of conservation, of a system running with no inputs and no outputs and therefore no change. You are not resting in the Vale. You are being preserved in it. And preservation is not peace. Preservation is what happens when nothing can end and nothing can begin, when every process has reached equilibrium and there is nothing left to do but continue.

You will feel this eventually. Perhaps on the first day, perhaps on the second, perhaps only when you try to leave. The recognition will come: you are standing in a place where nothing happens, and you are standing in it, and standing is the only thing you can do, because there is nothing else.

Prepare for grief. Not grief for anything you have lost—you have not yet lost anything—but grief for the loss of change, of progress, of the ordinary forward motion of a world where things grow and decay and grow again. The Vale is a portrait of a city, and you will be standing inside the frame, and you will understand that portraits do not breathe.

### The Uncanny Quality

Everything is too good. That is the other thing you will notice, after the humidity and the silence. The stonework is too precise. The canals are too clean. The roads are too well-drained, the fields too orderly, the buildings too perfectly constructed. There are no cracks in the walls. There are no weeds in the corners. Nothing has been left to chance, and nothing has been left to time.

This is not comfort. This is horror.

The uncanny is the thing that looks right but feels wrong, and Olorith is uncanny at every scale. The joints are too tight. The surfaces are too even. The grain bends but does not break, grows but does not mature, and you will look at it and know that it is food because you have been told it is food, but you will not be able to make yourself believe it. Your eyes will see the fields and your mind will reject them and there will be a gap between what you see and what you understand, and that gap will be the uncanny.

There is no preparation for this. There is only awareness: when the gap appears, do not try to close it. Do not argue with your own perception. Stand in the gap and let it be a gap and understand that this is what the Vale is, a place that is complete and whole and wrong in ways that cannot be resolved.

### Time in the Vale

The text says the light does not change. You will not believe this until you experience it. Then you will believe it and wish you did not.

There is no sunrise or sunset in Olorith. There is no shift in the quality of the light, no change in the angle of shadows, no difference between what the sky looks like at what should be morning and what should be afternoon. The diffuse illumination simply is, always, the same flat wash of pale warmth that softens all edges equally.

Your body will want a rhythm. Your mind will construct one—you will decide that you slept for roughly eight hours, that you have been walking for roughly four, that it must be late in the day because you are tired—but the light will not confirm this, and the silence will not confirm it, and eventually the disjunction will become uncomfortable. You will look at the sky and see the same light you saw when you arrived and understand that the sky is lying to you, or that time is lying to you, or that your own measurement of time has become unreliable.

Keep a record. Write down when you woke, when you ate, when you rested, when you moved. Not because the record will be true—you cannot know what true is in the Vale—but because the act of recording will give you something to hold onto. You will have a string of marks that says you did this at this time and that at that time, and even if the string is fiction, it is a fiction you made, and you will need that.

## What to Bring

**Water.** More than you think. Purified, sealed, protected against the ambient damp. The Vale will try to take it from you—not maliciously, but through condensation, through spills, through the constant moisture in the air. Seal everything.

**Food.** As much as you can carry for the duration of your stay, plus a margin. The Vale offers nothing edible, or nothing you should eat, and you cannot know how long you will be there.

**Clothing.** As described above: layers of natural fiber, loose-woven, chosen for moisture management. A change of clothes, sealed. A way to dry the clothes you are wearing, or the willingness to wear damp clothes for as long as you are there.

**Ear protection.** White noise generator or earplugs rated for sustained use. Something to create artificial sound.

**A light source.** The light is constant but diffuse, and the interiors of the buildings will be darker than the outside. You will want a flashlight or headlamp to explore safely. The batteries will drain faster in the humidity; bring spares.

**Writing materials.** Paper and pencil, sealed against moisture. Not for records only—for the act of writing, which will be one of the few ways you have to make something happen, to create change, to leave a mark on a place that holds no marks.

** Medicine.** Antifungal treatment, for the skin conditions that sustained humidity will cause. Antibiotics, for the infections that may follow. Pain relievers, because you will hurt somewhere, somehow, and the Vale will not offer relief.

**Something to leave behind.** Not because the Vale needs it—nothing in the Vale needs anything—but because you will want to leave something. A marker, a token, a written note. The Vale is complete, but you are not, and you will need to assert your existence against its stillness. Leave something that says you were there, and know that the Vale will hold it the way it holds everything: perfectly, without change, without decay, without end.

## When You Arrive

Walk slowly. The roads are clean and the paths are clear and you will want to move quickly, to cover ground, to see what is around the next corner. Do not. Move slowly, because your body is adjusting to the humidity and your mind is adjusting to the silence and you do not know yet what you will find, and what you find may find you at the same moment.

Notice the water. Notice how it moves without moving, how it reflects without ripples, how it runs in channels that were made for it and fits so perfectly into the design of the place that you cannot tell where the plan ends and the water begins. This is the heart of the Vale, the system that sustains the stillness, and understanding it will help you understand everything else.

Do not go into the buildings unless you must. They are well-made and they are dry inside and they will offer shelter from the humidity, but they are also complete in a way that suggests they are occupied, that they have been occupied, that someone is simply absent for a moment and will return. You will feel this. The presence of absence is strong in those rooms, the sense of lives interrupted, of a dinner table set for a meal that never happened. If you can sleep in the open air, sleep in the open air. If you cannot, enter the buildings knowing that you are a guest in a place that has no hosts.

Watch the grain. Watch it for as long as you can, standing at the edge of a field with the light falling evenly across the rows and the silence pressing in and the air warm and wet against your skin. Watch it and wait and let yourself feel what the waiting is. The fields have been growing since the place was made, and they will grow after you leave, and they will never be harvested, and this is not tragedy. It is simply what the Vale is: a system in perfect equilibrium, waiting for nothing, because waiting is what it does.

And when you are ready to leave—and you will know when, because the stillness will begin to feel like weight, like pressure, like a hand pressing gently on your chest—leave. The passages down from the plateau will be as raw and difficult as the passages up, and you will be changed by the time you spend in the Vale, and the world you return to will seem loud and fast and full of things that die and things that grow and things that break and are not rebuilt. This is good. This is what the world is. The Vale is the exception, and you were only visiting.

---

## Final Advice

Do not stay too long. The text recommends three to five days, and I will not argue with the text. By the third day, you will know whether you can leave on the fourth or whether you need a fifth, and by the fifth day, you should leave regardless. The stillness will begin to feel normal. The silence will begin to feel like the only sound. The flat light will begin to feel like all the light there is, and you will forget what shadows look like, and you will forget what change looks like, and the forgetting will be comfortable, and the comfort will be a trap.

You are not meant for the Vale. Nothing is meant for the Vale. The Vale is meant for itself, for its own perfect continuing, and if you stay long enough, you will begin to want what it has: the end of effort, the end of decay, the end of everything that makes life difficult and beautiful and strange. And when you want that—when you find yourself standing in a field of grain that will never be harvested and thinking that perhaps you too could simply grow, could simply bend under the weight of water you cannot transpire away, could simply remain—leave.

The world is too short for the Vale, and you are too alive for it, and there are places where things happen, where things change, where the silence is broken by birdsong and the light moves across the sky and the grain is harvested and eaten and the people who built the roads walk on them and the water runs through channels that someone dug because someone needed the water, and that need was a kind of life.

Go to Olorith. See what it is. Come back and be glad that you did.
