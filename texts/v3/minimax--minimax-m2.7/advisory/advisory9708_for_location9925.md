# Traveler’s Field Guide: The Undergrove of Caldtow

## Pre-Arrival Considerations

Caldtow is not a place you visit casually. The journey itself will color your arrival, and you should understand what you're walking into before you step onto the moss.

This is a settlement that has spent four centuries negotiating with itself. The negotiations are not finished. What appears static — a wall, a courtyard, a path — is actually in slow motion, changing in ways that register only over years, not hours. You will not perceive this motion directly, but you will feel its effects: the slight irregularity of ground that was once a floor, the way trees stand in places that make no architectural sense, the water that seems to be coming from nowhere and everywhere. Accept this. Do not look for explanations. The explanations are centuries long and written in root.

You will arrive damp. Plan accordingly.

---

## What to Wear

**The mistake most travelers make is dressing for weather.** Weather implies changes — sun, rain, wind, temperature swings. Caldtow has none of these in any dramatic form. The climate here is a single, sustained note: cool dampness. Not cold. Not wet. *Damp.* The way air feels in a basement. The way forest floors feel before dawn.

Your clothing should be:

**Layers, but thin ones.** You will not need insulation. You may need to shed layers if you sit in a sunny spot (they exist, briefly, in the middle of the day), but you will not need wool or down or any of the heavy materials that protect against cold or wind. A light jacket over a shirt will serve you for nearly all conditions.

**Synthetic or treated fabrics over natural fibers.** Cotton holds water and takes forever to dry. In Caldtow, where everything holds water and nothing dries quickly, this is a serious problem. You do not want to spend your first day in damp jeans. Nylon, polyester, or treated natural blends will shed the ambient moisture better and dry faster when you find a spot by the waterwheel or a hearth.

**Closed-toe shoes with good grip.** The ground here is not paved. It is not even strictly ground — it is moss over soil over brick over root, and it varies by the step. You will slip. You will stumble. You will step into unexpected soft patches where a foundation has long since turned to humus. Shoes with aggressive soles will save you from many falls. Waterproof boots are ideal, but if you choose hiking shoes, ensure they have good drainage and can handle constant moisture without degrading.

**A hood or hat.** Not for rain — rain is light here, more suggestion than precipitation — but for the damp itself. When you are sitting still, the moisture will settle on your hair, your scalp, the back of your neck. A hood keeps you warmer and more comfortable during the long, still hours when you are simply watching the water move.

**Gloves.** Optional but recommended. If you intend to touch things — the moss, the brick, the roots — your hands will be damp afterward. Gloves (thin, quick-drying) keep your hands comfortable and protect against the cold that seeps through wet skin.

**Do not bring umbrellas.** They are unnecessary for the rain and will catch on the vines. You will become tangled. You will look like a tourist.

## What to Bring

The people of Caldtow have what they need. You do not need to bring civilization with you.

**Water containers.** The water from the spring box is safe and tastes of iron and green things — pleasant, if unusual. Bring vessels to carry it. Do not assume you can drink from streams or the marsh without preparation; the water throughout the settlement carries run-off from inhabited areas, and while the residents have adapted to it over generations, your gut has not.

**Food, if you have preferences.** The residents produce bread, cheese, smoked fish from the marsh, and seasonal vegetables. If you have dietary restrictions or strong preferences, bring what you need. Otherwise, trust what is offered. The bread — made from grain grown in soil that was once a factory floor — is dense, dark, and sustaining. The cheese is soft, white, mild. Eat what is given. This is part of the negotiation.

**A small torch or headlamp.** The settlement is not dark at night — there are small electric lights threaded through the vine-covered streets, powered by the waterwheel — but the interiors are dim, and the paths between them are often unlit. A light source will help you navigate after sunset without disturbing the careful calm.

**Earplugs.** Optional, but useful for sleep. The silence of Caldtow is profound, and it makes small sounds enormous. The drip of condensation on your window. The creak of a nearby tree. The waterwheel's slow turning. If you are a light sleeper, these sounds will wake you repeatedly until you acclimate. Earplugs help.

**Paper and something to write with.** You will want to record what you see. The weirdness of Caldtow is mild — you will not experience anything overtly strange, nothing that violates your expectations in dramatic ways — but it is cumulative. The small wrongnesses accumulate: the way the roots have taken the shape of the bricks they replaced, the way the light falls through the canopy, the way a tree stands where no tree should stand. Writing these down will help you integrate the experience.

**A camera or sketchbook, if you are so inclined.** The place is beautiful in a quiet way. The moss glows. The brick turns green at the base and stays gray at the top, a gradient of time. The fig in the church is extraordinary — roots like veins, branches in the light. Document it if you wish. But do not let documentation become your primary experience. Be here first. Record second.

## Health and Safety

**The damp will get into you.** Not literally — you will not become moss, you will not take root — but your body will respond to the constant moisture in ways that may feel like illness if you are not prepared. Your joints may ache. Your skin may develop rashes in places it is usually dry. Your sinuses may become congested. These are not infections; they are your body's adjustment to a new environment. They will pass in two to three days. Drink water. Stay warm but not hot. Let your body acclimate.

**Do not drink from the marsh or the small streams.** The marsh water especially is rich with organic matter and the slow leavings of the settlement. It will make you sick. The spring box water, passed through its stone housing and fed by deep sources, is safe. Trust the spring box.

**Watch where you step.** The ground is deceptive. What appears solid may be a thick layer of moss over a void — the remains of a basement, a root system that has hollowed out the soil beneath. These are not common, but they exist. If the ground feels soft in a way that doesn't match your last step, stop. Test it before committing your weight. Falls here are rarely dangerous, but a twisted ankle in a settlement this small and quiet will leave you dependent on the hospitality of people who have no obligation to you.

**The trees are load-bearing.** This is not metaphor. Trees in Caldtow have become structural elements — they hold up walls, brace roofs, serve as columns in spaces that were once interior. Do not climb them. Do not hang from branches. Do not lean against them with your full weight unless you are certain of their stability. The old elms are sound; the younger oaks may not be. If you are uncertain, ask a resident. They will tell you.

**The fungi are not food.** Shelf fungi grow on every damp surface — orange, white, black, beautiful in the way of coral or deep water life. Do not eat them. Do not handle them extensively; some species cause skin irritation. Look. Photograph. Do not taste.

**The insects are not dangerous.** Gnats in the shafts of light. Beetles in the rotting wood. The constant hum of things too small to see. They bite rarely, and when they do, the bites are minor. The real pest is the damp itself, which attracts no-see-ums in the late summer months (roughly July through September, by local reckoning; the residents use a different calendar). If you visit in late summer, bring light, loose-fitting clothing that covers your arms and legs, and accept that you will be bitten regardless.

## Orientation and Navigation

Caldtow is small. The entire inhabited area fits within a valley floor perhaps two hundred meters across, bounded by hills that are green but not overgrown. You will not get lost. You may lose track of where you are in relation to the whole, but you will always be able to find your way back by moving downhill toward the sound of water.

**The water is your compass.** It moves through the settlement in channels beneath the moss, surfacing in places as pools or seeps or small waterfalls, always moving, always audible if you listen. The deepest water — and the largest concentration of it — lies in the lowest part of the quarter, where the valley floor dips into a permanent marsh. If you follow water downhill, you will reach the marsh. If you move away from the sound, you are moving upslope, toward the edges of the settlement where the buildings thin and the grazed hills begin.

**The waterwheel is the center.** Not literally — the settlement has no center, no focal point, no plaza — but the waterwheel marks the old heart of the place. It turns in the old millrace, powering the generator that charges the batteries for electrified households. If you find the waterwheel, you will find the old mill, and from the old mill you can orient to the rest: the church to the north, the residential quarters to the east, the marsh to the south, the edge of the settlement to the west where the road becomes moss and then hills.

**The paths are clear but not obvious.** The residents have laid flat stones through the living ground, cleared of moss, indicating where to walk. These paths are the product of centuries of negotiation — they follow routes that disturb the growth least while serving the needs of the people. Do not deviate from them. The ground off-path is soft, uneven, and easy to damage. If you must leave the path — for a photograph, for a closer look at something — step carefully, test your footing, and return to the stones as soon as you can.

**The fog will disorient you.** In the early morning, especially after rain, the fog thickens until you can see only a few meters ahead. This is not dangerous — the settlement is small enough that you will find your way eventually — but it will make navigation difficult. If you must move in the fog, follow the sound of water, follow the flat stones, and accept that you may arrive somewhere unexpected. The fog passes by mid-morning, usually.

## Cultural Etiquette

The residents of Caldtow are not unfriendly, but they are not welcoming either. They are simply *present* — a background condition, like the damp or the sound of water. You should treat them accordingly.

**Do not ask questions about the growth.** You will be curious. You will want to know how long the fig tree has been in the church, or how the residents convinced the ivy to grow in a particular direction, or whether the trees are the same species they were four centuries ago. These are reasonable questions. The residents do not answer them. Not because they are hiding something, but because the answers are too long, too intertwined with daily life, to be extracted in conversation. The growth is simply what happened. It is still happening. There is no story that explains it, only a process that continues.

**Accept hospitality when it is offered.** If a resident offers you bread, water, a place to sit, accept it graciously. If they do not offer, do not ask. The people here have made their accommodation with the place over lifetimes; they do not owe you an explanation of it, and they do not owe you comfort. Your presence is an intrusion, however welcome it may seem. Act accordingly.

**Move quietly.** The residents move quietly — their footsteps muffled by the living ground, their voices low, their movements slow and deliberate. This is not affectation; it is the appropriate way to move in a place where sound carries through damp air and the silence is part of the fabric. Walk softly. Speak softly. Do not laugh loudly, even if you are happy. The child you heard laughing was happy; the sound traveled clearly and was startling, an intrusion into the felt quiet. Allow the quiet its weight.

**Do not touch without permission.** The buildings, the trees, the water — none of them belong to you. If you want to touch something, ask. If no one is present to ask, don't. The fig in the church is extraordinary, and you will want to touch its roots, its bark, its leaves. Do not. It has been growing there for centuries. It will continue without your fingerprints.

**Do not take anything.** This includes water (drink it, but don't fill containers to take away), moss (leave it where it is), wood (even if it seems fallen and abundant), or anything else that might appeal to you as a souvenir. Caldtow operates on a principle of exchange: you give what you can, you take what you need, and the balance is maintained over time, not transaction by transaction. If you want something from here, make arrangements with a resident. Otherwise, leave it.

## Duration and Rhythm

**Stay at least three days.** You will not understand the place in less time. The first day is disorientation — the damp, the silence, the way trees stand where buildings were. The second day is adjustment — your body acclimates, your eyes learn to see the small wrongnesses, your ears tune to the water's constant murmur. The third day is integration — you begin to feel the place as the residents feel it, a place that is neither built nor grown but both, a place that has found its shape and is still finding it.

**Do not stay more than a week.** The damp will wear on you. The silence will become oppressive. The small weirdnesses will stop registering as strange and start registering as normal, and when you return to wherever you came from, you will not be able to explain what you experienced. A week is long enough to understand; longer is unnecessary.

**Follow the local rhythm.** The residents rise early, when the fog is thick and the light is gray. They work through the morning — tending gardens, repairing structures, managing the slow negotiation between growth and habitation. They rest in the early afternoon, when the light is warmest and the damp is most gentle. They resume activity in the late afternoon, when the shadows lengthen and the marsh turns copper in the evening light. They sleep early, after a meal of bread and cheese and smoked fish, in rooms whose walls are half-brick and half-ivy. Follow this rhythm. It will help you fit in, and fitting in will help you see.

**Leave in the fog, if you can.** The fog is the edge of the place — it obscures the boundary between settlement and hills, between the inhabited and the wild. If you leave while the fog is thick, you will step from one world to another without transition, without the moment of looking back. You will not see what you are leaving. You will simply be gone from it. This is appropriate. Caldtow does not want to be remembered as a destination; it wants to be remembered as a place you passed through, a place that was there and is still there and will be there after you are gone.

## What You Will See

**The moss.** It is everywhere. It covers the brick, the stone, the wood, the ground. It glows green when the light catches it, especially after rain. It holds water in its hollows, wearing a shine that looks like the surface of something living. Step on it. Feel how soft it is, how it yields beneath your weight. This is what four centuries of growth feels like.

**The trees.** Elm, ash, oak, and something that may be ash but has changed. They grow through buildings — not beside them, but *through* them, their trunks the load-bearing columns, their branches spreading through gaps where roofs used to be. Walk among them. Look up through the canopy at the sky that seems wider here, in this small valley, than it should. Listen to them creak in a wind you cannot feel.

**The water.** Beneath the moss, through channels cut by centuries of drainage. Surfacing in places as pools or seeps or small waterfalls over mossy stone. The pulse of the place. Drip, trickle, the soft burble of a rill finding its way through a crack. You will hear it everywhere, if you listen. You will stop noticing it, and then you will notice it again, and each time it will surprise you, like a heartbeat you forgot was there.

**The fig in the church.** Roots spread across the floor like veins. Roots meet the walls and thicken. Roots meet the ceiling and turn to branches. Branches have leaves. Leaves are in the light. This is the most unusual thing in Caldtow, though it is not alone in its strangeness.

**The spiral tree in the millrace.** Its trunk twisted by centuries of seeking light through a gap in the buildings. A shape that should be random, that looks like growth, but which has a logic to it — a patience, a persistence, a refusal to stop reaching.

**The willow archway.** It has grown across a courtyard and taken root on the other side, creating a living gate, a passage through green. Pass through it. Feel the leaves brush your hair, your shoulders. This is what the place offers: passage through growth, not around it.

**The fungi.** Shelves of them, orange and white and black, growing on every surface that stays damp. They turn the town into something between a forest and a reef. Look at them. Do not touch them. They are beautiful in the way of deep water life.

**The people.** Sixty of them, living in the parts that have been made stable. They move quietly. Their footsteps are muffled by the living ground. You hear a child somewhere, laughing. The sound travels clearly in the damp air, bright and odd against the moss.

## What You Will Feel

**Cool.** Not cold. Cool, the way a cellar is cool, or a forest floor at morning.

**Damp.** Moisture hangs in the air, a gentle damp that doesn't quite become rain. The valley catches clouds, holds them, lets them press against the stones and brick until the surfaces weep.

**Quiet.** A deep, felt quiet, like cotton in the ears. The silence is the first thing you notice. It is punctuated by water.

**Small.** The settlement is small — two hundred meters across. The hills are grazed, not forested. You are as large as anything gets here, except the trees.

**Slow.** Nothing moves quickly here. Not the water, not the growth, not the people. Time passes in Caldtow the way water moves — constantly, but in small sounds: drip, trickle, the soft burble of a rill finding its way through a crack.

**At home, eventually.** You will stop noticing the damp. You will stop noticing the silence. You will start noticing the heartbeat of the place — the water beneath the moss, the slow pulse of growth and decay and growth again. You will feel, for a moment, like you could stay. You will not stay. But you will feel it.

---

## What to Remember When You Leave

Caldtow is a place that has found its shape. The erosion is not of stone but of boundaries — between built and grown, between old and new, between the industrial and the organic. The wear of centuries has smoothed everything into one thing.

You came from a place where these boundaries are maintained. Where buildings are buildings and trees are trees and water is channeled and controlled and put to use. You came from a place where growth is either cultivated or eradicated, where the wild is kept out or kept at bay. Caldtow is not that place. Caldtow is a place where the boundaries have worn away, where the wild has come in and stayed, where the people have not won or lost but *negotiated*, a conversation centuries long and still ongoing.

Remember this when you return to your world. Remember that there are other ways to live. Remember that stone can become soil, that buildings can become meadows, that trees can become architecture. Remember the cool, the damp, the silence punctuated by water. Remember the fig in the church, the spiral tree, the willow archway.

Remember that you do not look back because you already know what it looks like.

It is still there. It will always be there.

You just passed through.
