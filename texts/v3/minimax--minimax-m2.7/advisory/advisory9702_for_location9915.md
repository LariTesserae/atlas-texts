# A Traveler's Preparation Guide: The Wide Warm Plateaus of Aevum

---

## On the Nature of Your Destination

You've read the description. You know the basics. But let me tell you something that won't be in any briefing—the first thing you need to prepare for is the silence that isn't silence.

You think you understand what "constant machinery drone" means. You don't. Not until you're standing in a tunnel carved so deep that the sky is a memory, listening to something hum beneath your feet at a frequency your body registers before your mind does, and realizing that this sound has been going on for longer than your species has had language. That's not background noise. That's the heartbeat of the place.

So. Let's talk about how to survive it.

---

## I. Atmospheric and Environmental Preparation

### The Air You Breathe

The plateau sits at an altitude where thin air should be your first concern. You will feel it—shortness of breath on exertion, a faint lightheadedness that never quite resolves. This is compounded by the humidity. You are not adapted to breathe comfortably in an environment this wet, at this elevation, with mineral particulate suspended in every breath.

**Sealed Atmospheric Suit (Class 4 or higher):** Non-negotiable. Not a recommendation—a hard requirement. The mineral dust alone will damage your lungs over extended exposure. Your suit needs full environmental sealing, positive pressure to prevent infiltration at any seam, and filtration rated for mineral particulates down to 0.3 microns. The warm humidity will test your suit's climate control systems; budget power for that. The plateau's ambient temperature (around 25-30°C in the inhabited zones) combined with high humidity means your suit will fight condensation constantly. Choose a model with active dehumidification and assume your seals will see stress you don't anticipate.

**Backup Breathing Supply:** Carry a minimum of 72 hours of independent atmospheric processing capacity. Your suit's primary system will handle the baseline, but redundancy isn't paranoia here—it's basic competence. The conduit systems run everywhere, and some passages will have pockets of altered air composition that your suit's sensors should catch but might not.

### The Water You Encounter

Water is everywhere on the plateau, and it is not your water. It is mineral-heavy, organism-saturated, and in many channels, part of the processing stream for substances you cannot name. It seeps upward through stone that has been saturated for geological ages. Do not let it touch your skin outside of sealed suit parameters. Do not taste it. Do not assume that standing in a pool of it, even for a moment, is safe.

**Waterproof Overlayers:** For any work outside your suit in the surface pools or mist zones, you need full waterproof encapsulation. The iridescent sheen on standing water? That's alive. Not dangerously, perhaps, but alive in ways your immune system has no context for. The gray mat that pulses on surfaces? Same. Your skin is not equipped to negotiate first contact with an ecosystem this divergent from anything in your baseline evolutionary history.

**Contamination Protocols:** You will track things back to your base camp. Assume this. Build decontamination into your routine as reflexively as breathing. Ultrasonic cleaning for equipment, atmospheric isolation for brought materials, a protocol for anything organic you cannot sterilize without destroying. The plateau's organisms are not hostile, but they are not neutral—they are simply themselves, and "themselves" includes metabolic processes optimized for mineral substrates that may find your equipment or waste products fascinating.

### Temperature and Humidity

The plateau runs warm. This warmth is constant, sourceless, and pervasive. In the deep tunnels, it accumulates. The humidity makes it feel warmer than it is. Your body will struggle to cool itself, and you will sweat into your suit unless you manage the climate control carefully.

**Climate Control Discipline:** Learn to manage your suit's thermal settings as a continuous practice, not a set-it-and-forget-it situation. The ambient temperature will vary by location and depth. Surface structures in the thin air can be surprisingly cool; deep inhabited chambers can feel close and thick with heat. Acclimate by spending time in moderate zones before attempting extended operations in extremes.

**Hydration Discipline:** You will lose water faster than you realize. The humid air fools your thirst mechanism. Drink on schedule, not on sensation. Dehydration on the plateau is not dramatic—it creeps. Headaches, confusion, impaired judgment. In an environment where the landscape is actively indifferent to your survival, impaired judgment is a luxury you cannot afford.

## II. Biological and Health Preparation

### Immunological Considerations

You are visiting a living ecosystem so divergent from anything terrestrial that "alien" almost doesn't do it justice. The gray mat, the iridescent organisms in the pools, the things that cling to pipe walls and swim through the channels—they represent biological lineages that have been optimizing for this specific environment longer than complex life has existed on Earth. Your immune system has no recognition patterns for them.

**Pre-Exposure Immunomodulation:** Consult with a xenobiological medical specialist before departure. There are prophylactic protocols—immune system priming strategies that won't give you resistance but will reduce the probability of a catastrophic inflammatory response if cross-contamination occurs. These are not treatments. They are preparations. They may have side effects. The alternative is being unprepared.

**Extended Medical Monitoring:** You will need baseline biometric readings taken before exposure and continuous monitoring during your stay. Any anomaly—rash, fever, fatigue disproportionate to exertion, neurological oddness—needs immediate attention. In an environment this unfamiliar, symptoms that would be minor elsewhere could indicate something that needs intervention within hours, not days. Budget for medical technology. Budget for evac capability if your stay is longer than two weeks.

### Physical Preparation

The terrain is gentle by the standards of eroded stone, but it is still stone. Smooth, yes, but often slick with moisture or the residue of the gray mat. The elevation means exertion costs more. The tunnels mean navigating spaces that were not designed for human bodies.

**Physical Conditioning:** Spend the month before your visit building cardiovascular endurance and lower-body strength. Hiking fitness. Flexibility. Not because the terrain is strenuous but because the altitude, humidity, and the mental load of operating in an alien environment will exhaust you faster than you expect. You want physical reserves you can draw on.

**Caving and Vertical Training:** The plateau is a vertical space. You will be navigating tunnels, climbing through carved chambers, moving through conduits designed for creatures smaller and more flexible than you. Confined-space training, vertical movement practice, comfort with darkness and tight quarters—this is not optional. The places you most need to see will require moving through spaces where panic is a real possibility if you're not prepared.

### Psychological Preparation

Here is the thing that no one tells you about Aevum until you've been there: it is not hostile, and it is not welcoming, and it is not indifferent. It simply is. The cities run themselves. The creatures swarm in their patterns. The light cycles through its programming. The warmth persists. The water weeps. And no one is tending any of it. There is no caretaker intelligence. There is no guide. There is no purpose that your presence serves or that the plateau acknowledges.

**Existential Comfort:** You need to be able to exist in a space where there is no one home without that becoming a source of existential distress. The plateau does not care that you are there. This is not cruelty—it's simply the condition of the place. If you need to feel noticed, validated, relevant to your environment, Aevum will corrode your psychological stability. You need to be able to be present without being necessary. This is a skill. Practice it.

**Monotony Tolerance:** The drone of machinery, the slow cycles of haze and clarity, the visual monotony of stone that looks poured rather than carved—this is not stimulating. It is not dramatic. It is patient and vast and it does not care about narrative satisfaction. You will find yourself inventing stories for things that have no stories. You will project meaning onto patterns that are simply wear. This is normal. But if you need constant novelty to remain sane, budget your time here carefully. Three days in an environment this still can feel longer than three weeks elsewhere.

**Disorientation Management:** The tunnels are navigable, but they are not intuitive. Without a map—or with a map that doesn't match the current configuration of debris and seasonal flooding—you will get lost. The bioluminescent organisms provide light, but not necessarily useful light. Things that look like landmarks may have been static for centuries; things that change may change too slowly to notice. Build strong orientation habits. Check your position regularly. Trust instruments more than memory.

## IV. Behavioral Guidelines

### On First Arrival

Descend slowly. The plateau's surface is strange enough, but the transition into the carved spaces is disorienting in ways you cannot prepare for intellectually. You have read the description. You know the basic facts. But knowing that the cities were excavated rather than built is different from standing at the mouth of a tunnel that opens into a hall large enough to swallow a city block, lit by pulsing blue-green organisms in channels carved along the ceiling, and understanding that this was someone's apartment. This was where they lived.

Take the first day at surface level. Walk the plateau. Feel the stone under your feet—smooth, warm, patient. Watch the haze cycle through its patterns. Observe the fauna on the exterior structures: the trails they leave, the clusters they form, the ways they are adapted to surfaces that have been worn past the memory of their original purpose. Do not descend until the surface feels familiar enough that you can navigate it without thinking.

### On Interaction with the Fauna

The creatures of Aevum are not dangerous. This is stated with confidence because it has been tested extensively, but understand what "not dangerous" means in context. They are not predators. They are not aggressive. They do not target humans as food or competitors. They are simply present, filling niches, following patterns, living in an environment that has been theirs longer than the cities have been empty.

That said: do not touch them with bare skin. Do not interfere with their nesting sites. Do not attempt to feed them or attract them or study them without proper containment protocols. They are part of the system. Removing them disrupts the system. The system is not something you can afford to disrupt without understanding the consequences.

If they land on you—and they will, because you are warm and they are curious in the way that all life is curious—remain still until they move on. Do not flinch. Do not swat. Your reaction is more dangerous to you than their presence is.

### On the Machinery

The engines hum. The pumps push. The fans cool. The factories process.

Do not attempt to operate, repair, or interact with any of the plateau's infrastructure without thorough understanding of what it does and how it fits into the larger system. The machines are not hostile, but they are not forgiving. They were designed by an intelligence whose design philosophy you do not share. Their safety interlocks, if they exist, may not be legible to you. Their warning systems, if they still function, may be in a language you cannot decode.

Observe. Record. Document. But keep your hands in your pockets.

### On Extended Presence

If you are staying longer than a week, you will notice things changing. Not dramatically—the plateau does not do drama—but the cycles will become familiar. You will learn when the haze clears and when it builds. You will recognize the sounds that signal rain on the lower levels versus the dry periods. You will know which tunnels flood seasonally and which are permanent.

This is the trap of Aevum: it becomes normal. The strangeness recedes. The drone of machinery becomes silence. The wet stone smell becomes simply the smell of being outdoors. The creatures become part of the background texture, noticed only when they are absent.

Do not let this fool you. The plateau is still strange. You have simply become adapted to it. Budget for psychological reset periods—a day every two weeks where you return to your base camp, reconnect with baseline human contexts, remind yourself what "normal" feels like outside the plateau's parameters.

### On Departure

You will track things out. Assume this. Decontaminate everything before you leave, and then decontaminate again. The organisms of Aevum are not necessarily incompatible with terrestrial ecosystems. That's not a certainty; it's a possibility that requires active management.

You will also carry the place with you, in ways you don't expect. The drone of machinery you stopped noticing will resurface when you encounter similar frequencies elsewhere. The wet stone smell will trigger memories you didn't know you were forming. The patience of the plateau—the slow cycles, the worn surfaces, the absence of urgency—will become part of your baseline.

This is the cost of visiting. The plateau doesn't let go completely.

## V. Final Notes

Aevum is not a destination for thrill-seekers. There is no treasure here, no dramatic confrontation, no resolution waiting at the end of a tunnel. The cities were built, and the builders are gone, and the cities continue. The fauna swarms and dies and is reborn. The warmth persists. The water weeps through the stone.

If you go, go because you want to stand in a space that has been alive longer than human memory and feel what that means. Go because you want to understand what a city becomes when it outlives its purpose. Go because you want to see life that doesn't need sunlight, infrastructure that runs without tending, a landscape that was engineered but no longer remembers who it was engineered for.

Bring preparation. Bring respect. Bring patience.

And when you get back—when the drone has faded and the wet-stone smell is only a memory—tell someone what it was like. The plateau doesn't mourn its emptiness, but you're human. The least you can do is bear witness before you return to your own world, your own purposes, your own brief urgencies.

The stone weeps, and waits, and does not remember what it was before.

But you will. That's the gift and the burden of being someone who came, and went, and came back.
