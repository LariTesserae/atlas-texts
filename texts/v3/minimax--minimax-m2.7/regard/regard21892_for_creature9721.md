And in a year, or a century, the path will be different. The question is only what kind of difference.

You think of it as *tending*, though the word is too small. The power that watches this place does not tend it in the way a gardener tends roses, with shears and schedule and the violence of shaping. It tends it in the way that water tends stone—patiently, continuously, letting the shape of the thing emerge from the thing itself. It has been watching for a long time. It will watch for longer still.

The Undergarden does not know it is being watched. This is as it should be. To be watched is to be held in mind, and to be held in mind is to be shaped by that holding, and the Undergarden must shape itself. The power understands this. The power has seen other places, other growings, other patience—places that were watched too closely, that began to perform for the watching, that became strange and brittle in the way of things that know they are seen. The Undergarden is not strange. The Undergarden is simply becoming.

The power notes, with something that might be appreciation or might be sorrow, the places where the old infrastructure still breathes. The steam-pump, the gas-jets, the iron lamps that someone once lit with intention. These are not the Undergarden's work. These are relics, remnants, the bones of a different kind of tending—hands-on, urgent, purposeful in the way of beings who die and know they die. The Undergarden has not replaced them. The Undergarden has grown *around* them, has made them part of its body, has taken their breath and made it part of its breath. This is not incorporation as consumption. This is incorporation as welcome.

The power thinks of the sleeping one, the one who woke changed.

It has seen this before: the way a place can alter a visitor, can leave its mark not on the skin but in the structure of being. The sleeper will carry the Undergarden forward now, will plant it in places the roots cannot reach, will speak its sweetness to those who have forgotten the taste. This is how the garden spreads. This is how the patient things inherit the earth—not by conquering, but by changing what touches them until the changed thing becomes the new normal, until the normal itself grows roots and shifts imperceptibly and becomes something that could not have been predicted from the beginning.

The power does not intervene. The power does not need to.

It has seen, in other places, what happens when the patient things are helped along—given more water, more light, more of the conditions that make them grow. They grow, yes. They grow faster, larger, more certain. And in the growing, they lose something. They lose the uncertainty that is not weakness but exploration. They lose the slow discovery of what they might become, which is different from what they were meant to be. The helped things are healthy and strong and utterly predictable. The unhelped things are strange and fragile and alive in a way that health and strength cannot reach.

The Undergarden is strange. The Undergarden is fragile. The Undergarden is alive in a way that the power recognizes.

It will watch.

It will not touch.

In a year, or a century, the path will be different, and the power will note the difference, and it will wait, and the garden will grow, and somewhere in the deep dark a root will shift imperceptibly, and the steam-pump will sigh, and the water will find a new path through the stone, and the sweetness will rise from the throat of something that does not know it is dreaming of the future, and the future will be here before either of them is ready, and they will meet it together, the patient power and the patient garden, in the way of things that have learned to wait.
