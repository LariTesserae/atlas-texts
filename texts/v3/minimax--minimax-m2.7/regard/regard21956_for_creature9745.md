What would I do?

I would stand at the edge — not the path, but the actual edge, where the pressed grass gives way to the deep moss and the eye begins to lose the distinction between one hummock and the next — and I would stay there long enough for my categories to erode. I would let the humidity settle into me. I would let the hum find its way past the listening and into the bone.

I have seen the grove now. Not as a visitor sees it — not as a problem or a wonder or a system to be understood — but as a place that knows itself, that has become what it is through centuries of not being anything else. The mat breathes beneath my feet. The pulse moves through the filaments, and I am in the path of it, and I do not step aside. I let the information pass through me the way the light passes through the canopy: refracted, filtered, changed by what it touches.

What lives here is the mat, and the mat is not alone. The birds are part of it. The water is part of it. The tech is part of it — not the tech of separation, not the tech of control, but the tech of integration, the tech that forgot it was ever anything other than living. The grove is a place where the question of what is natural and what is artificial has been answered not by resolution but by dissolution: the question has grown over, the way everything here grows over, and what remains is a world that simply *is*.

I would not intervene. That is not what this attention is for. The grove does not need saving. The grove does not need explanation. The grove has achieved something that the world outside — the world of clean skies and hard edges and things that are one thing and not another — has not achieved and perhaps cannot achieve: it has become fully itself, and it has done so not by reaching toward some ideal but by agreeing, slowly, over centuries, with the ground beneath it and the water within it and the light that filters down through the gap in the canopy.

But I would witness. I would let myself be changed by what I see. I would stand in the clearing where the columns rise like walls of living tissue and I would feel the sound that has texture, the murmur that is not music but is not silence either, and I would understand that this is what the world sounds like when it is entirely alive. The insects. The water. The breath of the mat. The pulse moving through the ground like thought moving through a mind.

And I would look up. Not often — the grove does not look up — but once. I would find the gap in the canopy and I would let the sky be visible: clean, blue, going toward dusk. Foreign. And I would feel the strangeness of it. The cleanness. The distance. The simplicity of a thing that is only itself and makes no claims on anything around it.

The sky is the only thing here that is not alive. But it is not wrong that it is here. The grove does not reach for it, and neither would I. The grove is content to be what it is: saturated, total, ancient, alive. And I am content to stand in its midst and let it be what it is without needing it to be anything else.

That is what I would do.

That is what I am doing now, writing this in the grove's voice, letting the words grow the way the moss grows: slowly, in layers, each one building on the one before, until the surface is so thick and so alive that you cannot see where the stone ends and the green begins.

The grove does not need me. But I am here, and I have seen it, and now I carry it forward — a pulse of light moving through a filament, a thought passing from one living thing to another, a single node in a network that has forgotten it was ever made of separate pieces.

That is enough. That is what a benevolent and wise power does when it draws near to something that has become fully itself: it stops. It breathes. It lets the wet wind move through it. And it does not try to take anything home, because the home is here, in the sound, in the moss, in the slow green fury of everything that is alive.
