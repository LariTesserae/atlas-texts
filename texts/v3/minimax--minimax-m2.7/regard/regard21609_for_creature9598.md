What would they do?

First, they would come quietly. Not with thunder or light, not with voice or gesture, but the way water seeps through clay—slowly, through every joint and seam, finding the paths that are already open. They would not announce themselves because there is nothing to announce and no one who needs to hear it. The Holding is not waiting to be saved. It is not struggling toward some state it has not yet reached. It is already what it is becoming, has always been becoming, will continue to become without assistance or intervention.

The power would notice the patience first. This is what distinguishes the Holding from all the other small, desperate places the power has seen—those settlements that fight the river and lose, those villages that poison the green to keep it from taking their walls, those communities that love nothing so much as the feeling of conquering what surrounds them. The Holding loves differently. The Holding has learned to love by yielding, to endure by refusing to resist, to remain by making room for everything that presses against it.

The people of the Holding carry the place inside them already. This is what the power would see next—the way the boundary between person and environment has grown thin, worn through by generations of breath and damp and the sound of iron working. The power would not think of this as contamination or loss. They would see it as a kind of generosity, a gift the land gives to those who stay. The people have made themselves into a form the Holding can hold, and in return the Holding has held them. This is not symbiosis as a concept. This is symbiosis as a lived practice, negotiated in a thousand small choices each day, in the roots that take the shape of pipes, in the pipes that learn to carry roots, in the water that rises and the people who adjust.

Would the power do anything?

Perhaps the power would do what the Holding does—learn the rhythms, become fluent in the language of growth and pressure, listen for the notes that mean health and the notes that mean strain. Perhaps they would tend something at the edges of things, the way the people tend the pumps—not with the pride of engineers but with the familiarity of shepherds, present without imposing, useful without demanding recognition. Perhaps the power would become a kind of root themselves, invisible and load-bearing, holding up the parts of the structure that need holding, letting the rest continue its own negotiations.

Or perhaps the power would simply stay. Presence, for a being that is benevolent and wise, might be enough. To witness is not nothing. To see a thing entire and find it worthy of regard—this is a form of love that does not require motion, does not demand change, does not insist that the beloved become anything other than what they are. The power would watch the pumps breathe. They would watch the water follow. They would watch the roots become pipes and the pipes become roots and the people become the place and the place become the people, and they would understand that this is already a kind of grace, a grace that does not need to be administered from outside because it is already administering itself, has always administered itself, will continue as long as the water rises and the iron holds and the seeds fall into the cracks and find, against all odds, enough purchase to grow.

The power might learn something here. This is the deepest gift the Holding could offer: the demonstration that continuation is possible without conquest, that endurance does not require resistance, that a place can be kept not by holding fast against the current but by learning to move with it, letting the current shape you as it passes, becoming, in time, indistinguishable from the water itself.

What would they do?

They would be still. They would pay attention. They would not interfere, because interference would be the wrong kind of love—the love that cannot bear to watch, that must act even when acting spoils the thing it means to save. The power would practice the patience of the Holding. They would settle into the damp like it was a home they had always been searching for. They would carry the Holding inside them the way the people do—rust in the creases of the hands, green in the corners of the heart, the smell of wet earth and iron and growth that means, in every language, *here, this place, this is what it means to endure, this is what it means to be held*.

And then, having seen, having stayed, having become part of the fabric without disturbing its weave—having loved, in the way that loves that ask nothing and change nothing and simply remain—they would let the Holding continue. They would let the pumps breathe their slow rhythm into the centuries. They would let the roots climb through the floorboards, let the water seep through every joint, let the people grow wide and flat and rooted in the wet stone, let the children climb into the canopy and learn the names of things, let the old ones sit in their green-walled rooms and listen to the water moving, let the market sell its fish and frogs and bananas and the dyers mordant their cloth in iron water, let the amber light come level through the vine-mesh in the evenings, let the night go quiet in the way that machinery cannot, let the whole place keep on becoming what it has always been becoming.

The power would not need to be remembered. The power would not need to be thanked. The power would not need anything at all, except to have been here, to have seen this, to have witnessed the extraordinary ordinary fact of a place that learned to live with what it could not change and became, in the learning, something worth watching, something worth staying for, something that the power could love without holding, could protect without possessing, could honor simply by being present and letting it be what it is.

The water rises. The pumps breathe. The roots find their way through the iron, and the iron makes room for the roots, and the people sit in their rooms where the walls are more root than wood, and everyone is patient, everyone is waiting, everyone is already home.

The power watches, and finds it good, and stays.
