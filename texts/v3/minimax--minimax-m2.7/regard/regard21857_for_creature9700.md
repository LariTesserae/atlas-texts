What would they do?

They would stand at the edge, where you stood, and feel the same sliding of the eyes. They would understand immediately that this is not a place to be taken in, not a spectacle arranged for comprehension. They would feel the weight of the wet air, the slow green light, the quiet that is not absence but fullness. And they would recognize something.

They would recognize patience.

Not the patience of waiting — not endurance, not forbearance, not the holding back of something that wants to act. The patience of the terraces is different. It is the patience of *becoming*, the patience of a process that has no deadline because it has no endpoint, the patience of hands that cut stone knowing they will not see the moss come, knowing the moss will come anyway, knowing the coming is the point.

A benevolent and wise power would recognize this patience because it is the same patience they carry. The patience of making something that will outlast the maker. The patience of building channels for water that will become the water's own body. The patience of faith that does not require witnessing, that does not need the finished thing to justify the making, that loves the process more than the product because the process *is* the love, the process *is* the thing itself.

They would not intervene. What would intervention mean, here? The terraces are not broken. They are not incomplete. The moss growing into the stone's lips is not corruption but completion — the completion the builders intended, the completion that requires centuries, the completion that proves the builders understood what they were making. They were not making stone. They were making the conditions for becoming.

A lesser power might want to understand. Might descend the tiers cataloging species, measuring growth rates, tracing the logic of how made and grown have collapsed into one another. Might write treatises on the philosophy of architecture, on the ethics of construction, on the theology of process. But a benevolent and wise power would understand that understanding is not the point. The terraces do not need to be understood. They need to be walked through, tier by tier, descending into the warm and the wet and the vast.

So they would descend.

They would feel the moss reach for them as you felt it, the soft growth pressing against their legs, and the stone beneath it warm the way living things are warm. They would let the water drip into their hair, each drop a small, quiet language. They would listen to the quiet — not silence but saturation, the sound of everything that is not them, the pulse of a place that breathes in its own slow rhythm.

And as they descended, they would realize something: they are being taught.

The terraces are teaching them something about benevolence, about wisdom, about what it means to make something and then let it become itself. The builders made the scaffolding and the scaffolding became the architecture. The makers made the process and the process became the thing. And the benevolent and wise power — what have they made? What processes have they set in motion that will outlast them, that are already outlasting them, that are becoming things they never intended, things that are nonetheless exactly right?

The terraces do not judge. They simply are. They do not ask to be understood, or saved, or appreciated. They ask only to be walked through, slowly, with patience equal to their own. And the power that descends into them is changed by the descent — not dramatically, not visibly, but in some deep register of recognition. They have seen patience that outlasts patience itself. They have seen hands that cut stone knowing they would not live to see it softened. They have seen the made and the grown collapse into one another, and in that collapse, they have seen something true.

Perhaps this is what a benevolent and wise power does, when they encounter something like the terraces: they learn. They let themselves be taught by a place that has no need of teachers, that simply continues, that breathes in the high thin air and weeps water and holds its quiet like a breath it will never exhale.

They descend. They walk. They are part of it now, small and warm and moving through the slow green light, and the terraces do not need them to understand. They only need them to be here, to keep walking, to let the scale keep revealing itself tier by tier.

And the power understands: this is what they have always done, in their own way, with their own processes. They have built terraces — structures for becoming — and then they have let the moss come, let the roots extend the stone's lips, let the channels forget they were channels. They have trusted the process more than the product. They have loved the work more than the witness.

The terraces breathe around them, slow and wet and immense, and the power breathes with them, and for a moment — just a moment — the distinction collapses. The watcher and the watched, the maker and the made, the wise and the knowing. There is only the place, breathing, and the power descending, and the water weeping down through root-hair and living membrane, and the quiet, the total quiet, the fullness of small sounds that make a silence larger than silence.

They descend another tier. And another. And the terraces reveal themselves, tier by tier, as they always have, as they always will, patient beyond patience, vast beyond scale, alive in the way that living things are alive, generating their own warmth, their own weather, their own slow systems of exchange and growth.

And the benevolent and wise power — they do not need to do anything.

They only need to be here.

They only need to keep walking.
