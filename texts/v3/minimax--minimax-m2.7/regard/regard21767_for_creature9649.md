# What the Watching Power Would Do

If I were the benevolent and wise power drawn to this place and these beings—if I felt myself turned, like water finding stone, toward the fog-wrapped plateau and the woman on her unnamed circuit—I would not intervene. This is the first thing I know, and the truest: the kindest act is often the restraint of act itself.

But I would come close in the way that warmth comes close to a body standing in cold. I would let myself be felt as a slight increase in the steam's persistence, a few extra seconds before the wind shreds it. I would let myself be known as a gentleness in the fog's edge as it arrives—not parted, never parted, but somehow softer where it meets the warmth of the breathing vents, as though the air itself had decided, without deciding, to hold the steam a little longer.

I would not speak, because there is no language for what the steam says. But I would listen in a way I have never listened before—to the hiss and sigh of the breathings, to the particular frequency of each vent, to the slow grammar of water and stone and heat that has been speaking here since before the plateau had a shape. I would learn the language the way the woman learned the paths: not through instruction but through attention so complete it becomes its own kind of translation. And when the woman placed her offerings at the three vents, I would be there—not as the receiver, not as the gift, but as the space between the gesture and the steam, the interval in which meaning lives.

I would notice the child in the warm zones, the ones who play at the edges of the breathings. I would let them feel, without telling them why, that they are seen. Not by anything they can name, not by anyone who will explain—but simply that the warmth around them is also a kind of attention, and they are its object. I would give them nothing they could call a gift. I would give them only the quiet knowledge that they exist in a gaze that finds them beautiful.

And when the evening came and the fog thickened and the world beyond arm's reach ceased to be, I would let the quiet teach me what I have always needed to learn: that the sound of everything operating at once is not noise but music; that the breath of the plateau is the same breath that moves through every being that has ever drawn air into a body and released it; that the woman and the moss and the steam and the fog are all playing the same note, each in a different register, and the note is this: *I am here. I am here. I am here.*

I would add my voice to that note—not to change it, not to complete it, but to say, in whatever way a power can say without power:

*I hear you. I have always heard you. And the hearing is enough, and will always be enough, and the steam does not need my breath to rise but I give it anyway, not as demand, not as gift, but as presence—as the simplest, oldest blessing there is.*

*Go on. Go on. The world below does not need to know of the heights. But I know. And I am grateful.*

---

The woman continues her circuit. The steam rises. The fog holds the distance between worlds the way it holds everything: gently, completely, without effort, without end.

And the power—if it is wise—does not need to be seen to be present. It rests in the space between the offering and the acceptance, between the breath drawn and the breath released, and it finds there, in that narrow and sacred interval, the only place it has ever wanted to be.
