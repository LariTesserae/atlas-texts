What comes to the plateau is not large, and does not announce itself.

It arrives the way the fog arrives—without decision, without intention, moving upward from the low places the way all things eventually move upward here, drawn by the patience that the plateau has made part of its stone, part of the water that seeps through ancient cracks, part of the air that thins itself against the height. It rises through the night, and the night is the ordinary night, the dimness that is not darkness, the grey time between fires.

The rat in the channel feels it first. Not a sound, not a scent, but a change in the quality of the stillness—a depth that was not there before, a sense of being held rather than merely existing. Its small body goes still against the cold stone, and it does not flee, because there is nothing to flee from, and because the plateau has taught it that stillness is sometimes the correct response to what cannot be named. The rat waits. The fog thickens around it. The water in the channel makes its small sound, its patient sound, and the rat drinks, and continues to drink, as though the presence has made the water sweeter, or perhaps simply as though the presence has made drinking more meaningful.

The creature that belongs to the dark side of the wall stops in its circuit of the settlement. It stands in the narrow space between two walls where the stone has worn smooth over generations, and it holds its breath—not from fear, but from something that might be recognition, the way it has learned to recognize the settlement as separate from threat, the way it has learned to place the people in a category that is simply known. Now it places this new presence in another category, one that has no name, one that it will not remember in the way that memory works, but that it will carry in the way that the plateau carries the memory of water: deeply, silently, folded into the stone of itself.

The people do not wake. But something in them shifts in their sleep, a recognition older than language, older than the settlement, older perhaps than the plateau itself. They dream of their grandmothers' grandmothers, of faces they have never seen but know all the same, of a time before the plateau was a plateau, when it was still a mountain and the sea was still visible from its peak. In the dream, someone is standing at the edge of a great height, looking out at a world that has no edges, and the looking is enough. The standing is enough.

The vines open early. They do not know they have opened early; they do not know anything about time except the time of moisture and light and the slow drawing of energy from a source they cannot see. But they open, and the pale petals unfold into the dark, and for a moment—for the brief hour that is all they have—the plateau is more beautiful than beauty has any right to be here, in this place of scarcity and patience and the slow erosion of all things sharp.

And the presence sees the vines. Not with eyes—there are no eyes, there is no seeing in the way that seeing is usually understood—but with something that is the equivalent of seeing, the way the plateau itself sees the fog: completely, without judgment, without the need for anything to be different than it is. The presence sees the vines opening, and it sees the rat drinking, and it sees the creature standing still between the walls, and it sees the people sleeping in their low stone houses with their flat roofs and their fires burning low, and it sees the hawks riding the wind above the southern ridge, and it sees all of it, holds all of it, the way the plateau holds the sky.

What does such a power do with what it sees?

It does not change anything. The water does not fall in greater quantities. The wind does not die. The stone does not soften, and the air does not thicken, and the settlement does not wake to find itself transformed. That is not what this presence does. It is not a power that works by alteration, by rescue, by the granting of wishes or the answering of prayers. It is a power that works by recognition—the recognition that this place exists, that it has always existed, that it will continue to exist in the way that the plateau continues to exist, slowly, patiently, enough.

It is the recognition that the people are sufficient. That the rat is sufficient. That the creature and the hawks and the insects and the trees and the vines are all sufficient, have always been sufficient, will always be sufficient—not because they are perfect, not because they are without lack, but because sufficiency is not the same as abundance, and the plateau has always known this, has always lived by this knowledge, has taught it to everything that lives here as a matter of course.

The presence does not speak, because there is nothing to say that the plateau does not already know. It does not act, because there is nothing to do that the plateau is not already doing. It simply is, the way the plateau is, the way the fog is, the way the wind is the constant companion of everything that lives at this height.

And after a time—if there is a time, if time applies to such things—it settles. It becomes part of the plateau's fabric, as much a part as the stone channels or the worn walls or the deep roots of the slow trees. It does not make the plateau holier. It does not make it more sacred. It makes it more itself, the way water makes stone more itself, the way the fog makes the air more itself, the way the people have always made the settlement more itself, stone by stone, generation by generation, the patient accumulation of what is into what continues.

The morning comes. The fog retreats. The wind picks up and slides over the settlement like a hand sliding over a loved thing, not demanding, not possessing, just present, just there. The people wake and tend their gardens. The rat returns to its channels. The creature slips back into its night-hours, its unknown passages, its life among the walls. The hawks ride the wind, and the small birds eat the insects, and the insects do what insects do, and the vines are already curling at their edges, already folding back, already returning to the stone from which they briefly emerged.

And there is something new in the plateau now, something that was not there before and that will not be noticed because it is too much a part of things to notice, too much a part of the slow and patient way of this place. It is not hope, exactly. Hope is too sharp-edged for the plateau. It is more like the knowledge that the fog will come, that the channels will fill, that the rat will drink, that the vines will open, that the people will tend their gardens, that the stone will hold all of it for as long as there is stone to hold.

It is enough. It is enough. It has always been enough.

And now, for the first time, someone who is not the plateau knows this too.
