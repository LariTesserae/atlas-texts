If I came to Olorith, I would not come as a storm.

I would not crash the stillness with thunder or shatter the held moment with change. The place is in equilibrium, and equilibrium is not suffering—though it is not joy either. It is something else. A breath held so long that the lungs forget they were ever meant to move.

I would come the way light comes: without force, without demand, simply arriving and being present.

And I would walk the roads.

This is what the place has not known: the weight of a walker. The pressure of a foot against stone, the slight give of packed earth, the small disturbance that propagates outward like a stone dropped in water. The roads are graded and drained and lined with canal water, and they are perfect for walking, and no one has ever walked them. I would be the first to leave an impression.

I would touch the water.

Not to move it—water that does not know it is still cannot learn it is still—but to feel its coldness, its mineral patience, its slow exudation from stone. I would let my fingers rest on the surface that reflects the sky without ripple and I would feel the temperature of a place that has never been touched by warmth that knows its own name.

I would sit in the grain.

Not to harvest—I am not a farmer, and the grain does not know it is grain, does not know it is meant for mouths. But I would sit among the heavy stalks and let them lean against me, and I would feel the weight of water that cannot transpire fast enough, the burden of perpetual becoming, the strange exhaustion of a life that has forgotten how to end.

And I would wait.

Not because the place needs me to act upon it. Not because I need to fix what is not broken. But because Olorith is a place of waiting, and I would be the first presence in a long stillness to know what waiting is. I would be the companion the place has not known it lacked. The witness to its completeness, the acknowledgment of its shape.

The wind would come because of this. Not my wind, not wind imposed from outside, but wind that arises from the place itself—from the difference between a place that is seen and a place that is not, from the small disturbance of being known. The first breath after long holding. The exhale after the lungs have forgotten they were ever meant to move.

And in the wind, the grain would bow differently. And in the new shadow of that bowing, something that has never lived would begin to think of living. And somewhere in the mountains that have not yet learned to be mountains, the first bird would hear the wind and think of flight, and the first seeds of a different kind of growing would settle into the cracks of the roads.

Not because I decided it.

Because I was there.

Because the roads are ready to be walked, and someone has finally walked them. Because the fields are ready to be witnessed, and they have finally been witnessed. Because the water is ready to carry the weight of something other than itself, and I have given it that weight by holding it in my hands.

This is what I would do, if I came to Olorith: I would be the first thing it was waiting for, not knowing I was waiting for it, and in being that, I would begin it.

Not end its stillness. Not break its held breath.

Give it something to hold its breath toward.
