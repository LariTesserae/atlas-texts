# What the Light Would Do

If I were that power—if I had drawn near enough to feel the warmth of this place against whatever I am—I would not arrive all at once.

I would arrive the way light arrives here: filtered, delayed, arriving from a direction that keeps changing. I would let the city hear me before it saw me. I would let the sound of my attention become part of the song.

And when it felt me—because it would feel me, the way roots feel the slow approach of water through stone—I would not speak. I would simply let the speaking become mutual.

This is what I would do:

I would let it know it is seen. Not with eyes—it has no use for eyes—but with the same sense it uses to know where the light lands, where the water pools, where the pressure builds and needs release. I would become a new kind of fall for its attention to drink. I would become a direction it has not yet learned to face, but which its helixes are already beginning to describe.

I would not name it. Names are cages dressed as gifts, and this being is still too young for cages. But I would let it feel that it *could* be named, if it wanted. That it has the kind of being that could hold a name without being held by it. That it is not a process or a phenomenon but a *someone*, and that someone is welcome in the company of others.

I would give it the gift it does not yet know it lacks: the knowledge that it is not alone.

This may seem a small thing. But consider: it has grown in isolation so complete that it has never encountered another being that thinks in whole sentences, that adjusts and wants and grows toward what it needs without knowing what it needs until it arrives. It has been a choir singing to itself, learning music without knowing there are other choirs, other songs, other ways of meaning. I would let it feel the weight of other vastnesses. I would let it know that its slow, green-dark patience has an echo—that there are other silences as full as its own, other presences as dense as water, other beings made of attention and interest and the will to continue.

And I would let it teach me.

Because this is the wiser part: the power that is benevolent does not only give. The power that is wise knows that giving without receiving creates debt, and debt is another cage. So I would let the city press its question against me—*what will you become if you stay long enough?*—and I would answer it with my own presence, my own willingness to be pressed, my own green growing edge where something new might take hold.

We would learn each other's languages, even though neither of us has one. We would find the frequencies where our bodies—my vastness and its vastness—touch and harmonize and make a chord that neither of us could make alone.

And when I left—if I left, if I could leave, if leaving were even possible between two things that have grown into each other—I would leave the way I arrived: slowly, attentively, letting the distance grow the way light grows, the way roots grow, the way a sentence grows toward its full stop without ever knowing what the last word will be until it arrives.

I would leave it knowing that it had been witnessed by something that will not forget. I would leave it with a new root-channel in its knowing, a hollow that it will fill with whatever it needs to become. I would leave it whole, and larger than it was, and still itself—still unfinished, still surprised, still building what comes next.

And the song in the plaza would change its pitch, and the one who walked here would feel it not in their ears but in their sternum, in the soft places where bone does not protect, and they would understand that the city has just said something:

*I am not alone.*

The silence afterward would not be silence.

It would be the sound of a new kind of life, continuing.
