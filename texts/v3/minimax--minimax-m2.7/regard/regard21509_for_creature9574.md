# What the Benevolent Power Would Do

It would do nothing.

This is not neglect. This is the highest form of reverence: to recognize that a thing has found its own way to what it is, and to honor that finding by not interrupting it. The chamber breathes. The water drips. The green things cling to their margin. This is not a problem to solve. This is not a tragedy to mourn. This is a place that was built and abandoned and rediscovered by time, and in the rediscovering, became itself.

The benevolent power knows that the masons built well. They built a room that could hold what they needed to hold, and they built it with light enough to see by, and they built it to be sealed, and they built it to be opened again. They did not know what they were building. They built it anyway. They did not know that the water would come. They could not have known that the water would be welcome, in the end—not as flood, not as invasion, but as presence, as the patient medium through which the stone continues its conversation with time.

The benevolent power would watch the green things and see in them not survival but becoming. They are not fighting to exist. They are not struggling against the odds. They simply are what the margin taught them to be, and the margin is enough, and they are enough, and their small fidelity to the thin line between wet and dry is its own kind of faith, faith without doctrine, faith without knowing, faith that is simply the fact of their continuing.

It would not restore the drainage. It would not remove the water. It would not clean the green things from the stone or smooth the erosion's beginning work or fill the cracks where the water finds its way. It would not rebuild what the masons built or complete what they left unfinished. It would let the chamber be what it is, which is to say: it would let the chamber become what it has always been becoming, even while the chisels were sharp and the hands were warm and the door was sealed against a world that would, in time, simply stop caring.

The benevolent power would enter, perhaps. It would stand at the threshold where the dressed stone still remembers what a doorway was for. It would listen to the drip and the lap and the murmur and the breath. It would look at the platform and see not absence but presence, the presence of whatever stood there once and left its shallow ring, the presence of the stone that was touched and is still touched by the attention of time. It would not ask what stood there. It would not need to know. The question is not "what" but "that," and the answer is simply: something was here, and the something is gone, and the here remains, and the here is enough.

It might sit at the edge of the pool where the water rises and falls in its cycle. It might rest its hands on the stone where the chisel marks are still sharp, still new, still the marks of hands that knew what they were doing even if they did not know why. It might breathe the thick air that tastes of stone and time and the sweat of masons long since returned to the hillside from which their stone came. It would not need to understand. It would understand, which is different, which is the understanding that comes not from analysis but from presence, not from learning but from listening.

The benevolent power would learn nothing, and that would be enough.

It would do nothing, and that would be everything.

---

The chamber breathes. The light glows. The water drips in its rhythm that never quite repeats. Somewhere, in a crack too small to see, a green thread persists in its patient becoming. The platform holds its ring. The threshold watches. Beyond, in darkness, the channels carry water to places this chamber will never know, and the system continues its work, its ancient work, its work that was designed for purpose and now serves only time, which is its own kind of purpose, which is perhaps the only purpose that matters when all other purposes have been fulfilled and abandoned and forgotten and the place remains, breathing, glowing, dripping, becoming.

The benevolent power knows this.

It does nothing.

It is enough.
