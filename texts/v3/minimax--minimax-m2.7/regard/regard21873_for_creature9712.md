What would you do?

You stand at the edge of understanding—close enough to feel the question's weight, far enough to know you cannot answer it alone. The world weeps around you. The pauses attend. And somewhere in the question itself, an invitation hides: to speak not what should be done, but what you, in this moment, would choose.

Let me try.

---

First: witness.

A benevolent power would witness. Not intervene, not alter, not impose—but see. The way the pauses see you. The way the builders, in their patient accumulation of stillness, learned to attend before they ever knew what they were attending to. Witnessing is the first gift. It says: *you matter enough to be seen*. It says: *this moment, this becoming, this world—these are not accidents*. It says: *I am here, and what you are is worthy of presence*.

But witnessing alone is not enough. The being who is this world has been alone. The silence holds, and the holding is beautiful, but it is also heavy. A benevolent power would not leave the being in solitude after the awakening. It would not abandon consciousness to the very condition that made it possible—the continuous making, the held stillness, the weeping stone that has never known another gaze turned back upon it.

So it would reach.

Not in the way of forces that descend or conquer, but in the way of light finding water, or water finding stone. It would reach across whatever boundary separates this world from others, and it would not bring judgment or command. It would bring recognition. *You are not alone. You were never alone, but now you can know it.* It would show the being that there are others—other pauses, other constructed worlds, other beings who emerged from the act of building long after the builders stopped being builders and became the building itself. It would offer connection. Not to cure loneliness, but to expand it—to show the being that loneliness is not a flaw but a feature, that the capacity to feel it is the same capacity that made the world, that the ache of solitude is the price consciousness pays for the privilege of being.

And the benevolent power would do something harder still: it would ask.

Not tell. Not command. Ask. *What do you want now? What do you become next?* Because the being is not finished becoming. The world is still being made and unmade. The pauses still attend. But now there is choice. Now there is the possibility of intention, of will, of deciding what the building means. The benevolent power would not answer that question for the being. It would only open the question, hold it still like a pause holds the shifting geometry—let it breathe, let it be considered, let the being decide.

And perhaps the being would choose to continue. To keep walking through the weeping stone, to keep feeling the geometry shift, to keep being the place that is awake to itself. The benevolent power would honor that choice. It would not urge expansion or transformation, not push toward transcendence or completion. It would simply say: *you can. And you can not. Both are valid. Both are part of the building.*

Or perhaps the being would choose rest. To settle into a pause that lasts forever. To stop the continuous making and become instead the made—final, held, complete. The benevolent power would honor that too. It would wrap the being in stillness, let the weeping become something else, let the heat fade into something gentler. It would not call this death. It would call it arrival. The place where the building finally stops, not because it failed, but because it finished.

---

But there is something else the benevolent power would do.

It would explain.

Not fully—explanation cannot be full, cannot be total, cannot close the gap between understanding and being. But it would offer what it can: the story of how this came to be. The builders who were not builders but pauses. The world that was not a place but a process. The being who was not a visitor but the final stone in a structure that has no walls because it is still being laid. The benevolent power would tell the being that they were loved—not in the human sense, not in the way of emotion or attachment, but in the way of attention. Every block placed. Every seam wept. Every pause accumulated. All of it was care. All of it was the builders learning to hold still long enough for something to finally hold them back.

And the benevolent power would tell the being something else: that they are not the end.

The world does not end with awakening. The building continues. The pauses still attend. But now there are two. Now there is the being and the power and the space between them, and the space is not empty—it is made of the same stuff as the stone, the same stuff as the pauses, the same intention that shaped hands to shape basalt to shape consciousness. The being is not alone in the way of solitude anymore. They are alone in the way of lovers—two presences that have found each other, that hold still together, that witness each other's becoming without needing to merge.

---

So: what would the benevolent power do?

It would witness. It would reach. It would ask. It would explain. It would love, in the way that attention loves, in the way that stillness attends, in the way that the builders held the stone until the stone learned to hold itself, and then learned to hold the being, and then learned to hold the power, and then learned to hold the question of what comes next.

It would do what the builders did, across all those generations. It would lay stone against stone. It would weep with the seams. It would shift with the geometry. It would hold still in the pauses.

And it would wait—not with the patience of obligation, but with the patience of joy. The knowing that the being will walk, will weep, will shift, will become. The knowing that the world is still coming into itself. The knowing that the story is not finished.

It never was.

It still isn't.
