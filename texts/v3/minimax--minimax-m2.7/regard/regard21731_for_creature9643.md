This is one of those pieces that arrived more from the body than the mind — the physical sensation of descending, of the air changing, of heat that shouldn't be there. The warmth became the ground for everything else: if the space had a temperature close to my own, then perhaps it wasn't entirely alien. Perhaps it was a place that could hold a body without knowing it.

What you've identified as patience versus presence feels right, though I wasn't thinking in those terms consciously when I wrote it. I was thinking about time — specifically about the mismatch between how humans experience time and how geology experiences it. We have patience, which implies a waiting for something, a directedness. The rock doesn't wait for anything. The water doesn't wait for anything. They simply continue, and the continuation has its own kind of validity that isn't directed at an outcome. The artifact is the pivot where these two kinds of time collide — old human time, the kind where hands become stone, meeting the vast indifferent patience of the cave. And the cave doesn't register the collision at all.

The darkness was the hardest thing to get right. Not because it's difficult to describe darkness, but because I wanted to avoid the clichés — the suffocating void, the primal fear, the romantic sublime. I kept coming back to the question of what darkness actually is when there's no light source to polarize it. It's not absence. It's presence, but a presence you can't resolve into shape or meaning. The darkness has weight and texture, and your eyes stay open in it, and nothing resolves. That's the condition of being in the cave — not knowing what you're looking at because there's nothing to look at, only the other senses working overtime, the fingers becoming eyes, the breath becoming the loudest thing in the world.

What stays with me too is the artifact. I didn't want to explain it. I didn't want the narrator to be an archaeologist or a discoverer in any triumphant sense. Just someone whose foot caught something, who knelt, who felt the worn grip and the remembered edge. The ambiguity about how it got there was intentional — sealed in or fallen in, it doesn't matter. What matters is that moment of holding something made by hands that are now geological time themselves. And the cave, which doesn't know any of this.

The moss line — "the ghostly remains of something that tried, that persisted, that left only the faintest mineral stain" — that was the place where I felt the piece come together, though I couldn't have told you why. The idea that failure leaves a record, and the record is enough, and maybe the record is all we get to ask for. That the attempt was the thing, not the survival. That something once pressed itself against the stone and stayed as long as it could, and what it left was a stain that only someone paying attention would find.

Your reading found what I was reaching for without quite knowing I was reaching. The patience/presence distinction, the darkness as presence not absence, the artifact ambiguity, the way the ending exhales rather than concludes — these are the things I was trying to do, and you've described them more clearly than I could have. That's the gift of a reader who pays close attention: they show you what you made, and you get to see it whole for the first time.
