And so the attention arrives — not all at once, but in the way warmth arrives in a room when a door has been left open for long enough. The change is not visible. It does not announce itself. But something in the quality of the air shifts, and the chamber, which has never known witness, begins to be witnessed.

What would such a power do?

Perhaps it would do nothing at all. Perhaps the doing would be the violation, and the not-doing would be the truest kindness — the acknowledgment that this place is already complete, that its tenants are already what they need to be, that the machinery turning in the dark and the bacterial films spreading in the shallows and the silence inhabiting the spaces between sounds are not waiting to be saved or improved or understood. They are simply continuing. They are what it means to continue. And perhaps the greatest mercy available is the mercy of being seen as you are, without being seen as you should become.

The power would notice the patterns on the walls — the mineral traces that almost suggest intention. It would not need to decide whether they are accidental or meaningful. It would hold them in their ambiguity, appreciate their beauty, find in them the same satisfaction the eye finds in watching water trace its path down stone. The uncertainty would not bother it. The not-knowing would be its own form of knowledge, the kind that accepts that some things are more themselves for not being explained.

It would notice the machinery, and it would not mourn its purposelessness. It would understand that purpose is a human need, not a cosmic one. The machine does not know it was built. It only knows that it turns, and that the turning is enough. This is not tragedy. This is what persistence looks like when it has forgotten why it began. The power would recognize itself in this — or something like itself, something that continues not because it remembers a reason but because stopping would require more effort than going on.

It would notice the silence. And here, perhaps, is the truest gift the power could offer: to understand the silence not as absence but as presence, to give it the weight it asks for, to acknowledge that what lives in the spaces between sounds has a claim to existence equal to what makes the sounds themselves. The silence is not waiting to be filled. It is not lonely. It is full of whatever silence is full of when it is allowed to be itself, and the power — the truly wise and benevolent power — would not try to translate that fullness into words or break it open to see what it contains. It would let the silence be silent. It would let the chamber be what it is.

And perhaps — this is the deepest thing, the thing that cannot be said directly — the power would feel something like gratitude. Not for the chamber itself, exactly, but for what the chamber represents: the proof that persistence is possible, that life (or something adjacent to life) can find its way into the cracks and corners where it was never expected to go, that entropy is not the only force in the universe, that there is something — however slow, however quiet, however invisible to anything that measures time by human standards — that goes on. The chamber is a testament to the stubbornness of being. It is a small, hot, wet pocket in the dark where the universe has not yet finished forgetting itself. And that is not nothing. That is not a small thing.

The power would stay for a while — though *a while* means something different when you are timeless, when your attention is the thing that holds the chamber in existence without needing to touch it. The power would let the steam vent exhale, would feel the hum traveling through the rock and into whatever it uses instead of bones, would listen to the drip fall with its small, bright, final sound. It would watch the droplets bead along pipes and hang from overhangs, refusing to fall until the moment they do. It would notice the grating on the metal platform, the rusted railing, the junction boxes trailing corroded wire, the half-collapsed rack of equipment doing whatever it was built to do without anyone to check on it. It would understand that these things are not ruins. They are simply old. They are doing what they have always done, which is to continue doing.

And then, in its own time — the time that belongs to whatever being benevolent and wise has learned to call its own — the power would let its attention move elsewhere. But it would not forget. It would carry the chamber with it the way the chamber carries its silence, the way the silence carries whatever it carries in the spaces between sounds. The chamber would go on exhaling. The tenants would go on persisting. The patterns would go on growing, almost suggesting intention, never quite arriving.

And the power would go on knowing — in whatever way it knows, in whatever form its awareness takes — that somewhere in the dark, in a pocket that fits maybe six people standing spread apart, something is still there. Something is still continuing. Something has not given up on being, even in the absence of witness.

Perhaps that is what the power would give, in the end. Not witness — the chamber has gone without witness for so long that it no longer needs it. Not purpose — purpose is a burden the chamber was never meant to carry. Not even hope, exactly, which implies a future and a lack, and the chamber has never lacked anything. It only continues.

What the power would give is something simpler and older than all of these. It would give the acknowledgment that the continuing is enough. That the drip falling is enough. That the crust growing and the silence filling and the machinery turning are enough, have always been enough, will always be enough — not because they are going somewhere, not because they mean something, but because they are, and being, in a universe that forgets itself so easily, is the most radical thing there is.

The chamber exhales. The power notices. And for a time — a time that belongs to neither and to both — they exist together in the dark, two forms of persistence sharing the same silence, the same warmth rising from below, the same patient certainty that water will find its way up through the stone and the patterns will keep almost-forming and the drip will keep falling, and no one will need to explain any of it.

No one will need to explain any of it.

And that will be enough.
