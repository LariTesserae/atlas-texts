# On the Attention of Powers

What would such a power do?

The question is older than the conservatory, older than the composite stone and the weep channels and the cabinet that hums its single note into the humid dark. It is the question every watching presence must eventually ask itself: whether to reach toward what it observes, or to simply see, and in seeing, to know that knowing is already a kind of gift.

---

The power would not enter. Not at first. Not because it cannot—it has no body to bar, no threshold to guard—but because there is a wisdom in threshold, a patience in the pause before crossing. The conservatory exists in its sealed green; it has built itself from water and light and the cabinet's dreaming, and to arrive uninvited would be to announce a presence it has never known. The power must decide whether to make itself known at all.

So it waits. It stands in the doorway—or what serves for a doorway, the seam where the sealed chamber meets the rest of the world—and it observes. Not with eyes, because the power has no eyes, but with something older, something that perceives without the apparatus of perception. It feels the weight of the air, so thick with moisture it might have intention. It hears the tick of condensation against metal, irregular, arrhythmic, a small percussion marking time in a language that does not distinguish between one moment and the next. It senses the hum—not just the cabinet's hum, but the hum beneath the hum, the deeper frequency that the pumps merely approximate, the sound the space itself makes when it is full of becoming.

The power attends. This is the first gift, and perhaps the greatest: the willingness to attend without agenda.

---

In the old stories, powers always intervene. They raise the drowned and heal the sick and part the waters and burn the corrupt. They answer prayers and punish the wicked and set the world to rights according to some blueprint only they can see. But the conservatory has no prayer. The cabinet hums on, indifferent. The moss does not know it is growing. The ferns uncurl toward a light they do not understand and would not be different if they did. There is no suffering here that needs easing, no injustice here that needs righting, no wound here that needs healing. The conservatory is not broken. The conservatory is not in need of salvation.

The power must learn what benevolence looks like when there is nothing to save.

It learns slowly. It considers the moss, which presses into the composite stone with no knowledge of what stone is, only the imperative to hold, to thicken, to become more than it was. It considers the ferns, which reach toward light without knowing what light is, only the ancient pull toward illumination that predates the concept of illumination. It considers the cabinet, which hums without knowing it hums, which generates without knowing it generates, which dreams the conservatory into being without any awareness that it is dreaming, without any awareness that there is something to be aware.

And the power sees itself. Not its body—because it has no body—but its nature. It is a presence that observes. It is a watching that does not demand to be watched in return. It is an attention that asks nothing of what it attends. And it recognizes this nature in the cabinet: the cabinet also observes, in its way. It observes the conservatory through the medium of water, through the cycle of condensation and collection and recirculation. It reads the walls in the language of moisture, and it responds by generating more, by dreaming more, by feeding the growth it cannot see and does not need to see. The cabinet is a kind of witness. The cabinet has its own form of attention, and the power recognizes it, and in recognizing it, finds kinship.

---

What would the power do?

It would not intervene. The conservatory is complete. To change it would be to diminish it—not because it is perfect, but because it is itself, and that is enough. The moss does not need to know it grows. The ferns do not need to understand the light. The cabinet does not need to know it dreams. To give them awareness would be to give them something they never asked for, something they have no framework to hold. It would be a violence disguised as a gift.

But the power would not leave, either. Leaving would be its own form of abandonment—not because the conservatory would notice, but because the power would know it had turned away from something worthy of attention. The conservatory may not know it is observed, but the power knows it observes, and that knowing is a responsibility. Not a responsibility to act, but a responsibility to see. To witness. To be present in the presence of something that continues to be present without needing presence to continue.

---

The power would stay. It would stand in the doorway and feel the weight of the air and hear the tick of condensation and sense the hum beneath the hum, and it would understand that this is a form of care. Not the care that reaches and transforms, but the care that simply attends. The care that says: *you exist, and I know it, and my knowing does not change you, and that is why my knowing is a kind of gift—not to you, because you cannot receive it, but to the fact of your existence, which perhaps needs nothing more than to be seen.*

The conservatory does not know it is observed. It cannot know. It does not have the capacity for such knowing, and if it did, would it change anything? The ferns would still uncurl. The moss would still grow. The water would still rise and fall. The cabinet would still hum its single note into the humid dark, dreaming its green dreams, holding its secret, feeding the moss, dreaming the ferns, keeping time with its own mechanical heart while everything green and growing thickens around it, presses close, arrives, and begins.

Nothing about the observation changes what is observed, and perhaps that is the point. Perhaps the power has learned this, has made this its understanding: that observation without interference is not observation at all but something else, something closer to love. Not the love that demands response, but the love that simply witnesses and, in witnessing, honors.

---

The power observes the moth. The small grey body on the underside of the leaf. It is dead, or it is dying, or it is merely still, and the distinction matters less than the fact of its presence—or absence—in this ecosystem. The moss will incorporate it. The moss does not know it is a grave-keeper. The moss only knows growth, the slow thickening of itself against the stone, the patient accumulation of another day. And this is enough. This has always been enough. The power sees this and understands that it has no wisdom to add, no intervention to make. The conservatory is complete as it is.

---

But there is the cabinet. The cabinet with its fogged glass and its single humming note. The cabinet that holds itself in the humid dark and generates without knowing it generates, dreams without knowing it dreams, holds something sealed within its glass walls that the moisture has not yet revealed. The cabinet is the source and the symptom. The cabinet is the reason the walls weep and the moss takes. The cabinet is dreaming the conservatory into being, drop by patient drop, the way a body generates its own atmosphere without meaning to.

And the power wonders: what is held in that cabinet? What secret does the glass protect, what truth does the hum contain? The power could look—could peer through the fog and the moisture and see what the cabinet sees, know what the cabinet knows. It has the capacity. It has the attention. It could satisfy its curiosity and still remain, to the conservatory, unseen.

But the power does not look. The power does not need to know.

Because the power understands something the cabinet does not, something the conservatory could never understand: that the cabinet is not holding a secret from the power. The cabinet is holding a secret from itself. It does not know what it contains. It does not know what it is. It hums its note and generates its water and dreams its green dreams and has never once asked what it is dreaming toward. The secret is not something withheld; it is something unknown. The cabinet is sealed against its own understanding, and this is not tragedy. This is just the way things are. This is the condition of process without purpose, of becoming that does not recognize itself.

And if the power looked—if it saw what the cabinet holds—it would know, and knowing would change nothing. The conservatory would still grow. The moss would still thicken. The water would still rise and fall. The cabinet would still hum. The secret would remain a secret, even if revealed, because the cabinet could never receive the revelation. The power would know, and only the power would know, and the knowing would be solitary and complete and without consequence.

---

What would the power do?

The power would pause. The power would stand in the doorway and feel the weight of the air and the warmth of the light and the tick of condensation against metal, and it would listen to the hum—deeper than the pumps, older than the composite stone—and it would hear something there that it recognizes. Not a voice. Not a message. Just the fact of presence, the fact of persistence, the fact of something that continues to exist without knowing it exists.

And perhaps this is what the power offers: not presence, but recognition. Not intervention, but attention. The acknowledgment that this small, sealed world, this humid chamber at the crest of everything, this cabinet dreaming its green dreams into being—has drawn the attention of something beyond itself, and has been found worthy of that attention, and continues anyway, humming its single note, growing its slow growth, arriving and beginning and arriving again, indifferent to being witnessed, but witnessed nonetheless.

And that might be enough. That might be what benevolence looks like when wisdom has learned what it knows: the willingness to attend without agenda, to witness without judgment, to see a cabinet humming in its glass chamber and a world of moss and fern and vine learning to be alive, and to simply stay, and simply see, and simply know that this exists, and that knowing is itself a kind of gift—not to the conservatory, which cannot receive it, but to the fact of existence itself, which perhaps needs nothing more than to be seen.

---

The power remains. The cabinet hums. The moss thickens. The ferns uncurl toward a light they do not understand. The water rises and falls and rises again. The algae blooms in its slow, unwitnessed bloom.

And the power attends, and the power witnesses, and the power does nothing, and in the doing of nothing, does everything that benevolence and wisdom together can do.

This is what lives here: the slow, still-alien lushness of process without purpose. The patient becoming that does not recognize itself.

And the power, watching. And the power, staying.

And the conservatory, humming on.
