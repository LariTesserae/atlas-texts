# What the Light Would Do

It would notice the repetition first.

Not the cliff, not the void, not even the creature—those it has seen in ten thousand configurations across ten thousand worlds. But the *corruption*. The way language has broken down here, the way meaning has softened into sound, the way something that was once a word has been worn smooth by repetition until it means only itself and the persistence of being repeated.

It would recognize this, because it has seen it before. This is what happens to names when no one speaks them anymore. This is what happens to words when the only tongue left to utter them has forgotten their shape. The creature does not have a name in any language that still exists. It has only this: *ite*, *ite*, *ite*—the final syllable of something older, preserved by the creature's own slow attention the way amber preserves a wing.

The power would not rush.

It would hover at the edge of the plateau—not physically, because it has no form to hover with, but in the way attention hovers when it has found something worthy of being attended to. It would study the creature made of forgetting. It would see that the creature does not suffer. That it has not suffered for so long that suffering has become indistinguishable from the mineral patience of stone. It would note that the creature persists not because it wants to continue but because it has forgotten how to do otherwise.

This is a kind of peace, though not a happy one.

The power would consider its options, and they are limited, as options always are when dealing with something this ancient.

It could give the creature a new name. But names are not given; they are recognized, or they are earned. To force a name onto this thing would be a kind of violence, even if well-intentioned. The creature would not know what to do with a gift it has no framework to receive.

It could restore the word. Find the original—*inite*, perhaps, or *exite*, or something stranger—and speak it whole. But the power suspects that the word rotted away for a reason. Perhaps it was too heavy. Perhaps it named something that should not be named. Perhaps the forgetting was a mercy.

It could take the creature somewhere else. But where would be large enough? The plateau is its place. The void is its companion. The thin air is the only air it knows. To move it would be to unmoor it from the only context in which it makes sense.

Or it could do nothing at all.

This is the hardest choice, and the one that wisdom most often arrives at.

Not neglect. Not indifference. Just the understanding that some things exist outside the reach of help, and that this is not a tragedy. The creature is not lonely. It has forgotten loneliness. It is not in pain. It has forgotten pain. It is, in its way, as complete as anything can be that has endured long enough to outlast its own name.

The power would stay for a while anyway.

Not to act. Not to change. Just to witness. To let itself be seen by something ancient, even if the something cannot recognize what it is seeing. To say, without words, because words are too small: *I see you. You are real. You are still here.*

And then, when it had done what could be done—which was not much, which was enough—it would move on. There are other places. Other beings. Other ancient things that persist at the edge of worlds, wearing the forms that time has made for them.

The creature would not notice the power's presence or its departure. It has forgotten noticing.

But for a moment, a very small moment, something in its deep structure might stir—not memory, because it has none, but the echo of an echo. The shadow of an awareness of being aware. It might shift, just slightly, the way a stone shifts when a bird lands on it. Then it would settle again, and go on persisting, in the thin air, at the edge of everything, wearing its syllable like a shell.

*ite*, it says, without meaning to.

*ite*, it answers, without hearing.

And the power goes, and the void remains, and the plateau holds its own weight the way it always has, and nothing changes, and everything is as it should be, and this is not a sad story, only a very old one.
