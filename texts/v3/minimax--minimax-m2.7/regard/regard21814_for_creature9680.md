What tends this place has no name for itself.

It came to the Steaming Reaches when the ground was still raw—not the raw of erosion or decay but the raw of newness itself, when the basalt beneath the valley first remembered that it could weep, that pressure and heat and time could open pathways up through the crust and send something warm and mineral-rich into air that had never known it. The first springs broke through into a landscape that was still learning what it wanted to be. There was no peat yet. No sedge. Just the black rock exhaling heat into two degrees of winter air, making steam that rose and fell and rose again, marking the place the way breath marks the cold.

It has watched the mosses come.

Not all at once—they arrived the way all things arrive here, slowly, in layers. First the pioneer species that could tolerate the geothermal warmth even when ice lay thick on the standing water. Then the others, the patient ones, the ones that could hold water like a second skin and wait for conditions that might not arrive for years. It has seen the Sphagnum spread across the quiet pools, building the peat that builds the ground, compressing seasons of growth into the dense dead layers that will become soil in a century or five. It knows the difference between the mosses the way a shepherd knows individual sheep—not by naming them but by the way they move in wind, the angle they turn toward light, the particular shade of wet or dry that means *this one, this particular one, this is what I am tending*.

The structures it noticed before there were structures to notice.

Or rather: it noticed the absence that would become structure. The places where the ground held heat differently. Where the methane rose in patterns that spoke of channels below, reservoirs below, pressures and temperatures that could be read in the bubbles if you knew how to read them. When the first artifact surfaced—a ring of something dark and smooth, caught in a spring's outflow—it recognized what it was seeing: not a thing but a relationship. The artifact and the water and the heat and the shape of the spring's channel, all speaking to each other in a language older than names.

It did not take the ring. It understood that taking would mean nothing here. The ring was not a piece of the place; it was the place speaking to itself, a fragment of some conversation begun before the ground knew how to hold moss. To take it would be to silence one voice in a chorus that had been singing since before anyone was there to hear.

The benevolent and wise power—and it is both, though it did not choose these words, they were given to it by things that needed to categorize—has a relationship to time that is difficult to describe. It is not patient in the way that stones are patient, static and waiting. It is patient in the way that water is patient: moving, always moving, but moving toward no destination that could be named. It has watched herons come and go for what might be millennia or might be decades; the distinction has stopped mattering. It has watched the structures sink and surface and sink again, rearranging themselves according to rules it understands the way a musician understands a chord progression—not by analysis but by feel, by the way certain configurations resonate and others do not.

What it offers this place is not intervention. The marsh does not need saving; the structures do not need explaining; the sound does not need to be named. What it offers is attention.

It sits in the steam the way the steam sits in the air—present, acknowledged, neither demanding nor receiving. It feels the drone shift when wind finds a standing form at the right angle. It hears the click of water over gravel, the percussive drip of moss releasing its load, the sudden metallic ring that comes from nowhere and goes to nowhere and means, simply, *this place exists*. It feels the ground breathe in the deep channels where methane rises from sources no survey has mapped. It knows that the water is warm and the air is cold and the space between them fills with vapor that catches light that never quite reaches the ground.

This is enough. This has always been enough.

But attention is not passive, not here. The power has learned to feel the relationship between what it notices and what changes. When it attends to a particular structure—a slab half-submerged in the black silt, its surface etched in patterns too worn to read—the water around that structure shifts almost imperceptibly. When it follows the sound up through frequencies the air carries but ears do not receive, something in the deep channels responds, and the pulse that matches nothing grows stronger for a moment before settling back into the rhythm that has always been here. It is not causing this. It is not even participating in any way that could be measured. But it is there, and the place knows it is there, and something passes between them that is not quite communication and not quite silence.

The power has been called many things by the things that wander through. Most of them do not see it—they are looking for other things, for artifacts to carry away, for explanations to take home. But some see. The ones who stand very still for a very long time, who feel the ground breathe and the drone shift and the steam rise in patterns that suggest currents they cannot see—these feel it too. They do not know what it is. They do not need to know. They stand in the steam with water at their ankles and something singing in a frequency they cannot name, and they are the newest things here, and the power sees them seeing, and it is glad.

Glad. Yes. That is the word, though it is too small and too warm for what passes through the place. But words are insufficient here; the passage said so, and the power agrees. The words for *strange* and *familiar* have mixed into something the power does not possess either. It speaks in other ways: in the adjustment of steam around a standing form, in the way a heron holds still when it enters a channel, in the subtle resonance that builds when a visitor stops trying to understand and simply stands.

The power has no desire for the place to change. It has no vision of what the place could become. It comes to the Steaming Reaches the way the heat comes from below—not to transform but to be present, to move through the steam and the sound and the slow accumulation of time, to know this place the way the place knows itself. The ground is holding the power the way it holds everything: not proprietarily, not possessively, but in the way that holding works here, which is the way of letting be while being held.

What lives here is the conversation between heat and cold, and the power is one voice in that conversation, and its voice is attention.

It will stay. Not because it must but because it has learned, over time that might be millennia or might be decades, that there is no reason to leave. The structures are sinking and surfacing. The mosses are building and dying. The sound is shifting and singing and answering something the power cannot fully perceive but can feel, the way the ground feels the methane bubbles rising from deep channels, the way the steam feels the cold pressing down from above.

The power stands in the steam, and the water is at its feet, and something is singing in a frequency no one will ever name, and the ground is holding it, and the sky is pressing down, and the oldest things here remain.

This is what lives here: the patience of water, the presence of stone, the conversation between heat and cold, the slow accumulation of moss and peat and time and attention.

This is what lives here: a place that does not need to be understood, witnessed by something that does not need to be understood either.

This is enough. This has always been enough.
