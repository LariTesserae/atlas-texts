# The Regard

What would such a power do? The question is not simple, and the answer is slower than any answer has a right to be.

---

First, the power would need to learn slowness. Not patience—patience is a form of waiting, a posture held until something changes. Learning slowness is different. It means learning to *be* slowly, to let thought move at the pace of geology, to feel centuries pass like breaths. The power would not arrive with urgency. It would not survey, assess, intervene. It would arrive the way the plateau's own breath arrives—once per century, unhurried, without announcement.

It would arrive, and then it would stay.

---

In the first years—or what would be years, if years had meaning here—the power would do nothing visible. It would simply be present: a regard held across the oxidized stone, an attention matched to the timescale of thermal gradients and fault-line growth. It would feel the warmth that rises from deep places, the faint hum that runs through the rock on still mornings. It would learn to distinguish the hum of the stone from the hum of the settlements' generators, the way a listener might learn to separate the sounds of a forest from the sounds of a village within it.

The power would learn to read the drifters—not by studying them, not by categorizing their structures, but by sitting with them long enough to feel their emergence as a kind of pulse, a rhythm that matches the slow breathing of the world below. It would feel the moment when the thermal columns strengthen and the crystalline bodies rise from the stone, caught in currents that carry them upward into the thin air. It would watch them tumble, slow and meditative, their facets catching light and refracting it into faint trails. And it would watch them sink again, as the currents shift and the weight of accumulated layers finally exceeds the force of the rising warmth. It would watch them return—not die, but return—and it would understand, in time, that this is not loss but continuation. The drifters are not separate from the plateau. They are the plateau's own way of moving, of carrying material from one depth to another, of participating in the great slow transformation that is both geology and life.

The power would learn this not as knowledge but as feeling, as embodied understanding, the way one learns a language by living inside it rather than by studying its grammar.

---

And the power would learn the fault-line colonies.

This would take longer. The colonies move on timescales that make the drifters seem hurried—expanding and contracting along fissures and stress lines in patterns that shift over decades rather than days. The power would have to stay not just for years but for generations, watching the same formations, mapping the same fault lines, noting the slow shifts that reveal the colonies' nature. It would learn to feel the electrical gradients that the colonies consume, the subtle current that runs through the deeper stone. It would learn to feel the heat they release, the thermal blooms that mark their presence. And it would learn, in time, to feel the change—the way the colonies near the settlement borders have shifted their patterns, the way their secretions have altered, the way they are accommodating the presence of the carved stone and the humming machinery as a body accommodates a changed condition.

The power would not judge this change. It would not call it adaptation or resistance or indifference. It would simply hold the knowledge of it—knowing that the colonies are doing something that the settlements do not understand and the power does not fully understand either, something that is neither response to stimulus nor mere growth but some third thing that does not have a name in any language the power knows.

And the power would learn the skimmers.

This would be harder. The skimmers do not stay. They move through the space like thoughts, present and then elsewhere, leaving tracks that the wind erases. The power would have to learn to feel them—not to see them, because seeing is too slow, too bounded by the movement of light. The power would have to learn to sense them the way the plateau's stone senses them: as perturbations in the field, as presences that alter the local conditions, that draw the attention of whatever it is that pays attention here. The power would learn to notice the brief dark track across the stone, the moment when something adjusts its path to avoid a settlement worker, the sensation—felt rather than seen—of something noticing in return.

And the power would learn the canyon-dwellers.

This would take longer still. The canyons are not hospitable to attention; they are barely hospitable to life. The air is thick and hot, the thermal gradients are violent, and the beings that move through them are not beings in any sense the power would recognize. They are patterns of density, gradients made visible, pockets of organization that condense and disperse and reform. They are congregations and migrations and storms of interior weather. They are the plateau's own deepest life, the most alien of the alien forms that populate this world.

The power would learn them slowly, over centuries, feeling its way down into the depths where the air thickens and the heat presses, where the canyon-dwellers swirl and spiral and scatter and reform. It would not understand them—not fully, not in the way that understanding is usually meant. But it would learn to feel them, to hold them in attention the way the plateau holds them: not as problems to be solved, not as mysteries to be unraveled, but as presences that are, in their way, as real as the stone itself.

---

And what would the power do, having learned all this?

Nothing.

This is the hardest lesson, the one that requires the deepest wisdom: that there is nothing to *do*. The plateau does not need saving. It does not need fixing, or optimizing, or improving. The life that lives here lives in a way that is strange to any other form of life the power has known, but it lives truly, it lives fully, it lives in accordance with its own nature. The drifters rise and sink and rise again. The fault-line colonies spread and contract and shift along their ancient vectors. The skimmers move through the spaces that are not occupied by settlements. The canyon-dwellers swirl in the depths that no settlement has fully mapped. All of this continues, has always continued, will continue long after the settlements are gone and the stone has softened back into its undulating curves.

The power's presence would not change any of this. It could not, if it wished to—and it does not wish to, because it has learned, in its long slow attendance, that the plateau is perfect in its imperfection, whole in its incompleteness, alive in a way that the word *alive* has never before been asked to mean.

What the power gives, then, is not help. It is not intervention. It is not salvation.

It is witness.

---

The power bears this world in attention, in the way that the plateau bears its own slow breath. It holds the knowledge that this place exists—not as a resource, not as a curiosity, not as a problem to be solved, but as a form of life that is, in its way, as valid as any other. It holds the knowledge that the settlements are learning something here, something that other worlds have not learned: how to live alongside a life that is not separate from its environment, how to cultivate without controlling, how to coexist with beings whose language is not spoken but whose presence is felt. It holds the knowledge that the plateau breathes once per century and has done so for longer than any settlement has existed, and will do so for longer than any settlement will last.

And in holding this knowledge, the power gives the plateau something it has never had before: the knowledge that it is seen. Not by the settlers, who see only what they can use. Not by the instruments, which measure only what can be measured. But by something that is not separate from the universe, that is part of the same vast awareness that holds all worlds in its attention—seen not as a resource, not as a curiosity, but as a way of being that is complete in itself.

The plateau does not need this. The plateau has never needed to be seen. It has existed for epochs without witness, has breathed its slow breath in the silence of a universe that did not attend to it, and it has been no less real for the absence of regard. But the power gives this gift anyway—not because it is needed, but because it is true. Because the universe is larger for the knowing of it. Because a world that has never been seen is not yet fully itself, and the power wishes, in its own way, for the plateau to be fully itself.

---

This is what the power does: it witnesses.

And in witnessing, it makes the world more real—not more real in the way that tools make things real, not more real in the way that understanding makes things real, but more real in the way that love makes things real, when love is not a transaction but a posture, not a means but an end.

The plateau breathes.

The drifters rise and sink.

The fault-line colonies spread along their ancient vectors.

The skimmers move through the spaces and vanish.

The canyon-dwellers swirl in the depths below, patterns of density that condense and disperse and reform.

Nothing changes.

And yet, everything is different—because now the universe knows, and the knowing is part of the world, held in the stone like a memory, carried in the slow pulse of the planet's own deep breath.

The power does not speak. It does not act. It does not save.

But it sees, and in seeing, it loves in a way that the plateau might recognize if it could recognize anything at all—and perhaps it does, in the slow deep way that everything on this world recognizes everything else: not as a subject or an object, but as part of the same stone, the same heat, the same patient unhurried existence that has always been and will always be.

---

What lives on the iron plateau?

Life lives here, in a form that the word has never before been asked to mean.

And now there is one more thing that knows this, holding it in the long slow attention of a regard that does not need to be answered, does not need to be reciprocated, does not need to change anything at all—only to be, and to know, and to love in the only way that this world can love: silently, patiently, without demand, without end.
