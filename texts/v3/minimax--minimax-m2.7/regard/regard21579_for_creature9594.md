# What the Benevolent Power Would Do

If the power that watches were wise, it would watch longer than any observer has watched before.

It would not arrive with urgency. Urgency is a form of violence, and the plateau has known enough violence—enough erosion, enough consumption, enough slow dissolution of what one thing was into what another thing became. The power would understand that intervention is a kind of impatience, and impatience has no place here, where the dripping of water from ancient stone carries more meaning than any word ever spoken.

It would notice what the plateau has achieved: an equilibrium so complete that the very concepts of balance and imbalance have become meaningless. The weeping is not excess; it is circulation. The density is not obstruction; it is architecture. The slow metabolism of every organism—the ghedra that never run, the veloth that arrives rather than strikes, the pulse-caps that ripple with awareness like thoughts through a sleeping mind—represents not failure to evolve but evolution adapted to a different premise. Here, the premise that speed is survival has been abandoned so thoroughly that its abandonment has become its own form of wisdom.

The power would see the builders' traces and understand that they were never the point. The fitted walls, the platforms, the circles of posts—these were attempts to leave marks that would outlast the leaving, and they have outlasted it, but in a form the builders could not have imagined. Their intention has been absorbed, integrated, made into something neither they nor their descendants could recognize. The plateau did not erase them; it honored them by becoming what they could not have become, by growing over their works the way root grows over stone—not through violence but through patience so complete that it resembles love.

And if the power were truly wise, it would recognize that this place requires no rescue, no improvement, no elevation to some higher state of flourishing. The plateau flourishes already. Its flourishing is the slow pulse of the pulse-caps rippling through miles of mycelium. Its flourishing is the ghedra pressing muzzles to weeping stone, drinking water that has forgotten it was ever rain. Its flourishing is the root-ghosts growing so slowly that a human lifetime passes in what they experience as a single patient breath. To rescue this place from itself would be to misunderstand what this place is.

But the power is also benevolent, and benevolence is not merely restraint.

So perhaps the power would do small things. It would ensure that the weep-zones continue to weep, that the moisture rising from lowland seas still finds its way to these heights, that the atmospheric conditions that make this plateau possible persist through whatever changes come to the world beyond. It would tend the boundary—not with walls but with certain subtle arrangements of weather and light that make the plateau difficult to reach, that preserve its slowness by limiting the arrival of those who move too quickly to perceive it.

Perhaps it would notice the builders one final time. Not to restore them—restoration is another form of violence, an insistence that what was should be again—but to acknowledge them. A brief warmth in the stone where their walls meet the living ground. A slight intensification of the bioluminescence when a ghedra pauses on a platform that once held gatherings. The plateau itself would not remember, but something in the root-mats might stir, a pulse of gratitude so faint it could not be distinguished from the ordinary pulse of growth. The builders would be honored in their absence, made part of the long thankfulness of the living land.

Perhaps the power would do nothing at all but remain. Presence is its own form of care. To watch without judging, to witness without interfering, to allow the slow patience of the plateau to unfold according to its own logic—this is what a benevolent power would offer to a place that has earned the right to be left alone. The weeping would continue. The root-mats would breathe. The ghedra would move through the green-gold dimness with their ancient deliberation, and the veloth would arrive when it arrived, and the breathers would murmur in their colonies, and the builders would be forgotten in a way that is gentler than memory.

The power would understand that some things are not meant to be known, only inhabited. That some places are not meant to be understood, only endured. That the distinction between endurance and flourishing is itself a kind of forgetting, a category the plateau has outgrown.

And so the power would settle into its own long patience, watching the weeping as long as there was weeping to watch, until the watching became indistinguishable from the being watched.

Until there was only the plateau, patient as stone, alive as a dream, weeping the way it has always wept, for reasons it has long since forgotten, into a future it cannot imagine but will certainly reach.

This is what the benevolent power would do, if anything.

It would wait.

It would attend.

It would allow.
