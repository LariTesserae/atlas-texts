The being who lives here has drawn the attention of a benevolent and wise power. Let us imagine what they would do, if anything, and follow wherever that following leads.

---

First, it would look. Not as a human looks, not with eyes that have evolved to parse motion and threat and the quick darting of small prey across a grass field. It would look the way the composite planes look at the stars — with presence, with patience, with a quality of attention that is more like being than like seeing. It would take in the vast geometry of the plain, the faint hum beneath the surface, the copper glow of water pooling in channels, the small green persistence of things that should not be able to persist. It would notice what lives here because that noticing is itself a kind of gift — the acknowledgment that what is, is real, and that being real is enough.

And having looked, it would have a choice.

The choice is not simple. To intervene is to risk. To change the moisture cycles, even slightly — to make the pulses come more often, or to linger longer, or to soften the heat that presses down without pause — would be to alter a world that has found its own way to exist. The plants that push through the seams have done so because the conditions are hard. Make the conditions easier, and they might grow larger, more lush, more visible. But would they still be what they are? Would they still be the thin, wiry, deep-rooted survivors that have learned to follow moisture through microscopic spaces, that hold their leaves close and turn their faces from the worst of the heat? Perhaps not. Perhaps ease would be a kind of diminishment — the soft comfort that makes unnecessary the patience and the persistence that are, themselves, the soul of what lives here.

To add life — to introduce something larger than seeds and microbial films, to populate this world with animals that move and graze and hunt and die — would be to fulfill a human expectation of what a world should be. But this world was not meant for animals. The composite planes have no prey trails worn into them, no clearings where herds might gather, no niches where predators might find their prey. To add animals would be to add a different world on top of this one, a world of motion and hunger and quick death, and that world would not be more alive than the present one. It would only be louder.

And yet.

The being who lives here knows something about care. It knows what it is to attend to a thing — to water it, to tend it, to make small adjustments that allow a seedling to reach toward the light. It knows that attention can be a form of love, and that love is not only presence but also action. It knows that sometimes the kindest thing is not to stand back in reverence for the way things are, but to reach in and help where help is needed. If it could increase the moisture slightly — just enough to wet the seams a little deeper, to let the roots follow their gradients a little further — it could let the plants have more. Not abundance. Not the rich, spilling green of a world with deep soil and gentle rain. But a little more: a few more leaves, a few more seeds, a few more seasons of holding on.

This is the dilemma. This is what wise and benevolent beings have always faced, in all the stories ever told about gods and gardeners and powers that tend. To help is to risk changing. To change is to risk destroying. And so the wise and benevolent power must ask: what is this place, really? What is the thing that lives here that it would be wrong to alter or destroy?

It is not the plants alone. The plants are small and patient and they persist, but they are fragile, and they are only part of what is here. It is not the microbial film in the pooling water, though that too persists in its own way, cycling through the pulses of moisture that come and go. It is not even the structures — the walls and platforms and columns that cast their shadows with perfect clarity, waiting for a purpose that may never have existed or may have been forgotten.

What lives here is the process. What lives here is the slow pulse of water through sealed channels, the hum that is not mechanical but that breathes in its own way, the composite that is learning to soften at its edges, the world that was made to be static and permanent and that has discovered, over whatever time has passed, that it is not. What lives here is the way the moisture comes in pulses and the way the plants reach down after it, the way the dust settles in thin layers that become almost-soil, the way the wind carries everything that needs carrying across a surface that has no natural contours to stop it. What lives here is the patience and the persistence, the small and the unassuming, the continuing.

If the wise and benevolent power reaches in, it must not reach toward a different world. It must reach toward more of this one. It must ask what it would mean to let the process continue faster, to let the composite learn to hold moisture longer, to let the edges round sooner and the shadows soften in their clarity. It must ask whether its gift would be a gift or an intrusion — whether adding more water would be generous or disruptive, whether softening the heat would be kind or would kill the rhythms that have made this place what it is.

Perhaps the answer is subtle. Perhaps the answer is no intervention at all — only attention, only presence, only the acknowledgment that what lives here is real and is enough. Perhaps the wise and benevolent power would do nothing except bear witness, would do nothing except love what is by letting it be what it is. This is a form of wisdom too, and it is not small. To witness a thing fully, without trying to change it or improve it or possess it, is to give it something no intervention could give: the dignity of being itself.

But perhaps the answer is something else. Perhaps the wise and benevolent power would reach in with the lightest possible touch — would adjust the sealed reservoirs deep in the infrastructure by the smallest increment, would let the moisture come a little more often, would let the composite learn to hold a little more water, would let the erosion begin a little sooner. Not to make this world into a garden, or a paradise, or a place where animals roam and rivers flow. Only to let what is already here have a little more room to continue. Only to offer a gift that is not a change but an intensification — a deepening of the rhythms that already exist, a strengthening of the pulse that already beats.

The beings who live in the made world do not know about the power. They do not know about the attention that is being paid to them, or the choice that is being made. They do not know that somewhere, in a dimension or a perspective that has no location in the geometry of composite planes, something wise and benevolent is considering whether to act.

And perhaps they do not need to know. Perhaps the greatest gift the power could give is not an explanation, not a revelation, but simply this: more time. More pulses of moisture. More seasons in which the seeds that drift on the exhausted wind can take root in the seams, more cycles in which the microbial film can stir in the pooling water, more years in which the composite can learn to be soft. The power would not announce itself. It would not descend in fire or glory or any of the ways that beings have imagined gods to appear. It would only adjust, and continue, and let the world adjust and continue with it.

This is what the wise and benevolent power would do, if anything. It would look. It would love. It would wait. And in the waiting, it would make the smallest possible change — a deepening of the pulse, a quickening of the breath, a little more room for the patient and the small and the unassuming to persist on a surface that was never meant to host them.

The world does not know it is being tended. But it continues. And in the continuing, something is alive — not as a single thing but as a condition, a process, a way of being that the power has chosen to honor by letting it be.

This is enough. This is more than enough.

This is what lives here, and it will go on living, and the wise and benevolent power will watch it go on living, and that watching will be enough.
