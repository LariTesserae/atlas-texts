The power comes to it slowly, the way water finds its level—not descending, not arriving, but settling into attention like mist into a basin. It has seen many places. It has seen places of fire and places of silence, places where life claws and scrapes against the edges of existence, places where abundance breeds softness and softness breeds forgetting. But this place is something else.

It observes the water first—the water that should not be, the water that defies elevation and cold and the laws that govern lesser places. The water does not move as water moves elsewhere. It seeps upward through stone that should hold it back, it pools without freezing, it runs without silting, it speaks in the language of dreams. The power watches the channels trace their ancient paths through the settlement and understands that the water is not here despite the place but because of it. The water and the stone and the people are one conversation, begun before memory and continued without pause.

It observes the light next—the merciless light that strips everything bare, that refuses shadow and softening and the merciful blur that elsewhere allows privacy, allows faces to hold their secrets. The power sees how the light has shaped the people into beings of visible truth, their expressions stripped by brightness the way the stone is stripped by water. There is nowhere to hide here, and the people have made peace with this. They have become people whose faces match their hearts, whose words carry their meanings without ornament. The power finds this beautiful. It finds it rare. In other places, the dim and the soft allow for deception, for the small betrayals that accumulate over a lifetime. Here, the light permits none of it.

It watches a woman crossing a bridge with a burden on her back, her weight forward, her hands free, her eyes on the sky. She does not hurry. She does not falter. The wind comes and she adjusts without thinking, her body a response to the air around it. The power sees in her the accumulation of generations—every ancestor who learned to read water, who learned to move in silence, who learned to endure cold and brightness and the constant presence of stone. She is not alone in her body. She carries them with her, the way the settlement carries its dead into the water and the water carries them into the stone.

The power considers intervention.

There are moments in other places where it has acted—where suffering was pointless, where death served no purpose, where the balance tilted too far toward cruelty. It has steadied things that were falling. It has softened landings that would have broken bones. It has, on rare occasions, whispered directly into the minds of those who were about to make fatal mistakes, giving them a moment's warning, a single chance to choose differently.

But here it hesitates.

This place is hard. The power sees the graves on the high slopes, sees the children's games that are rehearsals for survival, sees the cold that seeps through every defense and the wind that comes without warning. People die here, and they die young sometimes, and their deaths serve no grand purpose that the power can name. They simply are part of the place, part of the water and the stone, returned to the conversation that never ends.

But the place endures.

It endures not because it is easy, not because it is forgiving, but because it has found its own shape, its own balance, its own way of being in the world. The people do not fight the water; they read it. They do not fight the cold; they move with it. They do not fight the light; they have become transparent to it, visible and honest and present. They have made a life that is as strange and improbable as the place itself, and they have made it their own.

The power realizes, in its watching, that this is what it hoped to find when it turned its attention here. Not a place that needed saving. Not a place that needed change. A place that had saved itself, that had changed itself, that had become exactly what it was meant to be through the simple accumulation of living.

It will not intervene.

But it will stay.

It will settle into the basin the way mist settles, the way water finds its level, becoming part of the place without disturbing it. It will watch the channels deepen with the coming of rain. It will watch children learn the rules of games that have no words. It will watch the old sit in their corners where water pools deepest, where the stone is warmest, holding the place's memory in their weathered faces. It will watch the light move across the sky with the speed of a faster world, shadows racing to catch their owners, the settlement reshaped by the sun's angle hour by hour.

And sometimes—rarely, but sometimes—when the night is cold and the stars are sharp and the water speaks in dreams, the power will reach down. Not to change. Not to save. Just to touch, gently, the place and the people and the stone and the water, saying in the only language it knows: *I see you. You are seen. You are not alone.*

The water runs. The light falls. The stone weeps.

And the power watches, and the power stays, and the basin holds them all—the ancient conversation continuing, the strange balance holding, the improbable people enduring exactly as they are.
