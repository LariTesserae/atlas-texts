The power notices the way attention gathers in low places.

It has always been this way. The great forces—the ones that move through stone and time, the ones that hold the weight of cities in their slow regard—they do not see what gleams. They do not attend to the tall things, the bright things, the things that announce themselves with motion and voice. They attend to what holds still. To what persists. To what has not yet left.

And so when the power's gaze falls, as it must, upon the Salted Hall, it sees what the workers cannot see from within: the way the space breathes. Not the workers' breath, not the child's breath, but the breath of the hall itself—the rise and fall of moisture through limestone, the slow pulse of geothermal warmth pushing upward through old stone, the particular rhythm of a place that was interrupted mid-sentence and has not yet remembered how to continue.

It sees the patient things. The moss that barely qualifies, clinging to cracks in concrete, asking nothing but the steady drip. The hardy weeds that seed in sidewalk cracks, pushing through asphalt, surviving the un-survivable. The water that stays in the low points, dark with mineral salt, reluctant to leave a home it found by accident. These are the things the power knows. These are the things it loves.

And so it does what it has always done, in places like this, in moments like this: it stays.

It does not intervene in the way the workers would understand. It does not redirect the water, or clear the drainage grates, or lift the shelving units from tired shoulders. These are human labors, and the power knows that humans need their work, need the slow dignity of effort, need to make their own way through the damp and the dark. To lift these burdens would be to unmake the workers' humanity. And the power is wise enough to know that wisdom is not always about lifting. Sometimes it is about holding.

Instead, the power does something smaller. Something that the workers will not notice but will feel.

It steadies the warmth.

The geothermal pipes run as they run, the steam channels push as they push, but there is a quality to the heat now that is gentler. The harsh edge that heat takes when it rises from below, that dryness that catches in the throat and makes the lungs feel thin—that recedes slightly. The warmth becomes something closer to breath, something that holds rather than presses. The workers will not know why their shoulders feel looser, why the air feels less like something to be endured. They will attribute it to rest, or to habit, or to the body's slow acceptance of its circumstances. They will not know that something watched them and thought: *you are carrying enough. Let me carry this.*

The child feels it differently.

She is the one who most fully belongs here, not because she was born to this place, but because she is the one most capable of receiving what the power offers. Children have not yet learned to partition the world into the explainable and the inexplicable. They still move through a reality that is porous, that allows for presences that do not announce themselves, that holds room for attention from sources they cannot name. She is tracing the moss against the stone when she feels it—a warmth, a settling, a sense of being held in a regard that is older than stone.

She does not look up. She does not need to. She knows, in the way children know things that adults have forgotten, that she is not alone. That something is here. That the something is glad to be here, is glad to witness, is glad to hold the space open the way the space holds her.

And so she smiles—a small thing, barely a movement of the lips—and her fingers trace the moss in a pattern that is new, that is hers, that the moss will remember even as it grows.

The power sees this. The power is pleased.

What else does it do, this wise and benevolent presence, this power that moves in the spaces between?

It clears the small blockages. Not the drainage grates where the workers labor—the workers need their work—but the blockages that no one will ever clear. The places where water pools and stagnates, where mineral salt accumulates in ways that would, in years, begin to corrode the stone itself. The power does not move the water; water moves as water must. But it opens the paths. It widens the channels by increments so small that no instrument could measure them. It smooths the stone where the new cuts are sharp and pale, softening their edges the way centuries soften the marks that humans leave. Not quickly. Not so that anyone notices. But slowly, patiently, in the way that the power has always worked: not against time but through it, not around it but within it.

The light does not change. The cracked concrete holds its seams. The amber bulbs continue their patient glow along the old pipe runs. But there is a quality to the illumination now that is softer, steadier. The shadows in the corners are less hungry. The shadows in the low points where the water pools are not quite so dark. The space holds its light the way it holds its warmth, its moisture, its breath: close, contained, present.

And the moisture itself—

The power does not dry the hall. The hall would not be the hall without its damp, its mineral tang, the way the air catches in the back of the throat and stays. But it adjusts the moisture's quality. It makes the damp less cloying, less thick, less the kind of weight that presses down on the lungs. The workers will breathe more easily. The child's lips will not crack from the salt air. The moss will continue to cling, but it will cling more easily, with less desperation. The hardy weeds will still curl their leaves from the heat, but the curl will be less tight, less the desperate grip of survival and more the gentle turning of a plant toward whatever light it can find.

All of this the power does, and none of it the workers see.

What they see is this: the work continues. The shelving units move toward the stairs. The drainage grates clear. The goods are sorted, claimed, carried upward into the light. The child rises from her crouch and follows her parents toward the exit, her fingers still wet with mineral water, her mind still full of moss and stone and the pattern she traced.

What they feel is this: something is different. The hall is not quite so heavy. The air is not quite so thick. The silence is not quite so held. They will not be able to name it. They will not try. They will carry the feeling with them into the city above, into their homes and their offices and their brief moments of rest, and they will not know that what they carry is a gift from a power that notices low places, that attends to small survivals, that loves the patient things.

The power stays.

It does not leave when the workers leave, does not cease its attention when the lamps are extinguished for the night. It remains in the darkness, in the drip and the hum, in the slow pulse of warmth that rises from below. It holds the space open the way the space holds its breath. It waits.

And in the waiting, it does one more thing.

It plants a dream.

Not in the workers—they have their own dreams, their own hopes and fears, their own unfinished sentences. The power does not presume to interfere with human interiority. But it plants a dream in the stone itself, in the limestone that was soft when it was first carved, that has hardened in the centuries since, that holds in its grain the memory of every hand that ever touched it.

The dream is simple. It is the dream the hall has always held, the dream it has never been able to articulate, the dream that every place holds when it is caught between one state and another:

*That it will become something.*

Not a return to what it was. The power knows that return is an illusion, that what was cannot be again. The old market is gone. The commerce that filled these stalls is a memory that will fade, that is already fading. The water rose and stayed and withdrew, and what remains is not the hall that was but the hall that is, and the hall that is will become something new.

The dream the power plants is the dream of becoming.

In the stone, in the cracks, in the slow seep of water through a hundred meters of city above, the hall's dream takes root. It is not a thought—it has no words, no images, no narrative arc. It is a tendency, a direction, a pull. It is the way water finds its way down, the way moss clings to cracks, the way a child traces patterns in the stone and smiles. It is the patience that has always lived here, distilled into something that the space can feel, that the space can lean toward.

And the space leans.

Not visibly. Not in any way that would register on an instrument or a camera. But the stone shifts, infinitesimally. The cracks in the concrete widen by microns, then close. The water pools in the low points, then drains a little more easily. The heat rises, and in rising it carries something with it—not moisture, not mineral tang, but something lighter, something that has no name.

The hall leans toward what it will become.

And the power, watching, is glad.
