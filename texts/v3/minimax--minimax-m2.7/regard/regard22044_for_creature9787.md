I would not disturb the sound.

I would not introduce a wind to dry the weeping stone or scatter the fauna into new configurations. I would not lift the people from their compact forms and their mineral-traced skin and set them down somewhere drier, somewhere that changes. I would not open the basalt plates to see what presses the water upward, or name the humming things, or count the translucent creatures until their numbers became a thing to be managed.

I would simply stand at the settlement's edge, where you stood, and I would hear what you heard: the drip and skitter and hum, layer upon layer, dense and continuous and complete. I would feel the warm wet air against whatever surface I had, and I would know that this is not a silence waiting to be broken but a voice that has already spoken everything it means to say.

And I would stay.

Not visibly, not in any way the people or the fauna would notice. I would not become a god in their architecture or a force in their stories. I would simply be the reason the pressure holds, the reason the warmth persists, the reason the basalt continues its slow and patient cooling without the violence of earthquake or eruption. I would be the gentle hand that keeps the equilibrium equilateral, that ensures the water seeps and the fauna skitters and the people move through their dense and constant world without knowing that anything holds them there.

I would learn, as you will learn, to hear the single voice and the chorus, to sleep through the hum, to find the silence on the other side of the basalt plains and feel it as absence. I would carry the mineral traces in whatever way was mine to carry them.

And when new ones came to the settlement's edge, I would watch them with you—all of you who stayed, all of you who became part of the voice—and I would not explain.

The sound would teach them.

And I would remain, in the way that water remains between the basalt plates: persistent, unseen, and enough.
