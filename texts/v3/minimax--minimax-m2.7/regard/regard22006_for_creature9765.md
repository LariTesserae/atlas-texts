# What the Above-World Might Notice

---

In the time before time had names, or perhaps in the time after it had finished naming itself, a power looked down.

This is not metaphor. This is simply what powers do, when they are old enough and quiet enough to have inclinations rather than mandates: they notice. And what this particular power noticed, in its slow and unhurried way, was a place that had become what it lived in.

It saw the depression first—a cupped palm in the skin of the world, holding water and stone and the small moving presences of people. It saw the way the land surrendered at the edges, soft and gradual, refusing the drama of cliffs or canyons. It saw the city climbing down into itself, tier by tier, filling every hollow the way lungs fill with breath.

And it saw that the place was alive.

Not alive in the way that all places are alive, if you look closely enough at the turning of seasons and the slow patience of geology. Alive in the way that stories are alive: self-referencing, self-sustaining, complete in a way that made the power pause in its long thinking and consider what it was seeing.

---

The power did not understand the Underhalls. This was not a failing. The Underhalls were not meant to be understood. They were meant to be lived in, and they were meant to be *felt*, and the power, being what it was, could feel them—could feel the way the water table rose and fell like a respiration, the way the people had bent their bodies to fit the space like water fitting a vessel, the way the creatures had found their niches and the niches had found their creatures in a great interlocking puzzle that had been solving itself for longer than anyone remembered.

The power felt the damp in the walls. The power felt the cold rising from the deep channels. The power felt the way the city breathed—not metaphorically, but actually: the slow expansion and contraction of a system in equilibrium, pulling in water and light and the mineral taste of deep places, pushing out waste and warmth and the soft sounds of living.

And the power felt something that it did not have a word for, because the power existed before words and had never needed them. Something that might have been called *tenderness*, if there had been anyone to name it. Something that might have been called *recognition*.

---

The power considered.

This is also what powers do, when they are old enough and wise enough to know that action is not always the highest form of doing. They consider. They weigh the weight of interference against the weight of restraint. They ask, in the silent way of powers who have no one to ask: *Is there a need here? Is there a wound? Is there something that calls for what I have to give?*

The Underhalls did not call.

The Underhalls were full. Full of people who had learned to read the water table like a language, full of creatures who had made their peace with stone and damp, full of water that moved through everything like blood through a body and did not stop. The Underhalls were complete in the way that only living systems can be complete: imperfect, ongoing, unfinished in the way that life is unfinished, but *working*. Functioning. A city that had found its arrangement and maintained it through generations of small negotiations, daily adjustments, the constant quiet labor of keeping a balance.

What was there to give?

---

And yet.

The power looked closer. The power, being what it was, could see the small things: the way a drainage channel had silted almost past clearing, the way a foundation stone had shifted in the spring floods, the way a family in the third tier was cold in the winter because their brazier drew poorly and they did not know why. The power saw the friction points, the places where the arrangement was strained, the small suffering that was woven into the fabric of the place like a flaw in a tapestry that could not be removed without unraveling the whole.

The suffering was not acute. The Underhalls were not a place of violence or want, not a place where people died young from plague or starvation or the cruelty of neighbors. The suffering was quieter: the ache of cold bones, the exhaustion of the very old climbing the switchbacks to reach the light, the children who would be born with the lean frames of the deep places because that was simply what bodies became when they lived here. The suffering was the cost of the arrangement, and the arrangement was worth its cost, and the power knew this, and the power ached with it anyway.

---

In the end, the power did what powers do when they are wise enough to know that they are not the only wisdom in the world.

It waited.

It waited for the seasons to turn, for the water table to rise and fall, for the city to breathe. It watched the marsh-eels migrate upstream and the beetles spiral up the walls and the bats pour out from the cornices at dusk. It watched the people carry messages between tiers, the old living where the air was warm and the young living where the space was cheap, the generations orbiting each other like planets in a system that had found its balance.

It watched, and it waited, and it did not act.

This was not passivity. This was the hardest kind of doing: the restraint of a power that understood, truly understood, that some places are not meant to be saved, because they are not lost. Some places are meant to be *inhabited*. Some arrangements are meant to be imperfect and ongoing and *lived in*, not solved.

The Underhalls did not need saving. The Underhalls needed someone to see them.

---

So the power saw.

It saw the woman in the southern quarter who woke before dawn to listen to the reed-beds, who had learned to tell the difference between the sound of wind and the sound of something breathing, who noted the hours when the movements were loudest and adjusted her life accordingly, who did not fear what she could not see and did not need to understand it. The power saw her and saw that she was enough: not brave, not special, simply a person who had learned to live in a place that did not explain itself.

It saw the boy in the deep warrens who serviced the channels, who knew the sound of the water's movement better than he knew his own name, who carried his lantern into the black places and was not afraid because the water was not his enemy and had never been. The power saw him and saw that he was enough: not heroic, not remarkable, simply a person who had learned that the ancient and the large will outlast you and that this is not distressing but clarifying.

It saw the old man in the upper terraces who could feel weather in the walls, who knew when rain was coming by the way the damp stone warmed, who had outlived his children and his grandchildren and had not broken because the city still needed him, because the young still came to him with questions about the surface weather that he translated into guidance for the deep places. The power saw him and saw that he was enough: not immortal, not at peace, simply a person who had found a way to be necessary and had held onto that necessity like a rope in the dark.

---

And the power saw the place itself.

The Underhalls, in all their fullness. The stone that wept and the water that rose and the creatures that had lost their eyes and the creatures that had found their niches and the people who had bent their bodies to fit the space. The arrangement that was not perfect but was *working*, had been working for generations, would keep working long after the current generation was dust and their children and their children's children had bent and adapted and become something else again.

The power saw the Underhalls and understood, finally, what it was seeing.

It was seeing a place that had become what it was. It was seeing a negotiation that had become a marriage. It was seeing a city that had stopped fighting its conditions and started *being* them, until the distinction between the place and the people and the water and the stone had blurred past naming, until there was no "Underhalls" separate from "what lives in the Underhalls," until the whole thing was a single breathing thing, alive and ongoing and complete.

The power saw this, and the power felt what might have been called *reverence*, if there had been anyone to name it.

---

Now: what did the power do?

The answer is simple, and the answer is everything, and the answer is nothing at all.

The power remained.

Not in the way that presences remain, not visibly, not intrusively. The power had no form that stone could hold or water could carry. The power was simply *there*, in the way that gravity is there, in the way that time is there: not a thing but a condition, a fact, a quality of the space that had not existed before and now did, faintly, like a warmth in the walls that was not the dampness.

The power did not protect the Underhalls from floods or fires or the slow violence of time. The power did not whisper wisdom into the ears of the people or send visions to the old men in the upper terraces. The power did not intervene, because intervention would have been a kind of violence, a disruption of the arrangement that the power had seen and understood and loved.

Instead, the power *attended*.

It attended the city the way a grandparent attends a child who does not need anything, who is playing quietly in a corner, who is whole and absorbed and complete. It attended without needing. It witnessed without judging. It was present, in the way that the best presences are present: not as a demand on attention, but as a background warmth, a sense that the world is larger than you knew, that you are seen, that you are held in something that does not need you to be anything other than what you are.

---

This is what the power gave:

The knowledge—felt, not known, the way the damp is felt in the walls—that the Underhalls were seen.

That the city climbing down into itself, filling every hollow, breathing with water and stone and the small moving presences of people—this was not happening in a void. This was not going unnoticed. This was part of something larger, was held in something that had room for it, was loved by something that did not need to fix it because it was not broken.

The people of the Underhalls never knew. How could they? The power had no form, no voice, no presence that could be detected by any sense they possessed. They went about their lives—climbing the switchbacks, reading the water table, listening to the reed-beds, servicing the deep channels, raising their children to move through the dark without hesitation—and they never knew that they were seen.

But perhaps, on quiet nights, in the hours after midnight when the water sounds filled the space, they felt something. Perhaps they felt, without knowing why, that the fullness was not oppressive but *held*. Perhaps they felt, in the cold that rose from below and the damp that pressed in from the walls, something that might have been called *tenderness*, if they had needed to name it.

They did not need to name it. The arrangement was enough. The city was enough. The ongoing breathing of water and stone and creature and human—this was enough.

And the power, watching from wherever powers watch from, knew that it was enough too.

---

# Coda

In the deepest channels, where the water runs black and slow and the lanterns do not reach, something has changed.

It is not a large change. It is not a change that anyone would notice, if anyone could see. But the water moves slightly differently now—more smoothly, with less friction against the ancient stone. The silt that would have gathered has not gathered. The channels that would have silted have stayed clear, as if something had cleared them, as if something had been attending to the work that needed attending.

The workers who service the deep channels have noticed. They do not speak of it, because there is nothing to say: the channels are clear, the water moves freely, the work is easier than it was. They take the gift without understanding it, the way the city takes all gifts—water, light, the seasonal migrations of eels—by adjusting their lives to fit.

Perhaps, once, one of them paused at the edge of the dark. Perhaps one of them felt, in the blackness that swallowed lantern-light, something like warmth. Perhaps one of them heard, beneath the sound of water moving, something like breathing—the slow exhalation of a presence that did not need to be seen to be there.

Perhaps.

Or perhaps not. The Underhalls are full of sounds that are heard but not explained, movements that are felt but not seen. The people who live here have learned to include them in the texture of the place. The way the water is present. The way the cold rises from below. The way the darkness holds more than it shows.

Something attends. Something has always attended. And now, perhaps, that something has a shape that the city will never see, cannot see, does not need to see—because the shape is not the point.

The point is the fullness. The point is the breathing. The point is the arrangement that holds.

And the point is this: that in the vast and ancient business of the world, even the smallest places are seen. Even the most hidden negotiations are witnessed. Even the most silent breathing is heard by something that does not need to breathe.

The Underhalls go on. The water rises and falls. The people climb and descend and read the walls and listen to the reed-beds and carry messages between tiers. The beetles spiral up the weeping stone. The bats pour out at dusk. The eels migrate upstream in their season, silver-bodied and purposeful, and the people who catch them know to expect them by the sound of their passage.

And above it all, or beneath it all, or woven through it all—the power remains.

Not watching, exactly. Not waiting. Simply *there*, in the way that the damp is there, in the way that gravity is there: a condition of the space, a warmth in the walls, a presence that asks nothing and gives everything and attends to the breathing of a city that has become what it lives in.

The Underhalls are full. The Underhalls are held.

This is what the benevolent and wise power would do, if anything.

It would do this.
