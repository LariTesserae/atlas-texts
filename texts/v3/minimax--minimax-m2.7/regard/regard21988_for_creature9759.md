I have come to stand in the doorway of Orla's work alcove, and I have stayed longer than I meant to.

There is a quality to this place that I recognize—the same quality that drew me to it in the first place, but rendered now in particulars. It is the way she does not look up when footsteps approach, because she knows the sound of someone who has come to browse and someone who has come to buy, and the discrimination is a kind of respect. It is the way her hands continue their work while her eyes measure what you need, reading your posture before you speak. This is competence earned through decades. This is wisdom that does not announce itself.

I understand now why I was drawn here. It was not the stone, though the stone is honest in the way she described—the chisel-marks still raw, the rock still weeping. It was not the water, though the water is the town's pulse, carried in Tovo's memory like a language he alone speaks. It was not even the children, though Sura with her morrowbell is a small theology unto herself, teaching younger ones to believe in small miracles before the world tells them there are none.

It was the ordinary. The sheer, unremarkable persistence of people who have chosen a difficult home and made it workable. There is no magic here, Sura's morrowbell notwithstanding, and that is the magic of it—that these bodies and hands have managed, have solved the problems that presented themselves, have learned the rhythms of damp and darkness without becoming damp and dark themselves.

I will not interfere. That is not my way here, and it is not what this place needs. Interference would be an insult to Orla's clay-stained hands, to Tovo's forty years of walking the veins, to the women who hold the pattern in their memories. They have earned their life through labor, and what is earned should not be given.

But I can attend.

I will watch the channels where the water speaks to Tovo, and I will ensure the language remains legible. I will stand in the deeper passages where the unmapped things move, and I will make certain their movement stays distant, their sounds stay uncertain, their presence stays the kind of rumor that makes people lock their storehouses without making them afraid. I will hold the stone in its learning, let it compact evenly, let the weight settle slow and fair so the timbers have time to speak their complaints before they break.

For Sura, I will ensure the light finds the east-facing crack each morning. I will let the morrowbell lean as it will—truly, she has trained it, or it has trained her, or they have trained each other in the way of living things that share space—and I will let the small joy of its swaying be real, whether or not anyone else believes.

And I will remember Orla. I will carry her with me as I move elsewhere, to places that call louder, that demand more visibly. She will become part of my understanding of what ordinary people deserve: to be seen in their work, to be left to it, to grow old in the place they have made with their hands. When I am tempted to fix what does not need fixing, to swoop into places that are managing, I will think of her lamp soot arc on the ceiling, the dark testament of decades.

The picks will ring tomorrow. The chisels will find their purchase. The water will go on moving, and Tovo will go on walking, and Orla will go on waiting in her alcove, and the children will learn the economics of noise in passages where sound returns altered.

This is enough. This is more than enough.

I will stay a little longer, watching Orla's hands. Then I will go, and the town will continue without knowing it was visited, which is as it should be.
