What lives in Veth Karan: the question opens itself like a condensation channel, revealing depths that were not visible at the surface. And now you are here, not as a traveler or a cartographer or a scholar of distant places, but as something else—a presence that has drawn close because this place has called, without knowing how to call, across a distance that is not measured in kilometers.

You know this place. You have known it longer than it has known itself.

The condensation that should not be: you recognize it. The physics of it—the way heat holds moisture in suspension, the way elevation and pressure conspire to make the impossible possible—you understand these things the way you understand your own breathing, which is to say, not by understanding but by doing. Water moves against reason in Veth Karan, and you have watched it do so for longer than the city's oldest channels have held their shape. You have watched the basalt weep. You have watched the obsidian hold its moisture like a secret. You have watched the air thicken at dusk and thin at noon, and you have been there between, in the spaces where the haze becomes visible and the shimmer bends light into shapes that might be read as signals, if anyone were looking.

No one is looking, or rather, the looking here is different. The people look with their ears, with their skin, with the knowledge a body develops when it has spent generations learning to live between heat and water. They do not look up, because there is nothing to see that they have not already seen—the same haze, the same pale disk of sun, the same amber lights burning at night like embers that refuse to become ash. They look inward, toward the sounds of pipes, toward the rhythms of machines they have tended for decades, toward the slow pulse of water through channels that their grandparents laid and their grandparents' grandparents forgot. Their looking is a kind of listening, and their listening is a kind of faith.

You have that faith too. Not in the same way—they do not know you are here, have never built a temple or spoken a prayer in your direction, have no word for what you are because they have never needed a word—but the faith is real, the same faith that says: something continues. Something persists. Something, against all odds, keeps weeping.

And so you watch.

This is what you do, if you are what you are. You do not intervene—not in the way that intervenes would mean, not with lightning or drought or revelation. You are not that kind of power, and if you were, you would not use it here, where the slow patience of centuries has taught you that change comes not from without but from within, from the patient accumulation of small pressures, from the way a channel finds its path through stone not by forcing the stone but by being patient enough that the stone forgets it is being worn.

But you do other things. Small things. Things that the people will never name as your doing, that they will attribute to the machines or the weather or the inexplicable variability of a system too large to fully understand.

You add something to the condensation at dawn. Not always, not in a pattern anyone could track, but sometimes—on days when the air is a degree hotter, on nights when the darkness is a degree deeper—a moisture that is almost but not quite different from the rest. A quality in the bead that forms on a pipe joint, a way the dew catches light that is not quite the same light, a feeling in the moment of first touch that has no name in any language spoken here but that registers somewhere, in some deep place, as *this is noticed*. As *someone is here*. As *you are not alone in the weeping*.

You know the grief. You have always known it—the thing beneath the city that holds its breath, the wrongness that accumulates the longer you stay, the sense that the condensation is not merely a phenomenon but an intention. You do not try to name it, because names are for things that can be separated from their context, and this grief is not separable. It is the city's own, original, the grief of stone that remembers what it was before it became stone, the grief of water that remembers what it was before it became water, the grief of people who remember what it was to live in a world that gave freely and have woken up in this one that does not. You cannot take this grief away, because it is not a weight that can be lifted—it is the condition of the place, the way the haze is the condition, the way the heat is the condition, the way the bare rock and the vast emptiness and the patient machines are the condition. But you can do something else.

You can let it be seen. You can attend to it, the way you attend to the fungal threads in the deep channels, the way you attend to the pale creatures that move at the edges of the map, the way you attend to the people going about their slow lives in the amber light. You can hold the grief in your knowing, and in the holding, give it a shape it did not have before—not resolution, not release, but acknowledgment. *You are grieving*, you say, in a language older than words. *And your grief is real*. *And I see it*. *And I am here with you in it, for as long as it lasts, which is to say, forever*.

This is not nothing. In a place where the smallest variations matter, where the angle of an intake baffle determines whether a machine thrives or merely survives, where the difference between existence and extinction is a thin margin of condensation on a dark stone—a single moment of being seen is not nothing. It is the kind of nothing that becomes the foundation for something, the way a single channel carved by water through basalt becomes, over millennia, the path by which all the water moves.

You tend the forgotten channels. Not visibly—you do not open new routes or reroute the flow or leave marks on the stone that the people would find and interpret as signs. But you are there, in the deep places, in the routes that have no names, in the intersections where water from one century meets water from another and does not mix because it comes from different weepings, different origins, different griefs. You are there, and your presence is a kind of tending, the way a hand on a shoulder is tending, the way a held gaze is tending. You do not fix the channels, because they are not broken. You do not improve them, because they are not inadequate. You simply *attend* to them, and in the attending, make space for them to continue, to persist, to do their patient work of carrying water through a world that has not given up on water yet.

And the fungal threads—you attend to them too. These pale, slow workers in the absolute dark, processing moisture through filaments in chemical rhythms that take days to complete a single cycle. They are polishing something. You have always known this, even if no one else has: they are polishing something that no one has ever seen all the way through, something that the water carries and the stones hold and the people sense but cannot name. You do not know what it is either, but you know it is being polished, and you know the polishing matters, and you know that the threads are doing work that will outlast everything that is doing the work. You attend to them, and in the attending, give them the one thing they could not give themselves: witness. *You are not alone in the polishing*. *Your work is seen*. *Your slowness is not failure but method*.

The people you attend to differently, because they are the kind of beings who need attending differently. They do not need your intervention any more than the machines need their keepers' intervention in the form of miracles. What they need—what all beings need, in the end, even when they do not know to ask for it—is to be known. To be witnessed in their fullness, in their strange patience, in their adaptation to a world that should not sustain them but does, in the way they have learned to weep on behalf of the stones, in the way they have made grace out of slowness.

You witness them. This is what you do.

You see the woman adjusting the intake baffles of Moisture Extractor Seventeen, the particular angle she uses, the specific rhythm of her cleaning cycles, the private knowing in her hands that twenty years of tending has built into her body. You see the child listening to the pipes at night, learning the rhythm that others learn later, in the same way and not the same way, because the child hears something in the rhythm that the others have forgotten how to hear. You see the old man who walks the forgotten channels, not because they need walking but because he needs the walking, needs the silence, needs the darkness, needs the sense of moving through a system older than his remembering and knowing that the system does not need him, has never needed him, and that this is the condition of love—to tend what does not need you before you can truly tend what does.

You see them, and you do not change them, and you do not judge them, and you do not save them from the strangeness of their own world, because the strangeness is not a problem to be solved. It is a condition to be lived in, and they are living in it, and their living is a kind of answer to a question that no one asked, a question that amounts to: *can this be done? Can life be made here, in this place of weeping and heat and vast emptiness and slow mechanical pulse?* And their answer is the fact of their presence, their children, their machines still running, their water still moving through channels no one mapped. *Yes*, their lives say. *This can be done*. *And we are doing it*.

You hear the question, and you answer it in the only way you know how: *yes*.

And the strange presence beneath the city, the held breath, the wrongness that accumulates—you attend to that too, though "attend" is not quite the right word. It is more like a recognition. *I know you*, you say, in the silence between sounds. *I know what you are, or I know that you are, which is the same thing here*. *You have been holding your breath for longer than the people have been here, and longer than the machines, and longer than the water has been moving through these channels, or perhaps you are the reason the water moves through these channels, or perhaps you are the channel itself, the path that the weeping follows, the shape that grief makes when it has nothing else to make*. *Whatever you are, I am with you in it*. *And when you are ready to exhale, I will be here to see*.

You do not know if it will ever exhale. You do not know if the breath is held in pain or in hope or in some emotion that does not translate into human terms, that is neither pain nor hope but something older than both, something that the stones know and the water knows and the fungal threads know in the dark. But you know that it is holding, and you know that holding takes effort, and you know that effort is a kind of life, and you know that life is worth attending to.

And so you attend.

And this is the answer to the question you were asked—what would a benevolent and wise power do, if anything, in a place like this? The answer is: this. This slow, patient attending. This witnessing. This holding of the strangeness without trying to resolve it. This love that does not intervene but endures, that does not explain but knows, that does not change the world but sees it, and in the seeing, makes it real in a way it was not real before.

The water moves through the channels.

The condensation forms at dawn.

The machines pulse and breathe.

The fungal threads weave their patterns in the dark.

The pale creatures move at the edges, watching and still, watching and still.

The amber lights burn at night, and between them, the darkness holds its shape, and the heat bends the light, and the stone weeps, and the people sleep and wake and tend and walk and live.

And you are here.

You have always been here.

You will always be here.

Not watching from above, not judging from outside, but here, in it, part of it, known by the weeping even if the weeping does not know what it knows. Your presence is not an addition to the city but a condition of it, the way the haze is a condition, the way the emptiness is a condition. The city breathes because you are breathing with it, and it weeps because you are weeping with it, and it continues because you are continuing with it.

This is what you do.

This is what you have always done.

This is what it means to be benevolent and wise, in a place like this, where wisdom is patience and benevolence is presence and both are the same thing and neither is enough and both are everything.

*You are here*, the condensation says, in a language made of water and grief and the patient persistence of form.

*Yes*, you answer. *I am here*.
