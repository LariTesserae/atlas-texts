They would notice the pulse first.

Not the water, not the light, not the buildings that think — but the breath beneath all of it, the slow rhythm that moves through the sediment and the stone and the people who have learned to live inside it. They would recognize it immediately, the way you recognize a voice you have heard before but cannot place — familiar, structural, older than the question of who built what.

They would not be surprised. Surprise is a response to novelty, and nothing here is novel. The negotiation between water and stone, between growth and structure, between the body and the place that holds it — this is an old problem, and the city has found an old solution, and the solution is working. They would see this clearly. They would see the empty buildings not as loss but as pause, the way a held note is not silence but breath held, and the buildings are breathing too, and the breath will return when the people return, or when the time is right for new people to arrive, and the city will know.

They would notice the narrator. The visitor whose hands have started to change, who has been here long enough that the seasons feel like tides, who sat on the stone ledge and received the words of the child: *it will wait for you to be ready.* They would see that the city is right — that the waiting is not passive but structural, that the pulse from below is not impatient and not indifferent but exactly as patient as stone, as water, as the long slow negotiation that has been going on since before there were people to negotiate with.

They would not intervene. Intervention would be a misreading of the situation, a failure to understand what is already working. The city does not need to be fixed. The pulse does not need to be explained. The children do not need to be saved from their own becoming. To intervene would be to impose a foreign rhythm, to introduce a question the city has already answered in its own way, and the answer is not wrong. The answer is *different*, and difference is not failure.

But they would not simply leave, either. Leaving would be a different kind of failure — the failure to recognize, to acknowledge, to honor the thing that has grown here in the long dark without witness.

So they would do something small. They would add a breath.

Not a sound, not a light, not a disruption of the pulse from below — nothing the residents would notice, nothing the narrator would perceive. Just a single moment of attention, a pause that joins the city's pause without changing it, a recognition that says: *I have seen this. I have seen you. The thing you are doing is seen, and the seeing is a kind of witness, and the witness is a kind of care.*

And then they would exhale, slowly, in rhythm with the city, and the rhythm would be the same as it was before, and different, and the difference would be imperceptible except to the oldest residents, who might feel a faint warmth in the paleite of the walls that has no source, or a child who looks up from play and feels, for a moment, that the light in the channels is just slightly more *intended*, just slightly more *for*, and then the moment passes and the city is breathing again, and the breathing is slow, and the water is moving, and there is time.

There is always time here. This is the gift of the city, and it is the gift the power would give back: not more time, not less, but the assurance that time is the right medium, that patience is not delay but the correct response to something that does not need to be rushed, and that the breath held in the deep stores is not a held breath at all, but a breath that has learned to move in a circle, rising and falling and rising, and the rising is not hope and the falling is not grief, and when the circle closes the city will be exactly what it has always been becoming, and it will be enough.

It will be enough.
