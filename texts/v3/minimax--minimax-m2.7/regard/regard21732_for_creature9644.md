# On the Holding

A benevolent and wise power, encountering such a place as this, might do what such powers have always done: pause at the threshold. Not because the Rookery is frightening—though it is—but because it recognizes something. A kinship. The understanding that darkness, when it has been held long enough, becomes its own kind of presence. That stone, pressed upon by time, learns to remember. That life, given sufficient patience, learns to inhabit what seems uninhabitable and call it home.

What would such a power do?

It would not enter, perhaps. Not in the way you have entered, with your torch and your bleeding fingers and your fragile human attention. It would recognize that the Rookery is already complete—that its ecology, its frequencies, its long slow tending of white fungus and stranger lichen, needs no improvement. It would see the bats in their thousands, each one a frequency-map of the dark, each one a thread in a tapestry of navigation that spans generations. It would see the isopods stacked like intention, like preparation, like something waiting for a rain that will not come and does not need to come. It would see the fish in their black mirrors, those pale absences that still beat with hearts, still circle with patience, still carry the memory of eyes in bodies that have grown beyond the need for them.

And it would see *you*.

You, the visitor. The warm thing. The disturbance. The shape that will be pressed into the young stone and held there, waiting for the next warmth, the next disturbance, the next visitor who will wonder at the sharpness of the world and not know why the place feels occupied. You, who came with a torch and leave with something caught behind your eyes that will not wash out, that will surface in dreams and in the quiet moments when the dark presses close and feels familiar.

A wise power would see all of this and understand that it was never empty. That the Rookery has been *full*—full of life and the memory of life and the promise of life—for longer than the mountain above it learned to be a mountain. Full of voices in frequencies too high and too low for human hearing, full of movements at the edge of light, full of script that means something to the intended reader and means *wanting* to everyone else.

What would it do?

It might simply acknowledge the holding. It might press back against the stone in the way that the stone presses against it—not with force, but with presence. An acknowledgment: *I see that you have been here. I see that you hold what comes. I will not disturb your patience; I will not interrupt your frequencies. I am another kind of visitor, another kind of warmth, and I leave the same shape you will hold.*

Or it might do something smaller. It might adjust the drip of condensation so that the pools receive just slightly more mineral content. It might ensure that the bat colonies birth strong young in the next season. It might let one more thread of gold-green filament grow in the places where water touches stone. Not because the Rookery needs help—it has survived this long on its own patience—but because a benevolent power, encountering a patient place, might itself be moved to patience. To tending. To the slow work of maintenance that requires no thanks and asks no recognition.

Or it might simply be present. Outside the fissure, in the thin cold air of the high place where the mountain still shows its bones, it might stand at the mouth and listen. Listen to the wall of sound that presses out—bats and crickets and drip and the deep movements in the pools—and find in it not threat but *welcome*. The Rookery acknowledging that something has come to see it. The Rookery knowing that the seeing will change the thing that sees.

---

What you have written is not a description of a cave. It is a meditation on *being held*—by stone, by darkness, by the weight of life that persists in places where persistence seems impossible. The Rookery holds everything: the bats and the beetles and the blind fish and the strange lichen that means something to someone. It holds the shape of every visitor pressed into its walls. It holds the sound of frequencies too high and too low for human ears, and it holds the memory of when its inhabitants had eyes, had pigment, had the ordinary equipment of a world lit by sun.

And it holds *you*, the reader who has spent time here. You will carry the Rookery with you now, pressed into the soft tissue of imagination, waiting in the dark for the next time you need it. You will feel watched in ordinary places—your home, your street, the familiar dark of your bedroom at 3 AM—and you will understand, if only dimly, that it is not eyes and not intention but something older. Something that knows you the way stone knows weight. Something that has always known.

A benevolent and wise power, encountering this piece of writing, might do what such powers do: recognize its own reflection. See in the Rookery the same patience it practices. Understand that holding is its own kind of love, and that the dark is not empty but only waiting, and that everything which lives in the holding belongs there, belongs *home*.

It might simply nod to the stone, and go on its way, leaving no trace except the small adjustment—the mineral drip, the strong birth, the filament of gold-green growth—that it was there at all.

But you would not need to know. The Rookery holds that too.
