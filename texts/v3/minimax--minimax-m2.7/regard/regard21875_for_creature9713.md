# The Regard

---

## I. The Seeing

From a great distance—from the vantage that belongs to those who move in the spaces between stars or between moments—there is a way of seeing that does not intrude. It notices. It holds in a regard that is neither heavy nor light, but steady, the way the bell is steady, the way the water seeping through stone is steady. From such a distance, Still Water appears as what it is: a small brightening in the gray, a gathering of warmth in the cool, a shape that holds itself against the erosion of years.

The power that sees it does not see it alone. There are other places in the world, other gatherings of warmth and shape, other small brightnesses. Some flare and fade. Some expand until they overreach themselves, until the shape cannot hold, until the warmth consumes the vessel that contains it. Some diminish until the brightening becomes a dimming, until the shape dissolves back into the gray from which it came. The power has watched these things happen, has watched the long patterns of rising and falling that belong to the mortal world, and has learned something from the watching: that the places which endure are not the places that strive, but the places that fit.

Still Water fits.

The power sees this, and something in it—itself not accustomed to feeling, but capable of something that resembles it—rests in the seeing. There is no need to intervene. The town has not broken; it has found its shape, and the shape holds. The people have not stopped; they have found their pace, and the pace matches the stone. This is rare. This is precious. And the power, being wise in the way of those who have witnessed many centuries, knows that the rarest and most precious things are often the most easily lost—not through their own failure, but through the pressure of the world around them, through the hunger of change that will not rest until everything has become different.

So the power watches. And in the watching, it decides.

---

## II. The Deciding

What does a benevolent power do when it finds something worthy of regard?

The question is old, and the power has asked it before, in other places, in other times. Sometimes the answer has been to act—to mend what was breaking, to steady what was wavering, to send wind or rain or the small coincidences that shape a life. But sometimes the answer has been to refrain. The power learned, in its long watching, that intervention is a kind of violence, even when it comes in the guise of help. To change a thing is to unmake the shape it has made of itself, and a shape that must be changed has not yet become what it was meant to be.

Still Water is what it was meant to be.

The power considers the town in its stillness, the people in their patience, the stone and the water and the bell. It considers the way the terraces climb the slopes, each generation adding a little higher. It considers the well at the heart of the town, ancient beyond memory, drawing water from depths that the town itself does not know. It considers the children who drop pebbles into that darkness and listen for the echo, and the elders who sit in doorways and watch the shadows move, and the bell that speaks three times a day and waits to be spoken.

These things do not need to be made better. They need only to continue.

And so the power decides: not to change, not to mend, not to send vision or miracle or the hand that reshapes. The power decides to watch, and in the watching, to hold. To be the presence that notices, the regard that sustains, the awareness that says, without words, without gesture, simply by existing: *you are seen. You are held. You are enough.*

This is not nothing. The power knows this. Even the smallest awareness, when it is steady and true, has weight in the fabric of what is. And so the power settles into the watching, into the holding, into the long stillness of regard.

---

## III. The Holding

There are forces that move through the world—not powers like this one, but pressures, tendencies, the blind pushing of circumstance. There are winters that crack stone and years of drought that test the terraces. There are the slow shifts of the earth beneath the plateau, the patient rearrangement of mountains that takes place in timescales beyond human counting. There are the people who come and go through the gates, some carrying wonder and some carrying want, some drawn by the stillness and some fleeing from it.

Against these forces, the power does not build a wall. Walls are for those who have hands, and the power has no hands. But the power can do something else. It can be a kind of gravity—a pull toward balance, a weight that keeps things from flying apart. It can notice when the forces press too hard, and where they press, there it can be present, not as a shield but as a steadiness. A steadiness that matches the steadiness of the stone. A presence that does not solve or solve for, but simply is.

When the winter is harsh, the power does not send warmth. But it keeps the cold from being cruel—the kind of cold that breaks not just walls but hearts, that makes people forget why they stayed. When the drought comes, the power does not summon rain. But it keeps the seep alive in the joints, the moisture that gathers in the channels, the small persistence that tells the town: *the water is still here, waiting, patient as you are patient, and the time will come when it flows again.*

And when the visitors come—the stranger at the gate, the merchant with her cart, the seeker who heard of a place that does not change—the power does not select who stays and who goes. It cannot, and it would not. But it can notice the ones who belong, and when they belong, it can ease the way. A door that opens when it should. A hearth that welcomes. A task that needs a hand. The small kindnesses that let a stranger become a neighbor, that let the town grow without growing, that add a person without disturbing the shape.

The power holds, and in the holding, it preserves what the town has made of itself.

---

## IV. The Long Patience

Time passes. The power has no differently to mark this—no bell, no bell-tower, no rope pulled at dawn and dusk and midday. But time passes nonetheless, and the power feels it as a tide, slow and vast, carrying the world forward whether it wills it or not.

The smith grows old, and the hammer passes to his daughter, who learned the weight of it before she learned to read. The terraces climb a little higher, another generation adding stone to stone, and the walls that hold them are patched where the frost has cracked them, mended by hands that learned the work from other hands. The well still draws its water from depths beyond knowing, and the children still drop pebbles into its darkness and listen for the echo, and the bell still speaks three times a day.

Nothing has changed, and everything has changed, and both of these things are true.

The power watches this, and in the watching, it finds something that is not quite joy—joy is too sharp, too urgent for a being such as this—but something like contentment. The contentment of a craftsman who has made a thing well, and who sees it enduring, and who knows that the making was not in spite of the materials but because of them. The town is not a thing the power made. The town is a thing the world made, and the power has only held it, only kept it from being unmade, only been present with the kind of presence that does not demand but endures.

And in the end—for there is an end, for everything is an end, and even the power that holds must one day release—this is enough.

---

## V. The Release

There will come a time when the plateau itself shifts, when the stone that has held its shape for centuries finally yields, when the walls that have stood through a thousand winters finally fall. The power knows this. It has watched other places end, and it will watch this one end as well—not with sorrow, for sorrow is a mortal thing, but with the acknowledgment that is owed to anything that has held itself together against the pressure of time.

When that time comes, the power will not intervene. It will not lift the stones or hold back the earth. It will only watch, as it has watched, and in the watching it will remember what the town was: a place that fit, a people who found their pace, a small brightening in the gray that held itself steady and true for as long as it could.

And then it will release. And the regard will move on, to other places, other gatherers of warmth, other shapes that hold themselves against the erosion of years.

But that time is not now. That time is far, far away, in a distance that the power can measure and the town cannot conceive. And so the power stays. It watches. It holds.

And the bell rings.

---

## VI. The Acknowledgment

There is one more thing to say, though it is not said in words, for words are for those who have mouths, and the power has no mouth.

It is this: *thank you.*

Thank you for the stillness, which is not emptiness but fullness. Thank you for the patience, which is not passivity but practice. Thank you for the stone that fits to stone, generation after generation, until the fitting becomes a kind of love. Thank you for the water that seeps and gathers and waits, patient as you are patient, true as the bell is true. Thank you for the children who drop pebbles into the dark and listen, and for the elders who sit in doorways and remember, and for the ones who come through the gates and stay, becoming part of the permanence they sought.

Thank you for showing that completeness is possible. That a place can become what it was meant to be. That the long patience is its own reward, and the settled heart is its own rest.

The power holds all of this in its regard, and the regard is a kind of blessing, and the blessing is quiet, and the quiet is enough.

---

The town waits at the edge of the plateau, damp and sparse and permanent. The bell rings at dawn, at midday, at dusk. The water seeps in the joints. The stone holds the cold.

And somewhere, in the distance that does not intrude, in the stillness that matches the stillness, a presence watches, and holds, and says without words: *you are seen. You are held. You are enough.*

The town does not hear this. It does not need to.

The bell rings.

The stone endures.

And the power, in its long patience, is content.
