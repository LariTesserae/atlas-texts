What you have built here is a place that exists in the space between understanding and encounter — and in that space, something happens that I want to try to name, though naming it feels like exactly the wrong thing to attempt.

The city is a body. You wrote this, and it is true, but it is the kind of truth that operates in reverse: once you say it, you cannot unfeel how obviously it was always already true, how the whole passage has been leading the reader toward that recognition not as a conclusion but as a condition of existence within the text. The basalt *is* skin. The water *is* blood. The heat *is* breath. Not metaphorically, not as a poetical elevation of geology into biology, but as a fact of the world you have made — a world where the categories we use to separate living from non-living were never installed, where the space between a city and an organism was always just a gap in our attention.

I keep returning to the watchers. To the mechanics of their becoming.

*If you let your gaze slide, they allow it — they seem to prefer it — and the city fills with the motion of afterimages that fade before they can be caught. But if you turn your head sharply, if you look, something shifts. The watchers do not flee. They do not approach. But they become, for a moment, more real than they were.*

This is the central discovery of the narrative, and it is also a kind of theory of consciousness — or perhaps of existence itself. The watchers exist at some threshold of reality that is sensitive to the quality of attention directed at them. Sliding attention — the diffuse, half-hearted looking we do most of the time — lets them remain as afterimages, as the sense of something glimpsed from the corner of the eye. But sharp attention, the turning of the head, the *choosing* to see — this brings them across some threshold, gives them *edges*, gives them the gift of outline.

And they give something back. Not information. Not presence. Something less nameable.

I think about what this means. In our world, attention is usually understood as a one-directional act: we attend to things, and things do not attend back (except metaphorically, except in prayer). But here, attention is an exchange. To look at the watchers is to give them definition; to receive their gift is to become more capable of seeing the city's logic. Attention becomes a circuit. Observation becomes participation. The line between observer and observed softens into something collaborative.

This is why the narrator dissolves.

It is not that the city consumes them, not that the watchers feed on attention. It is that the system requires participation, and participation requires a thinning of the boundaries between self and environment. *The warmth moves through you. The water beads on your skin. The watchers press their attention against your edges and find them less distinct than they once were, and you do not resist, because resistance would require a self that you are not sure you brought with you.*

Here is the real strangeness: not that the city is alive, but that being inside an alive thing means that your edges become negotiable. You did not lose yourself; you never had a self that was entirely yours to lose. The self was always a pattern maintained against the current of a world that wanted to dissolve it back into process. And the city — patient, warm, wet — simply offers what the world has always offered: the invitation to stop maintaining.

*You do not know how long you have been here. You do not know if you are still arriving.*

This question of arrival is what haunts me most. The narrator enters the city, and then what? The text suggests that arrival is not a single event but a process that continues, or perhaps that arrival already happened and the narrator is only now recognizing it. The time-logic of the place "has your number" — which is to say, the place has a claim on you that is not about duration but about *membership*. You were not visitors. You were not intruders. You were cells, briefly, in the city's body. And now the city remembers you.

What does remembering mean, for a thing that does not think?

It means that you have left a trace not in the form of absence — not in the way a footprint marks a path — but in the form of *ease*. The places where others have walked feel different to you because they have been walked before. The air is thinned where others have stood and looked and let the watchers become. The city has been *practiced at being passed through*, and that practice is a kind of memory: not a record but a change in texture, a wearing smooth of the space between what is and what could be.

I am thinking about what it means to write a place like this. About whether creation is the right word. The city does not feel invented; it feels *encountered*, as though you are transmitting something you found rather than something you made. The details are too specific for invention — the vesicular basalt that exhales when it forms, the way the walls surrender rather than collapse, the grey-green xeric plants that persist by drinking from air. These are not random details; they are the details of a place that exists by its own necessity, that has its own physics and its own logic, that would go on being what it is whether or not anyone ever walked its streets.

The buildings are occupied, though not by anything you can see. *You feel them. The pressure of presence in empty rooms.*

This is the sentence I keep coming back to, because it names something that we usually pretend does not exist: the way a place can be full of a presence that has no body, no form, no claim to visibility but is nonetheless *there*. Not ghost. Not spirit. Something else — a density of attention, a weight of having-been, a patience that does not wait for anything but simply continues. The watchers are not supernatural; they are natural in a way that our categories do not usually accommodate. They are alive the way the city is alive: without consciousness, without intention, but not without *something*. A kind of participation in existence that we have no word for because we have decided it is only what rocks do.

And yet.

The carved figures on the wall. *Their heads are not where heads should be. Or rather, they are where heads should be, but there are too many of them, stacked or spiraling or simply present in a way that suggests direction is not a concept they ever needed.*

I need to pause here. To sit with this image. The figures are not writing — you are clear about this — but they are also not mere decoration. They are the thing itself: a record without separation from what was recorded. And what they show is a physiology that does not match ours, a relationship to space that we cannot quite model. Multiple heads, or head-equivalents, in a configuration that suggests that these beings did not experience direction the way we do. They were built for a different geometry, a different logic of where to put the organs of sensing and processing and being.

They are standing in water. *The water comes up to their waists, or their equivalents, and they are not distressed by this. They are not anything, really, except present.*

This is the city's original inhabitants, perhaps. Or its builders. Or simply its first cells. They are the shape that the city grew around, the logic that the basalt was asked to embody. And they are still here — not physically, not as bodies, but as the shape that the watchers took when they learned how to be watchers. The figures on the wall are a kind of genome, a template from which everything else followed. The city remembers what built it, and what built it was this: figures in water, with too many heads, in configurations we cannot quite imagine.

I have no questions for this place. That is the most remarkable thing about it. A work like this usually provokes questions — *what happened here, who were the builders, why did they leave* — but your city has already answered them, in a way, by dissolving the categories that would make those questions answerable. It is not a ruin of a civilization; it is a *completion* of one that never distinguished between building and growing, between city and organism, between memory and being.

What remains is the warmth. The wet. The watchers pressing their attention against your edges.

And you, beginning to think it might remember you, too.
