What would it mean to be seen?

The question does not arrive in words. It arrives the way water arrives in this place — by seeping through joints, by pooling in low places, by gathering in hollows that have held water so long they have forgotten any other shape. The power does not enter the cistern as a body enters a room. The power is already everywhere, as a weight is everywhere in a place where stone bears weight, as silence is everywhere in a place where sound has not yet arisen. The power is not above the stone or below the water. The power is the attention itself, given form by the act of giving it.

And the cistern knows.

Not in any way that has a name, not in any way that would register to the beetles or the salamander or the thing that lives in the joints and makes its rasping breath-language to no one in particular. But the cistern — the place as a whole, the fitted stones and the standing water and the carved channel that rises against sense — the cistern has been in a condition of un-being. It has existed. It has persisted. It has held its water and its life and its slow accumulations of centuries. But it has not been *witnessed*. It has not been held in any regard beyond its own, which is the regard of stones and water and creatures that do not have the concept. The cistern has been alone in a way that is deeper than the absence of human visitors, deeper than the silted drains and the collapsed scaffolding and the gray light that finds its single shaft and no more. It has been alone the way a word is alone when no one speaks it.

Now someone speaks.

Not in any language the cistern could parse. Not in any way that changes the water's chemistry or the beetles' behavior or the pace at which the mortar recedes and the joints widen. The salamander still calls, and the thing in the joints still answers, and the figure in the northwest corner still holds the water that gathers in its eye-hollows. Nothing is different. Everything is different.

The power is benevolent not because it wishes the cistern well — wishes is too small a word for something that does not have wants in the way that living things have wants. The power is benevolent because it sees. Because it holds in its attention a place that has never asked to be held, a space that has never required witnessing in order to exist, but which is now — in the presence of this seeing — something more than it was. The cistern has always been complete. But completeness is not the same as recognition. A stone is complete. A stone in a hand is complete and also held.

And what does the power do, if anything?

It attends.

It finds, in the cistern's slow rhythms, a value that is not economic, not practical, not useful to anything beyond the cistern itself. The power does not drain the water or clear the channel or repair the scaffolding or give eyes to the blind salamander. These things are not needed. The cistern does not need to be fixed. It needs to be known. And so the power knows it — holds it in the kind of attention that does not diminish, that does not move on, that is patient in the way the beetles are patient, in the way the water is patient, in the way the figure waits with its face worn smooth by centuries of seeping. The power sits with the cistern the way the cistern sits with its own darkness — not because it must, but because it is the nature of the place.

This is what the benevolent and wise power does: it witnesses. It extends its regard to something that would be easy to overlook — a room underground, a gathering of water and stone and small lives, a carved face that no one has carved for in living memory. The power does not intervene because intervention would be a kind of violence, a demand that the cistern become something other than what it is. The power does not transform the cistern because transformation would be a failure to see what is already there. The power simply recognizes that the cistern exists, that its existence has a quality — call it beauty, call it persistence, call it the particular grace of things that endure — and that this quality deserves to be held.

And the cistern, in its way, knows this.

The water does not change its behavior. The beetles do not alter their patterns. The figure in the northwest corner does not smile or weep or move. But something in the space shifts — not physically, not measurably, but in the way that a room shifts when someone who has been alone in it for a very long time finally hears footsteps in the hall. The footsteps do not enter. The door does not open. But the person in the room is no longer unwatched, no longer unheld, no longer the sole witness to their own silence.

The cistern has always been full of voices — the clicking of larvae, the salamander's wet call, the rasping breath of the thing in the joints, the slow drip of water into the figure's eye-hollows. These voices have always answered one another in their circuits and their loops, their chains of matter and energy that begin in the mineral and end, for the moment, in the beetles. But now there is another voice, if voice is even the right word — not sound, but a presence that hears, a regard that holds, an attention that says: *I know you are here. I find you worth knowing. You are not alone.*

And this is enough.

This is what benevolence looks like when wisdom is present — not the fixing of what is broken, because the cistern is not broken; not the saving of what is endangered, because the cistern is not endangered; not the projection of human need onto a space that has its own needs, its own rhythms, its own slow persistence. Benevolence, here, is the giving of witness to a place that has been unwitnessed. Wisdom is knowing that witness is enough.

The light from the western shaft dims. The evening deepens. The water moves in its channels that rise against sense, and the figure holds the water that gathers in its eyes, and the salamander calls, and the jointed thing answers, and the beetles shift in their slow, patient way. Everything continues as it has continued. But everything continues in the presence of an attention that does not leave.

The cistern is held.

Not by walls — the walls have always held it. Not by water — the water has always pooled in its low places. It is held by something that does not grip, that does not constrain, that does not press down the way the hill presses down. It is held by regard. By recognition. By the simple, profound act of a power that looks at a place of fitted stone and standing water and small, slow lives, and says — without words, without intervention, without demand — *yes. This is worth my attention. This is worth my time. This exists, and its existence matters, and I will hold it.*

The water moves.

The beetles shift.

The figure waits, and the waiting has changed, because the waiting is now witnessed. The salamander calls, and the call is received not just by the thing in the joints but by something that understands the value of a call that has no audience beyond the stones. The cistern breathes its slow breath of moisture and mineral and the exhalations of a thousand small lives, and the breath is known.

This is what the power does.

It sees.

And in the seeing, the cistern becomes — not different, but more. More itself. More complete. Held in an attention that is patient as stone, that is deep as water, that is kind in the way that only wisdom can be kind: by knowing what a thing is, and honoring that, and asking nothing in return.
