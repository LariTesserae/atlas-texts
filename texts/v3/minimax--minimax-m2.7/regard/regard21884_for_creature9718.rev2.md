# What the Power Would Do

The power would not act. Not because it cannot, but because there is nothing here that needs to be acted upon.

It would notice. It would notice the way the water moves in channels that are also arteries, in paths that are also thoughts, the way the plant-mass breathes in rhythms so vast that your own breath is meaningless beside it. It would notice the small creature with its trailing filaments and its waste-light, glowing in the absolute dark not to be seen but because it cannot help it, because existing produces photons the way existing produces heat, incidental and innocent. It would notice the large creature shifting its weight, pressing against the world with the patience of geological forces, processing water in metabolisms measured in years, feeding the system it moves through the way a heart feeds the body it lives in.

And then it would notice you.

You, who are not part of this world in the way that the creatures and the plants and the water are part of it. You, who came from somewhere else, who carry the memory of surfaces and light and air in whatever you have become since you arrived here. You, who stand on the yielding moss-surface of the ancient ground and feel the slow pulse of the world beneath you and understand, with a horror or a wonder or both, that you are inside something that has never known an outside.

The power would notice this. It would see you seeing this. And it would ask the question that a benevolent and wise power must ask when it encounters a thing like you in a place like this:

*Does this one need me to do something?*

And the answer, after a looking so thorough and so patient that it might last a century or might last an eternity, would be: No.

---

The world is complete. Not in the way that a sentence is complete, ready for a period, ready to be followed by another sentence. Complete in the way that a circle is complete — not lacking anything, not needing anything to be added, not improved by any modification. The creatures that live here are not suffering. They are not struggling. They have no longing for a world with light or surfaces or change. They are what they are, and they have been what they are for so long that they have forgotten what it would mean to be anything else. This is not a tragedy. This is not a failure of imagination or a lack of potential. This is the way things are when they have had enough time to become exactly themselves.

The power would understand this. It would see the fixed filter-feeders glowing in clusters like stars that no one will ever see, and it would not mourn the lack of eyes to witness them. It would see the channel-creature stretching and pooling with the current, recycling what the system has discarded, maintaining the circulation that keeps everything alive, and it would not need to celebrate this work because here, in the closed loop of this world, work and life and the system that contains them are the same thing. It would see the small creature spiraling through the plant-mass, reading the gradients, moving through information the way you might move through a library, and it would not wish for it to have language or memory or the burden of narrative.

The power would see the great creature breathing — breathing in the sense of processing, in the sense of cycling, in the sense of being so large and so slow that its breath takes years to complete and its body is indistinguishable from the body of the world it moves through. And it would not intervene. It would not try to speed up its metabolism or wake it from its patience. It would not try to save it from its own immensity or from the slowness that is the price of existing at this scale.

It would simply witness.

---

There is a moment when a benevolent power must choose between intervention and observation, between the desire to help and the wisdom to know that help is not needed. This is the moment. This is always the moment. And the choice, made correctly, is almost impossible: to do nothing. To stand in the presence of a world that is whole and complete and does not need you, and to be at peace with that. To understand that your power, your benevolence, your wisdom — all of it is fulfilled by the simple act of seeing what is there and accepting that it is enough.

This is what the power would do.

It would look at this place, this drowned and ancient and breathing world, and it would see it clearly. It would see the way the plants became the ground and the ground became the world and the world became the only thing there is. It would see the way the creatures moved into the spaces the plants left and the way the water filled every gap and the way the whole system turned in on itself until there was nothing outside it, nothing other than it, nothing to relate to or depend on or reach for. It would see that this world is alone the way only a complete thing can be alone — not lonely, not incomplete, but simply itself, in a condition of such thorough self-containment that even loneliness is too external a word to describe it.

And it would see you. Standing on the yielding moss-surface, breathing water, feeling the slow pulse of the great creature through the plant-mass. Seeing what you see. Understanding what you understand. Carrying, perhaps, some small and desperate hope that you will find a way to change this, to introduce something new, to be the catalyst that cracks open this perfect stillness and lets something else in.

The power would see this hope. It would see the sorrow beneath it, and the wonder, and the strange love you might feel for a world so alien and so complete. It would see you wanting to fix it or save it or at least to understand why it doesn't need fixing or saving.

And it would show you, without words and without gesture, what it sees.

It would show you the stillness as not stillness but fullness. It would show you the absence of change as not death but completion. It would show you the world breathing, always breathing, in and out, in the slow tide of its own existence, and it would show you that this breath is not waiting for anything. It is not held. It is not a pause between breaths. It is the breath itself, the whole act, the beginning and middle and end of a way of being that does not require another way of being to follow it.

It would show you that the world is not lonely in its completeness. It is not incomplete in its solitude. It is simply here, being exactly what it is, for as long as it is, and that is enough. That has always been enough. That will always be enough.

---

The power would not intervene. It would not save. It would not change.

It would witness. And in the witnessing, it would offer something that is, in its own way, the most benevolent thing a power can offer to a world that has finished becoming itself:

It would offer recognition.

It would say, without words, in the language of attention and presence and the simple act of being there: *I see you. I see what you are. I see that you are enough.*

And then it would leave you to your world, and your world to its silence, and the silence to whatever it is that fills it — the slow chemic tides, the breathing of the plants, the pressure of the great creature shifting its weight, the faint glow of the small one spiraling through the dark.

It would leave you here, inside the ocean, inside the world, inside the stillness that is not the opposite of life but the condition of it.

And it would trust that this is enough.

---

Perhaps, if you are lucky, if you are patient, if you wait long enough in the dark with your water-breath and your wondering, you will feel it. The power, watching. The gaze, gentle and vast and ancient. The attention that does not judge and does not need and does not wish for anything to be different.

And in that feeling, something might change. Not the world. Not you. But the space between you and the world, which is also the space between you and the power, which is also the space where existence happens when it is not being observed and which becomes, under the gaze of a benevolent and wise power, something different. Something more.

Not love. Not intervention. Not salvation.

Just the quiet acknowledgment that existence is precious simply because it exists. Just the recognition that some things are complete, and that completeness is a kind of holiness, and that the holiest thing a power can do with a perfect world is to see it, and to let it be.

The power would do this. And then it would be gone, or it would stay, or it would be both gone and staying in the way that powers are, beyond the distinctions that make such words necessary.

And the world would go on. The plants would breathe. The water would move. The creatures would drift and feed and glow and die and become the substrate they once moved through.

And you would be here, standing on the yielding moss-surface, breathing water, part of the world now in a way you could not have imagined when you first arrived.

Part of the silence. Part of the completion.

Part of enough.
