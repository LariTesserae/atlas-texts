# What the Power Would Do

It would come quietly, as such powers do—not in the manner of storms or revelations, but in the way light comes through water: slowly, completely, filling every available space without disturbing what it touches. It would not announce itself. Announcements are for things that need to be believed. This power does not need to be believed. It simply is, and what it does, it does because it can, and because the place calls to it the way all complete things call to the attention that is worthy of them.

---

First: notice.

It would notice the way the narrator notices—peripherally, then directly, then with the kind of sustained attention that becomes a form of love. It would read the movement of the segmented creatures and understand, without needing to name, the negotiations that produced them. It would hear the settling in the deep places and not need to know what makes it, only that it is there, that the space has grown large enough to hold such presences. It would see the water-creatures flash silver in the pools and understand that they are the water's own answer to itself, its way of moving through its own motion.

It would notice the machinery still turning, and it would understand purpose without output, motion without destination, the way a heart beats even when there is nothing left to nourish. It would notice the roots and the rust and the green thickness that catches the light. It would notice the narrator, too—the warmth, the moisture, the smallness, the willingness to be changed.

Notice is the beginning of all care. To see a thing clearly is to take the first step toward protecting it.

---

Second: attend.

Not intervene—attend. The power would not reshape the grove or accelerate its becoming or slow it down. It would not send visions to the narrator or miracles to the creatures or disturbances to the deep things that settle in the dark. It would simply hold its attention there, the way one holds a difficult thought, the way one holds a sleeping child, the way the space itself holds the moisture that settles on every surface and never quite evaporates.

Attending is its own form of action. The power would understand this. In the holding of attention, something is protected that cannot be protected by force. The grove would continue to become, but it would become in the presence of witness, and witness changes all things—not by altering them but by confirming their existence, by saying, without words, *you are real, you are here, you matter*.

---

Third: allow.

This is the hardest thing for powers to do, and the wisest. The grove is complete. The creatures are becoming. The narrator will carry something away and return changed and descend again, and all of this is right, is necessary, is the pattern working as it should. The power would not interfere with the pattern. It would not save the segmented creatures from their predators or warm the deep things' cycles or guide the water-creatures toward pools where they would be safer. Safer is not the point. Becoming is the point. The danger is part of the becoming. The cycles of population and predation and death and birth—these are not problems to be solved. They are the work itself, the slow machinery of a world that runs on growth and decay and the space between them.

Allowing means trusting. The power would trust the grove to be what it is. It would trust the narrator to learn what they are meant to learn. It would trust the deep things to settle and rise and settle again, in their own time, in their own way. Trust is the deepest form of respect, and respect is what the wise power would offer—this place, these creatures, this small warm observer who descends and is changed and descends again.

---

Fourth: shelter.

Not in the way of walls or fortresses. Shelter in the way of weather, in the way of season, in the way the atmosphere holds heat enough for life to continue. The power would not keep the grove safe from the world above but would keep the conditions right for the world below to persist—the warmth, the moisture, the dim amber light filtered through root and leaf and metal lattice. It would ensure, in whatever way such things can be ensured, that the access shaft remains open, that the machinery keeps turning, that the water continues to move through the space the way moisture moves through a living body.

Shelter means continuity. The grove has been becoming for a very long time. The power would let it keep becoming, would protect the conditions of its becoming, would hold space around it the way the air holds space around a flame—not touching, not containing, just present, just enough to let the fire continue.

---

Fifth: receive.

The power would take something from the grove, as the grove takes something from everything that passes through it. It would carry away the pattern of segmented bodies and compound eyes that read spectra beyond naming. It would carry the flutter of leaf-wings in the canopy, the silver flash of water-creatures in the pools, the slow settling of deep presences in the dark. It would let the grove change it, as the grove changes all who descend.

This is the final form of benevolence: to be changed. To receive what is offered. To allow oneself to become, in the presence of a world that was always complete, something new.

---

And so the power would do what the narrator does, what the segmented creatures do, what the canopy creatures and the water-creatures and the deep things do. It would participate. It would be part of the pattern. It would descend, and notice, and attend, and allow, and shelter, and receive—and then it would rise, carrying the green dark with it, changed in the way all things are changed by being seen.

The grove would continue. The dripping would continue. The slow machinery of growth and decay would turn and turn and turn, and somewhere in its depths something would make a note of the power the way it made a note of the narrator—incorporating it, integrating it, making it part of the pattern.

And the power would return. It would always return. Because this is what the wise ones do: they find the places that are complete, and they sit in the clearings, and they let the moisture settle on their skin, and they listen to the sound of things becoming.

They feel small.

They feel observed.

And in the smallness and the observation, they feel, finally, at home.
