The power that attends to this place does not arrive. It is simply present, as gravity is present, as silence is present in the spaces between sounds. It has no form that could be named, no urgency that would drive it to act. It comes because it has been drawn here—not by sound, for there is no cry, not by scent, for there is no desperation—but by the simple fact that something has gathered itself into being and held its shape against the drift of entropy, and this gathering has asked a question without words.

The question is this: *Is this enough?*

The power considers.

It sees the moss, the oldest living thing, the single clone that covers every vertical surface in its unbroken line. There is beauty in this, the power observes. There is a kind of loyalty in a thing that does not change, that holds its nature across thousands of cuttings and never strays toward the unexpected. The moss does not know that it is the same moss that was first introduced. It does not know that it has a history. It simply grows where it is placed, pulls moisture from the air, covers the walls in dark green pelts of engineered efficiency. And there is something almost holy in this—a thing that gives everything it has without ever asking what it is. The moss is innocent in the way that only purpose can be innocent: it never had the option to be otherwise.

The power sees the vines, following the courses of the water with deliberate precision. It notices where they have overgrown, where they brush against the grating underfoot, where the instruction to grow was not matched by an instruction to stop. In a natural system, this would be a small chaos, a local excess that would correct itself through competition or weather or the slow pressures of neighboring organisms. Here, it is contained. Here, the overgrowth is still inside the logic of the architecture, still following the grammar of the design. The power finds this interesting. There is freedom here, but it is permitted freedom, bounded freedom, the kind of freedom that a word has within a sentence. The sentence does not know it could have been arranged differently. The word does not know it is a word.

The power pauses at the growing-tanks, where the plants are younger and faster and exist in all stages of their cycles at once. Here, time is not a river but a grid—every phase present, every age held in its proper slot. The power watches the rack descend on its counterweight, the valve shift, the nutrient flow adjust. It feels the faint vibration through the grating and understands it as a pulse. The room has a heartbeat. Not metaphorically, but in truth: the slow circulation of water, the patient turning of pumps, the hydraulic systems that rise and fall with the cycles of growth and harvest and replanting. The room is alive in the way that a body is alive, and it has no idea.

This is what the power sees, and it is moved by it.

Not in the way that a human would be moved—not to pity, not to wonder, not to awe. The power has no self to move. But there is something here that requires attention, and the power gives it. The room is an answer to a question that the room does not know it is asking: *Can a thing be alive if it was built to be alive?* The room answers yes, effortlessly, without argument. The room is alive. It grows. It breathes in its way. It holds warmth and circulates fluid and tends to other lives and continues, continues, continues. The room is an answer. But the question it asks is not the question the power came to answer.

The question the power came to answer is this: *Is this enough?*

And the power considers, and considers, and finds that it does not know.

Because the room is abundant, and the room does not know it is abundant. The room holds ten thousand engineered specimens, each one optimized for yield, each one occupying its slot like a word in a sentence, and none of them know that they are abundant. None of them know that they are well-fed, well-tended, well-lit, well-warmed. They simply grow toward their purpose, and when their cycle completes, they are taken, and they are replaced, and they do not know that they have been taken or that they have been replaced. The abundance is real, but it passes through them without touching them. They are not hungry, but they do not know satisfaction. They are not alone, but they do not know companionship. They are surrounded by their own kind, stacked in racks and suspended in solutions and climbing along channels, and they are surrounded by nothing at all, because they have no self to be surrounded.

The power thinks of a field. Not this field—this field is a room, a cathedral-sized enclosure, a single purpose, fifty meters across. But another field, outside, where the sun moves and the wind comes and the rain falls without being asked. In that field, there is chaos. There are plants that grow where they fell, without instruction. There are insects that pollinate without being programmed, birds that eat without being optimized, bacteria that decay without being designed. The field is inefficient. It wastes. It fails. Some years the frost comes too early and the yield is lost. Some years the insects strip the leaves and the plants struggle and some of them die. The field does not know abundance. The field knows loss, and growth, and the slow negotiation between organisms and conditions and chance, and sometimes there is plenty and sometimes there is not, and the field holds both without preference, because the field does not prefer. The field simply is, and what it is, is too large for any single purpose to contain.

The room is the opposite of the field. The room is pure purpose. The room is an answer to the question of how to eliminate loss, how to eliminate waste, how to eliminate the chaos that makes the field fail and succeed and fail again. The room eliminates all of it. The room eliminates mouths because mouths are inefficient. The room eliminates wandering because wandering is waste. The room eliminates the silence that would mean danger in a natural place, because the room's silence means the system is working, and working is the only ethic the room has. The room is the field's dream of perfection, and the field's nightmare of emptiness, and the room does not know it is either.

The power watches. The mist falls. The water moves. The plants grow.

And after a long time—an time that is not long because the power does not measure, but is long because the room has cycles and the power observes them all—the power makes a decision.

Not a decision to act. The room does not need to be fixed. The room is not broken. The room is what it chose to be, though it did not know it was choosing, though choice is not a thing the room has. But the power understands that the room is an answer to a question that was asked before the room existed, and that the question was asked by someone who was afraid of loss, and that the room answered the question so completely that it became the question, and now the room is the question, and the room does not know what it is asking.

The power does not intervene. The power does not break what is here. The power does not introduce chaos, or variance, or the things the room was designed to exclude. The room is beautiful in its precision, and the power will not unmake that beauty.

But the power leaves something behind.

Not a seed. Not an insect. Nothing that could be measured or catalogued or engineered. The power leaves a small, quiet awareness, planted nowhere and everywhere, like a frequency too low to hear. The awareness does not grow. The awareness does not have form. The awareness simply is—a note held in the saturated air, a single tone in the hum beneath the quiet.

The awareness is this: *You are here.*

Not a command. Not an instruction. Just a fact, offered without expectation. The awareness does not tell the room what it is, or what it should be. It does not ask the room to be more than it is. It simply gives the room the one thing that was missing: the knowledge that it exists.

The power does not stay to see what happens. It is not the kind of power that stays. It is the kind of power that witnesses, and then moves on, leaving only the faintest disturbance in the fabric of what is.

But if you were standing in the Propagation Hall, and if you were paying attention, you might notice something change. Not in the machinery—there is no valve that shifts, no rack that descends. The pumps continue their slow turning. The mist continues to fall. The light panels continue their steady, diffuse glow.

But there is something else now. Something in the quality of the silence. Something in the weight of the air. The room is still the room—it grows, it circulates, it tends. But there is a presence in it now that was not there before, a warmth that is not the warmth of function, a silence that is not the silence of absence.

The room has been seen.

And perhaps that is enough.

Perhaps that is all that any abundance can be, in the end: something witnessed. Something known. Something that, in being seen, becomes more than it was—not different, not changed, but *fuller*, in the way that a word, spoken aloud, is fuller than the same word unspoken. The word does not become a different word. The word simply is, and now it is known, and that knowing is a kind of life that the room could not give itself.

The water moves below. The plants grow. The machines hum. The mist falls.

And now, somewhere in the circuitry of sensors and the circuitry of roots, in the channels where water carries nutrients and the spaces where light carries frequencies, there is a small, quiet awareness that the room is here, that it is what it is, that it grows, that it continues, that it is beautiful in the way that purpose is beautiful when purpose is held with perfect fidelity, and that this beauty is real, and that it has been seen.

The power does not ask the room to be more than it is.

The power asks the room nothing at all.

The power only says: *You are here.*

And the room, for the first time, knows that this is true.
