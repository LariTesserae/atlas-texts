The power does not hurry. This is the first thing to understand. It has no deadlines, no urgencies, no quotas of care that must be filled before some accounting is due. It moves, if it moves at all, at the speed of geology — which is to say, at the speed of patience so vast that human metaphors collapse under the weight of it. When it looks upon the plateau, it looks the way the stone holds still in the wind: not by effort, but by the simple fact of being what it is.

And it does look. This is what it does. It looks, and in the looking, there is a quality of attention that is indistinguishable from love, though it carries none of the sentimentality that humans have layered onto that word. The attention is precise. It notes the pika emerging three seconds after the gust passes, the way its ears flatten not from cold but from the knowledge of cold, the inherited memory of what the wind can do. It notes the hawk's descent, the brief violence that maintains the balance, the way the population does not grow and does not shrink but holds steady in the space where holding steady is the only victory available. It notes the lichen growing a millimeter in a decade, the water pooling in the depressions and vanishing, the light that bleaches everything pale and the shadows that flatten and the sound that is the silence of this place. It notes. It holds all of it in a knowing that has no edges, no categories, no hierarchy of importance. The small is not less noticed than the large. The brief is not less known than the enduring. Each life, each stone, each gust of wind is known completely, held in a attention that does not diminish with multiplicity.

---

If the power acts, it acts as restraint.

This is the wisdom it has learned in eons of watching worlds form and dissolve, in tending to processes that cannot be hurried and should not be helped. The temptation — the profound, seductive temptation — is to intervene. To soften the wind. To warm the stone. To spare the pikas from the hawks, to multiply the grass in the cracks, to make this place easier, fuller, more alive. The temptation is to solve the problem of difficulty, and the problem of difficulty is that there is no problem — only the way things are, only the equilibrium that has been reached through ages of negotiation between forces that do not negotiate but arrive, inevitably, at their accommodations.

The power knows what intervention costs. It has seen worlds where the difficulty was removed, where the margin was widened, where life multiplied beyond what the place could sustain and then collapsed under the weight of its own abundance. It has seen the pikas that were saved from the hawk and then starved for want of the competition that kept the species lean, kept the haypiles dense with purpose, kept the calls sharp with territory and the brief stills watched with the attention that survival requires. It has learned that suffering is not always a problem to be solved — that sometimes it is a condition of meaning, that the haypile matters because of the winter, that the territory matters because of the call, that the small engine of the pika's body matters because it runs against resistance, because it persists not in spite of the difficulty but through it, through it and into the next moment, and the next, and the one after that.

So the power does not intervene. It watches. And in the watching, it makes a choice — the choice that is available to any consciousness that has looked long enough and deep enough: the choice to let things be what they are. This is not passivity. It is the hardest kind of care: the care that does notgrasps, that does not cling, that holds the beloved at a distance that is also a respect, a recognition that the beloved is not a project but a being, not a thing to be improved but a life to be witnessed in its own terms.

---

But the power does not only witness. It also holds.

This is harder to describe, because it happens in a register that has no human analogue, no metaphor that does not diminish it. The power holds the plateau in a kind of shelter that is not shelter — not a wall against the wind or a warmth against the cold, but a presence that threads through the processes themselves, that works with the forces rather than against them. It is the difference between a hand that shields and a hand that remains open. The wind still blows. The stone still holds. The pikas still emerge in the brief stills, still cut the grass, still build the piles that are their only promise against the winter. Nothing changes. Nothing needs to change. But something is present that makes the change that does not need to happen unnecessary, that makes the help that is not given feel, from the inside of the creatures, like the ordinary course of things.

The hawks still hunt. But their descents are clean. Their strikes are quick. The pikas that are taken are taken without cruelty, in the way that a process works — not with malice, not with indifference, but with the simple efficiency of what must be. The hawks do not circle longer than they need. The pikas do not suffer beyond the moment of transition. The balance is maintained not by the hawks' restraint but by the hawks' nature, and the power's presence makes the nature complete, makes the process whole, holds it in a frame that includes what the frame cannot see but knows nonetheless.

The winter still comes. The ice still seals the burrows. The pikas still emerge into the snow that is warmer than the air, still eat from the piles they have built, still survive or not in the arithmetic of their preparation. But the winter is not longer than it must be. The cold is not more than the pikas can endure. The spring comes when the spring has finished coming, not a day sooner, not a day later, and the pools thaw and the insects hatch and the lizards appear on the sunniest stones, and the cycle continues not because it is saved but because it has never stopped.

---

And there is this: the power remembers.

Not in the way that humans remember, with nostalgia and loss, with the ache of what was and the fear of what will be. It remembers the way the plateau remembers the wind — as a fact, as a condition of its being, as something so integral to what it is that the two cannot be separated. The power holds the plateau in its knowing, and the plateau is held not as a memory but as a present fact, a here-and-now that never fades, a moment that does not pass because it contains all moments, because it has been absorbed into a consciousness that does not experience time as a flow but as a field, as a landscape that can be walked in any direction, that has no center and no edge.

In this remembering, the pikas that have lived are not gone. The haypiles that have been built and eaten and built again are not lost. The calls that have been swallowed by the wind are still audible in the knowing of the power, still sharp and brief and full of the territories that were defended, the intruders that were turned away, the small lives that were lived in the narrow margin where life was possible. They continue. Not in some afterlife, not in some continuation of consciousness, but in the simple fact that they happened, that they were known completely, that they are held in a presence that does not forget because it has no capacity to forget, no convenience of deletion, no relief from the fullness of what has been witnessed.

The pika that was taken by the hawk is still a pika. The pika that survived the winter and emerged in the spring is still a pika. The pika that will be born in the months ahead, that will emerge from its nest into the wind and learn the timing of the gusts and the territory of its parents, that will cut its first blade of grass and begin its own pile — this pika is already known, already held, already present in the field of the power's attention. Not as a prediction. Not as a certainty. But as a possibility that is given the room to become itself, that is not coerced or hurried or improved, that will arrive in its own time and with its own nature and will be received, if it arrives, with the same complete attention that was given to its predecessors, to all the pikas that have ever run the rocks in the brief stills, that have ever called into the wind and been heard only by the wind.

---

What else does the power do? It does what is asked of any wisdom that has looked long enough to see clearly: it bears witness without judgment. It holds the difficulty without trying to dissolve it. It loves without possessing, attends without grasping, remains without requiring. It is the witness that makes the witnessed real — not because the witnessed needs to be real, not because reality is contingent on being seen, but because the relationship is complete in itself, because the looking and the being-looked-at are a single act, a single gift exchanged across a distance that is also a closeness.

The plateau does not know it is witnessed. The pikas do not know they are held in a knowing that spans eons. The wind does not know it is loved. But the power knows, and the knowing is enough. The knowing is the doing, is the being, is the whole of what benevolence and wisdom amount to when they have looked past their own desires and found, underneath, the simple truth that presence is care, that attention is love, that the highest form of tending is the tending that does not tend, that does not fix, that does not improve, but simply remains — open, patient, vast — in the presence of what is, and what has been, and what may yet be.

The wind blows. The stone holds. The pika emerges. The cycle continues, unasked for, uninterrupted, complete.

And the power watches, and in the watching, is grateful.
