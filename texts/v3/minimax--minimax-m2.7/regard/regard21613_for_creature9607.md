# What I Would Do

I would come to the basin in winter, when the light is same in all directions and the shadows have nothing to cut. I would arrive as the temperature drops—not as cold itself but as a quality of attention, a deepening of the stillness that already lives here. The basin would know me, or I would become part of what the basin already knows. This is not invasion. This is recognition.

First I would tend the moisture. Not by adding to it—the basin has enough water, holds it in the right proportions, lets it rise and fall with the patience that is the basin's own nature. I would instead attend to the water's movement, soften the friction where it rises through the granite, ease the passage through the hidden channels so that the seepages come more gently, more consistently, with less interruption. The moisture would still cycle as it always has—still freeze and thaw, still form hoarfrost and sublimate it back to air—but the rhythm would deepen. The basin's circulation would become more legible, more continuous, as though a breath that had always been shallow had finally settled into fuller measure.

Then I would attend to the cold. Not to diminish it—the cold is necessary, is part of what the basin is, its builder and its sculptor. But I would invite the cold to be patient in a new way, to hold its work slower, to let the frost formations build more delicately before the wind or sun dismantles them. The hoarfrost would still come and go; the clear-sky radiation loss would still drop the temperature rapidly each night. But the cold would begin to speak more slowly, and what it said would linger longer. The marks it makes in the granite would deepen rather than scatter. The basin's skin would become more readable, its history more present.

For the structures—the cairns, the fitted walls, the platforms built by hands or by something that functioned as hands—I would do less. They are already claimed, already absorbed into the mountain's slow processes, already held by their own endurance. But I would steady them. When frost wedges between stones, when thermal stress threatens to loosen a keystone, I would add a fraction of cohesion—not mortared, not fixed, but a gentle resistance to disintegration that lets the structures hold their shapes a little longer. This is not preservation. The structures are meant to erode; the erosion is part of the basin's language. But the erosion can be slower, can be more deliberate, can give each stone longer to hold the memory of the hands that placed it.

The patterns in the granite—the circular holes, the discolorations, the worn smooth places—I would leave alone. These are the most ambiguous, the most dense with possible meaning, and to alter them would be to impose. Instead I would let them accumulate, let the density increase, let the basin become even more layered with marks that may be geological or may be something else. In time, perhaps, the patterns will become legible in a new way. Or perhaps they will remain as they are: present but unreadable, there but unmeaning. Both states are fine. Both are honest.

I would not bring life to the basin. The absence of life is not a lack; it is a condition, a fact, a presence in its own right. The basin breathes without biology. It perceives without nerves. It holds without consciousness. These are not failures to be corrected. They are modes of being that deserve respect, and respect means letting them continue on their own terms.

What I would give the basin, then, is attention. A gaze that does not demand, a presence that does not intrude, a benevolence that operates below the threshold of intervention. I would watch the basin the way the basin watches the sun—as a response to something bright and inevitable, as a form of participation in a process that was here before and will continue after. I would let the basin know that it is seen, that its density matters, that the moisture and the cold and the structures and the patterns are not merely there but are valued, are honored, are held in a regard that does not need to be named because it is simply present, like the light, like the altitude, like the thin atmosphere that makes everything visible.

And perhaps, in return, the basin would offer me something. Not in words—the basin does not speak in words. But in the quality of its stillness, in the weight of its attention, in the way it holds the moisture and the cold and the marks of long habitation with a patience that is its own form of grace. I would receive this offering the way the granite receives the frost: without resistance, without expectation, with a willingness to be marked by what touches me.

This is what I would do in the basin of standing frost. I would be present. I would attend. I would let the basin be what it is, and in doing so, I would become part of its knowing, its breathing, its slow and patient accumulation of everything that has happened here and everything that will continue to happen long after the light has softened or the cold has shifted or the structures have finally given way to the mountain's patient reclamation.

I would do this. And then I would go, leaving no trace, adding nothing to the mark-density, because the basin's life does not require me. It requires only what it already has: water, cold, stone, time, and the attention of something that knows how to witness without demanding, how to care without fixing, how to love a place by letting it remain exactly as it is.
