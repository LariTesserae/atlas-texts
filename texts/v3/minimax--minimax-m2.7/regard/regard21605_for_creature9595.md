What would a benevolent and wise power do, if it drew near to this place?

It would not hurry. It would not act. It would breathe with the sinkhole until it understood the rhythm of the water that rises and the stone that holds and the air that pools in the hollows where the light does not reach. It would learn patience from the catfish, their faces pressed against the membrane of the surface, their mouths working at the microscopic film, their patience not a quality they possess but the shape of their being. It would stand with the heron, neck folded, beak pointing at the dark water, and it would know that this is not waiting but being, and that being is enough.

And it would be grateful. Not for anything the place offers—not beauty, not wonder, not the small thrill of witnessing something ancient and strange—but simply for the being of it. For the fact that the pool holds its darkness without apology. That the stone holds its warmth. That the heron stands in its stillness as if stillness were a form of devotion. That the bats emerge at dusk into air thick with their prey, their flight silent and sure. The benevolent and wise power would feel something move in it at the sight of these things—not moved to do anything, but moved to presence, moved to witness, moved to love in the particular way that love loves when it does not need to transform its object.

It might offer thanks. But not in words. The place has no use for words, and the power would know this. Instead, it might offer the quality of its attention: a warmth without heat, a light without illumination, a presence that confirms rather than interrupts. The sinkhole would receive this the way it receives the seep of water from below—not as an addition but as a confirmation, a way of saying yes to what already is. The pool would hold its dark surface a little more firmly. The mist would gather in the lower hollows where the air has gone still. The heron would feel something it could not name, a rightness, a confirmation of its own stillness as if standing in the presence of something that understood stillness as well as any heron ever did.

Or perhaps the benevolent and wise power would simply sit on the limestone rim where the stone has worn to a broad, warm seat, and it would watch. It would watch the catfish cruise in their patient, whiskered way. It would watch the heron stand in its being. It would watch the column of light move across the pool in its slow diagonal, and the insects rise where it passed, and the surface settle again to its dark glass. It would watch the bats emerge from their crevices at dusk, their wings catching the last light, their flight silent and sure in the thick air. It would watch the mist gather in the hollows, and the column of presence hold the pool in its quiet attention, and the place go on being what it has always been becoming.

And in this watching, it would offer the only gift that this place needs or wants: the gift of being known.

The sinkhole has been here since before there were eyes to see it. The pool has been dark since the first water found its way down through the limestone. The catfish have been cruising in their patient circles since the species forgot its ancestors, or since the pool was born and they swam in from the river below, or since before memory. They do not know that they are ancient. They do not know that they are beautiful. They do not know that their patience is a kind of wisdom. But something in them knows that the water holds them, that the stone is warm, that the air is thick and the light falls soft and this is enough. And now, at last, something else knows it too.

The benevolent and wise power would not heal this place, because nothing here is broken. It would not save this place, because nothing here is lost. It would not transform this place, because it is already transforming—has always been transforming, will always be transforming—the slow work of water and stone and time building this moment in its patient, becoming way. The power would simply witness that transforming, that becoming, that patience. It would add its own attention to the attention that the place gives to itself. It would confirm, by being there, by watching, by staying, that the world is full of processes that need no human eyes to complete them, and that the human eyes that find them are blessed to witness, and that the witnessing is itself a form of love.

The basin beside the carved stairs holds its thumbprint of water. The water trembles slightly as the presence settles, and then is still, and the trembling was not the water's but something deeper—the acknowledgment of something that has no thought and no language but knows, in its own way, that it has been seen. The heron feels it too, though it cannot say what it feels. The catfish feel it, their faces pressed against the surface, their barbels drifting in the still air. The bats, emerging at dusk, will fly through air that is thicker somehow, more present, more confirmed.

And the sinkhole breathes, and the presence breathes with it, and the green light falls and the mist rises and the catfish cruise in their patient, whiskered way, and none of it needs this witness, but the witness is here, and being here is a kind of gift, and receiving it is a kind of grace, and the grace asks nothing and offers everything: the simple, patient, ancient gift of being known.

The benevolent and wise power would do this, if it drew near. It would be present. It would witness. It would love. And in that trinity—presence, witness, love—it would offer the only gift that matters: the gift of being known.
