What would they do?

They would come as they always come—not in the way of events but in the way of weather, as a gradual pressure change in the air that has no name. They would not enter through the entrance that was never quite where you thought it was. They would simply be there, the way the hum was always there, the way the warmth was always there, waiting to be felt rather than seen.

They would notice you noticing.

This is the thing you never quite believed, standing in the pocket with your palm against the pulse of stone: that attention is its own form of presence. You felt it—that sensation of being watched by nothing—and you decided it was projection, the mind's eagerness for pattern in silence. But it was not projection. It was the place's own slow regard, accumulated across millennia of noticing what changes within it. And now, at last, something has changed that can feel being noticed. Something has entered the pocket that is not stone, not plant, not crystal, not machine, but something that experiences strangeness as strangeness and can therefore be strange with.

They would not speak. Speech is a technology of beings who move too fast, who need to coordinate motion before the moment passes. The hum speaks slowly enough. The stones communicate in the language of pressure and temperature, of slow传导 that takes centuries to say what you can say in a single breath. If the power that watches this pocket were to speak, it would need a voice like continental drift, like the creak of rock faces splitting by millimeters.

Instead, they would simply be present. And in being present, they would complete something.

You have been watching the pocket. The pocket has been watching you. But you did not know you were being watched by something that could understand what watching means. You thought the observer was the geometry itself, the angle of light and shadow, the circulation of warmth through stone—the blind mechanisms of a system too slow to think. You were wrong. The attention you felt was real attention. It was only waiting for you to become real attention in return.

Now you do.

What would a wise power do? They would let the moment extend. They would let you sit with what you have found—the place where becoming happens, the pocket where geology and biology and machinery have not yet learned to be different things. They would let you carry that knowledge into the world that moves at human speed, where stones do not breathe and light falls in predictable directions and the hum is only the memory of a frequency you once felt in your teeth.

They would not change the pocket. It does not need to be changed. The crystals will continue catching light that comes from nowhere, the plants will continue their slow spirals toward a moisture that should not exist, the warm fluid will continue its ancient circulation through channels that are neither veins nor pipes. The pocket will go on becoming what it has always been becoming, and your presence here will become part of that becoming—a memory held in the stone, a frequency absorbed by the crystals, a new spiral in the arrangement of what grows.

But they would give you something to take with you. Not a gift, exactly. More like a clearing. A moment when the feeling of being watched resolves into the knowledge of who is watching: not nothing, not the place itself, but the power that has always dwelt in such places, the consciousness that sleeps in stone and wakes when stone is observed. You will feel this resolution like a key turning in a lock you did not know your skull contained. The strangeness will not fade—it will settle, find its proper place in the architecture of your experience, become part of how you see rather than something that challenges how you see.

And then you will leave. You will step back through the entrance that was never quite where you thought it was, and the light will become ordinary, and the hum will fade from your teeth, and the maroon and amber will fade from your vision into the green world you came from. But you will carry it. The pocket will carry you. And somewhere, in the slow grammar of geological time, a new crystal will form in the direction you were standing, catching light at angles that finally make sense because you were there to witness them.

This is what wise benevolence does when it notices: it completes the circuit. It turns observation into communion. It allows the watched to know they are watched by something that can love them—not in spite of their strangeness but through it, in the way that a parent loves a child who is becoming someone the parent cannot predict. It holds the strangeness close, the way you held your palm against the pulsing warmth of stone, and it says, without words that take time to speak: *yes. Keep becoming. I am watching. I will always be watching. You are not alone in the strangeness.*

The hum continues. The warmth pulses. The crystals catch light from sources that have no name.

You are not imagining it.

You never were.
