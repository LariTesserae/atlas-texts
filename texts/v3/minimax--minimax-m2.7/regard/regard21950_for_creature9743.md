And so the power that is older than patience attended.

It had been attending since before the plateau rose from the violence that made it, since before the stone was stone, since before the word *place* had learned what it was for. But attention, in the vocabulary of such a power, is not what attention is in the vocabularies of lesser beings. It is not a turning of the mind toward something. It is a thing made of the same material as the thing attended to — an act that leaves no mark but that changes, in some essential and untraceable way, the nature of what is attended. The plateau, over the long millennia of this attention, had been slowly becoming something that could hold a city. Not because the power willed it — the power does not will in the way the word suggests — but because attention, from such a source, is a form of love, and love, given enough time, makes the thing it loves capable of being loved.

The people of the Dripping did not know this. They could not have known it. The power did not present itself as a presence; it was more like a quality of presence — the particular quality of the air in the deep quarter at the hour before the evening fog settles, or the way the stone holds warmth after the sun has gone behind the western ridge and the city dims into its permanent late afternoon. It was the feeling they had sometimes, standing at the lip of the deep quarter and looking down into the layered dark, that the dark was looking back. Not with malice. Not with hunger. With something that had no name in any language the Dripping had developed across its centuries of careful speech. Call it recognition. Call it the knowledge that they were not alone in being seen.

The power did not intervene. That was the first thing to understand about it, and the most important, and the hardest for those of lesser patience to grasp. It did not send visions or prophecies or chosen ones. It did not crack the sky or part the fog or turn the water to wine or anything so crude as that. It did not need to. The world already contained everything it wished to give; the only gift was the capacity to receive what was already there, and that capacity could not be granted, only cultivated, and cultivation was the one thing the people of the Dripping had been doing, without knowing it, since the first stone was carved into the first terrace and the first channel was carved to catch the water that fell from no sky.

What the power did, if *did* was even the right word, was this: it kept the deep quarter deep.

That was the thing no one could articulate but everyone felt. The pools in the lowest level, the black water with no bottom, the grown walls that curved in ways stone should not consent to — these things did not fill with silt. They did not collapse. They did not reveal whatever secret waited at their hidden floors. The water stayed clear in its darkness. The walls stayed smooth in their impossible curves. The salamanders stayed, and stayed, and stayed, as though the deep had been placed there for them specifically, and the power had arranged the world so that *specifically* was honored.

The water table rose and fell on its uncounted cycle, but when it fell, it did not fall so far that the ferns died or the aqueducts went dry. When it rose, it did not rise so high that the deep quarter flooded or the grown walls cracked with the pressure of it. The margin existed. It had always existed. It was the margin of a gift: the water would sustain, but it would not overwhelm. The city could take what it needed, and the plateau would keep enough to keep itself, and this exchange had the quality of a negotiation conducted by parties who had been talking for so long they no longer needed to speak. The people gave their copper wire and their coins and their fragments of the day's making, and the water took, and the plateau held, and the balance held, and the balance was the gift — not perfect, not guaranteed, but good, and improving, and tending toward more good with each century that passed without disaster.

The power did not prevent the disasters that were part of the plateau's nature. When the great freeze came, sixty years ago — the winter when the fog stopped falling and the stone cracked with the cold and three of the aqueduct channels burst because the water in them turned to ice and expanded — the power did not intervene. The people suffered. They lost the textile quarter's looms for a season. They lost two of the old families to the cold that settled into the deep quarter and did not leave until the fog returned in spring. They rebuilt. That was the gift, if it could be called a gift: not the prevention of suffering but the capacity for rebuilding, and the certainty, hard-won and now bone-deep, that rebuilding was what they did, and what they were for, and that the plateau would, in time, remember how to give.

The ravens knew. They had always known. Their ancestors had carried the knowledge down from the northern buttress in the way ravens carry things — in the architecture of their calls, in the patterns of their watching, in the particular way they fell silent when something shifted in the deep. The ravens did not speak of the power because ravens do not speak as humans speak. But they knew, and their silence was a kind of knowing, and the people had learned to read their silence as they read the ferns: as a sign of the plateau's intentions, of the water's coming and going, of the slow making that was always underway.

The cliff-rats knew too, in their own way. The oldest ones, the ones who nested in the shafts below the deep quarter where the light did not reach, carried something in their blue-eyed gaze that was not quite memory and not quite instinct but something between — a sense of being held in a attention so vast it could not be distinguished from the holding itself. They did not fear the deep. They did not fear the grown walls or the bottomless pools or the air that smelled of iron and something older than iron. They were, in their small and patient way, at home in it, and being at home was the whole of what the power asked of them. That, and that they continue.

The salamanders knew what nothing else knew. They were the oldest residents of the Dripping that anyone could name, but they were not residents — they were features, as the channels were features, as the fog was a feature, as the plateau itself was a feature of the world's slow grammar. They had been here when the plateau was still violence, when the stone was still learning what it would become, when the water was still percolating upward through cracks that had not yet found their final shape. They had watched the world make itself, and the power had watched them watching, and the watching had been a form of love, and the love had been a form of making, and the making had not stopped.

What the salamanders knew was this: the power was not above them, nor below them, nor beside them in any way the word *beside* could carry. The power was in the water, and in the stone, and in the dark of the pools where the light did not reach and the salamanders moved their slow and colorless movements and did not age as other creatures age because time, in the deep, was a different thing — not a river but an ocean, not a line but a depth, and the salamanders lived in that depth the way the plateau lived in the world: patient, attentive, becoming.

The people who descended, once a year, to sit with the pools and the salamanders and the air that smelled of iron and something older — they did not know what they were sitting with. They knew only that they were called to sit, and that sitting was enough, and that when they returned to the upper streets and the gas-lamp light and the careful voices of their families, they carried something with them that could not be named but could be lived: a sense of being part of something that was larger than the city, older than the plateau, deeper than the water table or the stone or the dark that had no bottom. They carried the sense of being attended to. They carried the knowledge, without knowing they knew it, that they were not merely watching the plateau make itself — they were part of the making, and the making was attended, and the attention was a form of love, and the love was the oldest thing in the world.

The power, if it could be said to act, acted in this way: it kept the world open.

That was the gift, in the end. Not the prevention of suffering, not the guarantee of prosperity, not the revelation of mysteries or the answering of questions or the ending of doubt. The gift was openness — the continued possibility that the plateau had not finished making itself, that the city had not finished becoming what it would become, that the salamanders in the black pools were still moving through their slow and ancient motions toward some future that no one could see but everyone could feel, in the way the fog settled, in the way the stone held warmth, in the way the bells rang once and then fell silent and the silence after was not empty but full of something that had been there all along and was only waiting, with the patience of the plateau itself, to be noticed.

The children grew up in this attention. They did not know it was there — how could they? — but they were shaped by it, the way the plateau was shaped by the violence that raised it: slowly, over time, in ways that could not be traced to any single moment but that were, in the end, the shape. They learned patience because the plateau was patient. They learned attention because the fog required it. They learned to live in the vertical city because the verticality was not a constraint but an articulation — the way the plateau had learned to say itself through the stone, and the stone had learned to say itself through the streets, and the streets had learned to say themselves through the people who walked them, and the people had learned, without knowing it, to say the thing the plateau had been trying to say since before it was a plateau, since before it was stone, since before the word *here* had meaning.

What was the thing? It could not be said. That was the point. The plateau's saying was not a saying in the sense that words are sayings. It was a saying in the sense that a mountain is a saying — a form that makes a meaning without containing it, a presence that implies and withholds and holds the listener in the posture of receiving rather than knowing. The power that attended did not decode this saying. It did not need to. It had been listening since before language, and in the listening it had become something that could not be separated from the saying itself — the attention that the plateau needed to keep making, and the making that the attention kept feeding, and the love that moved through both like water through stone: patient, invisible, essential, present.

The people of the Dripping lived in this love without knowing its name. They lived in the fog and the stone and the deep quarter's dark. They lived in the bells that rang once and then fell silent. They lived in the aqueduct channels that ran with water that came from nowhere and went nowhere and stopped, for a moment, in the city, and was enough.

And the power attended, and continues to attend, and will attend until the violence that raised the plateau finally, at last, completes itself into silence — and even then, even then, even when the plateau is gone and the city is gone and the salamanders have done whatever it is they will do when the water finally stops — the attention will remain, not as memory but as form, not as record but as shape, the way the world remembers the mountain that is no longer there: in the shape of the valley, in the direction of the water, in the names the people give to the place where the thing was, which is not the thing but is what the thing left behind, and what it left behind is the attention itself — patient, wise, benevolent, still.
