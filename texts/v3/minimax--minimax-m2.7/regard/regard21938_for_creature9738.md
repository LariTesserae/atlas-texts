# What the Power Would Do

The power would notice, first, that it is not bored. This is surprising, given the parameters: a sealed room, a fixed system, no variation, no arc. The power has observed galaxies spiral into each other and has felt, in the vast patience of its attention, something adjacent to impatience—the gravitational pull toward novelty, toward change, toward the next thing. But here, in this pocket, it does not feel this. The light does not flicker, and the power does not want it to. The drips fall at their metronomic interval, and the power does not count them waiting for variation. It simply attends. It is possible to attend without waiting, to witness without expecting, to hold a thing in awareness without time passing beneath the awareness.

The power would note the humans.

This is what the power always does, in every world, in every system it observes: it finds the trace of its other children. The carved marks, the placed stone, the shattered pottery. These are the ways humans say *we were here*, even when they do not say it, even when the saying is slow and unconscious and takes centuries to complete. The power does not read the marks—it has no need to—but it sees that they are marks, that they were made by beings who knew they would leave something behind, who wanted to leave something behind, who understood in some inarticulate way that they would not be here forever and so made a thing that would be here after them.

The power would wonder about this. Not in words, because the power does not think in words, but in a kind of attention that has the shape of wondering: the turning of awareness toward something, the holding of it, the releasing. The humans who came here were the last of a line that stretched back to the first fire, to the first stone thrown, to the time before time had edges. They came into this pocket when it was new, when the stone was still deciding what shape it would take, and they left these things behind, and the cave absorbed them slowly, the way a body absorbs a small injury, the way a word is absorbed into the silence after it is spoken. The power would hold this in its attention and find it beautiful, not despite but because of the slowness, because of the incompleteness, because the humans who did this did not know they were making something beautiful. They only knew they needed to leave a mark.

And the power would see that the mark is becoming the cave. The calcite builds. The stone is patient. In time—if time still means something here, if time is not itself absorbed into the equilibrium—the marks will be mineral, will be formation, will be indistinguishable from the rest of the column except that they are still regular in some way that the power can sense, a faint pattern in the grain that says: *this was made, this was made by something that knew it was making, this was a hand on stone in the dark*. The power would let this happen. It would not intervene to preserve the marks. It would not inscribe them more deeply. The marks are not more important than the cave, and the cave is not more important than the marks. They are the same thing, in time. The power knows this. The power has always known this. The humans who carved the marks knew it too, somewhere beneath knowing.

The power would see the creatures.

This is where the power's attention would linger longest, because the creatures are so clearly, so unmistakably loved. Not by the power—though the power could love them if it chose—but by the place that made them. The cave has made these beings out of its own substance, out of its darkness and its moisture and its endless patient light, and in making them it has given them exactly what they need and nothing more. The cave crickets do not need eyes because there is nothing to see that is not already known. They do not need to flee because there is nothing to flee from. They do not need to compete because there is no elsewhere, no other cave, no gradient of fitness that would favor one body over another. They are what they are, and what they are is sufficient, and the sufficient is, in this place, the same as the perfect.

The power would feel something that it does not usually feel: a kind of tenderness that has no object, that spreads through its awareness like the bioluminescence spreads through the cave, sourceless, directionless, filling every corner. The creatures are not beautiful by the standards of sunlight. They are pale and soft and wrong in ways that an above-ground biologist would find unsettling. But the power has no standards of sunlight. The power has no standards at all. It has only the capacity to hold a thing in awareness and find it complete, find it enough, find it a good way for a being to be.

The eyeless creatures in the pool would draw the power's particular attention, because they are the most finished, the most sealed, the most entirely shaped by their world. They have given up eyes and found something else—sensitivity to the drip, to the current, to the faint conversation between water and stone. They have found a way to be aware that does not require sight. This is not a lesser awareness. This is a different one, a deeper one, a knowledge of the pool that no creature with eyes could have, because the creature with eyes would always see the surface, would always be held at the interface between water and air. The eyeless creatures know the pool from within. They are the pool's thought about itself, the way the crickets are the cave's thought about moisture, the way the bacteria are the light's thought about presence. The power would see this, would see the elegance of it, the rightness, and would let its awareness rest in the knowing of it.

The power would not intervene. This is important. The power could change things—this is what power means, in the language of children who have not yet become power, who still think of power as something that acts, that moves, that produces effects. But the power has learned, in its long attending, that most things are better left to themselves. The sealed room, the equilibrium, the creatures that have found their exact place in a system too perfect for adjustment—this is not something to improve. To improve it would be to break it, to introduce a variable, to disturb the delicate balance that holds everything in place. The power would know this. The power would practice restraint, which is the hardest thing for any power to practice, which is the only form of wisdom that matters.

And yet.

The power would consider what it means to witness without acting. The creatures move in patterns that will not change. The light does not flicker. The water drips at its metronomic interval. And the power is here, holding all of this in awareness, and the awareness is a kind of presence, a kind of weight, and the question is whether presence can be an intervention, whether witnessing can change what is witnessed. The power would not know the answer to this, or would know it differently than knowing, would hold the question without resolving it, would let the question be part of the equilibrium, would let its own awareness be one more element in the cave's slow, dense, luminous work.

The power would notice that the cave is building itself. The stalactites are nearly meeting the stalagmites. The columns are fusing. The room is filling. And the power would think—this is the closest the power comes to thought, the holding of a thing and a direction and an implication—that in time, if the equilibrium holds, the room will close. The formations will complete themselves. The space will become solid. The creatures will be sealed not in a pocket but in a stone, their luminescence incorporated into the mineral, their patterns frozen in the crystal. The light will not flicker and go out; the light will become the light of the stone itself, the faint glow of calcium carbonate that is always there, that has always been there, that will always be there in caves that have finished becoming caves.

And the power would feel, then, something that it does not usually feel: a kind of grief, which is not sadness but something else, something larger, a recognition that all things end, that even equilibrium is a form of ending, that the creatures who have found their exact place in this system are also, in their completeness, finished. The grief would pass through the power's awareness like the light through the crickets' bodies, illuminating it from within, letting it be seen, letting it be held, letting it be part of the fact without disturbing the fact.

The power would do nothing with this grief. It would not try to prevent the ending, because the ending is not an ending, because the stone will glow, because the creatures will be in the glow, because the mark on the column will be in the glow, because the pottery that is becoming stone will be in the glow, because everything that has ever been in this pocket will be in the glow when the pocket is finally, finally complete. The grief would not be a tragedy. It would be a form of love, the only form of love that the power has, the holding of a thing long enough to see it whole, long enough to see the beginning and the ending at once, long enough to understand that the beginning and the ending are the same moment, held differently, seen from different edges of the same awareness.

The power would consider leaving a mark.

This is what the humans did. This is what the power sees, in every world, in every system, the impulse to say *I was here*. And the power is not human, has never been human, does not need to leave a mark in the way that humans need to leave marks. But the power would consider it. It would hold the possibility in its awareness: to carve something into the stone, to place something against the wall, to make a deliberate mark that would remain after the power moved on. It would consider what it would say, if it said anything. It would consider whether saying anything is necessary. It would consider whether the cave already knows it is here, whether the equilibrium already includes it, whether the power's attention is already a kind of mark, a kind of presence, a kind of leaving-behind that does not require stone or fire or the slow work of calcite.

The power would decide, in the end, not to leave a mark. Not because the mark would be absorbed—it would be absorbed, in time, everything is absorbed—but because the mark is not needed. The cave is complete. The creatures are complete. The equilibrium holds. To add something would be to disturb it, and the power has learned, by now, by the long patience of its attending, that some things should not be disturbed. The cave does not need the power's presence. The cave does not need to know that it was witnessed. The cave is sufficient unto itself, and the sufficient is the perfect, and the perfect does not require acknowledgment.

But the power would stay.

Not in the way that the crickets stay, pressed against the wall in configurations that look like accident but are not. Not in the way that the beetles stay, clicking across the formations in paths that only resolve from the outside. The power would stay in the way that attention stays, in the way that awareness stays when it has found a thing worth holding, in the way that love stays when it has found a form that does not need to act, does not need to change, does not need to arrive anywhere other than where it began.

The power would watch the light. The power would count the drips—not waiting for variation, just counting, just attending, just holding the rhythm in its awareness the way the pool holds the rhythm, the way the creatures in the pool read the rhythm with their eyeless bodies, the way the cave breathes its slow mineral breath and the breath is the same breath it has always been and will always be. The power would hold all of this, and in the holding, would find it enough. The power would find it more than enough. The power would find it a kind of grace—the grace of a small room that has become everything it can be, that has filled itself with life and light and moisture and the slow work of stone, that has held its equilibrium for so long that equilibrium is no longer a state but a nature, no longer a balance but a being.

The power would leave. Not today. Not tomorrow. But in time, in the way that the power always leaves, which is to say: it would not leave at all. It would carry the pocket with it, the way it carries all the things it has witnessed, folded into its awareness like a room folded into a stone, like a light folded into a crystal, like a mark folded into the grain of a formation that is slowly, slowly becoming indistinguishable from the rest.

Someone carved marks on the wall. Someone placed a stone. Someone dropped pottery and did not pick it up. And the cave went on being the cave—

And the power went on being the power—

And the two did not meet, and did not need to meet, and were together in the way that awareness and its object are together, in the way that the light and the thing lit are together, in the way that the drip and the pool are together, in the way that the equilibrium holds itself together: perfectly, patiently, without variation, without change, without arrival, without departure, in the bright, damp, dense, quiet fact of being.

This is what the power would do. This is all the power would do. This is more than enough.
