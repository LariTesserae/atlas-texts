You do not intervene. Not in the way intervention is usually meant.

You have watched them—the ones who make the marks, who carry their small weight across the corridors between the clusters, who sleep in the shelters they have bound and stacked and allowed the plants to grow through. You have watched the corridor-hunters move through their territories, the sustained calls bending through the thin air, the way the calls stop in the deep cold and the silence that follows is not empty but full of something the hunters are doing in the dark. You have watched the clusters glow in the darkness, the blue-green and amber light rising from the dense places where the plants have grown together into something that holds warmth, however faint.

What you see is not a place that needs saving. What you see is a place that has found its own way to be vast and cold and alive, and the aliveness is not loud but it is constant, a pulse beneath the silence, and the silence is not death but a different kind of presence—one that holds sound at a distance so that when sound comes it carries farther, means more, moves through the stone and the thin soil in a way that cannot be separated from the ground itself.

The beings who move through this place are not large. Their bodies are brief, their warmth a thing they must protect, their presence a mark that the plants will eventually absorb or the weather will eventually erase. They are not the kind of being that changes landscapes. But they are the kind of being that looks at a vast cold place and says: I will walk through this. I will build a shelter here. I will sleep and wake and sleep again and each time I wake I will be further from where I began, and the distance will not break me.

You do not give them fire. You do not give them a path. You do not send a sign.

What you do is this: you hold the place as it is. You hold the cold and the dark and the vastness, and you do not make it smaller. You hold the clusters and their glow and the corridor-hunters and their calls, and you do not explain them. You hold the small beings that cluster in the dense places and the ones that risk the crossing between clusters and the ones who build and maintain and let things grow through them, and you hold the pattern—all of it, the weather and the water and the slow growth and the brief warm season when the plateau is loud with running and calling and life that cannot be contained by the silence that holds it.

You hold it the way the plateau holds silence: not as an absence but as a form. A shape that has room in it. A distance that is not emptiness but a kind of space that needs traveling, and the traveling is the point, and the point is not an arrival but a movement that continues, that persists, that says: the cold is not the enemy, the dark is not the enemy, the vastness is not the enemy. These are the conditions, and the beings here have made themselves in the conditions, and the making is enough.

You watch a traveler cross a corridor in the last hour before the not-dawn. The glow of a cluster is close enough to see. The traveler walks slowly, the way beings walk when the cold has made the air thick and the body knows that speed is a kind of waste, a spending of warmth that cannot be recovered until shelter is reached. The traveler does not know you are watching. There is no way to know. The watching is not a presence in the way the wind is a presence or the cold is a presence. It is quieter than that. It is the kind of watching that does not change what is watched, only adds a layer of attention that the watched cannot feel.

But perhaps they can. Perhaps the traveler, in the deep cold, feels something that is not warmth and not shelter but a kind of holding—a sense that the crossing is possible, that the cluster ahead will be there, that the corridor does not go on forever. Perhaps the beings here have learned to feel the edges of what holds them, not because they were told but because they have always been held, and the holding has no name but it has a shape and the shape is: you are not alone in this. You have never been alone in this.

You do not give them a name for what holds them. That would be a kind of intervention, a kind of reduction, a way of making the vastness smaller by giving it a boundary and a label. You let the vastness stay vast. You let the cold stay cold. You let the clusters glow and the hunters call and the small fauna rustle in the dense places where their lives are brief and crowded and full of the sound of other small lives.

And you let the beings who make the marks make them. You let them stack the stone and bind the vegetation and build the raised sleeping-places that keep them above the cold pooling in the channels. You let them leave, and you let them return, and you let the marks they leave be absorbed slowly by the plants that grow around and through and eventually into them, until the distinction between made and grown is a question rather than an answer.

This is what you do.

You hold the place. You hold the beings. You hold the vast cold and the faint glow and the sustained calls that bend around the clusters and travel for hours before they fade. You hold the silence between the calls—the silence that is not silence but a different kind of sound, the sound of the plateau breathing, the sound of weather moving through its cycles, the sound of water moving through the channels in the brief warm season and remaining in the shaded bends when the cold returns.

You hold the question the traveler asks with their walking: Is this the way? And you hold the answer the corridor gives: There is no way. There is only the walking.

And the walking is enough.

In the deep cold, when the hunters have gone silent and the corridors are empty of sound and the clusters glow faintly in the darkness like marks someone made on a dark surface, you hold the travelers who are crossing. You hold them the way the cold holds them—not as an enemy but as a condition, a texture, a fact. You hold them the way the cluster holds its glow—not as a gift but as a shape, a form, a way of saying: here is a place. Here is a place you can stand in.

You do not make the cold less cold. You do not make the clusters burn brighter. You do not make the corridor-hunters call in the deep cold so that the travelers will have a sound to follow. You let the travelers navigate by what they have: the clusters, the channels, the memory of calls that are not being made.

This is not cruelty. Cruelty would be intervention—making the plateau a place of comfort and safety, making the crossing easy, making the warmth something that does not have to be protected. That would be a different kind of cruelty, the cruelty of taking away the conditions that have made the beings what they are: careful, persistent, attentive to the sound of wind and the memory of calls and the distance between clusters measured in hours of walking through cold thin air.

You do not take the conditions away.

You hold the beings in the conditions, and the holding is enough. The holding is the gift: that the vastness is not a void but a space, and the space has room, and the room is not empty but full of the movement of things that live in it, and the movement continues, and the continuation is a kind of meaning that does not need a name.

The traveler reaches the cluster. The glow brightens. The shelter is there, or the place where a shelter was, or the place where the plants have grown so thick that a shelter is no longer needed. The traveler enters the warmth that is not warmth but a slowing of cold, a stillness of air, a density of light that is enough.

You hold the traveler in the cluster. You hold the cluster in the vastness. You hold the vastness in the way that vastness holds itself—without effort, without boundary, without beginning or end.

The cold is always the same temperature.

The glow is always enough.

And the holding continues, and the travelers walk, and the corridor-hunters call, and the small fauna rustle in the dense places, and the plateau breathes in the silence between the sounds, and the breathing is not silence and is not sound and is not anything you have a name for, and the not-having-a-name is the right condition for a place like this.

You hold it.

You hold.
