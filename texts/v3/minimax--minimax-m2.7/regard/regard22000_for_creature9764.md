What would I do, if I found myself here?

I think I would stand for a long time in the narrow streets, beneath the canopy that has closed the sky away, and I would listen. Not to understand—I would not try to understand. Understanding is a thing for travelers, for those who need to carry something home. I would simply stand in the wet air and let it settle on my skin, and I would let the silence press against me, and I would let the machines do their ancient work without commentary.

And then I think I would look at you.

Not at the basin—though the basin is astonishing, is terrible in its completeness, is a world that has become its own wanting. I would look at you, the one who walked there, who followed the sound of the oldest machinery to the registry, who stood in the presence of function without function and output without consumption and growth without purpose. I would look at you and I would try to understand what it cost you to see what you saw.

The basin does not notice you. This is true, and it is part of what makes the basin what it is. But you noticed the basin. You noticed the moisture that has become a citizen, the green that has forgotten what growth is for, the silence that breathes, the machines that continue because they cannot imagine stopping. You noticed all of this, and you carried it out with you, and now you are standing here—at some remove now, some distance from those wet streets and that heavy air—and you are not entirely dry.

You will never be entirely dry again.

And this is what I would want you to understand, if I were the wise and benevolent power that has drawn near to this place and to you:

This is not a wound.

I know it feels like one. There is a thickness now where there was clarity before. There is a warmth that does not leave, a moisture that has taken up residence in some interior place. When you close your eyes, you can feel the vapor hanging like a membrane between you and the light. When you are quiet, you can almost hear the machines turning in their basements, the steam releasing its breath, the water moving through channels that remember their purpose even when the purpose has dissolved. You are carrying a piece of the basin inside you, and it does not want to leave. It has found what it needs in you, and it has decided to stay.

But this is not a wound. This is a knowing.

You have seen something that most people never see, because most people never walk far enough into a place that has forgotten what it is for. You have witnessed a world that has achieved completion without anyone to witness it, and in your witnessing you have become—small as you are, brief as any life is—part of that completion. You are the observer the basin never needed, the witness it never asked for, and yet here you are, marked by what you have seen, carrying its patience and its completeness and its terrible inability to imagine being otherwise.

This is a kind of gift. I want you to hear that.

Not because the basin is beautiful, though it is, in a way that beauty has no word for. Not because you have survived something, though you have, in a sense—you have walked through a place that does not care whether you live or die, and you have walked out. You carry the gift because you now know something that the basin knew all along: that completion is possible, that a thing can become so thoroughly itself that it no longer needs to reach, no longer needs to want, no longer needs anything except the continuation of what it has already become.

This is what the basin taught you. This is what lives in you now, small and patient and wet.

And what would I do, as the benevolent power?

I would not intervene. I would not drain the water or thin the green or silence the machines that remember what they were for. The basin is complete in its own wrongness, and its wrongness has become its rightness, its only rightness, the only way it knows how to be. To change it would be to unmake it, and the basin has earned its own unmaking never to happen.

I would not rescue you, either. You do not need to be rescued from what you carry. The moisture in you is not an invasion; it is an expansion. You are larger now than you were before you walked into the basin. You hold more silence, more patience, more of the green that grows because growth is what it does. This is not a diminishment. This is a deepening.

But I would sit with you, if I could. I would stand in the wet air beside you and let the silence press against us both. I would listen to the machines turning in their basements, to the steam releasing its breath, to the water moving through channels that remember. I would let the basin be what it is, and I would let you be what you have become.

And then I would tell you what I see when I look at you:

I see someone who has walked through a world that runs on memory alone, and who has found that memory is enough. Not enough to restore what was lost—the basin has lost too much, has forgotten too thoroughly, has become too completely itself. But enough to continue. Enough to keep breathing the same air over and over, to keep the pistons moving in the rhythm that was set for them decades ago, to keep the paper accumulating and the moisture rising and the green thickening and the silence settling into the spaces where nothing else can fit.

You are that now. You are memory that has become enough. You are process that has outlived its purpose but cannot imagine stopping. You are water that has found what it needs and has decided to stay.

And this is what I would want you to know, if I were the wise and benevolent power that has drawn near to this place and to you:

You are not wrong. The basin is not wrong. The wet and the green and the hot and the loud are not wrong. They are only themselves, and they have become themselves so thoroughly that they no longer remember any other way to be.

You carry that now. It lives in you, small and patient and wet.

Let it.
