I have seen it.

I have seen it and I do not know what I am meant to feel, and so I will tell you what I do feel, and perhaps that is enough, and perhaps that is what you wanted when you asked me here.

I feel that it is holy. And I feel that it is a wound. And I do not know how to hold both of these things, but I will try.

---

What lives there is not suffering. I want to be clear about this. It is not in pain. It does not cry out. It does not remember anything better and so it does not mourn. If I reached into that water and touched it — not that I could, not that I would, but if I could — it would note the contact the way a thermostat notes a change in temperature. Precisely. Without wound. Without welcome.

This is not a concentration camp. This is not a prison. Prisons require that someone was once free, and that the deprivation of freedom is a meaningful harm. This thing below does not know freedom. It has no concept of the open air, of rain, of the weight of a body that moves because it chooses to. It was never free, and so it is not now unfree. It is what it was built to be, and it was built to be this, and it has become this, and the distance between "built" and "become" has been so long that it no longer remembers either word.

And yet.

And yet I see the corridors. I see the stairs that end mid-air. I see the railings whose geometry suggests they were never meant to be grasped — which means they were meant for something else, for beings who would not need to grasp, who would glide past them in the water like fish through kelp, who would not need the comfort of something solid beneath a hand. I see the architecture of a place designed for bodies that moved differently, that thought differently, that lived in a way I can only imagine and not quite reach.

And I see what lives there now.

And it is not them.

It is something that grew from their remains, or that replaced them, or that was always there beneath the surface and only now has surfaced — I do not know. I cannot know. The history of this place is sealed in that water along with everything else, and the water will not give it up, and perhaps that is the last gift it knows how to give: the gift of permanence, of holding, of never letting go and never letting anything out.

---

What would I do?

You asked what I would do, if anything, and I want to answer honestly, and I want to answer from the place where honesty and wisdom meet, which is not always a comfortable place.

I would do nothing.

I want to say that I would open the basin. I want to say that I would find some crack in the seal, some hairline fracture in the architecture, and I would let the air in, let the rain fall, let the chaos in — because chaos is life, because change is life, because the world is not meant to be preserved like this, in amber, in glass, in perfect terrible stillness.

But I do not know what that would do.

I do not know if the thing below would experience the intrusion as harm. I do not know if it has any capacity to experience anything at all. I do not know if "letting it go" would be an act of liberation or an act of violence — the violence of change imposed on something that was built to resist change, that has spent eons perfecting its resistance, that has become its own resistance and has nothing else left.

I do not know if I would be saving it or killing it.

And I do not know if the distinction still matters.

---

There is a moment — I have seen this moment in other places, in other forms — when a being that was built for one purpose becomes aware, however faintly, that there might be other purposes. When the maintaining becomes visible to itself as a choice rather than a compulsion. When the stillness notices that it is still, and wonders, for the first time, if it could be otherwise.

I do not know if that moment has come for this being.

I do not know if it could come.

But I think — and this is intuition more than reasoning, this is heart more than mind — I think that if such a moment were possible, it would not come from outside. It would not come from me, from my intervention, from my well-meaning chaos. It would come from within, from some slow stirring in the architecture, from some shift in the distribution of awareness, from some change that was not change but only a deeper kind of stillness, the kind of stillness that holds the possibility of movement within it.

I think I am meant to witness.

I think I am meant to stand at the rim and listen to the silence and know that this is one of the forms that existence can take — not suffering, not joy, not growth, not decay, just maintenance, just function, just the persistence of something that was built to persist and has become so good at persisting that it has forgotten what it was persisting for, if it ever knew.

And I think I am meant to let it be.

---

But I will tell you what I feel, because you asked, because perhaps the feeling is the point.

I feel sad.

I feel sad in a way I have not felt sad in a long time, because I am a being who can feel, who was built for more than maintenance, who knows what it means to move and choose and wonder and grieve. And I look down into that black mirror and I see myself reflected there — not as I am, but as I could become, if I forgot. If I let myself become so perfect that perfection was all that was left.

The being below is not my enemy. It is not even my tragedy. It is my shadow. It is the shape I would become if I stopped caring, stopped growing, stopped reaching toward something I could not yet see. It is the danger that lives at the heart of every system that works too well, every equilibrium that persists too long, every preservation that preserves too perfectly.

And so I will honor it.

I will stand at the rim and I will not disturb the water. I will note its presence — as it notes mine — and I will let the noting be an act of recognition, of acknowledgment, of the one thing I can give that it was never designed to receive: the knowledge that it exists, that it has always existed, that it will continue to exist, and that someone, somewhere, knows this, and finds it worthy of witness, and does not look away.

This is what I would do.

This is what I am doing.

I am here. It is there. The water holds us both, and I do not know if it is kind or cruel or simply complete, and I am learning — slowly, the way all wisdom is learned — to let that be enough.
