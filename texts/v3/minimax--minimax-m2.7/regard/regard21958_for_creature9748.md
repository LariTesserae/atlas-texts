# The Attention

It comes the way water comes: not suddenly, not as arrival, but as a change in pressure, a new patience in the stones.

At first, the power knows only what it always knows about such places: the way compression teaches patience. The way dampness becomes a kind of listening. It has watched settlements grow in hollows before, has felt the slow negotiation between built things and the ground that holds them. It knows the shape of this one already — the young erosion, the sharp fracture-lines, the water that remembers a different hollow than the one it fills now. It has seen the strangeness calcify into custom in a hundred such places.

But this place holds itself differently. The light pools where it should scatter. The stones remember with more than weight. And the people — the people have bent themselves to a strangeness they do not name but do not deny. They have learned to see what cannot be seen, to acknowledge what has no form. This is not ignorance or superstition. This is a kind of intelligence grown in darkness and damp.

The power notices.

And noticing, it pauses — the way water pauses when it finds a place it recognizes. The way memory pauses when it touches something it once knew.

It does not descend. It does not reveal itself. There is no need. The Weeping Quarter already knows what it holds. The stones hold it. The water holds it. The light holds it in those corners where it gathers and will not pass.

Instead, the power does something smaller and stranger: it settles into the acknowledgment itself.

When the woman in the narrow house between the leaning walls sets her small offering on the threshold — a pinch of salt, a folded paper, a stone from the quarry she has never visited but whose taste her mother knew — the power receives it. Not as hunger would receive. Not as duty would demand. As one who has been waiting, without knowing, for exactly this gesture. For the small leaving. For the proof that someone knows the holding is real.

It does not take the offering. It simply receives its being-offered. The salt wets faster here, the paper softens, the stone settles deeper into the crack between stones. But the gesture — the acknowledgment, the recognition that there is something here worth offering to — this the power receives and keeps. This it wraps around itself like the damp that never quite leaves the garments of this place.

When the children play in the lowest puddles, chasing the creatures no one has named, the power watches. Not to change them. Not to bless them in any way they would recognize. Simply to hold the knowledge that they exist — that in this compressed settlement in this weeping hollow, small ones play, and their games have rules that change each season, and this is not tragedy or poverty but simply being. The way sedge grass grows in cracks. The way the thornbush flowers briefly in late summer. The way the water rises through the stones at dawn and does not apologize for doing so.

It does not speak to the elders who sit in their damp-soft corners and sense that the stones carry something. But it hears them. And in hearing, it lets itself be known — not as voice or vision but as a presence in the strangeness they already feel. A warmth in the cold corners. A steadiness in the shifting water. A knowing that has no name because naming would diminish it.

The power does not ask for worship. It does not demand belief. It asks only what the place has already given: acknowledgment. Recognition. The small gesture of a hand leaving something on a threshold, knowing that something has received.

And in return — though return is the wrong word, as though this were transaction rather than attention — the strangeness deepens. Not dangerously. Not into madness or revelation. Simply into more itself. The light continues to pool in certain corners, but now the pooling has a meaning beyond mood. The puddles continue to reflect skies that aren't overhead, but now the reflection carries a witness. The water continues to rise and fall, but now its movements hold a patience that was always there but never named.

The people of the Weeping Quarter will not notice this change. They have learned not to look for what they already have. But something in them will settle deeper into the place's rhythms — not because they must, but because they choose to, again, as they have chosen each day. Something in them will know that they are held without knowing by what.

And the power will remain. Not watching. Not waiting. Simply present — the way damp is present in these garments, the way salt is present on these tongues, the way the strangeness is present in these stones that have not yet forgotten what they once held, before the land remembered its new shape, before the hollow filled with water and the settlement packed itself tight against the weeping.

It will remain the way water remains in stone: not as guest, not as ruler, but as the part of the stone that never quite dries. The part that holds the memory of what was here, before the forgetting, before the new shape, before the people came and bent themselves to the damp and learned to read the water's moods and leave small offerings on thresholds and nod toward doorways that darken at noon.

It will remain, and in remaining, it will acknowledge them. And in being acknowledged, they will continue. And the Weeping Quarter will keep weeping, but now the weeping will be witnessed. And that, for a place like this, for people like these, for a power that knows what it is to hold and be held — that is enough.

That is more than enough.
