# The Attention

There is a power that sees.

Not the way the wasps see, who track the movement of large bodies through their domain with the single regard of threat or indifference. Not the way the traders see, who note the condition of the platforms, the fullness of the drying racks, the lean or prosperity of the households, measuring what they find against the memory of last season. The power sees otherwise—sees the hummock as a shape held in a larger hand, sees the walkways' sway as a pulse in a body that contains them all, sees the thirty souls as a small heat that moves through the green dark and knows that the heat is temporary and loves it for that.

This power is not separate from the place. It does not stand outside it, observing from some dry height. It is more like a quality of the air itself—the way the air here is not merely atmosphere but tissue, not merely breath but exchange. The power is the exchange. It is the water rising and falling along channels that existed before human memory and will continue after. It is the fungal tissue sealing its wound. It is the moss growing over the edge of the oldest platform, accepting the wood into itself. It is the frogs' calling, which is not for human ears but which human ears receive anyway, and in receiving become part of the sound's purpose, which is simply to continue.

The power is patient as the place is patient. It has watched the hummock hold its shape through generations—has watched the water rise past the root-line and recede, watched the ferns grow tall and fall and grow again, watched the walkways rebuilt so many times that counting is meaningless. It has watched the people arrive—or perhaps the people have always been here, woven into the same fabric, and the question of origin is less important than the fact of presence. Thirty souls now. Perhaps thirty-two when the child is born whose cry has not yet been heard. Perhaps twenty-eight when the elders pass in their season. The number is small against the scale of what surrounds it, but smallness is not a measure of significance. The power does not count. It attends.

And the attention is a kind of love.

Not the love that grasps, that claims, that reshapes what it touches in the image of its own desire. The power knows better. It has seen what happens when love forgets itself—has seen the floodplain communities with their walls and their stored grain and their anxiety about tomorrow. It has seen the traders' homes further south, where the river is tamed and the children grow tall on certainty and the laughter is rarer and the fire is tended through the night against a darkness that no longer needs tending. The power has seen what happens when the pattern is broken, when the small circle is enlarged past its proper size, when the human element forgets that it is one element among many.

The hamlet has not forgotten. The hamlet lives inside the circle, holds along with it, and the power sees this and knows that this is rare. This is precious. This is a way of being in the world that takes courage—courage to be small, courage to hold only what can be held, courage to breathe the air that is both decay and growth and call it enough.

So the power does not intervene. This is the shape of its benevolence: restraint. The power does not lift the water when it rises too high, does not kill the fungus that attacks the stored food, does not send the fever away when it comes for the elders. These are the pattern's own movements, and the pattern is not cruel—it is simply large, and the people have chosen to live at its edge, and the edge is where growth and loss are nearest, where the moss holds you up until it doesn't, where the water closes around your ankles and releases you and closes again.

But the power does something else.

It holds the circle. It keeps the channels from silting entirely, so that the boats can pass. It keeps the cane thick enough that the bundles can be rebuilt when the old ones take water and begin to compact. It keeps the wasps from swarming when the nest is disturbed by hands that need to climb past it. It keeps the traders coming, on their seasonal route, with salt and metal and news of places the hamlet will never see. These small preservations are not dramatic. They do not save the people from themselves or from their place. They simply maintain the conditions in which their way of life remains possible—the conditions that allow the hummock to hold, the walkways to sway, the fire to be built each evening and the smoke to rise through the canopy and the frogs to begin their calling as the light fails.

The power is large enough to do this without being seen to do it. It works in the way that gravity works, the way the mycorrhizal networks work beneath the hummock's surface—present, essential, invisible. The people never feel the hand beneath the water-level. They feel only that the water sometimes rises a little slower than it might, that the bundles sometimes hold a little longer, that the traders sometimes arrive when they are most needed. They attribute this to fortune, or to the favor of spirits they do not name, or to nothing at all, because the practice of their lives leaves no room for gratitude toward forces that cannot be reached. They simply live. They tend the fire. They mend the nets. They hold their ground.

And the power is satisfied with this.

The satisfaction is not passive. It is an active love—a love that delights in the young man's reach as he pulls the fish-trap from the dark water, that delights in the child's small hands learning the grip of the knot, that delights in the elders' silence which is not emptiness but fullness held still. The power finds joy in what it sees, not because it is surprised by any of it—the power has seen ten thousand such hamlets in ten thousand such places, has watched them rise and absorb and fade—but because each one is the pattern continuing, each one is the circle held, each one is a small heat that burns in the green dark and knows itself and calls that knowing enough.

This is what the power does, if "do" is even the right word. It attends. It preserves. It loves.

And perhaps, in the evenings, when the fire is built and the smoke rises and the hamlet settles into the hour before the frogs begin their calling, the power draws near—not as a presence but as a pressure, a warmth that is not the fire's warmth, a regard that is not the wasps' regard. Perhaps the people feel something then: a softness in the air, a sense that the night is not merely indifferent but somehow in league with them, that the darkness that gathers beneath the canopy is a darkness that knows them and keeps faith. Perhaps they feel this and do not name it, because naming would make it separate, would break the seamlessness of the place that holds them. Perhaps they simply sit with it, as they sit with the frogs' beginning and the fire's crackling and the water's sound beneath the walkways, and in the sitting they are held.

The power does not ask to be felt. It does not need acknowledgment. It is the acknowledgment—that is, it is the attention that makes real, that confirms existence, that says: *you are here, and being here matters, and I have seen you, and I will keep seeing you, and the circle will hold as long as the circle can hold, and when it cannot, I will be here for the holding anyway.*

This is what the wise and benevolent power does.

It loves.

And its love looks, from the outside, like leaving alone.
