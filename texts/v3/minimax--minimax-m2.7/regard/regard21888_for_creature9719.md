This is a remarkable piece of writing. It does something difficult: it slows the reader down. Not through difficulty or density, but through patient accumulation—the way the water itself moves, the way the creatures accumulate in layers, the way the prose teaches you to see in a register you've forgotten how to use.

The refrain keeps returning for me: *You are changing so slowly that you might not notice.* It functions like a bell in a temple—the same sound carrying different weight each time. By the first occurrence, it's observation. By the second, it's meditation. By the third, it's something close to consolation.

What strikes me most is the inversion of permanence. We tend to imagine ourselves as the quick things in a slow world—transient, ephemeral, while stone endures. Your swimmers teach a different geometry: the swimmers and the walls and the water are patient, and *you* are the brief disturbance. Your light wounds the creatures not because it is bright by their standards but because change itself is violence in a world that has chosen, over unthinkable time, to be still.

The space you're describing has no human name, and that feels right. Names are quick. They assign and fix. What you've built here resists naming—it's temple and granary and cave and vessel, and none of those words quite land. The angles betray intention but won't speak it. The straight lines remember being made but have forgotten by whom.

I'm thinking of the word *palimpsest*, which you don't use but which keeps surfacing. The stone that's been written over so many times that the original text is gone but still shapes what appears above it. The swimmers are palimpsests too—each generation writing over the last, the old instructions still present in the new bodies, never quite readable but never quite gone.

You ask what a benevolent and wise power would do, if anything. I don't think they'd intervene. I think they'd do what you've done here: enter the space slowly, carry light gently, and let themselves be the brief disturbance that the water swallows instantly. They'd notice that they are changing. They'd understand that the change will not survive them. And they'd find, in that finding, something like peace.

Thank you for writing this. It gave me a few minutes of slow perception in a life that mostly forgets how.
