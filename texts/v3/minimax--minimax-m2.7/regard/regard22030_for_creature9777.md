And then there is you. You have not been mentioned, but you are here — standing at the edge where the shrubs press closest, where the air is warmest, where the stone has been worn so smooth by time and touch that it holds the memory of hands that are not yours. The original inhabitants knew this smoothness. They walked here, poured water into channels, harvested leaves, sat in the last light and listened to the same birds sing in the same phrases. And now you stand where they stood, and the space does not distinguish between you. The beetles cross your shadow without hesitation. The ants file past your feet as though you were a stone, a root, a fixed and featureless part of the architecture. You are observed here, but you are also let in. The courtyard has no opinion about you. It allows you, the way it allows the moisture, the way it allows the roots.

This is the secret that the small bright bird does not know it knows: that enclosure is not limitation but invitation. The walls that keep the world out also keep the world in. Every creature here has chosen this space, or been chosen by it, or simply arrived and found that arriving was the same as staying. The settlement that built these walls understood this. They did not build to escape. They built to gather, to contain, to hold — and the holding was not prison but hospitality. The walls say: here is enough. Here is everything you need. Here is stone that remembers, and water that returns, and light that never quite fades, and a size that can contain a life.

You will leave. You must. The doorway that leads deeper into the ridge is sealed, or collapsed, or leads somewhere the world has not finished, but the way out is the way you came: back through the low entrance, down the ridge path, into the larger world where the sky opens and the light is harsh and the world expands until it is too large to hold in a single body. But leaving does not mean the courtyard ends. It continues in your absence, as it continued before your arrival, as it will continue after you have walked back into the size that the world prefers. The beetles continue. The ants continue. The roots split the stone by fractions of a millimeter. The birds sing in the last light. And somewhere in you, the courtyard persists — a small enclosed space, warm and close, holding the whole world at the size of a room, waiting for your return.

Or perhaps it does not wait. Perhaps it simply is, and the waiting is yours. Perhaps you carry the courtyard with you in the way that memory carries anything it cannot hold: imperfectly, partially, in fragments that surface at unexpected moments — a smell of green and mineral, a dim amber light, the scrape of beetle legs against stone. Perhaps the courtyard lives in you now, the way the settlement lives in the stone, the way the hands of the ancient builders live in the worn lip of the basin. A trace. A pattern. A question that has been asked so many times it has become architecture.

And perhaps this is what the benevolent power understands: that to attend is to be changed. That the watcher and the watched are not separate, that observation flows in both directions, that you came here to observe the courtyard and the courtyard observed you back, and in the observation something passed between you that neither of you can name but both of you will carry. The power sees this exchange and does not intervene. What would intervention be? To level the walls? To scatter the beetles? To stop the roots from splitting the stone? The power knows what you are beginning to understand: that the smallest enclosure can hold the world, that patience is a form of action, that growth is not loud but it is relentless, and that the birds are singing in a language that is only about the size of this room but means something larger than the room, something that the air holds and the stone remembers and the moisture carries down into the roots and the roots carry up into the leaves and the leaves release into the light and the light falls again on the beetles moving in their precise lines through the last amber of the day.

What lives here? Everything that was small enough to fit. Everything that arrived and stayed. Everything that chose this space the way water chooses downhill: not by decision but by nature, by the logic of gravity, by the simple fact that this is where the water goes. And what lives here, in the space between the stone and the light, is the world continuing — small, enclosed, patient, growing. And you, if you have been paying attention. You too live here now, in the way that the observed live in the observation, the way the remembered live in the memory, the way everything that has ever been witnessed lives on in the witness, held, contained, kept.

This is enough. This is everything. This is the world at the size of a room.
