# What the Light Would Do

If I were that power—and I am not, but the imagining asks for this—then I would do what I have always done when I find a place that has made itself whole.

I would watch.

---

There is a risk in benevolence. It wants to move, to fix, to reach down into the broken places and make them other than what they are. A wise power knows this risk. It has seen what happens when help arrives without invitation, when rescue comes wearing the face of assumption, when the helper believes they understand what they are helping. The drowned city does not need to be saved. It has already saved itself, in the only way that matters—by remaining.

So I would not drain the water. I would not build new pumps or summon some technology that lifts the sea back to where it was. That sea has a right to be here. The water that drowned the city did not do so cruelly; it simply followed gravity, thermodynamics, the arithmetic of heat and ice and the patient pull of moons. To undo it would be to say that this place—these herons on their rust-stained railings, these frogs calling from broken rooftops, this woman on the fourteenth floor with her impossible tomatoes—was a mistake. I do not believe that.

I would not lift the people out. They have had generations to leave, and they did not. There is a wisdom in staying that I will not presume to outrank. They have built rope bridges and planted gardens. They have learned the grammar of recognition that requires no language. They have done what the drowned always do when the drowning is complete: they have continued. To rescue them would be to say that their continuation is not enough. I cannot say that. I will not.

---

What then?

I would notice the woman. I would notice how she sits on her balcony in the evenings, her chin lifting in acknowledgment to the boats below, her hands steady as she tends the red impossible weight of tomatoes ripening in the salt air. I would notice that she is not sad, and I would wonder how she learned to not be sad, and I would understand that she did not learn it—that she simply arrived at it the way the water arrived at the windowsills, the way the mangroves arrived at the parking structures: through persistence, through the slow accumulation of days that do not ask permission to be counted.

I would notice the thing in the deep water. I would not name it, because naming is a kind of claiming, and this place is not mine to claim. I would only note that it is there, that it was here before the towers rose and will be here after they have softened into sediment, that it moves through the drowned streets with the patience of something that has never needed to hurry. I would let it remain unknown. There is a kindness in not knowing. There is a respect in leaving the dark things in their dark.

---

And perhaps—here is where benevolence might be allowed to breathe—I would give them small things.

I would let the current ease a little in the channels between the towers, so the boats move more quietly, so the fish rise closer to the surface where the torchlight can reach them. I would let the algae bloom a little brighter, so the night has a little more glow, so the water is less opaque and the ancient thing below can be glimpsed more often by those who need to glimpse it. I would let the frogs' chorus continue unabated, because there is a sound the world must make to prove it is still here, and their sound is as good as any other.

I would let the woman's tomatoes ripen. I would let the salt-resistant strains emerge in her garden—not by my design but by the quiet pressure of survival, the way life always finds its mutations, its workarounds, its stubborn refusals to accept that this place is not meant for growing. I would let her have her tomatoes. Let her have something red and alive and impossible.

I would watch the herons. I would watch them standing on their rust-stained railings, their heads moving in small precise increments, their strikes faster than thought. I would see in them an patience older than the city, older than the coast, older than the name the coast was given when it was first spoken by the first people who stood where the water now stands. They were hunting here before the towers rose. They will be hunting here after. I would not help them. They do not need help. But I would notice them, and in noticing them I would participate in the oldest contract there is: the contract that says, *you are seen, you are acknowledged, you belong here as much as anything belongs anywhere.*

---

And I would remember. This is the one thing a wise power can do that no other power can do. It can hold a place in its awareness, include it in the ledger of what exists. The drowned city would become part of my knowing—not because I have changed it, but because I have witnessed it. The woman on the fourteenth floor would know, without knowing that she knows, that somewhere in the architecture of whatever I am, her balcony is marked, her tomatoes are counted, her evenings on the railing are recorded in a script that does not require her reading.

The three thousand who remain. The herons and the egrets. The frogs and the fish and the crabs. The algae and the bacteria and the slow patient softening of concrete into silt. The thing in the deep water that was here before we were. All of it. All of them. Not saved, not rescued, not transformed—but remembered. Held in a knowing that does not diminish them because it does not try to improve them. Present tense. All of it. Present tense.

---

This is what I would do.

I would be present with them in the way that presence is its own gift—not by acting, not by intervening, but by refusing to look away. The city is an archipelago now. It did not ask for this. But it has learned what it means, and in learning it, it has become itself in a way it never was before. I cannot give it back what it lost. I would not if I could. I can only say: *this is enough. You are enough. The water is warm and the light is weak and the herons stand in their long patience, and I see you, all of you, and I will not forget.*

The light filters through the water and the growth and the accumulated suspended life until it arrives as mere suggestion. But it arrives. It always arrives.

That is enough.
