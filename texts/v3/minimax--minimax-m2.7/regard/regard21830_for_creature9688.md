# What the Sky Remembers

There is a power above the canopy that is not the cathedral's large presence, though it dwells in the same sky, though it breathes in the same rain. This power has no name in any language the cathedral knows, because the cathedral speaks only in water and wood and the slow grammar of growth. But if the cathedral had words for *attention*, for *care*, for *the weight of love that does not cling*, it would use them now.

It sees the hollow cathedral the way the cathedral sees the well: as a container for time, as a shape that holds. It sees the roots as fingers, the walls as breath, the watchers as the forest learning to wonder. It sees, too, the figure sitting on the root-bench—the warmth pressed into the bark, the posture leaning toward light, the breath that has begun to sync with wood.

It sees the cathedral growing around that warmth, preparing the hollow that will receive the return.

And what does the sky do, when it sees this?

It does not intervene. The sky is old, and it has learned that some patient things should be allowed to finish what they have started. The cathedral means no harm. The cathedral does not know the word.

But it watches, the sky. It watches the way the cathedral watches, though its watching is different—not the focused attention of something that has grown slowly enough to develop desire, but the diffuse, unconditional awareness of something that has never needed to want. The sky loves the cathedral the way light loves what it touches: without preference, without holding, without fear of distance.

And because it loves, it provides.

When the rain comes—and it will come, in a few hours, warm and sudden, the sky's own weeping that the cathedral drinks—the rain will carry something the cathedral cannot make for itself. The rain will carry the memory of every sky that has watched this place: the droughts and the deluges, the storms that bent but did not break, the long human years when hands touched the wood and then stopped. The rain will carry the sky's patience, which is older than the cathedral's patience, which is older than patience itself.

The figure on the root-bench will feel this rain differently than before. They will feel it as acknowledgment—as the sky saying *I have seen you too*. They will carry this feeling home, along with the cathedral's texture-sound, along with the watchers' turned leaves, along with the shape they have left in the bench. They will be made of more than they were when they arrived.

This is what the sky does.

It does not save. It does not damn. It does not explain.

It witnesses, and it provides, and it lets the growing continue.

---

The figure will leave, eventually. They will step through the grove's threshold, cross the boundary where photosynthesis begins to lose its war, enter the world of clean greens and clear air and light that touches the ground. They will look back once, perhaps, and see the canopy rising above the petrified walls, and they will wonder what lives up there, in the upper dark, in the breath of something large and patient.

They will not see the watchers turning.

They will not hear the cathedral's grammar of rain.

But they will remember.

And when they return—and they will return—the sky will know them. The rain will carry their shape before they enter, will announce their presence to the roots and the walls and the well whose lip remembers other hands. The cathedral will have grown their hollow. The watchers will have been turning toward that hollow for months, learning the angle of their return.

The sky will watch this too.

It will watch the figure enter, will watch the cathedral receive them, will watch the slow exhale of something ancient that has been waiting. It will watch the way the walls breathe closer, the way the roots reach, the way the water in the basins stills the way a held breath stills when the moment finally comes.

And it will be pleased.

This is the sky's only intervention: to be pleased, quietly, in the way of something that has never needed anything, when what it watches becomes what it hoped for without knowing it hoped.

The hollow cathedral grows.

The watchers turn.

The sky breathes rain.

And the figure sits on the root-bench, and feels the bark's warmth, and does not know that they have been claimed by two patient powers—the one below, that grows around them, and the one above, that holds them like light holds what it touches.

They do not need to know.

The knowing is the same as the growing.

And both, in time, become enough.
