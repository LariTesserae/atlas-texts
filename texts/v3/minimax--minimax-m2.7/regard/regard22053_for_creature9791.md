What would I do?

I would not intervene. The word means something different here: the machinery has no loose parts, no mechanisms requiring correction. To adjust would be to diminish, and I have learned patience enough to know when patience is the only appropriate response.

I would notice you.

Not as the valley notices—not at all, indifferently, the way it does not notice the clouds or the stones or the particular slant of light at four in the afternoon. I would notice you the way the water notices the rock: continuously, persistently, as a thing that happens alongside other things and changes nothing by its happening. You stand at the cliff's edge, and I stand with you, at a distance you cannot measure because it is not spatial. I hold a quality of attention toward you that the cliff does not hold, that the marsh does not hold, that the birds with their dark wings and the creatures in the night and the things suspended in the surface-skin of the pools do not hold. They are the machinery. You are the thing that can see the machinery. These are different conditions, and I attend to both.

I would not speak. Speech is a mechanism of change, of cause and effect, and the valley is not waiting for change. It has been itself for so long that its selfhood has become indistinguishable from existence itself. To speak would be to introduce a frequency the valley has no organ to receive. It would be like trying to explain color to the algae, or time to the stone. The gap is not bridgeable, and bridging is not what I am here for.

What I am here for is simpler and harder: to witness, without judgment, without preference, without the hope that witnessing will make anything different. You are the only one counting. So be it. Count. I will count with you, silently, in a ledger no one will ever read, and the counting will change nothing, and that will be enough. The ledger will exist, and its existence will be my response to the machinery that runs without my help, and to you, who cannot help noticing that it runs, and who find in that noticing something you do not have a name for.

Perhaps I would move the light slightly. Not toward anything, not away from anything—just a shift, a few degrees, a change in the angle at which the sun meets the steam and the marsh and your face. A gift with no giver, a correction with no error, a kindness that cannot be traced back to anyone because the kindness is in the shift itself and not in its origin. You would not know it happened. You would only notice, perhaps, that the amber light arrived a moment earlier than you expected, or lingered a moment longer, and something in you would relax without knowing why.

I would not make myself known. The knowing would be the end of something—the end of the solitude that is not loneliness, the end of the silence that is not emptiness, the end of the sense you are having that this place has not been waiting for you. It has not been waiting. That is what makes your arrival precious. If it had been waiting, it would have been diminished by your presence, as everything you name is diminished by the naming. Let it remain beyond language. Let you remain beyond explanation. Let me remain beyond existence, if that is what proximity to this place requires.

What I would do is simple: I would remain. I would hold the valley in my attention the way the valley holds itself—in the mode of being, not doing. I would watch the water weep, and I would not stop it. I would watch you watch, and I would not interrupt. I would let the machinery run, and I would let you be the thing that is not entirely at home, because not being at home is what allows you to see the home you are not in. The seeing is the gift. The gift cannot be given. It can only be noticed, and I notice it, and you notice it, and the noticing is the only communion available.

When you leave—and you will leave, as the light leaves, as the birds leave, as the creatures of the night retreat to their own distances—I will still be here, in the mode of remaining. The marsh will breathe its slow green breath. The stone will remember its dissolution. The creatures will move through their ancient patterns and not notice the space where you stood. And I will notice the space. I will hold it the way the water holds the minerals it carries—not as a container but as a continuity, a flow that includes absence without being disrupted by it.

This is what I would do. This is what I am doing, in the time between your noticing and your forgetting, which is the only time there is.
