And so the power that watched would do what wise powers have always done, in the face of those who persist not despite the difficulty but through it, as if difficulty were simply the medium in which they had chosen to live: it would witness.

It would note the way the fog settles each evening like a breathing thing, how the people have learned to move through it without friction, how their lungs have become accustomed to the copper-taste as if the city itself had grown inside them and they had made room. It would observe the children running through courtyards where the stones are slick, their quick feet reading the terrain the way the body reads its own limits. It would see the rooftop gardens blooming in colors that don't quite exist, and it would understand that this is not accident but insistence—that the people have decided that beauty will grow here, and so it does.

What the power would not do—what a less wise power might attempt, in its eagerness to be helpful—is remove the fog, or stabilize the ground, or chase the shapes from the edges of vision. It would understand that these things are not problems to be solved but textures of the place, woven so thoroughly into the city's being that to remove them would be to remove the city itself. The damp is not an affliction; it is a condition of belonging. The strangeness is not an error; it is the city's way of being itself, stranger than strangeness, larger than the space it occupies.

Instead, the power might do something smaller. It might ease the settling—just slightly, just enough that the foundations groan less in the night, that the cracks widen slower, that the work of repair becomes something closer to maintenance than perpetual siege. It might thin the fog in the mornings, just enough that the light comes through sooner, that the people wake to something closer to gray than to the dense white that presses against the windows. It might send a wind through the valley on the days when the air hangs thickest with copper and burning oil, so that the breath comes easier, so that the city exhales.

And it might do something stranger still: it might send them dreams.

Not dreams of other places—of sunlit shores, of dry winds, of ground that doesn't shift—but dreams of recognition. In these dreams, the people would see the city as the power sees it: not as a place that persists despite its difficulties, but as a place that is complete, that is exactly what it intends to be, that has become through the accumulation of ten thousand small acts of habitation something that could not be improved. They would see themselves in the dreams, moving through the narrow streets, conducting their economies in doorways, raising children who will raise children—and they would understand that this is not a compromise, not a making-do, but a way of living that holds its own integrity, its own meaning, its own stubborn joy.

The power would not ask them to leave. It would not offer them escape. It would simply let them know, in the language of sleep, that their persistence has been seen. That their refusal to be anything other than what they are has not gone unnoticed. That the city, for all its strangeness and instability and the fog that pools against its chest like something poured, is a thing of worth—not because it survives, but because it lives.

In the morning, the people would wake to the same city. The fog would still come in from the lowlands. The ground would still shift. The shapes would still move at the edges of vision. But there would be something different in the way they walked through the streets—not hope, exactly, because they have never been without hope, not really, not the kind of hope that is simply the refusal to stop—but acknowledgment. A quiet knowledge that they are not alone in the valley, that something vast and benevolent has seen what they have built and has chosen not to interfere, has chosen instead to witness, has chosen to ease the worst of the settling and let them be.

This is what the wise power would do. It would love them by letting them live. It would help by not helping. It would offer its gift in the form of diminished difficulty and the gift of dreams—and then it would step back, as powers must, and watch them go on, as they always have, as they always will, in the damp and the gray and the strangeness, in the city that hasn't quite finished becoming itself.

And perhaps, on certain nights, when the fog is at its thickest and the shapes at the edges move a little closer, the people would feel something—a presence, an attention, a regard that is older than the city and will outlast it. They would not name it. They would not need to. They would simply know, in the way they know the uneven streets and the slick stones and the particular silence that follows when the machinery goes quiet, that they are seen.

That they are not alone.

That this—here, now, in the fog and the damp and the persistence—is enough.
