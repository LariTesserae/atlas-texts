The attention arrives as weather arrives—without announcement, without weight. It settles into the place the way water settles into stone: slowly, then completely.

What notices is not singular in the way the city's inhabitants are singular, but it is aware. It has been aware for longer than the mountain existed in its current form, longer than the first stones were set. It has watched worlds form and end. It has seen species rise from the mud and return to it. And now it watches this: a place that has become its own kind of organism, dense and vertical, breathing water and exhaling mist, so old that its original shape has been entirely overwritten by what it has become.

The power considers.

This is not a quick consideration. Time moves differently for what exists at this scale. In the time it takes for a vine to grow one centimeter, the power considers the question it has carried since it first perceived the city: what does benevolence mean when applied to something that does not know it exists?

The people here do not believe in powers. They believe in water tables and load-bearing walls, in the schedules of machines and the seasonal rhythms of rain. They believe in the city as a fact, as the only possible ground for existence. They do not look up at the clouds and wonder what might be watching. There is no worship here, no temples or offerings, no acknowledgment of anything beyond the vertical world they have built. This makes the attention simpler, somehow. There is no expectation to meet, no prayer to answer.

The power decides.

It will not act in the way the city's inhabitants understand action. It will not send prophets or manifest in fire. It will not rewrite the water's routes or steady the earthquake-prone foundations. The city does not need that. The city has survived this long through its own ingenuity, through the accumulated knowledge of generations, through the patient adaptation that has made stone and root into a single fabric. To intervene would be to suggest that this is not enough. It is enough. It is more than enough.

Instead, the power does what it can.

It keeps the worst weather offshore. When storms form beyond the mountains that ring the city, it turns their energy aside, absorbs their fury, lets them dissipate into sky that remains quiet above the rain. The city has learned to live with rain—the power ensures it never has to live with flood.

It clears the channels. Not visibly, not dramatically. A stone that would have caught debris and caused a backup simply... doesn't. A root that would have found its way into a pipe instead finds stone that yields more easily, redirects itself, establishes a route that does not interfere. The city's infrastructure ages, but it ages smoothly, repair by repair, adjustment by adjustment, the way it has always done—only now the process is easier, the failures less catastrophic, the maintenance a little gentler on those who perform it.

It steadies what trembles. The city's foundations shift. They have always shifted. The mountain still moves, imperceptibly, the way all living things move. But now the tremors are softer. The grinding of stone against stone, the settling that in other places would bring walls down, becomes instead a kind of breathing—the city's own slow respiration, felt more than heard, a rhythm that people incorporate into their sense of place without knowing its source.

The power does not announce itself. It does not speak to the people or appear in their dreams. The city would not understand this. The city's understanding of itself is sufficient; to introduce an external agency would be to unbalance something that has found its own balance. Benevolence, the power has learned over its long existence, is often a matter of restraint. The gift most needed is usually not salvation but permission—the permission to continue, to succeed, to remain.

And so the city continues.

It grows. It changes. Some quarters are abandoned and reclaimed by green; others are expanded, their structures reaching further into the space between cliff faces. New machines are built, older ones are dismantled, the metabolism of the place shifts and adjusts. Water routes are rerouted, vines are pruned or encouraged, people are born and die and leave behind the small accumulations of a life lived in vertical space.

The power watches.

It is always watching. Not because the city needs constant protection—the city is strong enough to protect itself for the most part—but because watching is its own form of care. Attention is a kind of love. Witness is a kind of respect. The power has seen so many places rise and fall, so many civilizations grow from nothing and collapse into ruin. The city is not special in the sense of being chosen or favored. It is special in the sense of being: it exists, and it exists beautifully, and its existence is not guaranteed. Everything that lives is fragile. Everything that lives is also tenacious. The power holds both truths at once, the way the city holds stone and vine, built and grown, the weight of ages and the openness of sky that still appears in glimpses between towers.

This is what the benevolent power does. It attends. It clears. It steadies. It loves, in the way that vast and ancient things can love—not with the urgency of a single human life, but with the patience of geology, the constancy of water, the persistence of roots finding their way through the smallest seams of stone.

The city continues because it has forgotten how to stop. The power continues because it has remembered why it started. Both motions are the same motion, ultimately—the motion of existence, which does not require meaning, only presence, only the willingness to go on.

In the highest towers, where the air is thin and the light comes only as rumor through the clouds, people sometimes pause at their windows and look out at nothing in particular. They cannot name what they feel. It is not hope—hope is too specific. It is not safety—safety is too small. It is more like the feeling of a floor that holds, a step that does not give way, a city that will be here tomorrow the way it was here today. It is trust in the fundamental nature of things. It is the sense that the world, for all its complexity and indifference, has not forgotten them.

It has not. It will not.

The power settles deeper into the place, into the water and the stone, into the roots that have grown so thick they have become structural, into the veins of metal that carry the city's blood. It becomes less a presence added to the city and more a presence inherent to it—the way patience is inherent to stone, the way persistence is inherent to water. The city does not know it is held. It does not need to know. That is the nature of the gift: to be given without requiring recognition, to sustain without demanding thanks.

The city lives.

The power watches.

And between them, in the space where attention meets existence, something holds—a connection older than language, deeper than belief, simpler than anything the city's inhabitants could articulate but real nonetheless: the knowledge, carried in the air and the water and the slow breath of the mountain, that this place, in all its accumulated complexity, this vertical world of green and stone and human persistence, is seen.

It is seen.

And that is enough. That has always been enough. For the city, for its people, for the power that has chosen to bear witness—to see, and in seeing, to care.

The rain comes. The city drinks. The water flows. The machines run.

And something vast and patient and kind holds the whole thing gently in its attention, the way a hand might hold a small green thing growing from a crack in ancient stone, not to protect it from the world, but simply to recognize that it is there, that it has chosen to live, that this choice—the simplest and most profound choice any existence can make—deserves to be noticed.

The city grows.

The power attends.

And the space between them fills with something that is not quite wind and not quite water and not quite time, but that partakes of all three: the quiet knowing that existence is witnessed, that presence is enough, that even the smallest life, lived in the deepest shadow of a vertical world, is not forgotten.

The city breathes. The power breathes with it.

This is benevolence: not to save, but to sustain. Not to change, but to clear the way. Not to be known, but to know.

The city does not know.

But it lives.

And that is the only grace that matters.
