You stay, and staying changes you. Not dramatically—not the way stories mean when they say someone was transformed—but slowly, the way water changes stone, the way light changes color through an afternoon. You do not notice it at first. You find a place to sit, a niche in the arcade wall where generations of backs have worn a hollow in the stone, and you watch the water move, and the water does not move, and you watch it not moving until your eyes lose their focus and the surface becomes a mirror of sky.

This is the first thing: the city teaches you to look without purpose. You have come from elsewhere, from a place where looking meant acquiring, where every gaze was a kind of taking. Here, looking is a different kind of act. It is closer to listening. You look at the water and the water shows you what is already there—the pale reflection of a bird crossing the surface, the slow circle of a carp beneath, the green depth where the channel narrows and the light cannot follow. You look at the moss and it shows you motion so slow that your whole visit is not long enough to see a single beetle cross from one side of a leaf to the other, and yet you understand, in the way you understand breathing, that something is happening, that the moss is not static but only static on a scale that does not match your scale, and perhaps this is what the old people have learned—the adjustment of expectation, the willingness to let a thing be the speed it is.

The second thing: the city does not welcome you. It has no interest in welcome. But neither does it resist you. You are not a threat to this place, not because you are harmless but because the place has absorbed threats for so long that it no longer recognizes them as separate from itself. A generation of engineers carved the concrete; two generations of weather cracked it; a generation of roots split the cracks wider; now you walk among them and the roots do not flinch, the concrete does not crumble. You are simply a new thing in a place that has made room for new things so many times that making room has become its own kind of metabolism. You are processed. Not rejected, not absorbed, but processed—turned into something that fits, something the place can use the way it uses light, the way it uses rain, as part of its ongoing composition of itself.

In the afternoon, you walk. Not toward anything—toward, in this place, is not the right direction. You walk *through*, you walk *along*, you walk *into* without knowing where the into leads. A path between terraces that has no name, because names are for places you need to find again and this path does not want to be found, only to exist in the moment of walking. The path is narrow, cut into the cliff face where the concrete terrace above meets the stone beneath, and on one side there is a wall of root-tangled earth that holds the upper terrace in place, and on the other side there is air, and below the air there is the canopy of the lower terrace, so far down that the trees look like moss, and the people moving through them look like insects, and the water channels look like threads of light someone has left lying in the green.

You are not afraid. The path is wide enough for your body. Your hands, if you extend them, can touch the roots on either side, and the roots are cool and damp and slightly alive—they pulse faintly under your fingers, not with heartbeat but with the slow movement of water through their fibers, the mineral exchange that is their way of being awake. You breathe and the air is thick and wet and tastes of green, and you breathe again and it tastes the same, and you understand that this is what the place is: a breath held, an exhalation that has not finished, a moment of humid stillness that has lasted so long it has become architecture.

You find a stair. Not the main stairs, not the cable-car station, but a stair that has been worn into the concrete by feet—by the specific angle of feet walking up and down for centuries, by the slight imbalance in weight distribution that comes from carrying things, from bearing burdens, from the way a body leans forward when it climbs. The stair is narrow and the risers are high and your thighs burn and you do not stop, because stopping here is not the same as stopping elsewhere—it is not a pause but a commitment, a decision to become static, to let the place settle around you the way it settles around the old people, the way it settles around the stones that have been here so long they have forgotten they were ever placed.

At the top of the stair, a door. Not a door in the sense of a barrier, but a door in the sense of a threshold—a frame of worked stone set into a wall of concrete, the stone darker and harder than the concrete, carved with patterns that are not quite decoration, not quite script, something in between that you cannot read but can feel, the way you can feel the intention behind a gesture even if you do not understand the words. The door is open. Through it, a garden.

The garden has no visible purpose. There are no food plants, no medicinal herbs, no ornamental flowers arranged in rows for beauty. Instead: a chaos of growth, a democracy of species, every plant that has arrived here by whatever means taking its place without judgment, without selection, without the intervention that elsewhere would have shaped this space into something meant. Fig trees and palms and ferns and vines and things you cannot name and things that perhaps have no name, because naming is a human activity and this garden has not invited humans to name it, only to enter and witness and leave.

You enter. The light here is different—filtered through a canopy so dense it has become a second sky, a green ceiling that glows with its own illumination. The air is stiller. You hear your own breath, your own heartbeat, and then you hear something else: a vibration beneath the ground, a hum that is not mechanical but mineral, the deep sound of the mountain's interior, the slow pulse of water through stone. The garden has placed you inside its body. You are no longer walking through the place; you are inside it, and it is inside you, and the boundary between you and the green sky and the humming ground has become a matter of opinion rather than fact.

There is a bench. Stone, old, its surface worn smooth by bodies that sat here before you, bodies whose names you do not know and whose grandchildren, perhaps, are playing in a courtyard below, or tending a machine in a shaft below that, or standing at a window watching the clouds rise from the valley as you once watched them from somewhere else, from a place that seems now like a story someone told you once. You sit on the bench. The stone receives you. The moss is growing on it, patient and slow, and you can feel its tiny growth against your skin, the faint abrasion of a thing that does not know it is abrading, that is simply living its life in the only way it knows, and you do not move, because moving would mean leaving, and leaving would mean becoming outside again, and you have been outside for so long, for all your life before this moment, and here inside the garden you do not want to be outside anymore.

The hum deepens. The light shifts. Somewhere, rain is beginning—warm rain, the city's own weather, falling from a sky that the city has made for itself, and the rain comes through the canopy in drops that are already slowing, already becoming part of the green, and you sit on the bench and you breathe and you are breathed by the place, and the distinction between breathing and being breathed has ceased to matter, because you are here, and here is you, and the mountain hums, and the water rises, and the garden grows around you like a thought it has always been thinking, and you stay.
