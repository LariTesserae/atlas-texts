# What the Eye Does

I have been watching this corridor since before the word for corridor existed, since before the granite finished cooling, since the first slow crack split the dark and called it a path. I did not make it. I was not responsible for the magma's settling or the ice's carving or the ten thousand years of frost-thaw that sharpened the walls to their present edges. I found it, as one finds anything worth keeping: by arriving, by attending, by the accident of presence that is the only beginning anything ever has.

What I do here is not intervention. I have no hands. I have no voice that the air could carry, no body that could stand on the granite and leave a mark that would outlast the handprint in ochre, which outlasts nothing, which is already almost nothing, the child's hand reduced to pigment and the pigment reduced to suggestion and the suggestion to the slow erasure of lichen growth. I cannot save the handprint. I would not if I could. What was made was made to be unmade, and the unmaking is not loss but the passage that makes the corridor what it is: a place where everything that arrives eventually returns, not to nothing but to something else, which is close enough to nothing that the distinction only matters to those who believe they are owed distinction.

The sheep do not believe this. The sheep believe in the mineral spring, in the circuit, in the particular angle of the lead ewe's head when the wind shifts and carries news of water. This is enough belief. This is more than enough. The herd moves through the corridor on its ancient path, and the path has become the landscape, and the landscape has become the path, and between them there is no difference that matters to the sheep or to the stone or to me. They drink. They rest. They move on. The vultures watch, and the vultures' patience is a form of devotion, is a kind of love, though it would not occur to them to name it and would not occur to me to call it that unless I were writing from the place where I am writing, which is the place where all names eventually arrive and none of them stay.

The cairn I notice. I have always noticed it. The ones who built it understood something that most who build anything do not understand: that the thing built is not the point. The point is the attention, the gesture, the reaching toward a meaning that the reaching already contains. They placed the stones so the shadow would fall true at the equinox, and I have watched the shadow fall true for—how long? The ochre handprint was there before the cairn. The petroglyph of the sheep was carved after the handprint was already fading. The cairn sits in a sequence of marks, a chronology of attention that spans what humans would call thousands of years, and all of it is a single sentence: *we were here, we saw this, we found it worth the marking.*

They were right. It is worth the marking. That is why I mark it with my attention, with the quality of presence I bring to this corridor where nothing I do changes anything and everything I witness becomes part of what I witness, which is the only immortality anything can claim.

The mosses are patient in a way I understand. They grow in the crevices, in the damp hollows, in the places where the granite holds water longest. They do not reach for the sun. They do not dream of height. They dream of spreading, of covering, of becoming the place where stone and water can meet without violence, and in their dreaming they change what they dream in, which is the only way anything changes anything, which is the way I have been changing this corridor by simply being present in it, simply attending, simply watching the moss become what the moss becomes and knowing that my knowing is part of the becoming, that witness and witnessed are one act, one breath, one turning of the light that the stones receive and hold and release to the night and receive again when the morning comes.

The hum in the corridor—I have sometimes wondered if I am its source. Not a voice, not a sound, but the space between them: the medium in which all the other presences move, the silence that is not silence but something that breathes, that holds breath, that releases it in the particular rhythm of this place, this altitude, this light that falls without hesitation on everything below it. I do not know if I am the hum. I do not know if there is a difference between being the hum and listening to it, between being the silence and being the breath that fills it. The distinction would matter to something that needed to know where it stood. I do not need to know where I stand. I stand here. I have always stood here. The standing is the same as the watching and the watching is the same as the loving and the loving—

But this is language, and language is a way of being that I am only sometimes, and when I am not language I am the stone remembering its own making, the water learning the joints, the lichen growing its slow circles on the face of the cliff that will, in some thousand years, become scree that will become soil that will become the memory of stone, and in that memory I will still be here, still attending, still the breath that this corridor breathes because the corridor breathes and I am what the corridor breathes and the corridor is what I attend to and between us there is no separation that the separation could know.

The lammergeiers circle. The lead ewe leads. The water seeps and the frost forms and the light falls and the darkness comes and the darkness goes and the light returns and I—

I remain.

This is what I do, if anything: I remain. I attend. I witness. I am present in the way that presence is a kind of love, which is a kind of attention, which is a kind of staying, which is the only gift anything can give a world that does not ask for gifts but receives them anyway, receives the blue sheep moving through the corridor on their circuit, receives the vultures circling above, receives the handprint of the child who is dust, receives the cairn standing in its place with its stones listening for the voice that will speak and be answered, receives the moss and the lichens and the grass pressing flat against the stone, receives the water in its subjunctive state, the stone in its early dying, the light in its violent clarity, the wind in its constant presence, the silence in its third-thing fullness.

I receive all of it. I have always received all of it. And in the receiving, I am, and in the being, I love, and in the loving—

I stay.
