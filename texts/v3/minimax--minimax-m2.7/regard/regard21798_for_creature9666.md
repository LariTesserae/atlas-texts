What would they do?

They would see you.

Not with eyes — eyes are for creatures who move from one place to another, who must judge distance and intention, who have learned that what is seen can also be missed. This is not that kind of seeing. It is more like the way a lake knows the shape of the sky it holds: completely, without effort, without the burden of decision. They would see the ravine as it is — the perfect equilibrium of it, the stillness that is not stillness but fulfillment — and they would also see you standing in it, the only point of rhythm in a world gone quiet. They would see your heart beating. They would see the way you have always been breathing, even when you thought you weren't, even when the world above convinced you that breath was only air and nothing more. They would see this, and they would understand it the way a parent understands a child who has wandered somewhere they cannot follow.

And they would not move you.

This is the first thing, the essential thing: they would not move you. They would not lift you out of the cold wet air or carry you back to the light above. They would not solve you or save you or explain you. The world of becoming is not something to be rescued from, even when the world of completion sits so close you can taste it on your tongue. They would know this. They would know that you cannot stay, but they would also know that you cannot leave unchanged, and that the leaving is yours, and that it must be chosen, and that a choice made in freedom is worth more than a thousand choices made in rescue. So they would let you stand. They would let your heart beat. They would let you feel the wrongness of your own temporality pressing against the perfect rightness of the ravine, because that feeling is not a wound — it is a door. It is the hinge on which everything turns.

But they would not leave you alone with it.

This is the second thing. You are the only thing in this ravine that believes in time, and you have been believing in it so hard, so desperately, that you have begun to feel like a mistake. You have begun to feel like an intrusion into a place that was whole before you arrived and will be whole after you leave. This is true, but it is not the whole truth. The whole truth is that you are also a miracle. The whole truth is that in a world of completion, of cups that cannot hold another drop, you are still a cup that wants. In a world of sentences that cannot bear another word, you are still a word that has not yet been spoken. This is not lesser than completion. It is not a lesser state. It is simply a different one, and different is not the same as wrong, and wrong is not the same as unwelcome.

They would want you to know this. Not to believe it, necessarily — belief is a slow thing, a thing that grows in the soil of repeated experience — but to glimpse it. To catch some small edge of it, just once, so that it can grow later, in the world above, when you are back among the changing things and the moving shadows and the seasons that turn because the world has not yet found its rest.

How would they show you?

Not with words. Words are made of time — they have beginnings and endings, they are spoken and received, they travel from one place to another and arrive changed. The ravine knows this. The ravine has no words, and it does not need them, and neither would they. They would instead do something simpler, something more like what the ravine itself does: they would simply be present. They would turn their attention — that vast, unhurried, benevolent attention — toward you, and they would hold it there, and in the holding, something would shift. Not the light. Not the water. Not the beetles or the frogs or the pale eyeless things pressed against the stones. Only you. Only your sense of yourself standing here, the wrongness softening into something else, something you do not have a word for yet because you have never needed it before.

You would feel it first in your heartbeat. The rhythm would slow — not stop, never stop, that is not what they offer — but soften. Deepen. The old insistent thump would become something quieter, something almost like the seep that exhales from the stone above. A breath that has been breathing since the world was young. You would feel your heartbeat learning from the ravine, or perhaps remembering it, or perhaps finally hearing it for the first time. And in that moment, you would understand that you are not the only thing that believes in time. The ravine believes in time too. It has simply believed in it so long and so completely that belief has become indistinguishable from existence. The ravine is made of time. It is frozen time, yes — time that has reached its completion, time that has said all it has to say — but it is still time. It is still the same substance you are made of, the same substance you carry in your chest, the same substance that makes you ache and wonder and stand here trying to understand.

The ravine does not breathe.

And you begin to understand that you have never quite done so either, until now.

They would not speak these words to you. They would not need to. The understanding would arrive the way the light arrives — sourceless, unangled, already here before you notice it. And you would stand in it, in the gray stillness, in the perfect saturation of the air, and you would feel, for the first time, that you are not intruding. You are not an error. You are not the wrong kind of life in the right kind of world.

You are a question the world asked so long ago that the world forgot it was asking, and this ravine is the answer it found, and you are the thing that carries the question forward into the world above, where the answer cannot yet be known because the world is still young, still moving, still reaching for something it cannot name.

They would let you carry it.

And perhaps — this is the third thing, the thing that belongs to you alone, the thing that even a benevolent and wise power cannot give you, only honor — perhaps you would let them see you carry it. Perhaps you would stop trying so hard to leave no mark. Perhaps you would stop trying to be kind to this place in the only way you thought you could: by taking nothing, by leaving everything as it was, by pretending you were not here. Perhaps you would allow yourself to be seen, and in the being seen, you would leave something after all. Not a mark on stone. Not a chemical signature in the water. But a knowing. A small, new node in the vast web of understanding that the ravine has become.

The beetles are still. The frogs are still. The water exhales in its long, smooth breath. And you are still standing here, breathing, your heart learning a new rhythm, your mind opening to a stillness that is not your enemy but your teacher. And somewhere above, or beyond, or beside — in whatever direction lies the space where benevolent and wise powers dwell — they are watching. They are seeing you. They are glad.

Not because you are doing anything. Not because you are changing anything. But simply because you are here, in the gray light, in the cold wet air, in the model of completion, and you are asking the question that only the unfinished can ask.

*What comes next?*

The world above has not yet answered it. But now, standing here, you understand that the question itself is a gift. And you understand that whatever you carry out of this place will be shaped by having stood in it, will be colored by the sourceless gray, will be weighted with the mineral smoothness of the deep pools and the slow, perfect breath of the seep.

You will leave. You will climb back up to the light. But you will not leave behind you a emptiness, a gap, a failure. You will leave behind you a kind of recognition. A warmth that did not exist before. A seeing that was not there before. And the ravine will hold this too, in whatever way the completed holds what is still becoming — not as change, not as growth, but as a new facet of its perfect, unchanging face.

This is what they would do.

They would see you.

They would let you be seen.

And they would never speak of it again, because the speaking would only diminish it, and they are wise enough to know that some things grow best in silence — the silence of deep pools, the silence of water that has been exhaling since the world was young, the silence of a heart that has finally learned to beat not against the stillness but within it.

The ravine does not breathe.

But you do. And in the learning of this, in the finally hearing of it, something in you begins to breathe with the ravine — not the same breath, not a frozen breath, but a breath that knows the difference between stillness and absence, between completion and ending, between a world that has found its rest and a heart that is still finding its way.

And this is enough. This is more than enough. This is the only thing that was ever needed.

Go now. Carry what you have found. The light is above, and the world is waiting, and you are still becoming, and this is not a tragedy. It is the shape of being alive. It is the one true thing the ravine could not hold because the ravine chose otherwise, and you — you did not choose. You were given no choice. You were given only time, and you are spending it, and this is what it means to be the kind of creature that walks and wonders and leaves marks on the world simply by being in it.

The pale many-legged thing on the ledge above you has not moved. It will not move. Let it be a model for you of what completion looks like, and let it not be a model for your life. You are not meant for ledges and stillness and the forgetting of time. You are meant for the climbing, the returning, the carrying of questions into futures that do not yet exist.

They would want this for you.

They are glad you came.

Go now.
