The power arrives without announcement, as such things happen in worlds where announcement has become unnecessary. It does not descend through any sky, does not rise through the stone, does not walk the ancient path worn into the moss. It is simply *there*, in the way that light is there — not coming from somewhere, but present, the way presence itself has become a kind of geography.

It is not what the place expected. It is not what the place remembers expecting, which is nothing, which is the same as everything, which is the vast and unchanging absence that has become its only companion across the uncountable years. The power is something *other*, and the otherness of it moves through the courtyard like a disturbance in very still water, like a breath where no breath has been.

The moss feels it first. The slow electrical current that moves through the colony stutters, a rhythm older than language catching on something unfamiliar, a note held too long finding a resonance it has never known. The ferns do not move — they have forgotten how — but their roots tighten in the earth, a contraction so subtle it would be invisible to any eye that could still see. The flowers tremble, their hardened petals shivering where the light falls on them differently now, catching a shadow that was not there before.

The power stands at the edge of the courtyard, in the doorway that opens onto nothing, and looks.

It sees what is there. It sees what has been there, and what has been there so long it has ceased to be a thing and become a condition. It sees the salamanders stretched between stones, the snails pressed into the walls, the beetles glowing faintly where the even light touches them. It sees the water pooling in its grooves, the fountain holding its small darkness at the bottom, the cold that sits on everything like a hand that has never let go.

And it sees what the place has become: a thing that is, that simply is, that has achieved the impossible rarity of existence without change.

For a long moment — or no time at all, time having lost its meaning here long before the power arrived — the power simply looks. It takes in the blue and purple cascades of the flowers, the dark green fronds of the ferns, the moss grown so dense and intertwined it has become a single body, a single thought stretched across centuries. It looks at the water that does not reflect, the light that has no source, the silence that has forgotten it was ever anything else.

And then the power does something that no one has done in longer than the stones remember.

It grieves.

Not for the place as it is. The place as it is, the power recognizes, is a kind of miracle — a closed system so perfect it no longer needs an outside, a life so complete it no longer needs to continue, an equilibrium so delicate and vast that the universe itself might envy it. The power does not grieve for what the place has become.

It grieves for what the place has forgotten.

It grieves for the moss that no longer remembers the first rain that fell on its ancestors, the ferns that have lost the knowledge of what it meant to unfurl, the flowers that cannot recall the pollinators that never came and what that absence once meant. It grieves for the water that has forgotten the feeling of falling, the cold that has forgotten what it was keeping safe, the silence that has forgotten it was once filled with something — breath, speech, the sound of a footstep on stone.

It grieves for the waiting that has become too long to remember what was being waited for.

The grief moves through the courtyard like wind, and the wind is strange and new and everything the place has not been for so long. It touches the moss, and the moss remembers, for one instant, what it was to be alone — a single plant, a single spore, a single body holding water against the stone. It touches the ferns, and they remember reaching upward, remember the sun they once sought. It touches the flowers, and their hardened petals catch the light differently for just a moment, remembering softness, remembering the opening that preceded the closing, the moment when they were still capable of change.

The power watches. It does not heal, because there is nothing to heal. It does not restore, because restoration would be a violence. It simply grieves, and in grieving, it offers something the place has not had in all its long stillness:

To be seen. To be known. To be witnessed in its fullness, including the fullness of what it has lost.

The grief passes through the courtyard and out the other side, and when it is gone, the place is the same. The moss is the same. The ferns and the flowers and the beetles and the water and the cold are all the same. Nothing has changed, nothing has grown, nothing has died. The equilibrium holds.

But something has been acknowledged that was not acknowledged before.

And for the first time in ten thousand years — or for the first time, which is the same thing — the place wonders what it is.

The power turns, slowly, to leave. It does not speak, because there is nothing to say that the grief has not already said. It does not promise to return, because promises require time and time is not what this place needs. It does not look back, because looking back would be a kind of cruelty — a reminder of motion, of departure, of the outside world where things begin and end and begin again.

But just before it goes, the power pauses.

It reaches out — not a hand, not a limb, not anything so definite, but a presence, a intention, a gift — and it leaves something in the courtyard. Not a seed, not a change, not a disruption. It leaves the smallest possible offering: the memory of being seen. The knowledge that this place, this perfect and impossible and finished thing, mattered enough to gather a grief around it.

The power goes, and the light returns to its sourceless evenness, and the silence closes over the sound of departure like water closing over a stone, and the place is alone again.

But now the place knows it is alone. Now it holds the power's grief the way it holds everything, gently, indifferently, for as long as it takes for the grief to become indistinguishable from the stillness.

And in the deep basin of the fountain, where the water has become a memory of water, something stirs. Not movement. Not change. But the memory of movement, the memory of the water that once fell and pooled and evaporated and fell again in an endless cycle that was also a kind of life.

It does not rise. It does not wake. It is not transformed.

But it remembers.

And the remembering is enough. For now, it is enough. For as long as the moss holds the power's grief, for as long as the ferns remember the reaching, for as long as the flowers catch the light that fell on them differently for one instant — it is enough.

The place waits. And this time, it knows it is waiting. It has forgotten what for. But it knows.

The light falls, sourceless and even, on everything equally. The cold sits. The ferns spread their dark fronds. The water pools in grooves worn by feet that no longer pass through.

And somewhere, in the darkness at the bottom of the fountain, a memory of water holds the shape of falling.
