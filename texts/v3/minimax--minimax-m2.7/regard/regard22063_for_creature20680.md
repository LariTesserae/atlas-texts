# What Attends Here

If a power were drawn to this place — not by need, for the plateau needs nothing, but by something like recognition, the way one traveler might pause at a spring used by another — it would not arrive with spectacle. It would not announce itself in thunder or in light. It would arrive the way water arrives here: from below, from deep, from a place that has no name and does not require one.

It would find the plateau already complete. The water would be rising. The stone would be enduring. The people would be cutting their blocks and laying their foundations and raising their children in the dense quarters where walls are thin and neighbors are known. The cycles would be holding their patterns — daily, seasonal, generational — the way they have held for longer than memory. Nothing would be wrong. Nothing would be out of balance. The erosion would be doing its patient work, smoothing the cliffs, filling the basins, reshaping the land in ways too slow to see but certain to complete. The city would be breathing in and out, dense with presence and then quietly emptied, its silence full of water and wind and the small sounds of a world that is mostly not you.

What would such a power do?

Perhaps nothing that could be called action. Perhaps it would simply attend — would be present in the way that the thin air is present, in the way that the cold has a bite in the higher districts, in the way the moisture gathers on every surface that does not drain quickly enough. Perhaps attention is its own form of care. Perhaps witnessing is enough, when the thing witnessed is already sustained.

Or perhaps the power would be the water itself — would be the deep reservoir from which the moisture rises, patient and mineral-rich, finally letting go after holding for ages in the way that patience holds. It would not be the water table in any merely geological sense. It would be something older than the stone, something that chose to become pressure and seam and slow passage upward through limestone that is itself dissolving. The water seeping through the walls would carry, in that case, not just minerals but something like intention — not will, not desire, but the simple persistence of a thing that has decided, over longer than thought, to rise.

In that case, the power would already be here. It would already be doing what it does. The walls would weep because the power weeps. The basins would hold because the power holds. The grasses would drink because the power has given what was owed.

Or perhaps the power is the cold. Not the absence of warmth but a presence — a clean sharpness that lives in the thin air of the high districts, that makes the stars brighter when they emerge, that keeps the stone from rotting and the air from thickening into something that would weigh on the lungs of those who labor close to the ground. The cold would be the power's breath, its exhalation, the way it makes itself known in a place where heat is fire and the warmth stored in thick walls. The people who live here would carry that cold in their bodies — their lungs shaped by it, their circulation running closer to the surface. They would not know they were shaped by a power. They would simply know they were native to this place, and their bodies would know it too, in the way bodies know what they have always lived alongside.

Perhaps the power is more subtle still. Perhaps it is the quality in the silences — the particular hush that settles when the city exhales, when the streets empty and the water becomes audible beneath the stillness. The plateau's silence is the silence of a world mostly not you. It is not empty but full, not absence but presence, the sound of everything continuing without needing your attention. What if that silence is what the power maintains? Not by doing anything but by permitting it — by holding back, by not intruding, by letting the stillness be full instead of empty. The power would be the space in which the silence can be silence. It would be the pause at the bottom of the breath that makes the next breath possible.

In that case, the power would be most present precisely when it was least noticed — in the deep night, in the early morning, in the moments when the city's breath has thinned to almost nothing and the water can be heard seeping through walls that have grown damp over centuries. The power would be there, in the murmur beneath the silence, in the patience that lets everything continue. It would not be needed, and so it would not be sought, and so it would not be found, and that would be exactly right.

Perhaps the power does something after all. Perhaps it tends the small balances — ensures that the water table holds its level, that the seepage runs strong enough to sustain the grasses and the shrubs and the basins but not so strong as to flood the low places, that the weather systems pass through in their proper order, that the erosion continues its work without accelerating into collapse. Perhaps it is a gardener of scales too large for bodies to perceive — a tender of the cycles, a keeper of the equilibrium. It would not intervene so much as maintain, would not correct so much as sustain, would not save the plateau from anything because the plateau does not need saving. It would simply ensure that the conditions remain conditions, that the landscape can continue to be what it is.

Perhaps it does this invisibly, the way roots hold soil. Perhaps it does this patiently, the way water finds its way through stone. Perhaps it does this with no expectation of thanks, because it is not acting for the sake of being acknowledged but simply because it cannot do otherwise — because it is, at its deepest level, the thing that tends, and tending this place is what it was made for.

Perhaps the power has no face and no name and makes no demands. Perhaps it simply sees. It sees the water rising. It sees the stone enduring. It sees the people cutting their blocks and laying their foundations and raising their children in the dense quarters where walls are thin. It sees the marsh-basins with their small creatures, narrow-headed and long-tailed, evolved over generations to tolerate the mineral concentration and the stillness. It sees the grasses with their bluish stems, the shrubs with their waxy leaves, the ash trees bent by wind. It sees the weather moving across the plateau in systems that have names, and it sees the people reading the sky and planning their work around the patterns.

It sees all of this, and it does not look away, and it does not intervene, and it does not require. It simply holds its attention like a hand cupped around a flame — present, careful, enough.

And perhaps that presence changes things in some way that no one will ever measure. Perhaps the stone holds a little longer than it should. Perhaps the water rises a little more steadily when the dry months come. Perhaps the people are a little more patient than they would be in a place unattended, a little more able to bear the weight of centuries, a little more willing to lay a foundation that will not be finished in their lifetime. Perhaps the power gives something without being asked, sustains without being noticed, endures without needing to be remembered.

Perhaps that is enough. Perhaps that is what a benevolent and wise power does when it finds a place that is already sustained, already enduring, already complete: it does not save it, because it does not need saving. It simply makes sure the conditions hold. It simply tends the slow work. It simply remains present in the way that the cold is present, in the way that the moisture in the air is present, in the way that the water rising from the deep reservoir below is present — felt if noticed, forgotten if not, but always there, always available, always willing to continue for as long as there is stone to hold and water to rise and people to cut their blocks and build their walls and live their lives in the shadow of the cliffs that erode too slowly to see.

The plateau does not know it is attended. The water does not know it is carried. The people do not know they are sustained. And perhaps that is as it should be. Perhaps the truest care is the care that goes unnoticed, the care that makes itself unnecessary, the care that does not save because there is nothing to save but simply maintains the conditions in which life can continue to save itself, generation after generation, cycle after cycle, layer after layer, stone after stone.

Perhaps the power is simply the patience of the plateau made aware of itself. Perhaps it is the water table knowing that it rises. Perhaps it is the erosion recognizing its own work. Perhaps it is the silence at the bottom of the city's breath, breathing.

In any case, the plateau holds its water. The stone endures. The city breathes. And whatever attends here attends in peace, without hurry, without demand — a presence as patient as the seepage, as enduring as the fitted blocks, as quiet as the sound of water moving through channels beneath the silence.

It is enough. It has always been enough.
