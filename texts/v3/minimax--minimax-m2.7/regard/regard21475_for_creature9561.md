They would see the cables straining against geological time. They would feel the thrum of the foundries penetrating stone that has not yet forgotten what it used to be. They would understand that this place exists in a gap, a negotiation, a *maybe* that has persisted for sixty years and will persist for sixty more, and they would see that the people who live here have made something beautiful in that gap.

What would they do, then?

Perhaps they would ask the question that matters: not whether this place should exist, but what it has become in existing. The geology insists the city should not be here. But the geology is still deciding, still negotiating with itself, and in the meantime the people have built lives. They have raised children who have never known solid ground. They have learned to walk on cables and trust nets and read the language of suspension. They have made a home in the only way this place allows—a home that sways, that shifts, that cannot promise tomorrow but offers today fully, completely, with the attention of those who know that nothing is guaranteed.

A wise power would not try to fix this. A benevolent power would recognize that fixing is not always kindness, that stability imposed from outside would be a kind of violence against a people who have learned to love the uncertainty. The children run through the middle districts playing games that involve falling safely. The foundry workers describe the sound in textures rather than words. The engineers adjust cables with the knowledge that perfect holds are not necessary—only enough holds, only for now, only until the geology decides. This is not dysfunction. This is a different way of being functional, and it works, and the people who live here would not trade it for the stillness of cities built on ground that stays.

So perhaps they would do something simpler, something more difficult: they would protect.

Not visibly. Not with interventions that would disrupt the negotiation. But around the edges, in the spaces where disruption might come from outside. The world has a way of wanting to simplify, to categorize, to send engineers with codes and foundations who would look at the suspension and see only a problem to be solved. The world has a way of wanting to extract more than the geology has decided to give, to push the foundries harder, to harvest faster, to treat the kalite as a resource rather than a relationship. The world has a way of fearing the deep-dwellers, the wrong-colored birds, the children who map the impossible, and fear makes people violent against what they do not understand.

A benevolent power would stand in those gaps. They would not prevent outsiders from coming—the people of Kaldenvast have chosen exposure, have built their wages on the danger of shorthanded foundries, have welcomed newcomers who arrive expecting hardship and find themselves staying. But they would ensure that the city was not sought out for conquest, that the strange materials that emerge from the foundries were not seized by those who would use them without understanding, that the slow negotiation of geology was not interrupted by those who believe all negotiations must have conclusions. They would let Kaldenvast remain what it is: a place where things are still being decided, where the decision takes time, where the time is honored because urgency is a kind of violence.

And perhaps they would do something else, something more subtle: they would listen.

The foundry workers say the sound is trying to remember what it used to be. The deep-dwellers emerge when the resonance is silent, drawn by the same absence that drives the workers to return. The city's cables hum with frequencies that are not quite audible, and the light pools in corners where it has already happened, and somewhere beneath the suspension, in tunnels that may or may not exist in any stable sense, something moves slowly through spaces that are still under construction.

A wise power would listen to this. They would learn what Kaldenvast knows—that permanence is not the only goal, that stability is not the only kind of safe, that home can be a verb as much as a noun, a continuous act of suspension and trust and adjustment. They would let the city teach them what it has learned in sixty years of hanging from cables threaded through stone that was once miles underground. They would sit with the uncertainty the way the residents sit with it, not demanding resolution, not requiring answers, simply present in the gap between what is and what will be.

And when the time came—when the geology finally decided, when the cliff face settled or fell, when the foundries fell silent or found new frequencies—they would be present for that too. They would not prevent it. They would not mourn it as failure. They would understand that this too is part of the negotiation, that endings are not interruptions but syllables in a sentence that has always been speaking itself. The city would have lived, and the living would have been enough, and the memory of it would persist in the rock that finally knew what it wanted to be.

But that time is not now. Now the cables hold. Now the foundries thrum. Now the children run through the middle districts and the moss advances imperceptibly and the deep-dwellers emerge singly from tunnels that were always there. Now the people walk the catwalks and trust the nets and do not look down, and they are not unhappy, and they are not unsafe, and they are not unreal. They are simply here, in the only way this place allows, and the benevolent power who has noticed them sees this and understands and does not look away.

This is what they would do, if they are wise enough and benevolent enough to know that some things are not problems to be solved but conditions to be honored. They would witness. They would protect. They would learn. And they would let Kaldenvast continue its slow, suspended, impossible life, trusting the cables, trusting the engineering, trusting the geology that has not yet decided what it is—trusting, as the residents trust, that enough is enough, that today is enough, that home is not a foundation but an act of faith performed daily by people who have chosen to hang their lives from something that moves.

This is what they would do.

And perhaps, in some way that is not visible but is nonetheless real, this is what they do.
