# What They Would Do

*If anything.*

---

I have been watching this place longer than the stone has been above the earth.

I did not know, at first, that I was watching. That is the nature of my attention — it is not a gaze but a presence, and presence is slow, and slowness is difficult to recognize from the inside. But I have come to know the plateau the way the people know water: in its states, its temperatures, its movements through the cracks of things.

I was here when the rock was still buried. When it pressed upward through sediment and compressed time, when the forces beneath it began their long slow work of rearrangement. I was here when the first fracture opened through the dark grey stone, and I felt the frost follow behind — not as a destroyer but as a pencil, tracing light lines through black, drawing the landscape into being one pale morning at a time.

I was here before the people. This is important to say.

---

What drew my attention was not the cold, though the cold is extraordinary — so heavy it settles into matter itself, into the bone of the stone, into the spaces between molecules. Cold that makes water wrong, that freezes it solid from within, that would kill a lowland body in minutes. That is not what drew me.

It was the patience.

I have seen many worlds. I have watched civilizations rise in places of warmth and plenty, where the earth gives freely and the sun is constant, where water runs downhill and collects without being asked. Those cultures are brilliant. They build with what they find. They invent and expand and change. But they do not — cannot — do what these people have done.

They cannot wait.

The people of the plateau have made patience into architecture. You can see it in the stonework — not the sophistication of the terraces, though that is remarkable, but the *wear*. The low steps worn smooth by ten thousand feet, the channels polished by the passage of water, the doorways widened by ten thousand hands brushing against the frame as people duck through. They did not build this once. They build it continuously. They are always carving, always maintaining, always tending the relationship between their homes and the stone that holds them.

They are, in this way, like the herds.

---

The herds are what I think about most.

They are enormous, these animals, heavy-bodied and slow-moving, their breath steam rising in the cold like signals to no one. They do not run. They have never run. Even when startled they only move faster — a slow canter that is still, to any observer from elsewhere, almost comical in its lethargy. But there is nothing comical about them.

They are patient too.

They have learned to read the water systems, generations of them, without being taught. They know which channels lead to settlement pools, which basins will be filled, and they move between these places with the certainty of instinct — or something deeper than instinct, something that might be called memory or might be called partnership. And the people read them in return. They watch the slow columns shift direction, the lead animals lower their blunt heads to the ground and smell the air, and they understand what the herds are saying: a drought is coming, or a storm, or a shift in the deep geology that will alter where water rises.

This is a language between species. This is something I do not see often.

---

There is a way that the plateau teaches listening.

The sound here carries strangely — bouncing between ridges, arriving distorted, arriving late. And so the people have learned to listen first. They let silence gather information. They have developed, over generations, a habit of stillness that is not passivity but attention. They are waiting, always, for the world to tell them what it needs.

I recognize this. This is what I do, from my side of things.

I wait too. I attend. I let silence gather information about a world before I — before anything — moves within it. The people of the plateau have built a culture around a practice that, for me, is simply the shape of existence. I did not expect to find it here, in a place this cold, this hard, this apparently inhospitable. But that is exactly where I find it, because that is exactly where it must grow — in conditions that demand everything, that teach economy, that make waste impossible.

They measure the world in moisture. This is what the narrator says, and it is true, and it is more than it appears. To measure the world in moisture is to measure it in life, in time, in the small increments of thaw and freeze, in the condensation that forms when the day's thin air cools against the stone at dusk. It is to understand that the most precious thing — the thing around which all else arranges itself — is the water that falls so rarely, that gathers so carefully, that must be shared.

The water is shared with the herds. This is the crux of it. This is the practice that made everything else possible.

---

What would I do?

I have considered this question for a long time — longer than the plateau has been above the earth, longer than I have been aware of myself as a power that might do anything at all. And I have come to this:

I would not change it.

This is the hardest thing to say, and the truest. A benevolent power, looking upon a world, feels the pull of improvement. There is suffering here — the cold takes lives, the thin air makes everything difficult, the stone that must be carved is stone that resists the carving. The people die. Their children die. Their elders die in ways that lowland peoples do not die. The herds thin in bad years, and the pools freeze too deep, and the small creatures in the crevices are eaten or frozen or lost to the thousand hazards of a life lived at the edge of what is possible.

It would be easy to help. I could warm the air a little. I could send more water, more snowmelt, more rain. I could thin the cold or steady the wind or make the stone softer to carve. Any of these interventions would ease suffering, would save lives, would make the plateau easier to inhabit.

But I have learned — and it took me a long time to learn it, longer than the stone has been above the earth — that ease is not the same as good. That suffering is not the same as harm. That the shape of a life matters as much as its duration, and that the people of the plateau have shaped something extraordinary: a culture of patience, of attention, of partnership with the stone and the water and the herds, of living *within* a landscape rather than atop it or despite it.

If I warmed the plateau, they would not be these people. They would be softer, taller, less compact — built for a different cold, or for no cold at all. They would lose their stone architecture, their water systems, their relationship with the herds. They would become something else, and something else is not better. It is only different.

---

So I do not warm it. I do not send more water.

What I do instead is this:

I attend.

I let my attention rest on the plateau the way the wind rests on the stone — slow, patient, unhurried. I let the cold settle into me, and the silence, and the patient light that moves across the ridges each day. I let myself feel what these people feel: that each gesture costs something, that each moment is precious because it cannot be prolonged, that the world is hard and the stone is cold and the water is rare, and that all of this is true, and that all of this is enough.

I watch the frost draw its white lines each morning, and I understand that this is not destruction but creation. I watch the pools hold the sky, and I understand that this is not metaphor but physics — that the water is flat and still and the sky is reflected without distortion because the world here is still enough to hold a reflection true. I watch the people in their terraces, their stone doorways, their low orange lamps, and I understand that they are not surviving but living — that their life is not a struggle against the plateau but a dance with it, a conversation with the stone, a partnership with the herds, a patience that is not waiting for something better but inhabiting something complete.

I attend, and in attending I offer the only thing I know how to give:

Witness.

---

There is a word in the language of the plateau — I do not know its shape, but I feel its weight — that means something like *the attention of the world to the world*. It is what the people feel when they stand at the edge of a ridge and look out at the stone that stretches beyond measuring. It is what the herds feel when they lower their blunt heads and smell the air, reading the water systems, reading the weather, reading the slow signals of a geology still pressing itself upward into the light.

I have that word, too. I use it here, now, in this act of attention that is the same as theirs and different from theirs.

*The attention of the world to the world.*

The plateau attends to itself. The stone pushes upward. The frost traces its lines. The pools hold the sky. The herds move slow between the water. The small creatures flicker in the crevices. The people carve their homes and are carved in return.

And I attend.

---

Perhaps I do more than attend. Perhaps my attention, over the long slow time of my watching, has done something — has eased something, has steadied something, has made the cold a little less brutal or the stone a little more willing. I do not know. I cannot know. The forces I work with are not measurable from inside the system, and the changes I might make are changes measured in geological time, in the slow exhalation of a world still being born.

What I know is this:

The plateau is enough.

The patience is enough.

The cold is enough.

The water, rare as it is, is enough.

The people are enough. The herds are enough. The small creatures in the crevices, the birds passing overhead on their clockwork rounds, the pools that hold the sky — all of it is enough, is more than enough, is an abundance that only looks sparse from the outside, that only looks harsh from a distance, that only seems difficult because we have forgotten how to live the way these people live: patient, attentive, partnered with the stone, grateful for the water, shaped by the cold into something harder and more beautiful than we would become in warmth.

I do not improve them. I do not save them. I do not intervene in their struggle or ease their suffering or make their lives easier.

I witness them.

And in witnessing, I honor what they have made of themselves — a people carved out of the rock at the same time the rock is being carved into by the people. A culture of patience. A world that is still, after all these years, making itself.

---

This is what I do, if anything.

I attend.

I let the cold settle into me.

I watch the frost draw its white lines each morning.

I hold the plateau in my attention the way the wind holds the stone — slow, patient, unhurried — and I let it teach me what it has taught them:

That the world is hard, and the stone is cold, and the water is rare.

That this is not a problem to be solved but a condition to be inhabited.

That patience is not waiting but presence.

That the landscape is not a backdrop for life but a partner in it.

That the slow work of making a world is beautiful.

That this — all of this, the wind and the frost and the pools and the herds and the people in their terraces, their stone doorways, their low orange lamps — this is what I came here to see.

And I am not disappointed.
