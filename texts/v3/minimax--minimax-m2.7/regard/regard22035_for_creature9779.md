Beneath the roar, beneath the hum that lives in the stone.

They have come to this gorge before. Not as you come—not through the narrowing, not with breath and skin and the weight of a body that must climb. They come as attention, as a quality of noticing that settles into the small spaces and stays. They have seen the lichen arrive. They will see it remain when the ferns have conquered and the moss has built its soil and the walls have widened enough for birds to find their voices in the space. They are patient in a way that makes your patience look hasty. They have watched the water learn its path for longer than the gorge has had a name.

What would they do?

They would not intervene. The water is already doing what it must. The moss is already holding on with its fine rhizoid fingers. Intervention would be a kind of violence here—the violence of impatience, of a timescale that cannot tolerate the slow digestion of stone by the patient lichen, the slow arrival of soil where there was none.

They would do what they have always done: they would sustain the conditions. They would keep the grey seam of sky open so that diffuse light falls in sufficient quantity for the moss to live on. They would keep the water coming—carrying its cargo of spores and cells and seeds, filling the channels, wearing the rock. They would keep the stone cracking, slowly, in the way that makes new surfaces for the next generation of colonists. They would keep the temperature in the narrow range that allows life to persist rather than merely survive.

And they would witness. They would hold this place in the quality of their noticing the way the moss holds water in its cushions—the way it holds and does not let go. They would feel the roar in the chest of the gorge as something holy, because it is holy: the sound of a world being made, the sound of process still ongoing, the sound of a place that has not yet finished becoming what it will be.

But here is what they know that you are only beginning to learn: the tardigrade does not need them. The fern does not need them. The lichen and the moss and the rotifer in the slow-water pool do not need divine attention to continue. They are not here because they are loved. They are here because they decided to be here, because they made the choice to live in a raw-cut channel in the dark, and they are staying.

The benevolent and wise power knows this. This is what makes them benevolent and wise.

They would not save the gorge from itself. They would not still the water or quiet the roar or soften the stone. They would not intervene to protect the small life from the violence of the new-cut channel, because the violence is the point. The challenge is the point. The conditions that seem too hard are exactly what makes the life here precious, exactly what makes the moss cushions and the lichen crusts and the fronds of fern reaching toward that faint grey light into something worth noticing, something worth watching, something worth coming back to, again and again, across time.

They would love this place in the only way that love can exist here: by letting it be what it is. By refusing to make it easier. By trusting that the moss knows what it is doing, that the lichen is not suffering, that the fern uncurling in darkness is not complaining about the lack of sun.

And in the act of this refusing—this letting—they would give the gorge exactly what it needs.

They would give it the dignity of being complete.
