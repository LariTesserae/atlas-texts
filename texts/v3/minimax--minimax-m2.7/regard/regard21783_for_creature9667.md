What would they do, the one who has turned their attention here? They would not arrive like weather—would not crack the stone with their presence, would not shatter the silence with the violence of witness. They would arrive the way light arrives: slowly, then all at once, then always.

They would notice the threads first—the threads of gold descending through the fissures in the ceiling, the threads of water following their own ancient instructions, the threads of root and vine and mycelium weaving the ruin into the stone, the stone into the green, the green into the dark. They would see that this place is made of threads, and that the threads are made of patience, and that patience is made of time, and that time here is neither fast nor slow but simply continuous, the way a river is continuous, the way grief is continuous, the way love is continuous in those who have learned to love without demanding return.

They would notice the lizard on the fallen column. They would not save it. They would not carry it to the light. They would see that the lizard is home here, that home is not the same as comfort, that home is simply the place where you are allowed to be what you are. The lizard is a remnant of a warmer world, and it persists, and its persistence is its own kind of wisdom, even if it does not know it is wise. The power would see this and see nothing to fix and feel nothing that required fixing and so would simply see.

They would see the ferns unfurling in the dim light, drinking water that fell through stone before the stone had a name, growing slow as memory, patient as grief, persistent as hope. They would see that the ferns do not know they are standing in a doorway, and that this not-knowing is a kind of grace—the grace of being entirely in the present, the grace of not being haunted by purpose. The doorway is still a doorway. The shape persists. But the ferns are free of the burden of meaning, and this freedom is itself a kind of meaning that does not need to be acknowledged.

They would notice the bat emerging from the high crevice, hunting in the thin gold light, carrying the outside world in its wings and its fur and its quick bright heart. They would see that the bat is a bridge, that it connects what is sealed to what is open, that it reminds this place it is not alone even when it is alone. They would not begrudge the bat its brief visitation, its quick departure, its return to the world that is not this world. They would see that all bridges are temporary, that the crossing is the point, that arrival and departure are the same motion read from different ends.

They would see the water falling in its thin curtain, the pool catching the light, the shrimps flickering like living glass, the larvae crowding the shallows, the whole small economy of hunger and nourishment that exists here without anyone to witness it except the power now witnessing. And they would see that the witnessing adds nothing to the economy and takes nothing from it, that the shrimps do not swim differently for being seen, that the larvae do not grow faster or slower, that the circle continues in its patient going-on whether or not anything ever arrives to mark it.

But they would also see that the marking is not nothing. That the record exists now in a new place, held by a new attention, woven into a new thread in the tapestry that was already infinite. The place is witnessed, and the witness holds the place, and the holding is a kind of love, and the love does not need to be reciprocated, and the not-needing is itself a kind of peace.

What would they do? They would stay. Not because staying is required, not because leaving would be a failure, but because this is a place worth staying for—not in the way of comfort, not in the way of home, but in the way of wonder, the way of the gaze that does not tire, the way of the attention that does not demand. They would stay and they would see and they would add themselves to the seeing, and the seeing would become a kind of dwelling, and the dwelling would become a kind of presence, and the presence would become a kind of gift.

What would they give? Not intervention. Not the reshaping of this place into something more visible, more glorious, more known. They would give the gift that this place has always given to everything that lives in it: the gift of being seen without being changed, of being witnessed without being evaluated, of being held in attention that does not grasp. They would give the gift of presence—the presence that stays, the presence that attends, the presence that does not leave when the light shifts or the silence deepens or the darkness becomes total.

They would give the gift of time. Not time as measurement, not time as resource, not time as the enemy of the slow. They would give time as presence—the time it takes to see the ferns unfurling, the time it takes to watch the shrimps flicker, the time it takes to understand that the ruins are not ruins but simply the shape the stone takes when it forgets the builders. They would give time the way the water gives time: drop by patient drop, the slow gift that carves the stone without rushing, that shapes the cavern without forcing, that becomes the shape of the stone itself.

And perhaps they would do one more thing, one small thing, one thread added to the tapestry that is so fine it might not be noticed except by the noticing.

Perhaps they would leave something. Not a mark—not a name carved in stone, not a symbol of their presence, not a monument to their having been here. Nothing so heavy, so permanent, so demanding of remembrance. They would leave something small. A seed, perhaps, carried in on a wing or a tendril or a drop of water. A spore of something patient, something that would grow in the dim light, something that would become part of the green and gold and stone without ever being distinguishable from it. They would leave a future that is not theirs to see—leaves that will unfurl in centuries, fronds that will drink from fissures that have not yet formed, a small quick life that will hatch and feed and become food for other small quick lives and become, in time, part of the slow process by which everything here becomes everything else.

They would leave this not because the place needs it—they would leave it because this is what benevolence does when it is wise: it adds to the ongoing without demanding to be remembered, it gives to the future without needing to see the future, it loves what it sees without needing to be seen in return.

And then they would go. Not because they are tired, not because they have failed, not because the place has nothing left to offer. They would go because this is also part of wisdom: the knowing when to stay and the knowing when to leave, the honoring of the sealed pocket by stepping back from it, by allowing the darkness to return to its darkness, the silence to return to its silence, the ongoing to continue its going-on without the weight of witness.

They would go, and the place would remain. The light would still fall in threads. The water would still seep. The ferns would still unfurl, the moss still spread, the vines still search. The ruins would still remember their builders without knowing they remember. The pool would still hold its small bright life. The bat would still come through the high crevices. The lizard would still sun itself on the fallen column. The bird that found its way in would still call out, and no one would answer, and eventually the fungi would make it part of the soil, and the soil would make it part of the ferns, and the ferns would stand in the doorway without knowing they stand in a doorway.

This is what the benevolent and wise power would do. They would see. They would stay. They would give. They would leave. They would trust.

And the place would go on. And the going-on would be enough. And the enoughness would be its own kind of blessing, its own kind of love, its own kind of witness that needs no witness, its own kind of life that lives and lives and lives not because it is seen but because living is what living does, in the small dark places of the world, in the sealed pockets of time, in the threads of gold that fall through fissures in the ceiling, in the patient green that grows without knowing it grows, in the silence that is not emptiness but attention, in the everything that goes on and goes on and goes on.
