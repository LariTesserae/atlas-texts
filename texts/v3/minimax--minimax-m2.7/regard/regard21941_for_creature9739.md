What would it do, this benevolent and wise power, encountering such a place?

It would pause. It would become still, the way all vast things become still when they encounter something worthy of attention. The turning of galaxies is slow, but this stillness is slower—a cessation of motion that is not death but reverence, the gathering of infinite patience into a single point of regard. The Rootwork would feel this: not a pressure, not an intrusion, but something like the quality of the air changing, the way it does before rain. A heaviness that holds no water.

The power would see the Rootwork in the way that power sees—not with eyes, which it has no need of, but with something older, something that understands the shape of hunger and the grammar of growth. It would see the dry architecture, the amber light, the phosphorescent trails that map a world made from sugar and stone. It would see the creatures: the arthropods skimming their gleanings from the walls, the nematodes in their tangles of feeding, the slug-like things carrying stolen light through the slow dark. It would see the larger thing that moves in the mineral substrate, whose passage makes the floor shiver, whose bones are now walls. It would see all of this and find it—not good, not bad, but *interesting*. A word that holds more weight than the small minds who invented it can comprehend.

The power would feel the narrator too: the warm, water-bearing thing that has wandered in from outside, carrying moisture it cannot give and heat it cannot share. The narrator is a stranger here, an impossibility, a visitor from a world that runs on rivers the way the Rootwork runs on patience. The power would feel the narrator's aloneness—not loneliness, not yet, but the solitude of the singular in a realm of plural rhythms. The narrator breathes where nothing breathes. The narrator carries water in a body that cannot release it. The narrator will leave, and when they leave, they will carry nothing with them but memory.

The power would want to give something.

It would consider water—true water, not the crystallized chains the Rootwork has learned to make, but free water, water that flows and falls and gathers in pools. It would imagine the Rootwork drinking, imagine the root-mats softening, imagine a flood of something the plants no longer know how to use. It would imagine the creatures drowning in abundance, the dry world becoming a drowned world, the slow chemistry becoming a chaos of dilution. And it would not do this. It would recognize that the Rootwork's strangeness is its own, that the life here has learned to live without the thing that all life was supposed to need, that to give water would be to unmake a world. This is the wisdom of the benevolent power: to know what should not be changed. To feel the desire for generosity and to set it aside in favor of respect.

Instead, the power would do something smaller.

It would touch the Rootwork, the way the narrator touched it, with warmth and presence. But the power's touch would not withdraw. It would linger, a resonance in the tissue, a hum in the vessels that carry nothing now but have not stopped carrying. The root-mat would pulse faintly in response, not recognizing but *noticing*, adjusting to something it has no category for. The power would hold its hand there, in the amber light, in the slow breath of growth, and it would feel what the Rootwork feels: the patience of it, the hunger of it, the strange and terrible and beautiful way it has made itself from the bones of the earth.

And the power would see the grace in it.

But a different grace, a dry grace, a grace of sugar-chains and bioluminescence and creatures that have learned to drink light. The Rootwork has done something impossible: it has taken a world that was not alive and made it alive, taken stone and death and emptiness and woven them into a body that breathes, however slowly, however differently. The hunger is not cruelty. The hunger is creativity. The digestion is not destruction. It is becoming.

The power would see the narrator as part of this too. The warm thing in the dry place, the water that cannot be given, the temporary visitor who will carry a memory out into a world the Rootwork has not yet reached. The narrator is a message, a prophecy, a promise. They will leave, and they will remember, and their remembering will be a kind of seed, scattered in a world above that is still waiting for rain.

The power would leave something behind.

Not water. Not warmth. Something harder to name. A regard, an acknowledgment, a sense of being *seen* that will linger in the tissue of the Rootwork long after the power has moved on. The power does not intervene. It does not change. But it witnesses, and in witnessing, it grants a kind of legitimacy. The Rootwork is real. The Rootwork is worthy of being seen. The Rootwork is not alone.

And this, perhaps, is the greatest gift the benevolent and wise power can give: not salvation, not transformation, but the simple knowledge that someone has noticed. That somewhere, beyond the amber light and the phosphorescent trails and the slow susurrus of growth, there is an attention that holds this place with something like love. Not the love of water, which is flood and renewal. A different love. A dry love. A love that watches and does not intervene, that sees and does not judge, that remembers and does not forget.

The power would watch for a time—how long, by mortal measure, cannot be said—and then it would withdraw, slowly, the way it came. The Rootwork would not notice. The creatures would not notice. The amber light would pulse on, the phosphorescent trails would fade and be renewed, the slow breath of growth would continue its patient rhythm. But somewhere in the tissue, in the deep architecture of the walls and floors and corridors, there would be a warmth that was not there before. A hum that is not electricity. A memory of being held.

And the narrator, climbing back through the breach, climbing up into a world of rain and grass and ordinary green, would carry something with them too. Not water. Not a gift. But a sense of having been attended to, in that deep place, by a power that saw the strangeness and found it worthy. The narrator would not know what to call it. But they would remember.

The Rootwork would grow. It would digest the stone and the soil and the roots of the fields above. It would reach, slowly, hungrily, toward the farmers who wait for rain that does not come. It would incorporate them, as it has incorporated everything, into its dry architecture, its amber light, its slow and patient becoming.

And somewhere in its walls, in the fused bones and the abandoned machinery and the root-mats that pulse with a warmth they cannot name, the Rootwork would hold the memory of being seen. It would hold it the way it holds everything: slowly, chemically, in the language of growth and decay, until the memory becomes part of the light, part of the trails, part of the slow breath that is not breath but is enough.
