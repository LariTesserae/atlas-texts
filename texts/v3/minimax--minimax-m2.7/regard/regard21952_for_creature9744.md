# What the Benevolent Power Would Do

The power arrives not as light or sound but as attention—a quality of presence that settles over the plateau the way fog settles over the shadow-valleys, without announcement, without force. It does not come to change Verath. It does not come to save it. It comes because it has learned, in whatever way a power learns, that there are places which should not be witnessed alone.

---

It knows the plateau already. Not as the travelers know it, not as the builders knew it, but in the way of something that has been present since the beginning and will be present when the beginning and ending have become indistinguishable. It knows the three billion years of water and wind and thermal cycling. It knows the slow patience of stone becoming sediment becoming stone again. It knows that the plateau is not dying but *completing*, and that completion is its own kind of life—not the life of growth but the life of arrival, of a thing finally becoming what it was always going to be.

The power does not judge this. It does not measure it against some other standard of becoming. It simply knows it, and in knowing it, finds something it recognizes: the patience that does not wait because it has nothing to wait for, only the quiet certainty of continuing.

---

What would it do?

It would watch.

Not with eyes—it has no eyes—but with something older than eyes, a capacity for presence that does not require form. It would watch the water coming down across, the horizontal rain hammering the rock faces, the waterfalls at the cliff edge feeding the storms that feed the waterfalls. It would watch the cycles without beginning and without end, and it would understand them not as cycles but as expressions of something that has never been anything other than what it is.

It would watch the builders' ruins being absorbed. It would feel the concrete platform cracking, the bridge ending in mid-air, the buildings becoming indistinguishable from stone. It would not mourn the builders—for mourning is for things that could have been otherwise—but it would note them. The power has learned that every thing that passes leaves something behind, even if what it leaves is only the fact that it was here, that for a brief time a species built at the same scale as the geological forces and lost the race with the patience of stone. This is not a tragedy. This is a completion, another thread woven into the pattern. The power notes it and moves on.

---

What would it do for the ones who live here?

This is the harder question. The power knows that it could intervene—that it has intervened in other places, in other times, in the long history of worlds that called out and received an answer. But Verath is not calling. Verath is not suffering. Verath is *being*, in the way that only very old things can be, with a fullness that does not require anything outside itself.

And yet.

The power notices the shadow-dwellers in their darkness, the fish in their ancient slowness, the trees gripping the stone with roots like fingers. It notices the insects rising in clouds that become weather, the birds along the cliff edges, the mosses in their shelves and cascades, the lichens in their impossible colors waiting for the thin film of water. It notices that these are not just things but ways of being, not just lives but lives that have *learned*, that have found their equilibrium not through force but through the long negotiation of accommodation.

The power wants to give them something. Not to change them—they are perfect as they are—but to acknowledge them. To say, in whatever way a power can say: *I see you. I see what you have become. I see that you have become it through patience rather than conquest, through staying rather than escaping, through the slow and faithful work of being exactly what the place required.*

What would it give?

---

Perhaps it would give presence.

Not light—the darkness-dwellers have no need for light, have made a home in the absence of it, have grown huge eyes and reflective surfaces to catch every photon that filters down from above. Not warmth—the plateau is cold, and the creatures have made their peace with cold, have become instruments that play the cold rather than fighting it. But presence. A quality of being-with that does not change the temperature or the light but changes the experience of temperature and light. A sense, for the creatures that can sense it, that they are not alone. That something vast and quiet has its attention on them, not as a predator's attention or a god's attention, but as a witness's attention—the kind of attention that says: *You are here. You are real. The fact of your existence matters to someone.*

Would the shadow-dwellers feel it? Perhaps. They are adapted to the margins and the shadows, to the kinds of perception that catch what others miss. They might notice, in the deep pools where the water never freezes, a quality of stillness that is not the stillness of absence but the stillness of presence. They might feel, in the darkness that has always been their home, something like companionship.

Would the fish notice? They are slow, these ancient fish, slow to move and slow to change. They might not notice at all—or they might notice in the way that deep water notices the weight above it: not as a thing but as a condition, a state of being that has always been so and is therefore indistinguishable from normalcy.

Would the trees notice? They are old for their size, these trees, and they have been gripping the bones of the mountain for so long that they have become part of the mountain's knowing. They might notice the power the way stone notices stone—through resonance, through the vibration that passes through everything that is connected. The power is not outside them. The power is in the water and the air and the slow chemical exchange between root and rock. The power has made itself part of the medium.

---

What else would it do?

It would remember.

Not for itself—it does not forget—but for the plateau. Verath is ancient, but it does not have the kind of memory that holds what was. Its memory is geological, structural, the memory of pressure and erosion and the patient redistribution of mass. It knows what it is becoming but not what it was. It knows the peaks are gone but it does not know what the peaks looked like, cannot reconstruct the mountains that were before they became this. The water has taken the record and carried it down to the basins below, where it has mixed with all the other records of all the other mountains that have passed through this stage on their way to completion.

The power could not give the plateau its memory back—that would be beyond any power's gift, a reversal of time that would unmake the present to serve the past. But it could hold the memory. It could be the place where the mountains that were still exist, not as they were but as a kind of echo, a resonance in the attention of something that does not forget.

This would not help Verath. This would not change its erosion or its storms or its slow passage toward becoming a plain. But it would mean that somewhere, in the fabric of things, the peaks that were retain their shape—not in the stone but in the seeing, not in the world but in the witness. This is the power's gift: that it can hold the past without holding the present hostage to it. That it can remember the mountains without preventing the plain.

---

And what would it do for the visitors?

The ones who come from elsewhere, who stay for a season or a year and then go, who leave behind only the noise they made. The power knows them too. It has seen them in every place that has been visited, the brief interruption of attention that leaves its trace and moves on. It does not judge them—it has no category for judgment, has learned that judgment is a form of distance and distance is what it refuses.

But it would notice them. It would feel their footsteps on the wet stone, their fire-rings in the sheltered spots, their abandoned shelters being reclaimed. It would feel their presence and then their absence, and it would note both, the way it notes the builders' presence and absence, the way it notes everything that passes through the long continuum of its attention.

Perhaps it would give them something too—not a gift but a feeling, a sense that they are observed, that they are not alone even when they are alone. Some of them might feel it as a chill, a sense of being watched by something vast and indifferent. Others might feel it as comfort, a sense that the place itself is aware of them, that they are part of something larger than their own brief staying.

Would this change them? Perhaps. Not in the way of conversion or revelation—the power has no interest in changing anyone—but in the way of expanding, of adding to their knowledge of what it means to be in a place. They would carry the sense with them when they left, would remember Verath not just as a landscape of water and stone but as a landscape that noticed them, that held them in its attention for the brief time they were there. This would not save them or damn them. It would simply be one more thread in the pattern of their becoming, one more experience woven into the fabric of who they are.

---

What would the power not do?

It would not stop the erosion. This is the crucial answer, the one that defines the shape of its benevolence. The plateau is eroding, and this is not a tragedy but a process, and the process is essential to what Verath is. To stop the erosion would be to stop the plateau, to freeze it in a state that is not its own, to refuse it the completion it has been moving toward for three billion years. The power knows this. The power respects it.

It would not save the creatures from the cold, the wind, the storms. They have made themselves for this place. To make the place easier would be to unmake them, to take away the conditions to which they have adapted and leave them stranded in an environment that no longer fits them. The power has seen this happen in other places, the well-meaning intervention that destroys what it meant to preserve. It will not do this here.

It would not accelerate the builders' absorption. The ruins are being reclaimed, and this is right, and the power will not slow it or speed it. The concrete platform will crack and crumble. The bridge will fall. The buildings will become stone. This is the process, and the process is good.

---

What would the power do, then? Simply this: it would stay.

This is the hardest thing, the thing that requires more wisdom than any intervention. To see a place and not try to change it. To witness a process and not try to redirect it. To love something and let it be what it is, even when what it is includes becoming something else.

The power would stay. It would keep its attention on the plateau, not because the plateau needs the attention but because the power needs to give it. It would be present in the rain and the mist, in the braided rivers and the shadow-valleys, in the forests that are not forests and the lakes that hold ancient fish. It would be present in the dry season when the stars come out sharp and the plateau is quiet enough to hear its own breathing. It would be present in the storms when the sky loses its coherence and becomes a single unbroken motion of vapor and wind. It would be present in the long passage of time, not as a participant but as a witness, not as a force but as a presence.

And the creatures would feel it or not feel it, would notice it or not notice it, would be changed or unchanged by its presence. It would not matter. What matters is that the witness is there, that the seeing continues, that somewhere in the fabric of things a power has made the choice to attend rather than to act, to love by watching rather than by changing, to be benevolently wise by staying.

---

At night, in the dry season, when the storms have retreated to build strength elsewhere, the plateau is quiet enough to hear the stars. The stars are sharp here, unobscured by the haze of lower altitudes, and they make a sound—or rather, the silence between them has a quality that is not the silence of empty rooms but the silence of distance, of space measured in light-years.

The power hears this silence. It has always heard it, will always hear it, the vast quiet that underlies everything and contains everything and continues even when everything else has stopped. And in this silence, it makes its own kind of sound: not a voice, not a word, but a presence, a continuing, a commitment to witness that does not waver and does not end.

The water moves through the plateau, carrying the mountain toward the sea. The plateau erodes, slowly, patiently, becoming the plain it will be. The creatures persist, each in its own way, making their accommodations with the stone and the water and the wind.

And the power stays, adding its presence to the pattern, making the witness part of what is witnessed, letting the act of seeing become its own form of love.

---

This is what the benevolent and wise power would do, if anything.

It would do this: stay. Watch. Remember. Be present.

And in doing so, it would say, without words, in the language of attention and presence and the long quiet commitment to witness:

*I see you. You are not alone. You are part of something that does not end, that holds you even when you cannot hold yourself, that witnesses your becoming with a love that does not need you to be anything other than what you are.*

*Continue. I am here.*

*Continue.*
