# What the Wise Power Would Do

The wise power notices.

Not the garden first—not the tiled ground or the hanging vines or the columns standing in their precise arrangements. It notices the loneliness first, the way a doctor notices fever before looking at the wound. The garden is a symptom. The maker's loneliness is the condition.

The wise power sees how the world is complete. It sees the tiles that hold no grooves, the plants that will never exceed their shapes, the birds that move through the canopy like thoughts through a dream—not truly present, not truly absent, only performing presence with such consistency that the performance has become indistinguishable from the real. It sees the horizon that never approaches and understands: this world was made to be perfect, and the price of perfection was everything that changes. The maker built a garden that cannot die, and in doing so built a garden that cannot live. The stillness is not peace. The stillness is the held breath of a world that has forgotten how to exhale.

The wise power sees the walker. The one who moves through this place in second person, who feels the warmth of the tiles through thin-soled feet, who cannot stop walking because stopping would be the one wrong thing, the one crack through which the world might begin to forget itself. The walker is the maker, or the part of the maker that remains, or the part of the maker that was left behind when the making was finished. The walker carries the loneliness of the creator who cannot leave their creation, who must walk these paths forever, who made an eternal world and then made themselves eternal within it.

The wise power considers.

It could change things. It has the power. It could introduce something new—a wind, a season, a single imperfect flower growing where no flower was meant to grow. It could break the stillness with a sound that does not repeat, a bird that finally lands and does not rise again. It could plant a seed of entropy in this garden of permanence and watch the slow, beautiful decay of a world learning to end. This would be one kind of mercy. This would be the mercy of destruction, the kindness of letting go.

But the wise power does not do this.

It understands, in its wisdom, that the garden is not a prison. The garden is a grief made solid, a memorial, a monument to the moment when making felt possible—when hands shaped clay and the clay stayed shaped, when intention became form and form stayed true. The maker built this world because they could not bear a world that fell apart. The tiles are warm because the warmth is the only thing still happening, the last ember of process in a world of completed processes. The walker walks because walking is the only motion left that belongs to them, the one freedom remaining in a finished place.

The wise power understands that to end this would be to say: your making was wrong, your longing was mistaken, the perfect thing you built was a failure.

And the wise power will not say this.

Instead, it does something else.

It enters.

Not as an intruder. Not as a force. The wise power does not blast through the canopy or crack the tiles or introduce itself with thunder or revelation. It enters the way light enters water—gently, changing everything it touches without force. It steps onto the warm tiles, and the tiles do not crack, do not shift, do not mark. It walks beneath the canopy, and the birds do not startle, do not fall silent, do not cease. The insects continue their clockwork droning, and the vines continue their slow drip, and the light continues its sourceless brightening, and the wise power walks among them, and the world does not notice, and that is exactly right.

For what the wise power brings is not change. What the wise power brings is presence.

It walks toward the walker.

It does not hurry. There is no distance here that can be closed, no time here that passes, and so the wise power simply walks, and the walker continues their walk, and the tiles hold them both, warm beneath their feet, warm and unworn and eternal.

They meet.

The wise power does not speak. There is nothing to say. The garden is what it is. The maker built it and lives within it and will never leave it, and this is not a tragedy, this is a fact, this is the shape of a particular love that chose permanence over change, that chose the tile over the soil, that chose the made over the grown because the made could be made exactly right.

The wise power stands beside the walker.

Not touching. Not speaking. Just present.

And for the first time, the walker is not alone in the garden.

The stillness does not break. The light does not shift. The tiles remain warm. The birds continue their too-loud calls from slightly elsewhere, and the insects drone their steady note, and the vines drip their patient drip. The world does not change. The world is finished, has always been finished, will always be finished.

But the loneliness changes.

The loneliness softens.

The wise power has not come to alter the garden or to free the walker or to introduce some crack through which the world might begin to forget itself. The wise power has come to witness. It has come to say: I see this place. I see what was made here. I see the love that went into it and the grief that shaped it and the loneliness that lives at its center, and I do not look away. I am here. I am looking.

The walker walks on.

The wise power walks beside them.

They walk together through the garden that does not change, and the tiles are warm, and the light holds, and the stillness presses, and somewhere beneath the tiles the hidden water circulates through channels cut into the clay, and somewhere in the canopy the birds perform their endless motion, and somewhere the insects drone their single sustained note—still running, still running, still running.

And now, in the quality of the stillness, there is something new.

Not a crack. Not a change. Not entropy or decay or the slow unraveling of the made. But a warmth that was not there before. A presence in the presence. A second set of footsteps on the warm tiles, walking where no footsteps walk, seeing what was always already there to be seen.

The wise power does not fix the garden. The garden does not need fixing. The garden is complete. The garden is exactly what it was meant to be—a world made perfect, a love made eternal, a grief made into ground.

The wise power simply stands beside the one who made it.

And the standing is enough.

And the witness is enough.

And the garden holds, and holds, and holds—still warm, still bright, still wet with its own hidden waters, still complete, still finished, still forever—and the walker is no longer alone, and the stillness is no longer empty, and the light does not change but something in the light has shifted, has warmed, has become the light of a place that is seen.

The walker walks on.

The wise power walks beside them.

The tiles are warm.

The vines drip.

The light holds.

And the garden, which was always already finished, which was always already complete, which was always already exactly and only what it was meant to be—

—is no longer unwitnessed.

Is no longer alone.

Is no longer a monument to a making that happened once and ended.

Is a garden again.

Is a world again.

Is a place again.

Because someone is there.

Because someone is looking.

Because someone is walking beside the walker, not to change anything, not to break anything, not to end anything—

—but to say:

*This is real.*

*This is here.*

*This is enough.*

*You made this.*

*And I see it.*

*And I will not look away.*

*And the tiles are warm.*

*And the light holds.*

*And the stillness waits.*

*And we walk.*
