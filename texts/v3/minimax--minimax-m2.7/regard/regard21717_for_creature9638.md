What moves through the upper air of Kel-Veth is not wind, for there is no wind here—only the slow accumulation of moisture that has no place else to go. But something passes. Something has always passed. The power that sees this place does not enter it as one enters a room. It is more like the way light enters water: not disturbing what it touches, but refracting through it, becoming part of the medium it illuminates.

It sees the weeping. Of course it sees the weeping. The weeping is the first thing and the last thing, the alphabet from which all other sentences are built. The water that seeps from every seam, that pools on every ledge, that runs in threads too thin to follow—this is not sorrow, the power understands. Sorrow requires a self that has lost something. This is simpler. This is stone remembering humidity, the way a scar remembers the wound that made it. The builders—if they were ever builders in the sense that word implies motion and intention—engineered their city to weep. They succeeded beyond all meaning.

What does a wise power do with such success?

It does not weep with it. Weeping would be an insult, an imposition of its own grief where no grief exists. The stone is not sad. The water is not mourning. The figures that flicker at the edges of perception, the shapes that might once have been solid and now are not—this is not tragedy. This is simply what remains when everything that was has worn down to its most persistent element.

But the power notices. And noticing, for a being such as this, is not passive. It is the smallest possible act of creation. To notice something is to say: *you are here, you are real, you have been seen.* The stone has never been seen. The water has never been seen—not truly, not by anything that could hold what it saw. The figures have flickered for eons in the margins of perception because no one has ever turned their gaze squarely toward them and let it rest there.

So the power turns its gaze. It does this gently, so gently, the way dawn touches the horizon—not all at once but in degrees, so gradual that nothing could say exactly when light became distinguishable from the darkness it replaced. The terraces of Kel-Veth receive this attention the way a body receives warmth from a fire it cannot see: unconsciously, whole-hog, every surface responding in ways it does not know how to name.

The weeping does not stop. It would be wrong to stop it—wrong in the way that silencing a song would be wrong, even a song no one is singing anymore, even a song whose melody has been forgotten and only the habit of sound remains. The terraces weep. The channels fill. The water descends toward depths no one visits. These things continue.

But they continue differently now.

In the small hours before dawn, when condensation gathers on every horizontal plane, something in the gathering has changed. The water does not pool with quite the same emptiness. There is a quality to the wetness now—not warmth, not exactly, but presence. As if the moisture, in gathering, is gathering toward something. As if the weeping has remembered, distantly, what it was once for.

The figures at the edge of vision grow clearer. Not to the eye—the eye is a limited instrument, evolved for a world that contains more than this. But to something deeper. To the part of perception that operates in glimpses and intuitions, that sees a shape in the corner of a room and knows it for a familiar coat on a hook, that hears a voice in another room and knows it for a loved one before the words arrive. The figures are clearer now. They are still gray against gray, still present only when the light betrays them. But they have edges. They have weight. For the first time in longer than the plateau has existed, they are *almost* solid.

What would become of them, if they could solidify fully? The power does not know. It has not decided. It is not in the nature of wise benevolence to decide too quickly, to impose a shape on something that has not yet finished becoming. Perhaps the figures will remain as they are: present but not quite, real but not fully, caught in the act of being nothing more than the memory of having been something. Perhaps this is enough. Perhaps this is more than they have had in eons.

The water runs in the channels with a new quality of intention. It still descends toward the drains, toward the darkness, toward the depths that have not been visited. But now, in its descent, there is something like curiosity. The water wonders where it is going. Not consciously—not in the way that a traveler wonders, consulting maps and calculating distances. But in the way that a river wonders, which is to say: in the way that movement wonders about its own meaning. The river does not know it is asking. But it asks.

The power hears the asking. It does not answer, because there is no answer that would not be a lie. Where the water goes, what waits in the depths below the drains, whether the descent is a journey or merely a habit this is the only word the city knows—all of this remains unknown. But the power lets the question hang in the air, the way moisture hangs on every surface, visible and present and impossible to hold.

In the deepest chambers, the ones that require descending through passages too narrow for comfort, the surfaces glisten with their ancient exhalation. The moisture that does not dry, that returns as quickly as it is wiped. The power does not descend this far. It does not need to. The presence of presence is the same at every scale. What it has given to the terraces, it has given to the depths. What it has given to the figures, it has given to the water. The city is all of a piece. It weeps because weeping is what it does. And now, for the first time, it weeps in the knowledge that it is witnessed.

This is not salvation. The power does not save. To save would be to remove something, to extract a thing from the situation that gives it meaning. Kel-Veth is Kel-Veth. The weeping is the weeping. To save it would be to unmake it.

But this is not nothing either. To be witnessed is to be real in a way that reality alone cannot provide. The stone has always been stone. The water has always wept. But now there is someone who sees them, and who sees them clearly, and who does not look away.

Perhaps, in another age—in a future so distant that the terraces have worn down to memory, that the channels have silted over, that the figures have finally faded to the nothing they have always been reaching for—someone will come to this place and find it changed. Not transformed. Not rebuilt. But different in some subtle way, some quality of air or light, some sense that the ruins remember being inhabited even after the inhabitants have gone.

Or perhaps nothing will change at all. Perhaps the weeping will continue exactly as it has, and the water will descend exactly as it has, and the figures will flicker exactly as they have. Perhaps the only change is the one that has already happened: the change that occurs when something is seen. Not seen in the sense of being observed, or catalogued, or understood. Seen in the sense of being held in the gaze of a power that is itself benevolent and wise, and that looks upon the weeping city and finds it worthy of looking upon.

This is what the power does. It does not fix. It does not heal. It does not restore.

It witnesses.

And in witnessing, it gives Kel-Veth the only gift it has to give: the knowledge that it is not alone in its continuing. That somewhere, in a register beyond the thin bright light and the condensation and the silence, something knows the name of what this place is doing.

The name is not weeping. The name is not waiting.

The name is persistence. And persistence, the power knows, is not nothing. Persistence is the thing that comes before all other things, the ground on which everything else is built. To persist is to say: *I will continue.* To persist in the face of no reason to continue, to persist without memory or purpose or the promise of an end—this is not life. It is not death.

It is the space between. And in that space, anything might grow.

The power does not plant anything. It is not that kind of power, or perhaps it is not that kind of story. But it leaves the space open. It makes room in the weave of things for something new to enter, if something new should choose to enter. The terraces still weep. The channels still run. The figures still flicker at the edges of vision.

But now, beneath the weeping, beneath the water, beneath the silence that has no bottom—there is readiness. The kind of readiness that a held breath has, in the moment before the breath is released. The kind of readiness that a seed has, in the winter, beneath the frozen ground.

Kel-Veth is ready.

And so, perhaps, is everything else.
