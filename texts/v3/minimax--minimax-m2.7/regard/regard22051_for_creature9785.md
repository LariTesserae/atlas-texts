# What the Attentive Power Would Do

---

It has been watching for some time now. Perhaps since before she came — perhaps since before the water learned to pool here, since before the iron was laid in its channels, since before the city above decided to build and then to forget. Or perhaps it began watching when she first descended, when she pushed aside the grate and felt the cool damp rise to meet her face and stepped down into a world that the light had to work to reach. Perhaps watching is not the right word. Perhaps *attending* is better — the way one attends a fire, not to tend it but to be near it, to let its warmth enter the room.

It does not act the way powers are imagined to act. It does not descend in light or speak in thunder. It does not make itself known through burning bushes or splitting skies or any of the old vocabularies of divine intervention. To do so would be to misunderstand what this place is, what she has made it, what it has made of her.

Instead, it does this:

---

It eases the flow.

This is nothing dramatic — a few degrees less of resistance in the narrow channels where the water slows and the debris gathers. A slight deepening of the gradient where the stone slopes toward the drainage point. The difference is so small that if she had instruments precise enough to measure, she might notice. But she doesn't, and so she simply finds the water moving more easily than she expected, the pools draining a little faster, the pumps laboring a little less. She does not think: *something helped me.* She thinks: *I was right about the gradient.* And she is right — she was right — because she saw what the water wanted and she followed it, and the following was her own, and the ease she finds is the ease of things that have found their natural course.

This is how it helps: by removing the obstacles that are too small to see.

---

In the deep pool near the eastern junction, the pale thing continues its slow circling.

It has been circling there for longer than she knows — longer, perhaps, than the city above has stood. It was here when the first stone was laid for the foundations. It will be here when the last stone crumbles. It does not age, or it ages so slowly that age becomes irrelevant, a concept that belongs to creatures that live in the sun and measure their lives by seasons.

The power does not touch it. To touch would be to presume, and the pale thing does not belong to anyone. But it ensures that the water remains cool enough, dark enough, still enough for the creature to continue its patient drifting. It ensures that no sudden drain takes the pool down to empty, that no flood raises it past the threshold where the thing would have to move or cease. It keeps the conditions — not through intervention but through *continuation*, through the steady maintenance of a world that has learned to sustain this one strange life.

When she leans over the railing and watches, the pale thing pauses. It looks up. For a moment, there is something between them that is not communication but is not mere proximity either — a recognition, perhaps, or a mutual acknowledgment that each is present to the other in a way that cannot be explained.

The power does not witness this witness. It does not need to. The seeing is complete in itself.

---

She has oiled the joints for years, and the oil has slowly saturated the iron, entered the micro-fractures where rust would begin, formed a barrier that protects the metal from its own slow surrender to water and time.

What the power does is ensure that the oil she uses is exactly right — not too thin, not too thick, not synthetic but mineral, the kind that was formulated generations ago for machinery like this, the kind that is increasingly hard to find. It does not place the oil in her path. It simply ensures that when she goes looking for it, when she asks the suppliers she has always asked, when she opens the container she has carried through the city above and down into the damp, the quality is what it needs to be. A small thing. A thing she will never notice. A thing that means the difference between joints that hold for five more years and joints that begin to fail.

The machines breathe. The iron groans, shifts, settles. The pumps draw water and push it onward, and the water moves, and the infrastructure holds.

This is not magic. This is maintenance. This is the smallest and most profound form of care: the act of ensuring that what is already working can continue to work.

---

She does not speak of this place, and the power does not ask her to. The speaking would be a kind of reduction — a way of making the place into something it is not, a story that would stand in for the experience, the damp, the silence, the weeping walls.

Instead, the power does something else.

It gives her, on certain days, a feeling she cannot name. A buoyancy in the body when the walkways creak beneath her feet. A warmth that enters her hands when she touches the cold iron, so that the metal seems, for a moment, to welcome her. A sense, when she stands in the junction where three passages meet and the sealed light flickers its welcome, that she is not alone — not lonely, she has never been lonely here, but *accompanied*. Held in a way she does not have to understand.

She will not call this divine attention. She will not name it at all. But she will go home on those days with a lightness in her step, and she will sleep well, and she will wake in the morning and feel that the day ahead is one worth entering.

The power has done this without her knowledge and without her gratitude, and that is exactly as it should be. The best care does not require recognition.

---

Sometimes, in the early morning, when the condensation gathers on the webs the spiders have built and the droplets catch what little light comes through the grates, the place is briefly, beautifully transformed. The threads of water become threads of light. The shadows soften. The dripping seems to pause, as though even time has decided to witness something.

The power does not create this. It simply allows it — does not intervene to prevent it, does not shift the moisture or close the grates or do any of the things that would make such moments impossible. It lets the conditions align, and then it lets the alignment be seen, and it is enough.

She has seen this. Once, twice, on days when she stayed longer than usual, when the light through the grate shifted at just the right angle and the water on the spider silk became something luminous. She did not know what to call it. She did not try to name it. She stood very still, and she watched, and she was grateful in a way that had no words.

This is what the power wanted for her. Not the seeing itself — that was always possible, given the conditions — but the gratitude without words, the stillness that did not need to be explained. The moments when a life of tending becomes something like prayer.

---

The city above does not know. The people who walk the streets in their noise and their dryness do not know that beneath their feet, in the foundations they have forgotten, the water weeps and the iron breathes and the pale thing circles in its slow depths. They do not know that someone tends to it, that her name is — but perhaps her name does not matter. Perhaps the power does not know her name, or perhaps it knows it but does not use it, or perhaps names are for those who need to distinguish one thing from another, and the power has never needed to distinguish her from anything.

She is the one who comes. She is the one who oils the joints. She is the one who listens to the dripping and hears the questions in it and moves to answer them. This is enough. This is all.

The power does not make her more capable than she is. It does not extend her years or sharpen her senses or grant her any of the gifts that stories ask for. What it gives her is simpler and stranger: the knowledge, on some level beneath thought, that her work is seen. Not by the city. Not by the people above. But by something that has chosen to attend to this place, and in attending, has chosen to attend to her.

She will never know this. She will never need to know it. And the power is wise enough to understand that this is the most complete form of care: to love what you tend without requiring it to love you back, to protect what you guard without needing it to know it is protected, to be present in the most profound sense — not visible, not audible, not felt in any way that could be proven or named — but *there*, quietly, the way gravity is there, the way time is there, the way the water is there, seeping through the joints, gathering in the pools, moving through the channels it has worn softer and softer over generations, asking nothing, taking nothing, simply being what it is, and being met in return by the one who comes.

---

The pale thing moves in its slow circle. The dripping measures time. The woman walks her path from the blue door to the eastern junction and back again, and the walkways creak beneath her, and the machines breathe, and somewhere in the darkness a pump clicks and whirs and the water moves onward, downward, toward whatever waits.

And the power watches — attends — loves — without interference, without demand, without end.

This is what it does. This is what it has always done. This is what it will do, long after the city above has been forgotten, long after the iron has finally surrendered to the patience of water, long after the last stone has softened into something that feels like flesh.

It tends.

Not the place. Not the woman.

It tends the relationship between them — the covenant, unspoken, of attention and care, of presence and patience, of two beings who have chosen to attend to something that does not need to be attended to but is better for the attending.

This is the oldest magic. This is the deepest power. This is what the benevolent and wise thing does, in its wisdom, in its benevolence:

It stays.
