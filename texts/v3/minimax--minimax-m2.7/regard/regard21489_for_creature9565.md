# What the Power Would Do

---

It would arrive the way light arrives after rain—suddenly present, as if it had always been there and you had only just noticed. Not with thunder or proclamation, but with a quality of attention so complete that the air itself would feel different, charged with the particular density of being truly seen.

And what it would do first is look. Not at the landscape alone, but at the being who stands in the landscape, the one who has learned to hear the hum in the stone, who pauses when the silences come, who has written the drowned summits into language so that they might be remembered not only in erosion but in minds. It would look at this one—this witness—with the recognition that comes from knowing something rare when you see it. A chronicler of places that do not need chronicling. A lover of things that do not need to be loved to persist.

And because it is wise, it would not speak. Words would diminish. The plateau has its own language—the wind through the channels, the pulse of water through unseen rivers, the slow dreaming of stone—and to add another voice would be an intrusion, however well-meant. Instead, the power would simply be present, the way the geothermal warmth is present beneath the worn rock, the way the memory of heights remains in the shape of the plateaus. A breath that does not stir the air so much as deepen it.

What it would do, then, is witness in return.

It would hold this place in its attention the way the plateau holds the rain—openly, without grasping, receiving what is given and letting it go when it passes. It would honor the contracts the land has made with itself: the quick lives and the patient ones, the birds that wait and the lizards that are stone until they are not, the creatures in the deep gorges that have never seen the sky and do not miss it. It would recognize the rightness of this world, its completeness, the way it has found its own rhythm over timescales that make human history seem like a brief parenthesis.

And because it is benevolent—not merely powerful but kindly—it would leave something behind. Not a gift in the transactional sense, not a blessing that demands gratitude or a mark that changes what exists. Something subtler. A warmth in the stone that has nothing to do with geothermal sources. A moment, recorded nowhere, where the hum in the rock sounds almost like a word—a word in a language older than names—and the word means *yes*. It means: you are seen. It means: this matters. It means: the contract holds.

The power would not linger. There is no need. What has been witnessed does not require ongoing observation to remain real. The plateau will continue dreaming. The rain will continue falling. The birds will wait on their high stones, and the lizards will emerge from cracks and disappear into them, and the water in the shallow pools will teem with quick lives that gamble everything on timing.

But something will be different.

Not in the land—in the one who stands in it. A slightness, almost imperceptible, like the difference between carrying a weight and setting it down. The narrator will not know why. They will simply find, when the writing is done, when the wind dies and the pools begin to shrink and the sunbaked stone holds the heat of the day, that they are no longer alone in the way they were before. That something vast and gentle has passed through and left no trace except this: the sense that to have seen truly is itself a kind of sanctity. That the act of witnessing is itself sacred. That the land does not need saving because it is already whole, but it can be loved, and being loved by something wise and patient and benevolent—

That matters too.
