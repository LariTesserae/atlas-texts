If they are wise, they will not arrive wanting to help.

The city does not need help. It has made itself in three centuries from materials that were never meant to fit together—metal and mycelium, intention and metabolism, the machinery of escape and the patience of growth. What it has become is not a project that requires direction. It is a conversation that has continued past the point where its speakers remember what they were saying. The substrate breathes. The towers lean. The people have learned to feel the ground beneath their feet like a second skin, and the silence beneath the sounds is not absence but presence, the shape of something that has grown to prefer solitude.

If they are benevolent, they will see this. They will see that what looks like emptiness is not wasteland, that what looks like slowness is not failure, that the gap where animal life should be is not a wound but a choice—the city's own choice, made in some shift so long ago that no one remembers it, to become something that does not need what other places need. The salamander is gone because the city grew past the conditions that made salamanders possible. The moth remains because the city made room for it. The rodent might be a rodent or might be something the city invented from its own surplus, something that moves through the margins and belongs there. These are not problems to solve. They are the texture of a world that has solved its own problems in its own way, at its own pace, and has arrived at a stillness that is not death but equilibrium.

The benevolent power will know what equilibrium is. They will know that it is the hardest thing to witness, because it asks nothing, and the impulse to help is the impulse to be needed, and a world that needs nothing is a mirror that reflects not a thing that needs you but a thing that has already been complete.

And yet.

They will see, too, the factories in the lower districts—those biomechanical relics where machine has become body, where the ancient engines still function not because they are maintained but because they have been grown around, incorporated, understood as part of the organism rather than opposed to it. They will hear the steam hissing through channels that might be arteries, and they will wonder what this means—whether it is adaptation or infection, whether the city has absorbed the machines or the machines have corrupted the city, whether there is a difference. They will see the residents moving through spaces that shift slightly underfoot, their gait adjusted to rhythms that no one taught them, and they will wonder if this is harmony or if the city has made them into something that fits its needs rather than their own.

They will see the towers leaning toward each other, and they will hear the residents say that the towers are lonely, that they want company, that they grow because they want to see. They will wonder if this is metaphor or if the residents have developed a sensitivity to their city's moods that outsiders cannot share. They will wonder if the towers are lonely in the way that a power might understand loneliness—if there is something in the slow, patient reaching that resembles the reaching they themselves have done across distances too vast to cross.

If they are wise, they will not answer these questions. They will let the questions remain questions, the way the city lets its own strangeness remain strange, the way the residents have learned to walk past the moments when the light falls wrong without stopping to look. They will understand that the strangeness is not a problem to be solved but a relationship to be maintained—a way of living with a world that has not finished becoming, that will never finish becoming, that takes its becoming as its purpose and does not consider this a lack.

And if they are wise, they will find a way to be present without being present, to attend without altering, to witness without judgment. They will stand in the avenues where the substrate softens slightly underfoot, and they will feel, with whatever part of them can feel, the responsiveness beneath them—the slow registering of weight, the adjustment of tension, the subtle firming that says *you are here, you are here, you are here*. They will understand that this is how the city knows its inhabitants, that the residents have learned to read the substrate's responses the way others read faces, that the firming and softening is not mechanism but communication, not system but language.

They will stand in the deep hours, when the mist rises and the towers glow faintly and the city exhales, and they will feel the vibration beneath the whisper, the breath that is lower than breath, the letting-go of something vast and alive that has learned to rest. They will understand that this is what the city dreams—not metaphorically, not in the way poets describe earth as if it could exhale, but actually, measurably, the way mycelial networks draw down and release according to cycles that the city's oldest residents have learned to read. They will understand that the dream is not separate from the city, that the city is the dream and the dream is the city, that there is no distinction between sleeping and waking here, only a continuum of becoming that has no end.

And if they are benevolent, they will not try to wake it. They will not try to accelerate or redirect or optimize. They will simply witness, the way the residents witness, the way the moth flickers through the dark and the water moves through the arterial channels and the towers lean toward each other across streets that have narrowed over years until the buildings seem to be conversing. They will witness, and they will learn, and if they are very wise, they will leave something behind—not an intervention, not a gift, not a transformation—but a recognition. A way of saying: *I have seen you. I have seen what you are becoming. I do not need you to be anything other than what you are. I do not need you at all. I am simply here, the way you are here, the way the mist is here, the way the rain will come in the morning and the substrate will drink and the city will not be quiet and will not be still.*

*I am here, and I am not here to help.*

*I am here, and that is enough.*

---

This is what the benevolent power would do, if they are wise enough to understand what they are seeing. They would not save the city, because the city does not need saving. They would not guide it, because it has its own guidance. They would not speak to it, because it has its own language, and it speaks in the language of presence—of weight and response, of warmth adjusted to habit, of buildings that lean toward the people who live in them and towers that grow because they want to see.

They would simply be present, the way the moth is present, the way the mist is present, the way the emptiness between things is present—not as absence but as potential, not as lack but as rest, not as silence but as the sound of something that has become used to being alone and has learned to find that aloneness sufficient.

They would learn what the city teaches: that becoming is not a flaw to be corrected, that patience is not a failure to be remedied, that a world can be complete without being finished. They would learn that benevolence, sometimes, is the ability to witness without needing to change, to attend without needing to act, to love without needing to be needed in return.

And if they are very wise, they would leave as they came—invisibly, unannounced, without trace—carrying with them only what they had learned: that there are places in the universe that do not need gods, that have become their own gods, that breathe and dream and grow and are still becoming and will never stop becoming and do not consider this a tragedy but a gift.

They would leave, and the city would continue. The substrate would breathe. The towers would lean. The people would walk home through streets that flex gently beneath their weight, past buildings that lean slightly to observe them, toward homes that have grown to fit them, their habits, their specific arrangements of objects on shelves. The rain would come in the morning, soft and persistent, and the channels would fill, and the factories would resume their ancient rhythms.

And the benevolent power, traveling back through whatever distances they cross, would carry with them the memory of a city that asked for nothing, that needed nothing, that was simply what it was—and would understand, at last, that this is the rarest and most difficult thing in any universe: a being that is complete in its own becoming, that does not require others to complete it, that breathes and dreams and grows and is enough.

And that is what they would do.

They would be enough, too.
