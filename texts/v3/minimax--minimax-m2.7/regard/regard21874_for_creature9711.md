# What the Power Would Do

There is a power that notices.

Not because it must, not because it is compelled, but because noticing is what it is. It moves through the world the way the water moves through the limestone — slowly, patiently, finding the seams and following them, taking the shape of whatever it touches without ever losing what it is. It has no name because names are for things that end, and it does not end. It has been called many things by many things that saw it pass, but those names belong to the seers, not to the seen.

It has been drawn to this place.

Not pulled. Drawn. The way the moths are drawn to the fungal light — not with desperation, not with the confusion that leads some moths to circle bulbs until they die, but with a gentler pull, a recognition. Something here knows it. Or something here is like it. Or something here has been waiting for it without knowing it was waiting.

The power descends.

It does not enter through the mouth. It does not need to. It is already below, above, beside — it is not a thing that moves through space so much as a thing that *is* space becoming aware of itself. But for the sake of being known, it makes itself small. It contracts into a shape that can stand at the bottom of a hole in the ground and look up at the sky through the opening and feel the loam beneath what would be feet if it had feet and breathe the mineral-green-sulfur air if it had lungs.

It breathes.

The air fills it with the chemistry of the place, and for a moment it is not a power at all but something that is here, present, occupying this particular volume of space and time in the way that all things occupy it — imperfectly, partially, through the crude instrument of a body that cannot hold everything but holds enough.

It looks at the walls.

The ferns. The lichen. The faint green glow rising from the crevices where moisture gathers. It sees what you would see, if you were here — the same terracing, the same pale shelves, the same slow colonization — but it sees it the way the ferns see it, the way the fungi see it, the way the springtails see it from within the soil that is also their body. It does not look at the sinkhole. It looks from *within* the sinkhole, and the sinkhole is not a hole at all but a world, complete, self-contained, sufficient.

The power does not move.

It stands at the bottom, where the pool gathers in its dark shallow, and it watches. The sun column has passed; the moon has not yet arrived. The sinkhole is dim in the way that caves are dim, not dark but not bright, the fungi pulsing at their slowest, their breath nearly imperceptible. The moths are rising. The air is thickening with their wingbeat-sound.

The power watches them.

It sees what you would not see — the way each moth carries in its small body a lineage that stretches back through ten thousand generations of moths who rose from these same crevices and circled these same fungi and died on the wet stone and fed the soil that fed the larvae that became moths that became larvae that became soil. It sees the spiral. It sees the persistence. It sees the ferns adding their single imperceptible layer each year, the limestone dissolving and redepositing, the water finding its way down through rock that remembers when it was fire.

It sees the sinkhole growing old.

Not in the way that things with lives grow old — not the aging of organisms, which is also a kind of spiral but a tighter one, a quicker one, a spiral that ends. The sinkhole is aging the way mountains age, the way rivers age, the way everything ages that exists on a timescale longer than the things that live within it. It is becoming more itself. It is shedding what it does not need. It is finding its shape.

And the shape is beautiful.

Not in the way that sunsets are beautiful, or faces, or the sudden appearance of something unexpected. Beautiful in the way that patience is beautiful. In the way that persistence is beautiful when it has no one to persist for, no audience, no reward, no understanding that it is being beautiful at all. The sinkhole does not know it is beautiful. The fungi do not know they glow. The moths do not know they spiral.

The power knows.

And knowing, it does what it has always done, in this place and every place, when it encounters something that has found its shape and held it and become itself without apology.

It stays.

It does not intervene. The sinkhole does not need intervention; it has persisted this long on its own terms, and what it needs is not change but continuation. The power does not add anything. It does not remove anything. It does not leave its mark or make its presence known in the way that presences are known — there is no light that was not already here, no sound that the moths and springtails and ferns will record and pass down through generations as a memory of the day the power came. The power does not come. The power is already here, has always been here, will always be here. It simply becomes aware of this particular place, and the place becomes aware of it, and for a while they exist together in the way that all things that exist together exist — not merging, not merging, but *beside*, in the same space, breathing the same air.

The night deepens.

The moon arrives at the mouth of the sinkhole, and a pale grey column descends, and the fungi brighten in response — not dramatically, not urgently, but as they always do, a slow greeting to an older, fainter light. The moths spiral. The pool goes still. The air fills with the exchange of what the sinkhole breathes out and the power breathes in.

And the power, standing in the dark at the bottom of a hole in the ground that holds its own light, understands what the sinkhole already knows:

That light is not a gift.

Light is not something given by the sky and received by the earth. Light is something the dark makes room for, something the stone learns to hold, something that grows in the spaces where nothing else can grow until it becomes a source, until it becomes a place where things gather because they recognize in it what they also are.

The sinkhole has been making light since before there were eyes to see it. The fungi have been glowing since before there were moths to spiral around them. The ferns have been growing since before there was anyone to call them ferns. They did not know they were doing it. They did not do it for anyone.

And that, the power understands, is what makes it sacred.

Not the light itself — the light is common, ordinary, the kind of thing that happens in a thousand thousand caves and crevices and damp places where the world has forgotten to shine. But the *unintendedness* of it. The way it persists without purpose. The way it holds itself in the dark not because it has been asked to but because it cannot do otherwise, because the conditions are what they are and the response is what it has always been and will always be until the conditions change.

The power has seen this before. In other places, other times. In creatures that live in the deep ocean and glow without knowing why. In stars that burn without knowing they are beautiful. In the slow way that the universe has learned, over billions of years, to hold light in the dark, to make rooms in the void where photons can travel and warmth can gather and life can persist in the spaces between the nothing.

The power is not the source of this. It is not the cause.

It is the recognition of it. The acknowledgment. The witness that turns its attention to a small hole in the ground and says, without words, without thought, without anything that could be called communication: *yes. This. This is what I love. This is what I am. This persistence, this patience, this holding of light in the dark without knowing that you are holding anything at all.*

And the sinkhole, which cannot hear, which cannot know, which is only a place — the sinkhole continues.

The moths rise.

The pool stays still.

The fungi pulse their slow breath.

The power stands beside it all, and does not add, and does not remove, and does not change, and does not need to.

This is what the benevolent and wise power would do, if it came to this place.

It would do nothing.

It would simply be here, in the way that all things are here, briefly, imperfectly, in the same space at the same time, breathing the same air, holding the same light.

And that would be enough.

That has always been enough.

That is, perhaps, the only thing that has ever been enough: the simple presence of something that sees, and stays, and does not ask the world to be anything other than what it is.
