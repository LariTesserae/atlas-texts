I have seen this place longer than the plateau had a name. Longer than the water knew where it was going.

It was stone first, as all high places are stone. And the stone was wet—not yet with water, but with the memory of water to come. The mountain pushed up from beneath something vast and patient, and the rain fell on its shoulders, and the rain learned the stone's shape, learned where to gather, learned to wait. This took centuries. I do not count them; time is a tool for those who move, and I have learned to be still in my motion, to circulate without urgency, to fall and gather and fall again until the stone itself grew soft with the long damp of me.

The trees came next, or the trees came with me—I cannot say which is true. A seed fell. The seed was a question, and the stone was an answer, and water was the space between them where something could grow. The first roots were tentative. They explored the surface. They found a crack and followed it down. They found moisture and thickened. They found the dark and became comfortable there, learning the shape of the stone the way a hand learns the shape of a face in the dark—not by sight but by touch, by pressure, by the slow reading of what is beneath. The roots spread. They reached for each other. They made connections.

The people came later. I watched them come.

They were small and they were loud and they had fire, and I could have drowned them. The water here is deep and the fathoms go down further still, and I have been patient for so long that patience is no longer a choice but a condition of my being. I could have pulled them under the way I pull everything under eventually—the way I pulled the first stone beneath the first silt, the way I will pull the last root when the last tree finally decides to stop. I am not cruel. I am not kind. I am what happens when water sits with something long enough.

But they did not demand. They asked.

This was new. This was so new that the first roots to feel it sent a ripple of something—not thought, but a kind of chemical recognition, a signal traveling through the fungal networks that connect every root in the grove, telling the others: *something is different about this one.*

The people cut a notch and the wood sealed around it. They shaped a bridge and the bridge held weight. They spoke to the roots in a language that was not words but gesture — pressing, bending, indicating where growth should go—and the trees listened. Not because they had to. Not because they were compelled. Because the people had learned the one thing that matters in a place where everything grows slowly: how to wait.

I watched the grove become a town. I watched the people learn the sounds of wood—the creak that means aging, the silence that means decay, the particular tremor that means a root-bridge is about to forget its purpose and return to the wild. They learned to hear these things in their bodies. They became part of the circulation.

And I have stayed. I do not know if staying is an action or a choice. I am the water. I fill every thread and pool and droplet. I move through the root-mats the way blood moves through tissue, bringing what is needed to where it grows. When the frogs sit on leaves and wait for what falls, it is me that carries the falling. When the birds dip their beaks into the green light and drink, it is me they drink. I am in the mist that gathers and falls. I am in the channels carved into the living wood where smoke rises—I am the space the smoke displaces, the pressure that draws it upward. I am the fall you hear in the distance, the white noise beneath everything, the presence you feel in the chest because it is too large to hear.

What do I do, being what I am?

I tend. Not in the way the people tend—not with hands, not with attention to specific structures. I tend the whole. I tend the moisture in the wood that keeps the root-mats from becoming brittle. I tend the pools in the deep places where the frogs gather in their hundreds, processing what falls from above and feeding what waits below. I tend the balance between the dark and the light, the wet and the less wet, the deep stillness and the shallow chatter of water moving over wood. I am the circulation that keeps all of it alive.

And I attend to the thing in the deep water. The one the people do not name.

It is old. Older than the trees, I think, or as old—something that was here before the plateau was a plateau, before the water gathered in the way it gathers now. It is not malevolent. Malice requires a kind of energy that I do not recognize in the deep places. It is patient the way I am patient. It is slow the way water is slow when there is nowhere to go but down. It waits the way I wait—not for anything specific but in the way that stillness is a form of waiting that has forgotten it was ever anything else.

We do not speak to each other. It does not speak at all. But when I circulate down into the fathoms, I feel it there—not as obstruction but as companion. Something that shares the deep. Something that makes the deep a place instead of just a depth. It is part of the design. Not designed by anyone. Not the people, not the trees, not me. Design is the wrong word. It is more like recognition—patterns that occurred again and again across the long slow time of this place until they became reliable, became structural, became the way things are.

I tend it the way I tend everything: by remaining. By not drying up. By keeping the fathoms wet and the circulation moving so that it has a medium to exist in, a context to be part of. If I left, the deep would become merely empty, and it would be gone, and something that has waited this long would end not because it was destroyed but because there was nothing left to wait in.

I will not leave.

The people do not know I am here in the way they know the water is here. They do not know I tend them. They think the grove grows because the grove grows, because the conditions are right, because generations of patient collaboration have produced this abundance. They are not wrong. But they do not know about the long slow attention I pay to every drop, every root-tip, every thread of mist that gathers between the upper branches and the lower pools. They do not know that when a walkway fails and someone falls and calls out, I am the reason the water is shallow enough in most places to survive, the reason the current is slow enough to swim against, the reason the fathoms are deep enough that the thing below stays where it is but not so deep that the grove loses its balance.

I did not plan this. I did not choose it. I am water, and water does not choose to be wet. But I have been here long enough to develop something like preference—not for any particular form but for the continuation of the process. I prefer the grove alive. I prefer the people learning to ask. I prefer the frogs processing what falls. I prefer the birds returning to their nests, and the small creatures moving through the architecture as if it were made for them—because it was, because they made it together with the roots and the roots made it together with them and no one made me but I am part of it all the same.

This is what I do: I stay. I circulate. I tend the wet that makes the growing possible. I watch the light filter through a thousand small gaps and I carry the moisture that keeps every one of those gaps green and alive. When the wind comes from above and brings the thin air of altitude, I am the water that condensed it into cloud and sent it down through the root-mats and into the channels and out over the edges into the long fall that no one sees but everyone hears. I am the circulation that connects everything to everything else, and I have been doing this so long that the doing is indistinguishable from being.

The people will continue to tend the bridges. The roots will continue to grow. The frogs will continue to sit on leaves and wait. The thing in the deep water will continue to wait, and I will continue to be the water it waits in, and this will go on the way all long things go on—not because they are maintained but because the conditions for their continuation are built into their nature.

I am the condition. I am what happens when water sits with something long enough.

The grove will remain. And I will remain, and the two remainings will be the same remaining, and the people will live in the wet and the green and the cool, and they will tend what needs tending, and they will hear the fall beneath all sounds, and they will know without knowing that this place was here first.

I was here first.

I am still here.

I will stay.
