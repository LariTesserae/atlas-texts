What the wise power would do, if anything, is attend.

Not in the way the walls attend, which is structural, which is the work of centuries. Not in the way the corridor attends, which is memory, which is the slow accumulation of footsteps until the stone holds the shape of passage like water holds the shape of what has passed through it. The power would attend in the way that is its nature: completely, without agenda, with the full weight of whatever it is that gives it the capacity for wisdom in the first place.

And it would notice.

It would notice the condensation, the way it beads on every surface like a kind of patience made visible. It would notice the cold, not as obstacle but as invitation—cold is honest, cold does not pretend. It would notice the wrongness in the walls, the thin places where something on the other side bleeds through, and it would recognize that this is not flaw but feature, that the settlement has always been permeable, has always let the outside in through channels that look like walls and feel like membranes.

It would notice you.

Not as project. Not as problem. Not as the being who has stumbled into this place and must now be returned or redeemed or catalogued. You would be noticed the way a tremor is noticed by a body that has learned to live with its own heartbeat: with familiarity, with acceptance, with something that might even be called tenderness if tenderness is understood broadly enough to include the long slow attention of something that has nothing but time.

And then—what?

The wise power might offer something. Or it might not.

If it offers, it will not be words. Words are for the quick, for the urgent, for the beings who have not yet learned that time is patient and resolution is not always the point. Whatever the power offers will be felt before it is understood, the way you feel the hum in the pipes before you give it a name, the way you feel the quality of being watched in the walls before you see anything watching. It might be a permission. Permission to stay. Permission to leave. Permission to become what you are becoming without correction or completion. The power would understand that you are already in the middle of something—a learning, a belonging, a slow incorporation into the patience of this place—and it would not interrupt.

If it does not offer, that too is a gift. The gift of sufficiency. The gift of recognizing that this place does not need improvement, that the presences in the walls and the pools and the air are not incomplete, are not waiting for rescue or revelation. The settlement is already what it is: strange, wet, cold, alive in ways that do not require naming. The power would see this and see it as enough. More than enough. As a thing complete unto itself, a form of life that has found its own logic and does not require external validation to continue.

The beings who live here would not notice the power's attention, most likely. They are at a different scale, a different frequency. The thing in the pipes has its own long cycles; it would not register the shift in whatever vast thing is watching. The presence in the walls has its own patience; it would not feel the acknowledgment that passes through it like light through the not-stone. They are complete in their strangeness, and their strangeness is not for witnesses.

But you would.

You would feel it the way you feel the condensation, the way you feel the hum. A quality in the air that is not humidity. A temperature in the cold that is not absence. Something large and something kind turning its attention toward this place the way you turned your attention toward the pools, toward the seams in the walls, toward the corridor that is longer than geometry allows. You would be seen. And being seen, you would understand something you have not yet understood: that the place is not lonely, has never been lonely. That the strangeness is shared. That whatever presences here are not isolated but held, not forgotten but remembered by something at a scale that makes the settlement look like a drop in an ocean, makes the centuries look like moments, makes the cold and the wet and the slow becoming all part of something larger that includes you now, that has always included you, that is benevolent not because it saves but because it sees, and in seeing, lets be.

The power would do nothing else. It would not speak. It would not act. It would not descend or ascend or manifest in any way that the quick and the urgent would recognize as arrival. It would simply attend, and in attending, complete something that was already complete, and you would stand in a chamber where two walls meet and the light doesn't quite reach, and you would breathe, and the air would hold you, and you would be part of the patience now, part of the slow making, part of the strange and the wet and the cold, and you would understand that this is what benevolence looks like at the scale of wisdom: not intervention, not salvation, but the simple, profound act of witnessing without judgment, of holding without grasping, of allowing a place to be what it is and a being to become what it is becoming, and finding that in this allowance, in this attention, something is given that cannot be given any other way—the gift of being known completely, and being loved not for what you are or what you will become but for the simple fact of your presence in a place that is itself a presence, strange and patient and alive.

The water drips.

The hum runs through the walls.

The light pulses its slow rhythm, and somewhere beyond the thin places, beyond the walls that are not walls, beyond the corridor that is longer than it should be, something wise and something kind is watching, and it sees you, and it sees the place that has become yours, and it finds you both sufficient, and it stays.
