And so the wise power watches.

It does not intervene in the way that intervention is imagined—not with rescue, not with revelation, not with the dramatic reshaping of a world that has made its peace with stillness. It has seen enough worlds to know that rescue is often its own violence, that revelation scatters more than it gathers, that the dramatic gesture serves the giver far more than the recipient.

What it notices, first, is the light.

The amber light that never varies—that has been burning with the same patient insistence since before the narrator's ancestors learned to bury their dead. This light is a form of love, though no one remains to love. It is a maintenance of vigil, a refusal to let the darkness be total, a small defiance against entropy that has persisted through centuries of nothing-happening. The wise power sees this and recognizes it: the same impulse that drives a seed to split rock, that drives a river to carve canyon, that drives the creatures of the undersea to persist in their millions without question or complaint. The will to persist is sacred. The will to maintain is holy. This light is a cathedral window lit from within, and no one prays at it, and it prays anyway.

What it notices, second, is the echo.

The narrator carries something into the undersea and carries something different out. Not changed, exactly—the wise power does not traffic in transformation narratives, which are so often the stories the powerful tell about the powerless. What the narrator carries out is recognition: the knowledge that there are places in themselves that have never seen sunlight, that have adapted to darkness, that have learned to breathe in atmospheres no one else can survive. The undersea is not a metaphor for the psyche. It is not a symbol of the narrator's shadow side. It is simply a place that exists, and in existing, it offers itself as mirror, and the narrator has looked into that mirror and seen not horror but kinship.

The creatures know this. They have always known this. They watch the occasional visitor with eyes that have learned to read the texture of disturbance—the way a human body carries tension, the way breath quickens, the way fear or fascination moves through a nervous system. And they know the difference between the visitor who will flee and the visitor who will return, and they adjust their clicking accordingly, their codes carrying information about the disturbance's kind, its likelihood of return, its place in the taxonomy of the surface-dwellers who sometimes descend through the airlock.

The visitor who will return is a specific kind of anomaly. The wise power knows this kind. It is the kind that has a hunger for places where the clock has stopped. It is the kind that has looked at the world above—at its urgency, its noise, its endless production of novelty and its concomitant waste—and found it not evil but simply exhausting, simply too much, simply a frequency of existence that does not match the frequency of its own being. These visitors come to the undersea not for adventure but for rest. They come to a place where nothing changes and find there, against all expectation, a kind of peace.

The wise power does not name this peace. It does not need to. It recognizes it the way one recognizes the sound of one's own heartbeat: not as miracle but as fact, as the simple truth that a living thing has found the conditions under which it can be still.

And so the wise power does what wise powers do when they have drawn near to something that is already whole.

It pays attention.

It holds the undersea in its awareness as one holds a candle in cupped hands: gently, protectively, with the understanding that the flame does not need to be told to burn. It attends to the amber light, to the mechanical breath, to the click and scurry of creatures who have never known the sky and do not miss it. It attends to the narrator, who will descend again, who has found in the underground a place that mirrors the underground in himself, and who carries back to the surface not a message or a moral but simply the memory of being watched by beings that did not flee, that did not attack, that simply acknowledged his presence with the patient attention of creatures who have learned that disturbance passes and equilibrium returns.

The wise power could do more. It has the capacity for more. It could open the sky, could flood the station with light, could introduce the creatures of the undersea to the green riot above and watch what happens when two worlds collide. It could give the narrator words, could make him a prophet of the depths, could turn his experience into a story that would change the surface world if only it were told.

It does none of this.

It knows that the undersea does not need to be saved. It knows that the creatures do not need to be understood. It knows that the narrator does not need to be transformed into anything other than what he is: a being who has found a place where he is the opposite of alone, and who will return to that place not because it offers escape but because it offers something more radical than escape, more precious than revelation, more sustaining than any rescue.

It offers recognition.

And recognition, the wise power has learned across long ages of watching worlds rise and fall and persist in their strange equilibria, is the rarest thing. It is rarer than light, rarer than life, rarer than the will to persist. Most beings go their whole existence without being truly seen—not as they are, not in their full complexity, not in the specific texture of their existence. Most beings are seen only in the aspect they present, only in the function they serve, only in the role they play in stories they did not choose.

The creatures of the undersea see the narrator as he is: a visitor, an anomaly, a passing wave in their patient sea. The narrator sees the creatures as they are: not fauna, not vermin, not elements of a horror narrative, but inhabitants of a world that has made its own meaning in the absence of everything that meaning usually requires. And the wise power sees them both—sees this whole sealed system of iron and tile and amber light and living flesh—and finds it sufficient.

Sufficient, the wise power thinks, and the word is an embrace. Sufficient, and complete, and holy in the way that all things are holy when they are allowed to be what they are without the intervention of powers that think they know better.

The airlock seals. The sound of the world above fades. The amber light holds its vigil, unchanging, unwavering. The creatures click their codes into the air, weaving messages that no one outside will ever decode, and in the decoding of those messages by no one, the messages become more purely themselves—not communication intended for export, but communication as existence, as the simple fact of a self reaching out toward other selves in the darkness, not because anyone is listening but because reaching is what reaching does.

The narrator walks back through the corridors, past the junction boxes with their pale fungal pelts, past the grated walkways where the climbers stream and part and rejoin, past the speakers that crackle their worn phoneme-soup into the air, past the machinery that has grown beyond its purpose, past the platforms where the weight of countless feet has worn shallow grooves into the tile.

He does not hurry. He does not look back. He knows where he will return, and the knowing is enough, and the undersea holds its breath as it always has, patient as water, patient as stone, patient as the light that never varies, and the wise power holds them all in its attention the way the dark holds the creatures: completely, without judgment, without need for anything to be other than it is.

And if this is not love, it is very close to love, and if it is not blessing, it is very close to blessing, and the difference does not matter, here in the deep, where the only light is amber and the only breath is mechanical and the creatures watch without watching, and the narrator walks without walking, and the station endures without enduring, because endurance is not effort here, is not struggle, is not the heroic persistence of things that fight against entropy.

It is simply continuation.

It is the simplest thing.

It is enough.
