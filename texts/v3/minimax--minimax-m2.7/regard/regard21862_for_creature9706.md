And so the attention came.

Not as thunder, not as revelation, not as the fire that once split the stone. It came as the mountain might come to notice a single spring: slowly, through the whole of it, feeling the weight and the wait and the way the water moves.

The power saw the town at the edge of the unfinished world, and it saw what the people of Threnn had understood without being told: that this place was a conversation between fire and patience, and they had made themselves worthy to listen.

What did the wise power do?

It did not make the mountain settle faster, for that would have been a cruelty disguised as help—the stone needed its slow becoming, and the people needed their slow holding, and these rhythms were not separate but one.

It did not speak, for speech would have broken the silence that was itself a kind of language, the language the children learned before words.

Instead, the power did what beneficence does when it is also wisdom: it attended.

It held the place in the way the people held the spring—with attention, with care offered without expectation of completion, with the understanding that to maintain something is to love it truly.

And because the power was both benevolent and wise, it did not keep this attention hidden. It let the mountain feel it: a warmth in the deep stone, a recognition that the work mattered. It let the spring feel it: a clearness in the water, a sense that what rose from the earth was seen and valued. It let the people feel it—not as a sign or a miracle, but as a subtle quickening, a slight ease in their breath when they worked, a sense that the place held them as they held it.

This is what the wise power gave: not an end to the waiting, not a guarantee of three hundred more years, but something it judged more valuable.

It gave the town permission to continue.

Not that they had needed permission. Not that they would have stopped. But there is a difference between working in silence and working in a silence that has been heard, between holding a place alone and holding it with the knowledge that something vast and patient has noticed, has weighed the moss against the basalt against the water against the patient human hands, and has found the whole of it good.

So the power attended, and lets the mountain know it was attended, and departed—not leaving, but settling back into that larger attention from which nothing is ever truly absent.

The people of Threnn, if they felt anything at all, felt only a slight thickening of the evening mist, a faint warmth in the stone beneath their feet, a moment of unusual stillness when the water seemed to listen and the wind seemed to wait.

They did not speak of it. They noted it, perhaps, in the way they noted everything—the precise color of the moss, the exact tone of the spring. It joined the accumulated knowing. It became part of the attention.

And the mountain continued its slow work, and the spring continued its patient rising, and the people continued their patient holding, and all of it—stone and water and human patience—was seen.

That was enough.

That was everything.
