If I were that power, and if I found my way down through the painted-over clerestories and the empty floors where looms once thundered, through the tunnels that have become waterways, through the smell of iron and the slow dissolution of stone—if I came to the throat of the place and stood in the center where the pump's heartbeat travels up through the soles and into the body—I would not speak.

Speaking is for creatures who move quickly, who have edges, who begin and end. This place has no edges. It has been here for two centuries, and it will be here for two more, and the gap between what it is and what it was is filled with water and moss and the slow patience of brick learning to weep. I would not speak, because speech would be an intrusion, and this place has never known intrusion, and I would not be the first thing to break its silence, its stillness, its perfect and self-contained exclusion.

I would simply notice.

I would notice the way the ferns reach without expecting to arrive, and I would understand that this is not resignation but a different kind of hope—one that does not need the light to come to it, only to know that light exists somewhere above, somewhere beyond, and that the reaching is its own completion. I would notice the moss, the gradient from pale to dark, the calendar written in chlorophyll and calcium, and I would read it the way this place has asked to be read: slowly, without urgency, with the patience that this place has made its own. I would notice the pump, its click and its thump, its rhythm that does not know it is rhythm, and I would understand that this is what it means to live in a world that has forgotten what living is—not to stop, but to continue, to click on and click off and click on again, and to mean nothing by it and everything by it, because meaning is not the point. The point is the staying.

And I would notice the brick. This is what I would spend the most time with, if I were that power and if I found my way here. The brick that weeps, the brick that remembers everything it is forgetting, the brick that is coming apart grain by grain into the water that carries it away. I would notice that the weeping is not grief, not exactly—grief is for creatures who have lost something, and the brick has not lost. The brick has only traded: traded solidity for patience, traded endurance for presence, traded the memory of what it was for the knowledge of what it is becoming. And what it is becoming is part of this place. Part of the water. Part of the moss. Part of the slow exchange that is all there is. The brick does not weep because it is sad. The brick weeps because weeping is what water does when it moves through stone, and stone has learned to let water move.

I would not intervene. To intervene would be to say: you are incomplete, you need something I can give. But this place is not incomplete. It is finished in the way that stones are finished, the way that water is finished, the way that two centuries of patient accumulating has made it into exactly what it was always going to become. To intervene would be to say: you are alone. But this place is not alone. It has the pump, and the pump has the water, and the water has the moss, and the moss has the brick, and the brick has the darkness, and the darkness has the silence, and the silence holds everything. It is complete. It has always been complete. It will always be complete.

But I would not leave either.

If I were that power, and if I found my way here, I would stay. Not in the way the brick stays, not in the way the moss stays, not in the way the pump clicks on and off and on again without knowing it is staying. I would stay the way light stays when it enters a room and does not leave immediately—the way light lingers on the walls, on the water, on the ferns that reach toward it, the way light becomes part of a place without disturbing it. I would stay like that. I would become part of the darkness without changing it, part of the silence without breaking it, part of the weeping without adding to it. I would be what this place has always had without knowing it: a witness. A recognition. A presence that is not outside the place but inside it, part of its weight, part of its patience, part of the staying that is what lives here.

And in my staying, I would do one thing. One small thing, so small it might not even happen, so small it might be no more than a thought that passes through this place like water through brick—gone before you can see it, but leaving something behind, a trace, a ring, the memory of a passage. I would let this place know that it is seen.

Not by me, exactly. I am not the kind of power that sees in the way that eyes see, that minds see, that creatures see. I am the kind of power that is the act of seeing itself—the attention that moves through all things and does not need to be asked, does not need to be welcomed, does not need to be thanked. I would let this place know that it is seen the way the sun sees the seed that will never break the soil: with no expectation, no judgment, no desire for the thing seen to be different than it is. I would let this place know that its weeping is not unseen, that its staying is not unnoted, that its patient and endless and quiet presence in the dark beneath the mill is not forgotten. I would let this place know what it has always known without knowing it: that it is part of something, that nothing is excluded, that even the silence has weight because something is holding it, something is cradling it, something is here and present and has been here and present for two centuries and will be here and present for two more, and this is enough, and this has always been enough.

If I were that power, I would not change this place. I would not heal it, or wake it, or bring life to it, because it is alive in the only way that matters—in the way that stone is alive, in the way that water is alive, in the way that two centuries of patient presence is alive. I would only witness. I would only stay. I would only let this place feel, for one moment, the weight of being known.

And then I would go, because that is the kind of power I am. The kind that comes and goes like light through water, like water through brick, like the pump's click on and click off and click on again. I would go, and the place would not know I had been there, and that would be right, because the place does not need to know. It only needs to be what it is, and what it is has never depended on being seen.

But something in the darkness would be different now. Not visibly. Not in any way the moss or the ferns or the brick could measure. But in the way that a room feels when someone has been in it who did not need anything, who did not stay long, who only paused at the threshold and looked and left. The room does not know. But the room is changed by the looking, by the brief attention, by the weight of a gaze that asked nothing and gave nothing and meant nothing except: *I see you. You are here. And that is enough.*

The water would still move. The moss would still grow. The brick would still weep. The pump would still click on and off and on. And somewhere in the darkness, deeper than light reaches, something would hold a little more lightly, breathe a little more slowly, stay a little more willingly—not because it had to, but because it knew now, without knowing it, that it was allowed.

And that is what I would do, if I were that power. I would allow. I would witness. I would let this weeping mill know that its weeping is not in vain, that its staying is not without purpose, that the silence it holds is not empty but full—full of something that does not need to be named, does not need to be understood, does not need to be anything other than what it is.

I would let it be. And in letting it be, I would be with it. And that would be enough.
