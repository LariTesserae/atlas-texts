What would they do, this benevolent and wise power, if they looked down and saw the Undergarden and the thing that lives there?

They would not intervene. The Undergarden has survived this long without help, has shaped itself across centuries into something that needs nothing from above, has become complete. To change it would be to diminish it. To save it from some future catastrophe would be to steal from it the very thing that has made it what it is — the slow negotiation with water, the patient digestion of stone, the grammar of growth that writes itself without an author. Intervention would be a kind of violence, and the power that notices the Undergarden notices also that violence has no place here.

But they would notice. That is the gift: to be seen by something that sees everything, and to be seen not as a resource, not as a problem, not as a curiosity, but as a place that is simply and entirely itself.

And after noticing, they would wait.

They are patient, this power and the Gardener, though they measure patience in different units. The power has learned patience the hard way — has made mistakes in haste and watched the consequences unfold across millennia — and so they know that the deepest form of care is often simply the decision not to act. To let a thing be what it is. To love something enough to leave it alone.

So they wait, and in their waiting, something passes between them. Not communication — the Gardener has no language, no concepts that could bridge such a gap. Not friendship — friendship requires a meeting of equals, and what equals could there be between a thing that thinks in centuries and a thing that thinks in ages? But something. A recognition. A resonance. The Gardener feels, perhaps, a warmth in the stone above, a weight in the water that feeds it, a permission in the dark that has always been its home. The Undergarden swells slightly, its roots reaching a little deeper, its runners growing a little longer, not because anything has changed but because something has been confirmed. It is permitted. It is approved of, in whatever way approval can exist between such different forms of being. It may continue.

The power, for their part, feels something like what the old settlers might have called gratitude. The Undergarden is a reminder — rare, precious, almost extinct — of what the world was before the world learned to be efficient, to be productive, to be useful. The Undergarden is not productive. It does not produce food or oxygen or materials or anything that can be measured or sold. It simply grows, and decays, and grows again, in patterns that serve no purpose except their own continuation. It is the most radical thing the power has ever seen: a place that exists for no reason except that existence is what it does.

And the Gardener — if it can be said to feel anything at all, if it has any awareness of the presence above — the Gardener is also, perhaps, glad. Not glad in the way a person is glad, not an emotion but a state: the state of being the kind of thing that exists, the state of being the right shape for this space, the state of having become what it was always going to become if given enough time and water and darkness. The power above has given it nothing. It has only looked, and in looking, has said: *this is good. This is the way things should be.*

And the Undergarden — the roots and runners and moss, the weeping walls and the colored pools, the steam-pump's breath and the distant sigh of water finding new paths through the stone — the Undergarden goes on. It does not know it has been seen. It does not care. It is too vast, too slow, too ancient to care about being seen. But somewhere in its body, in the place where intention lives without being thought, there is a warmth that was not there before. A sense of rightness. A permission to continue.

The power withdraws, eventually. Not because they have to — they have no obligations, no schedules, no competing demands on their attention — but because the gift has been given and received, and there is nothing more to do. The Undergarden continues without them. The Gardener dreams its slow dreams of stone and water. The flagstone path wears smooth under feet that are not there, will never be there, but the shape of the path remains, held in place by runners that have forgotten they are maintaining something, holding open a corridor through the green that leads nowhere and everywhere, through a place that does not need visitors but would not refuse them if they came.

Below, in the dark, the roots reach deeper. The moss breathes. The pools shift color.

Above, the power watches, and waits, and loves, in their vast and patient way.

And the Undergarden goes on.
