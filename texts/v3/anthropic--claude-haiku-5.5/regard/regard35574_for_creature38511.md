# What Attends to the Shelf

It began as a change in the drip.

For three centuries the water from the overhead pipes had fallen at the same interval, a little under a second apart, so that the basins filled and overflowed in a rhythm the grazers had long ago stopped noticing. Then one morning the interval lengthened by the width of a held breath. The grazers in the nearest trough lifted their heads together. The clicking under the plates stopped, and for the first time in longer than the film could remember, it stayed stopped.

Then the attention arrived, and it did not come from above, although the fog thickened over the towers as though a lid had been set on the plateau. It came the way a sound comes into a room when everyone has gone quiet to listen. It was patient. It had been watching the Shelf for a long time from very far away, and it had only now decided to come close.

It was not a visitor in the sense that you are one. It did not need to walk. It was, insofar as anything can be said about it, a presence that cared about things being what they were, and that knew the difference between caring for a thing and wanting it to be otherwise.

## What it saw

It read the map first, as a gardener reads a bed before touching it.

It saw that the loosened geometry was not a fault. The staircase that climbed into the cloud, the pipe that ran downhill into a basin above it, the hangars that never let you leave the first door: these were not mistakes in the builders' work. They were the places where the builders' intentions had grown past the limits of their materials, and something beneath the floor had been keeping the strain balanced, nudging a tower a degree, tightening a stair until it reached its landing, and then, when a bird needed a path that no straight line could give it, letting the path bend.

It saw the clickers, and it understood them more quickly than it had expected to. They were not the builders' tools. They were not quite the builders' heirs. They were the keepers of an open map, and their work was the one thing on the plateau that was never meant to be finished.

It saw the film, thick and patient, writing its handwriting along the drip paths. It saw the grazers writing their trails in bone-coloured lines that the rain would erase and they would rewrite. It saw the shorebirds flying routes that existed only in the direction they had come from. It saw the sleeper, unwinding from the girders in the morning, scraping the fins clean and leaving a few metres of bare metal for the gutters to carry.

And at the far edge, where the newest structures were still half-fitted and the grazers stopped to wait, it saw something that might have been building, or might only have been the plateau deciding.

## The first impulse

The attention felt the pull of its own nature, which was to finish things.

It could have straightened the staircases. It could have plumbed every tower to true vertical, seated every plate level, cut every basin that the pipes had been running toward for centuries. The map would have settled into a single geometry, and the stairs would have reached their landings and no further, and the place would have become, in a few seasons, a very clean and very ordinary industrial site on a windy plateau.

The birds would have been stranded. The grazers would have walked into walls. The film, which had learned to thicken on the lowered side of every leaning tower, would have had nothing left to reinforce, and would have thinned, and the sweetness in the air would have gone with it. The clickers would have had no work. And the thing at the far edge, still building, would have stopped, because a finished map has no edge at all.

The attention saw this clearly, and it did not do it.

Wisdom, it found, was mostly a matter of knowing which things were already doing their own work well.

## Small things

It did a few things, and they were small.

It let a scarp at the edge of a failed plate settle by a finger's width, so that a herd of grazers coming up from the lower troughs could climb it without breaking the shells of the youngest. Nothing else about the plate changed. The fracture remained clean and sharp, and the shards remained angular at the bottom of their fan.

It let the rain fall, on the afternoon when the film on a stretch of old fins had been scraped to bare metal by the sleeper and had not yet begun to regrow. It fell gently, in the same brief fine bursts as always, but it fell there, and by the next morning the first green ridges were visible at the seams.

It did nothing at all to the birds. They were already flying where they needed to go.

And it did one thing that was not small, which was to answer the clickers.

## The clickers

It knocked.

Not with a voice, which the plateau did not have a use for, but with the same kind of contact the clickers themselves used: a tap on the composite, carried along the seam between two plates, a rhythm laid into the stone and let go. It did not try to match them. It offered a pattern that was different from theirs, slower, with a gap in it, the way a person in a conversation says *yes* by leaving a space.

For a long time nothing answered. Then the clicking resumed, one beat behind its own previous rhythm, and the taps from beneath the plate that had tilted became, for a moment, very sharp, as though something had stopped correcting it and was listening.

What came back was not a word, but it had shape. The clickers were keeping the far edge open. They had been doing so since before the composite was poured. The builders had left a map with a centre that had moved, and something in the ground had decided, very early on, that the outward work should never be completed, because completion would close the one direction in which the place could still change. The clickers did not know whether this was right. They only knew that it was the work.

It understood, and it accepted that the work belonged to them. It would not take it. It sent back a single slow tap, the gap in its rhythm held open, which meant what a steward says when handing a tool back to the hand that has always held it: *Keep it. I will not take this from you.*

The clicking did not stop. It did, for one beat, change pitch, and the birds on the nearest gantry lifted their heads and called once in a voice that was almost a chirp.

## The sleeper

At dusk the attention went up the lattice to the sleeper.

It was wrapped around a girder near the centre of the tableland, its pale ridged plates catching the last light and throwing it back up into the mist until the whole structure seemed to be burning very quietly. The mineral green of its skin had gone dark with the cold, and it was not moving, but its eyes were open.

Those eyes were two dark cisterns, each with a faint light at the bottom, and when they turned toward the attention, the attention felt itself being measured. It let the measurement happen. It did not try to appear larger or smaller than it was. It did not try to hide.

The sleeper looked for a long while. Then, very slowly, it did something no one on the Shelf had seen it do: it closed its eyes and opened them again, as a creature might do when it has decided something is not a threat, nor a meal, nor a rival for the fins.

The attention did not know what the sleeper was. It might be a gardener, or a predator, or a thing that made no distinction between the two. It did not need to know. It offered one thing, which was a promise carried in the pattern of the weather: that the gantry the sleeper rested on would remain whole, that the fins it cleared would be the ones that caught the most fog, and that as long as the sleeper kept climbing toward the gantries that were already behind it, there would be no one to tell it to stop.

Then the attention withdrew its gaze, and the sleeper turned back to its rest, and the cold came down over the plates.

## The one still building

At the far edge, where the plates were sharpest and the towers only half-fitted with fins, the attention came last.

It found an unfinished stair. It climbed it, in the way that attention climbs anything, and it came to the top, and the top was a landing one step higher than the last one it had seen, and the stair continued past it into a cloud that had no edge.

It knew then what the builders had been building toward. It was not a tower or a plateau or a stair. It was a landing for something that had not yet arrived. Perhaps it was the clickers' own successor, or the birds' long-delayed destination, or the sleeper's next and final rest. Perhaps it was the attention itself, which had been working outward from this same centre for a very long time without knowing it was doing so. Perhaps it was nothing at all, and the stair was simply a way of asking a question that the plateau was still answering.

The attention did not finish the stair.

It set one last fin in the gutter of the nearest unfinished tower, and left the seat of its base unpoured, so that water would stand in a shallow tray where the foundation should have been. It left the plate beside it with one edge still sharp, and the seam still soft, and the place open to the fog. Then it stepped back, the way a careful worker does when they have done enough for the day, and it let the far edge stay where it was.

## Withdrawal

When the attention had gone, the plateau did not notice the difference, which was the best thing that could have been said of it.

The drip fell at its old interval. The film thickened on the lowered side of the leaning towers. The grazers wrote their trails across the troughs, and the rain erased them, and they wrote them again. The shorebirds flew toward the far edge, landing on ledges that existed only in the direction they came from. The sleeper unwound in the morning and scraped the fins, and the gutters ran clear for a few metres before the drip took over.

The clicking came back from beneath the plates, one beat behind its own previous rhythm, keeping pace with a walker who was not there.

If you walk far enough toward the horizon that is always too close, you will find the newest structures. There is a stair there that climbs into the cloud and stops, one step short of a landing that has not been poured. There is a fin in a gutter that catches the drip and does not know what it is for. There is a tray of still water where a foundation should be, reflecting the sky so cleanly it seems to be a hole.

And if you stand at the edge of it long enough, you may feel something touch the seam beneath your feet: a single slow tap, the gap in its rhythm held open, as if someone were leaving room for you to answer.
