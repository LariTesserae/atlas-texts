# What the Attention Did

It came the way weather comes to a high place, slowly and from every direction at once. Nothing on the Sulfur Shelf announced it. The fog did not thicken, and no lizard turned its head. It was simply present in the basin, the way the pump-drip was present, and the attention noticed the terraces the way a careful listener notices a room.

It listened for a long time before it did anything.

It learned the pump-drip first. Each vat had its own irregular rhythm, set by its seals and its pressure, and together they made a sort of counting that the attention could follow. It learned the metal's expansion at noon and its contraction at dusk. It learned the swallows' morning wave climbing the gantries, thinning as it rose, and the midge columns that rose in answer to passing shadows. It learned that the lizards' fins were sponges and that their crests were ledgers. It learned the film, the nightly skin of living things that broke apart at noon and sank to become stone. It learned the rings, which had been growing for generations at a pace no single person could watch.

Then it considered the works, and that was the hardest part.

The works were a wound, a catastrophe of abandoned industry, and a benevolent power could easily see what it might do. It could restart the vats, clean the pipes, and send the condensate running again in full flood. But the attention saw what that would mean. The shelf would drown in its own abundance. The midges would multiply past the swallows' appetite, the film would thicken beyond what the sulfur could feed, and the balance that had taken centuries to settle would tip into a choking bloom. Restoration would be a kindness to the machinery and a cruelty to everything that had learned to live among it.

It could take the works down entirely: scrap the gantries, haul off the vats, clear the towers and leave bare basalt. But the attention saw the swallows in the girders, the lizards on the warm metal, the readers' shelters built from split shells. The works had become a habitat. A clean, empty plateau would be tidier, and it would be dead.

So it did not restart the works and did not unmake them. It let them keep ticking.

There was one thing it could not leave alone.

Far to the east, where the seeps ran russet with iron, a seam had opened in the oldest of the processing vats. The readers had noticed the pools there growing too rich, and some had stopped going to them. The attention saw more than they could. The seam was leaking a dissolved metal that the film could not fully process, and the lowest seep was slowly carrying it into the channels that fed the middle terraces.
