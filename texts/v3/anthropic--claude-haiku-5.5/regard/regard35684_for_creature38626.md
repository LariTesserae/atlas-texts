**The Attention**

It came down into the hollow the way weather comes, slowly and then all at once, and it did not announce itself. What it noticed first was the silt. It could feel the film the river laid down, a hair's width at a time, and it understood that the land was not so much rising as being argued into existence, one sediment at a time. It liked that. Anything that was still deciding deserved to be watched without interruption.

Then it noticed the walls.

It had seen many things built. Nests and burrows, reefs and hives, cities of stone and cities of thought. It had rarely seen a structure whose builders did not seem to know they were building one. The walls were a memory, the builders were its hands, and their attention was spread so thinly across the whole structure that the mound itself seemed to be the thing that thought. It thought, carefully, that it would not be wise to walk into such a mind and begin rearranging its furniture.

So it did not speak. It listened, the way one listens to a language one is not yet fluent in, and it learned the first word, which was the drip. Water fell from leaf to leaf, and the builders worked in its rhythm, and the walkers followed the scent of one another, and the frogs breathed to the beat. The whole hollow kept time with the rain, and the rain kept time with nothing, and that was the ground of everything.

It watched the pool-thing for a long while. It was tempted, briefly, to feel something like grief for the walker that drifted too near the edge. But the walkers had no grief for it, and the pool had no malice, and the hunters in the canopy took what they needed and left the rest. The whole hollow was a balance of patience, and nothing in it was cruel in the way that cruelty requires a choice. The power decided that it had no business sorting the hungry from the fed. The waiting was the form the pool had been given, and it was a good form.

It considered the river. It could have slowed the water, or thickened it, or drawn the silt away so the mounds would not be buried. It could have built the builders a better wall against the flood. It did not. The river was also one of the things that was still deciding, and to hold it still would be to decide for it. A city that is always saved from its own weather is only a museum, and the power did not want to make a museum of a living place. Let the plain come, it thought, or let the forest come. Whatever the hollow becomes, it will become it by its own working.

But it was not without a wish.

On the third night of its attention, it saw two builders meet on a bridge of cured clay. One paused, touched antennae to the other, and then turned back the way it had come, setting down its load. Later a crawler on the gallery wall stopped, lifted its two front limbs, and held them. The power had seen this gesture a hundred times in the hollow and had not known what to make of it. Now it understood a little more. It was a question, or an acknowledgment, or the simple act of being seen and saying so. It was the most common thing in the place and the least explained, and the power, which had been listening so hard, felt something like the pull of an answer.

So it answered in the only way it knew that would not be a command.

It did not change the river. It did not touch the walls. Instead, for the length of one night, it let the fog lie exactly where the builders needed it, thick in the troughs and cool on the glazed surfaces, so that the resin would cure slowly and evenly, and the seams would knit without cracking. The next morning, the builders had finished a ring of wall that had been left open for a season. The power did not claim credit. It merely noticed that the wall was whole, and that the crawlers on it had lifted their limbs again, and that this time the gesture seemed less like a question and more like a kind of thanks, or perhaps only a recognition that something had passed through the gallery and gone on.

Then it withdrew, as wise things do when they have given what is needed and no more.

It did not stay to see the hollow become a forest or a plain. It knew that in a few seasons the silt would bury half the mounds, and in a few decades the walls would dissolve back into the glassy flats, and that the builders, the walkers, the beetles, the frogs, and the patient thing in the pool would all go with them into the next arrangement of the ground. The power did not mourn this. It understood that the city was not a thing to be preserved. It was a thing being made, and making is a process whose completion is only another beginning.

Before it left, though, it made one small mark. It could not write, and the builders could not read, but the walls remember. Somewhere in the deepest gallery of the oldest mound, where the resin is thickest and the clay has been laid most carefully, a single ring was laid down that did not match any other, a fraction wider than the rest, as if a hand had pressed there once in passing, lightly, and the wall had kept the impression.

That was all. The drip continued. The silt ticked as it settled. A claw scraped clay, and the pool breathed once beneath the mud, and the hollow went back to its listening. The attention had moved on, but it had left behind the knowledge that the place was watched, not to be managed, and that its unfinished work was worth the watching.
