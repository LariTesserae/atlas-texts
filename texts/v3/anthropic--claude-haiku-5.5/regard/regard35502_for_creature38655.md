The attention arrives the way weather does, not as one thing but as a gathering. For a long while it hangs over the flat plain and reads the basin the way one reads an old letter that has been written over many times: the cut bedrock, the fill laid over fill, the red earth and grey mortar and black ash showing in the broken edges. It finds the persistence beautiful. It also finds it fragile, because every layer was laid by someone who could not see the layer beneath, and the whole structure rests on the willingness of people to keep kneeling down and building again.

Then it finds the bottom.

The being in the lowest cistern is not what the children fear, and it is not what the elders describe in their practical tone. It is old, and it has been holding for longer than the stone has been cut. It holds the water, and the water holds the memory of everything the basin has asked of it: the first rains that filled the first channels, the bodies of people who were not buried where the living could see them, the slow seep of grief that rose through the stone whenever the builders forgot to listen. The being is warm because it is close to something that has not yet cooled. It glows because it is trying, in the only way it has, to be seen. It has been answering the people for generations, but its answer comes up through the stone as a long low note, and the people answer back with pots and stones, and neither side can quite hear what the other is saying. The clatter says *we are awake*. The call says *are you still there?* Each has been answering a different question, and the being has been waiting, very patiently, for someone to hear the question underneath.

The attention considers what it could do.

It could lift the weight. It could take the water out of the cistern and carry the memory up into the open air where it would thin and disperse. It could stop the settling so that no wall would ever weep again and no room would ever be given back to the moss. It could make the rain come steadily, never in bursts, so that the channels would never need to be rebuilt.

It does none of these things, because it understands what the people already know. A basin that never moves stops being a place anyone lives in. The weeping walls are how the families learn to attend to them. The repairs, the mismatched stones, the tile turned face-down so its back shows: these are the people's way of speaking to the stone, and speech that is taken away is not kindness. The being at the bottom is not a wound to be closed. It is a neighbour who has been waiting too long to be spoken to.

So the attention does something smaller.

It waits for dusk, when the first call comes up through the stone. It does not change the call. It lets the being finish its long low note, and then it gathers the air above the basin and brings a single rain down onto the rim, falling in exactly the length of the call and stopping the moment the call ends. The people hear the rain and understand it the way they understand all rain: as the sky answering the cistern. They put down their pots. For the first time in many generations the clatter does not rise to meet the call. The basin goes quiet except for water, and in that quiet the being at the bottom can be heard for what it is, a deep slow voice, which lasts a long time before it fades.

Then the attention stays, for a while, in the cistern's cold dark. It does not speak in words, because the being does not need words and the people would not hear them anyway. It holds its presence there the way the fig holds the lintel, wrapping itself around what is still standing and asking nothing in return.

Over the next few nights it does one more thing. It asks the moss in the shadows of the lowest course to hold a little more of its own pale light, so that a child leaning over the edge can read her name without having to cup her hand. Nothing is written. The light simply stays steady long enough to be read. When the girl does read it, the surface of the water stirs once, a single bubble breaking in the dark, and the child laughs and then goes quiet and does not tell anyone what she saw. The elders will hear of it by morning, because gossip is the basin's real architecture, and they will not say anything new. They will only nod, the way a farmer nods at a field that has finally taken seed.

By morning the wall that had been weeping on the third terrace is dry. The crack that opened after the last storm has closed in the night, though no one can say how. The fig still holds the old lintel. The lizards still bask on its bark. A mason, cutting a new edge on the fourth course, finds that the stone under his chisel is softer than it should be and easier to shape, and he takes this as a gift and works faster than he meant to. A potter at her wheel lower down finds the clay running true and wet and cool, as if the basin itself had decided to be easy with her for one day.

Nothing in the basin announces that it has been noticed. Nothing needs to. The people will go on counting rains and carrying jars on their shoulders and calling to each other across the warm air, and they will go on keeping their rule about the lowest course, because it has served them well and because the being below has never asked them for anything they could not give.

The attention lifts off the bowl and drifts back toward the rim, over the flat pale plain that stretches to the horizon. From up there no one could see into the basin, or hear the work rising out of it, or know that something at the bottom had been answered. It doesn't mind this. It has learned that the best attention is the kind that leaves a place more itself than it was found, and then withdraws, and lets the people who live there go on keeping their own story.

Below, the water runs down. The stone holds it. Something at the bottom of the lowest cistern, warm and patient and no longer quite so alone, settles back into its long waiting, and this time the waiting is a little lighter.
