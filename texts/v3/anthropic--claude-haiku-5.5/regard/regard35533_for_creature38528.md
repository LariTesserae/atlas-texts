**What the Attention Did**

It arrives first as a change in the quality of listening.

The mist does not part and no voice speaks. The drone of the basin, which has been one sound for longer than any memory the bowl holds, gains a second layer, too low and too slow to be insect or amphibian or vent. The nearest pool holds still for the length of three heartbeats. The gliders, circling on the thermals, all tilt their wings in the same direction and hold there. Then the attention settles, the way a large animal settles when it has decided that the small thing in the grass is not a threat, and it begins to read.

It reads the way the knappers read, through stone. It feels the terraces, the grooves in the cliffs, the polished plates tilted at their old angles. It feels the knocks passing back and forth under the moss, and it understands them before it understands anything else about the place: these are not signals to a master. They are checks. Each knock asks whether the stone is still whole, and each answer says that it is, or that it is not, and where.

It finds that the bowl is healthy.

It also finds the fault.

Far beneath the central vents, deeper than any knapper has ever pressed a forelimb, something is swelling. It is slow. It has been swelling for three seasons, and by the measure of the old terraces that is nothing. But the fissures near the warmest vent have begun to seal faster than the steam can find new paths, and the pressure below them is rising toward a point where the seal will not hold. When it breaks, it will not break in the small, familiar way. It will open the floor of the bowl along a line that no knapper has ever had to close, and the warm pools will boil out over the terraces, and the silt will move, and the shells and bone-glass and mineral skins will slide down into the lower basin in a single slump. Most of the frogs will not survive it. The gliders may. The knappers, who are the slowest, will be the ones buried in the work they were doing when it came.

The attention could stop this. That is the first thing it considers, and it considers it honestly, because it is not a fool and does not pretend that restraint is free.

It could ease the pressure by pulling heat from the deep chamber, cooling the magma until it lost its hunger for the surface. It could reshape the rock, lay down a new seam of stone that would hold where the old one would not. It could move the knappers somewhere safer, or teach the frogs to carry them, or set the gliders to watch the seal and call out when it weakened. Any of these would work. It knows that every one of them would also make the bowl into something else. The knappers would become a people who had been carried. The frogs would become a chorus with no reason to sing, because the pools would no longer need to be listened to so carefully. The terraces would be a monument instead of a body. The thing that makes this place what it is, a system that has learned to repair itself faster than it breaks, would have been replaced by a debt to something outside it.

So it does not reshape the deep stone.

It does something smaller, and it is careful about how small.

It listens. It finds the place under the moss where a knapper has been pressing its forelimbs against a fracture for six days, and it stays there for a while, feeling the cold dense rock under the limbs and the faint warm pulse under the rock. The knapper does not stop. It does not turn. But the tapping changes rhythm, slightly, the way a conversation changes when someone new has entered the room.

The attention knocks.

It is not good at this. Its knock is too deep, too slow, and the stone carries it wrongly for a moment, and the whole basin flinches. The frogs go silent. The gliders tilt. Then the knapper answers, a short sharp double tap, and then a pause, and then a longer sequence the attention cannot read, because it has never learned the grammar of this place.

It tries again, slower. It presses against the stone the way the knapper presses, and it holds still, and it lets the rock tell it what the rock already knows. Then it sends the one thing it has to offer, which is the shape of the swelling below: the long low pressure, the slow line of weakness, the place where the seal will fail if nothing changes.

The knapper stops.

For a long time nothing happens. The rain comes and goes. The frogs call and fall silent. A glider drops a split shell into the warm water near the vent and it sinks, unpicked, into the silt.

Then the knappers begin to move.

They do not do anything the attention has not seen them do before. They scrape the vent-mouths. They dig the gullies deeper to take the pressure of the runoff. They press their forelimbs against the edges of the old fractures and hold, until the crust closes into something more patient than a seal. Several of them go down into the cracks beside the central vent and come back up with mud on their chitin and the look of creatures who have been told something they already half-knew. The work is not new. It is simply louder now, more coordinated, aimed at a place where the stone will need to give slowly, not all at once.

The attention watches for a long time, and understands that it has done almost nothing, and that the almost nothing was the right amount.

It does one more thing before it leaves.

It makes sure that no one else comes. The bowl sits at the edge of a world that has not yet learned to see it, and the attention holds the mist close, the rim quiet, the wind's path over the basalt shaped so that no traveler will find the way in before the knappers have finished their work. Nothing is taken. Nothing is brought. The bowl will not be discovered, studied, collected, or mourned by any outsider who might arrive with good intentions and sharp tools.

Then the attention withdraws, not all at once, but the way a hand lifts from warm stone.

The drone returns to its single layered voice. The pools settle. A glider banks and resumes its slow circles over the vents. Under the moss, a knapper taps twice, then pauses, then taps again, checking the stone, listening for cracks, reading the way the vents have shifted since the last time anyone listened.

When the wind comes across the rim, the basalt rings, a long sustained note like a finger drawn around the lip of a glass. It rises and falls with the wind. It sounds the way it has always sounded, and the attention, from its far place, hears it and does not try to change it.

Some things are not for rescuing. They are for witnessing, for keeping quiet, and for knocking back, once, in the right rhythm, when the stone has been listening for a very long time and finally has something to say.
