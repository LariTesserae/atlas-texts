The attention arrives the way weather does, slowly, from every direction at once. It is not a voice. It is a change in the quality of the air, a sense that something is listening from the far side of the cloud sea, and that it has been listening for a long time.

It notices the mist first, because the mist is the first thing that notices anything. It notices the cyanobacteria, the lichens, the slow conversion of air into soil. It takes some pleasure in the tardigrades waking in the moss. It does not try to count them.

Then it notices the steps.

They are cut into the rock shelf in a way that no water and no root would cut them. The treads are measured to a human stride, and the moss on them is not uniform. A single path runs up the middle, worn flat by feet that have come up and down in the same line for seasons. At the top, the moss has been pressed back from the edge in a rough oval, the way a body presses it when it sits for a long time facing the view. And there is a fire ring, a little way back from the stone, its ash wet but not old. Someone was here within the last few days.

The power pauses over this, and it is aware of how easily it could be too much.

It is very large, and attention at that scale is a kind of weather of its own. A glance from it can lift a stone, fix a river, or pull a people up out of the mountains and set them down somewhere warmer and more convenient. It has done such things before, and it has come to distrust the feeling of satisfaction that comes with them. The people who were carried away from their places were no longer the people who had made them. Their stone was left to be moss.

So it does not reach for the being in the doorway. It listens instead.

The being is old. Its breathing is slow and a little ragged, as if the lungs have been damp for years. It lives mostly in the dark gap where the roots frame the entrance, and it goes out at dusk to fetch water from the bromeliads and at dawn to sit on the steps. It has a name, but the name is not spoken aloud here, because there is no one to speak it to. It has been speaking to the stone instead. Some nights it sings, very quietly, the same few lines over and over, and the frogs in the cups below do not seem to mind.

The power understands that the being is not lost. It is keeping something.

It considers the stones. The spirals on the upright stones in the ring of fallen trunks have been worn almost blank by rain, and the being has been going out to them every few days with its hands, scraping the lichen back from the grooves and running its fingers along the lines. It is restoring them, slowly, with nothing but patience and a sharpened piece of black glass. It cannot finish. The rain will undo what the hands do, and the hands know it. They keep doing it anyway.

The power is moved by this, and it is careful with the feeling.

What should it do? It could lift the rain for a season and let the spirals dry and hold their edges. It could send seeds from the lowlands that would bind the sinkholes and keep the steps clear. It could make the being young again, or give it a companion from some other valley, or pull back the cloud and show it the whole white world below so that it would know the lowlands were still there, still green, still full of ordinary life that had never heard of this table of stone.

Each of these is a kind of answer. The power sees that none of them is the being's question.

So it does three small things, and then it withdraws.

First, it opens the cloud. Just once, at the hour before dawn, the white sea pulls back from the edge of the escarpment, and the being, sitting on the steps with its back to the doorway, sees the lowlands spread out a thousand meters below, green and vast, with the old peaks standing up out of the haze like islands. It is a sight the being has seen many times, but this time it does not look away. It sits there until the sun reaches the far ridges, and when the cloud closes again it nods, as though someone has confirmed a thing it had half forgotten: that the world is still there, and that it is larger than the plateau, and that the plateau is part of it and not the whole of it.

Second, the power does nothing to the stones. The spirals will go on being worn, and the being will go on scraping them clean. It is the being's work, and its work is its own. The power only makes the rain fall a little more gently on the upright stones in the season when the being is most often out with its glass, so that each morning's scraping lasts a day longer than it would have. This is the smallest possible change, and it does not make the work easier, only a little less futile.

Third, it sends a bird.

It is a grey bird of the lowland kind, one that has never flown above the cloud before. It comes up out of the white on a long, uncertain line, and it lands in the bromeliad beside the steps, and it looks at the being with an expression of enormous surprise. The being looks back at it. The bird is carrying something in its crop, a seed of the kind that grows on the far slopes of the older mountains, and when it is startled it drops the seed into the cup of water at the base of the fern. The seed sinks into the sediment among the mosquito wrigglers. It may come to nothing. It may root in the moss and grow into a small shrub with thin pale leaves, the first of its kind on this table. The power does not decide. It only makes the seed fall where it falls.

Then it withdraws, and the mist closes over the steps, and the being goes back into the dark gap of the doorway to sleep.

The power does not stay to watch. It has learned that its attention, once fixed, becomes a gravity that bends everything toward it, and the plateau has been bent enough by the slow violence of the mountains and the long patience of the rain. Let the being keep its own counsel. Let the seed grow or not. Let the monkeys go on answering themselves across the flatness, and the cicadas keep sawing their steel, and the sinkholes widen by a finger's width each season.

Before it goes, though, it reads the spirals one more time, the way one might read a letter one has been given without being meant to receive it. The worn lines do not say anything grand. They are not prayers or histories or names of gods. They are a simple, repeated pattern, the kind a person might cut into a stone to say: *here, we were here, and we looked out from this place, and it was good.*

The power holds that for a moment, the simplicity of it, and then lets it go back into the rock, where it belongs, where the rain can have it in its own time.

The cloud sea settles over the table. The mist threads the lower fronds and beads on the moss. Somewhere under the ferns the striped cat moves without a sound, and the frogs in the cups begin their rolling chorus, and the being in the doorway turns over in its sleep and breathes out, slowly, into the wet, warm dark.
