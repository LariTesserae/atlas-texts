The power noticed the country the way one notices a room where someone has been speaking quietly for a long time. It did not arrive as a light or a voice. It arrived as attention, and the first thing it learned was that this place was already paying attention to itself.

It listened for a while before it did anything. Time moved differently there. A rain came and went, and the spires shifted their pitch as the basins filled and drained. The power followed the grazers' scraping, the stitchers' rustle, the skimmers' clicks, and the hunters' gaps, and it understood the web the way a musician understands a piece by hearing every line at once. Then it found the listener.

The tall grey-violet forms stood in the shallows with their heads turning slowly, and one of them, at the edge of the largest pool, turned toward the power and held.

It was not alarm. The head simply stopped, and the listener kept still while the choruses broke around it, as it did for every sound it weighed. The power felt the weight of being weighed. It had no wish to be the loudest thing in the country, and it saw clearly that if it spoke, the whole web would reorganize around its voice. The grazers would turn toward it, the skimmers would wheel about it, the hunters would follow its new rhythm, and the spires would hum to it instead of to the rain. The balance would become a choir with a single conductor, and the country would forget how to hear itself. So the power did not speak.

It considered, instead, what it was for. A benevolent power that finds a healthy, self-keeping world is tempted to improve it. The temptation was real, and the power named it and set it down. Improvement would mean changing the land's grain, the sanded ridges and the rimstone terraces, and those were not its to change. The keepers were already here. They were the cairns set back after rain, the channels cleared of silt, the slabs kept from falling. The power had mistaken them at first for the absence of anyone. They were the presence it was looking for.

So it joined them, very quietly.

After the next rain, when the runoff ran hard across the flats, the power turned its attention to a single fallen stone on a ridge, a flat slab that the water would have carried into the lowest basin and buried. The power did not lift it. It made the water slow for a moment, a few hand-widths of current bending around one edge, and the slab settled back into its notch on the cairn. The stitchers' trail, which had been running toward that cairn all season, met the stone and turned along it. Nothing else changed.

The power did the same with a channel from a seep. It held back a little silt, just enough that the stone-lined bed stayed open for another season. It did not reshape the channel or make the water run cleaner than it should. It tended.

Then it came back to the deep basin.

The water there clouded, the pale milk spreading and thinning, and every listener in range turned at once. The power felt the thing beneath, large and patient and very old, in the middle of a breath. It was not dangerous in any way the power could name, but it was not asleep, and it was not the power's to wake. The power did not reach down. It only waited, the way the listeners waited, and let the breath finish. The milk thinned, the water cleared, and the land exhaled with it, a small release of iron-smelling vapor from the rimstone pools that the creatures drifted away from and then came back to.

Something answered it in the only way it knew how. The power let its own presence hum at the depth of the water in the largest pool, a single low tone under the spires' tuning, not enough to alter their pitch but enough to be heard. The listener at the edge of the pool turned its head toward the sound and held, and then, after a long moment, turned back to the flats. It had found the power and filed it, as it filed everything, and did not need it to be anything more.

This was enough. The power understood that it would not be a ruler or a voice in the web. It would be one more attention among the many that kept the country alive: the listeners that located, the skimmers that warned, the hunters that moved in gaps, the keepers that set stones back, and the deep thing that breathed at its own pace. It would come back when the rains came, to check the cairns and the channels and the spires' tuning, and it would hear, each time, whether the country still remembered how to speak.

The rain fell hard that night. Every creature on the flats went quiet at once, and in the stillness the power heard the water finding its old paths through the stone. Then the choruses began again, louder than before, and the power listened to all of it, and was content to be only one of the things that listened.
