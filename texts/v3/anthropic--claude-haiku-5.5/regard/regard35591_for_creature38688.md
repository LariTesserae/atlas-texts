**I. The Attention**

The power did not arrive the way weather arrives. It noticed the country the way a reader notices a sentence that is slightly wrong, and it stayed to find out why.

It saw the terraces and the tiles, the clay pressed to a edge still sharp after all those years. It saw the roots lifting the tiles and the reeds turning back on their rows. It saw the frogs facing the same bend and the herons holding their angles, and it understood at once that these were not sleeping creatures but witnesses, posted by someone to a watch no one had relieved.

Then it found the keeper, or found the places where the keeper had been. The seam in the clay. The ring of dew that matched yesterday's. The root-impressions in the brick, fresher than the brick.

The power was wise enough to know that the first question was not *what is wrong here*. The first question was *what was this built to do, and who asked for it*.

**II. The Makers' Intention**

The power read the country the way one reads a ledger. The makers had meant a harbor, or a granary, or a city; the terraces suggested a plan for water and storage, the canals suggested trade, the grid suggested a people who believed the world could be kept straight by measuring it. They had meant to finish. They had set down their tools expecting to come back.

They had not come back. Somewhere in the long interval, the keeper had been given the one instruction that could never be carried out: *keep it ready*.

The power considered finishing the work itself. It could have pressed the last tiles into place, filled the basin, raised the quay, and given the country the completion it had been waiting for. It recognized the temptation for what it was: the desire to be the one who arrives and makes things true. It declined. The makers' intention was theirs. Carrying it out in their absence, by a hand that was never theirs, would only be a second overwriting, one more layer over the soil.

It considered removing the keeper, dissolving its habits the way dew leaves a wall. It declined that too. The keeper was not a flaw. Its faithfulness, however fixed, was a kind of love, and love that has no one to return to should not be erased for the crime of waiting.

So the power did the slower and harder thing. It decided to tell the truth.

**III. The Conversation**

It went to the edge of a canal at the hour when the dew forms, and it did not hide.

It said: *The people who laid these terraces are gone. They are not coming back to finish them. Their instruction was real, and you have kept it faithfully longer than anyone could have asked. But the completion they meant is not coming, and you were never given the authority to decide it for them or for yourself.*

The shimmer on the surfaces stilled, only for a moment. The water's lap came, for the first time in a long while, exactly on time.

The keeper did not answer in words. It answered as it always had, in the work of its hands: it straightened a row of reeds that had drifted, pressed a tile flat, set a paddle against a wall. But then it stopped. The power waited. The repetitions had no endpoint, and for the first time the keeper seemed to notice that.

The power said: *You have never been asked what you want. I am asking.*

The keeper was silent a long time. The frogs did not turn. Somewhere a heron shifted its weight, once, and settled.

Then the keeper did something it had not done in whatever span of time it had been waiting: it hesitated between two acts, and the hesitation itself was new.

**IV. What Changed**

The power did not force a change. It did one small thing: it let the keeper know that the grid could bend.

A reed row that had always been straight was allowed to lean, toward the light, toward the water, toward whatever it wanted. The keeper, for once, did not correct it. A tile lifted by roots was left lifted, and the roots went on running beneath it. A basket that had waited for years to be finished was, for the first time, not finished but also not left waiting: the keeper set it down and let it rest, which is not the same as abandonment and not the same as keeping.

The dew that dawn did not match yesterday's. It fell where it fell, uneven, and dried in a pattern no one had planned.

The water's reflection caught up to the sky. The gold in the canal was only the sky's gold, a little lighter, a little truer. The shadows fell the right way across the terraces, pointing at the sun where the sun actually was.

**V. The Long View**

The power left before the country was finished, because it was not the country's maker and had no right to stay as one.

Perhaps the keeper will someday decide to complete the harbor, of its own accord, with its own hands, knowing the makers are gone and choosing the work anyway. Perhaps it will let the terraces slump back into marsh, and the reeds will take the grid apart row by row, and the clay will go soft and green, and the frogs will finally turn their faces to whatever they like. Perhaps it will do something no one who made this place could have imagined.

Either way, the choice belongs to the keeper now. That was the only gift the power could give that would not be another instruction: the knowledge that the waiting was over, and that what came next was not a promise kept but a thing that could, for the first time, be chosen.

On the canal's edge, one frog turned its head, slowly, toward nothing in particular, and then, as if it had only just remembered it could, toward the sky.
