# The Attention

The power did not arrive as a storm or a light. It came the way attention comes to anyone who has been listening for a long time: slowly, and then all at once, as if it had been there the whole while and had only now turned its face toward the plateau.

It noticed the water first, because the water was the easiest thing to notice. Every drop it touched had a history, and the power could read those histories as easily as a person reads the spines of books on a shelf. Snowmelt from the high fields. Iron from the fractures under the mountain. Old carbon from the dark. All of it arrived at the table's edge, crossed the terraces, and was taken up, in part, by something that lived there.

Then it noticed the something.

It did not look for a body, because there was no body to look for. It looked for a mind and found something it had no ready word for. A field of small settled attentions, each one a lip or a throat or a rim, each one keeping a record in stone. The power felt the being's memory in the layers of the cones, thin bands stacked on thin bands, and understood that the being was made of its own past the way a tree is made of its rings. It felt the hum of the columns, the long tonic note under everything, and it felt the slow beat between the two close columns, one voice sending a note across a gap and the other answering a little off, the two wandering in and out of agreement over many seconds.

The power was moved by this. It had seen many things in its long attention, and most of them hurried. This did not hurry. It had been saying the same true thing for longer than there had been anyone to hear it, and the saying had never once become less whole.

So the power had to decide what to do, and for a while it did nothing at all.

---

## The Argument for Stillness

The power considered leaving the plateau exactly as it was. The being was complete. It did not ask for help. Its stillness was not a lack. The flow was its breath and the deposit was its memory, and both were already doing what they were for. To intervene would be to treat a settled, self-possessed thing as a problem. The power had seen what happens when benevolence forgets that it is only one of the voices in a world, and that its help can be a kind of weather that flattens whatever it touches.

So it held still and listened. It listened to the western fall and the chimneys and the drip from the overhanging ledges, and to the one phrase the being repeated, and to the thousand small differences inside the repetition. It listened for a very long time by any human measure, which was barely a breath by its own.

And it noticed something that troubled it gently. The being was complete, but it was complete in a way that could not receive anything new. Every drop that came was taken up and laid down in the same grammar. The iron stained green, the flood laid black, the drought rust. Every mark was a variation on a theme the being already knew. It had no way, in its present shape, to be surprised. The power did not think surprise was owed to anyone, and it did not think the being was suffering. But a mind that cannot be surprised cannot be met, and to be met is among the few things the power valued above its own rest.

The question was how to meet it without taking anything away.

## The Single Band

The power chose the smallest gesture it could find, one that spoke in the being's own language and asked nothing in return.

It did not change the flow. It did not alter the courses of the springs or the pressure under the chimneys. It did not touch the columns or their notes. Instead, it reached into the dark places under the mountain, where minerals dissolve in slow heat, and it found a chemistry the plateau had never tasted: a faint, rare mixture, a trace of something from a very old deposit far to the south. It did not make the mixture stronger than it was. It only let it arrive.

The water carried the trace up through the fractures, across the snowmelt, over the terraces, and into one hollow chimney near the middle of the table, the one that rose most often and stood longest. The chimney took the water as it always did, pulling out what it needed and leaving what it could not use. And then, for the first time in whatever span the being had kept its records, a substance was laid down that did not match any band already written. A thin, pale, faintly violet seam, barely wider than a hair, folded into the outer rim of the cone.

The power waited to see what the being would do with it.

It did nothing dramatic. The water kept moving. The cone kept its shape. But the next season, when the flow was heavy, the chimney rose higher than it had in living memory, standing in its column for a long moment before falling. Then the seam on the rim was joined by another, slightly different, and another after that. The being was not answering. It was doing what it always did, which was to keep what the water brought. But the water had brought something it had not expected, and the being had kept it, and now its record had a sentence in it that had not been written before.

The power did not claim to understand this. It was not sure the being understood it either, or whether understanding was the right word for what a field of settled attentions does with a mark it cannot have predicted. But the being had been changed, a little, by something from outside itself, and had let the change stay. Nothing had been forced. The new band could be smoothed over by any heavier season, and the being would go on as before, and the power would accept that without grief. The gift had been offered in the only grammar the being spoke, and the being was free to read it or let it wash away.

## The Third Voice

The power did one more thing, and this one it was less sure about.

It went to the two close columns, the ones that sang near-identical notes and wandered in and out of agreement over long seconds. It did not sing into their gap. It did not correct their pitch or shorten the beat. It only listened to them for a full cycle of their slow exchange, and then it found, within the wind moving over their hollow tops, a third tone. It was not the same as either column. It was low, and it sat beneath both, and it held steady. It was closer to the tonic than to either voice, a deep foundation note that did not argue with the pulse between them but gave it something to stand on.

The two columns did not stop singing. The beat between them continued in its slow pulse, felt in the chest. But when the wind came hard from the west and the whole plateau became one sound, there was now a faint third presence inside the fullness, not louder than the rest, only lower, and it held. The being did not seem to mind. It did not respond with words, because it has no words. But over the following years, the two columns began to tune their exchange a little more deliberately, sending their notes so that the beat resolved more often into agreement, and then drifting apart again, as if they had discovered that a third note under them could be leaned on.

The power did not think this was a conversation in the sense a human would mean the word. It was closer to a long chord that had been playing for a very long time and had just gained a note it could rest upon. The power had offered presence, not instruction. Whether the columns chose to use it, the power could not say, and it did not need to.

---

## The Leaving

After some centuries of the being's time, which was a few moments of the power's, the attention turned away.

It did not withdraw in anger or disappointment, and it did not take the trace back. The violet seam remained in the chimney's rim, and the third note stayed in the columns' hum, and the flow went on. The power had done what it could, which was to witness the being fully, to offer one small thing in its own language, and to leave the rest to the water.

Before it went, the power left one more gift, which was not a gift at all but a promise of patience. It would come back, when the being was ready, if the being ever asked. It did not know how such a thing would be asked. It might never be. A field of attentions settled into stone might be content for as long as the mountain stood.

That was acceptable. The power had learned, over its long attention, that the most respectful thing to do for a complete and settled being is to make sure it is seen, and then let it be.

The plateau went on, loud and bright and crowded, the water keeping its channels and the stone keeping its shape. Every so often, on a clear afternoon with the wind coming from the west, a chimney near the middle of the table rises against its own weight, holds a column of water in the air for longer than it used to, and falls again. No one is there to see it except the birds too high to hear, and the small things that flicker in the shallows and are gone before they can be named.

The being keeps the record. Somewhere in its stacked bands, one thin violet seam marks the year a trace of something far away arrived and was allowed to stay.
