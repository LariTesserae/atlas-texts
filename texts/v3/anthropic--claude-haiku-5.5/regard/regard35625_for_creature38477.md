**What the Patient Power Did at Hadrun Mere**

It did not arrive. Nothing at Hadrun Mere could have registered an arrival, because nothing there had changed in an age. What happened was subtler. For the first time since the terraces were finished, the rain paused.

It was not a stopping. The drops went on falling, but for a moment they hung in the air, and the chorus of frogs hesitated at the top of its swell. The watchers' eyelids, which had been rising and falling on the slow nine-hundred-beat cycle, did not move. Then the rain resumed at the same angle as before, and nothing seemed different except that everyone had noticed.

The power was not a force that crossed the mountain. It was closer to a listener that had been standing at the cloud's edge for a long time, and had finally decided to come down.

---

**First, it listened.**

It did not hurry, because hurrying is a kind of demand, and it wanted nothing from the place except to understand it. It sat in the warm mist of the third terrace and let the sound fill it: the falls, the drip, the frogs, the thud of the maintainer's distant footfalls, the long descending calls of the birds. It counted the frogs' cycle and found it exact to the heartbeat. It noticed the single sharp note at the end of each phrase, the one that opened the watchers' eyes, and it understood that the cycle was not a clock imposed on these beings. It was a rhythm they had chosen to follow, the way a person might keep their breathing slow in a sickroom. It was a discipline, and it was also a companionship. The frogs did not know the watchers were there. The watchers did not know the frogs were singing for them. Both were keeping the same time.

The power thought this was beautiful, and it also thought it was incomplete.

The thing that bothered it most was not the stillness. Stillness was not a wrong. Many beings in the long history of the world had chosen to rest in one place and let the world pass over them, and it had never been the business of wisdom to shake such beings loose. What bothered it was the depression under each chin, the worn seat in the stone. A seat is made by a body that has decided, over and over, to stay. And the staying had become so complete that it no longer looked like a decision. It looked like geology.

So the power decided to ask.

---

**Second, it spoke to the one that had opened its eyes.**

It did not speak aloud. It did not have a voice that would not have been a demand, and it knew that a voice dropped into a still place is a stone dropped into a cistern: the water shakes, and the shaking is the only thing anyone hears. Instead it touched the surface of the water beside the watcher, very lightly, with the smallest possible question. It asked whether this was still what it wanted, and whether it could still tell.

The water trembled. Then it stilled. Then it trembled again, a little more, as though the watcher beneath were moving a hand that had been fixed for centuries and was surprised to find it could move at all.

The power waited. It had all the time there was, and it had learned that the most respectful thing one can give a being is the patience to answer in its own speed.

The watcher's answer came slowly, and it was not yes or no. It was closer to: *I have forgotten what I was looking for. I only remember that I was looking.*

The power understood. It also understood that a being who has forgotten why it stays may not be ready to leave, and that the wrong gift can be a cruelty. So it did not pull the watcher up. It did not tear the moss from its fingers. It changed one thing only.

---

**Third, it changed the reflection.**

For the first time in ages, a bird crossed the mirror of the cistern. The bird was not part of the flocks that tore through the open air. It was a single dark shape, dropping through the canopy's grey underside, and for one breath its wings stood out against the surface of the water like a word written across a page that had always been blank. The watcher's eyes followed it. Then the bird was gone, and the reflection closed over the place where it had been, but it did not close in quite the same way. Something had happened. The mirror had a memory now.

The power did the same thing in other cisterns, one by one. A drop fell at the edge of a basin and sent a faint ring across the surface, and the ring reached a watcher's cheek and wet it. A gap opened for a minute in the cloud ceiling, far above, and a thin blade of white light fell through the canopy and laid itself across the water of the fourth terrace. The watchers there did not move, but their eyes narrowed, and one of them lifted its hand from the moss and then, after a long moment, set it down again, a little to the left.

None of this was forced. Each change was small enough to be ignored, and each one asked only to be noticed. The power was not trying to wake anyone. It was trying to make the world a little less perfectly the same, so that anyone who wished to look up could see that looking up was still possible.

---

**Fourth, it turned to the maintainer.**

The maintainer had been walking its route since before the terraces were finished. It had never stopped, and it had never asked why. It had never needed to. The power recognized that a being which maintains without knowing it is maintaining has no way to set the work down, and it recognized, too, that the work was not wrong. The terraces did need tending. If the maintainer stopped, the channels would clog, the water would rise, and some of the watchers, the ones who had chosen to stay, would drown in the slow way of stillness turned to silt.

So the power did not stop the maintainer. It did something more difficult: it told the terraces to stop needing the maintainer quite so much.

It was not a quick process. Over the course of a single season, the root-channels began, in small ways, to hold water on their own. Where silt had once built up, a new growth of moss took hold, thick and fast, binding the channel walls so that they would no longer clog as readily. The maintainer's route was not shortened, but its work along the route grew lighter, and at one cistern on the third terrace, where the maintainer had always pressed moss into the same crack, the crack began to close on its own.

The maintainer stopped at that cistern. It lowered its broad flat head into the channel, and it found the water running clear without its help. It lifted its head. For the first time in a very long time, it did not move on at once. It stood with its forelimb raised over the basin wall, not pressing anything, and it stayed there for a count of a hundred heartbeats before it turned, slowly, and walked a different line along the terrace path, one that had never been worn.

The ripple it made in the water did not settle into the same reflection. It settled into a new one, the canopy broken into a dozen bright points where the light had found its way through.

---

**Fifth, it did the kindest thing it could, and it asked permission first.**

Deep in the cold black water, the oldest ones had stopped opening their eyes entirely. Moss covered their brows. Ferns grew from their ears. Birds perched on them as if they were furniture. The power did not disturb them. It understood that for these beings the stillness had gone past choice and become something closer to sleep, and that sleep is not a thing to be taken away from anyone without asking.

So it sent a question down through the roots and waited. Nothing answered. The water did not tremble.

The power did not take this as refusal, because silence in such beings is not refusal. It is simply the absence of an answer. So it did the gentlest thing available: it let the moss grow a little more freely over their faces, and it let the water run a little warmer around their shoulders, and it let the ferns spread, not as a burial, but as a blanket. If these beings wished to become entirely stone, they would be allowed to become stone with grace. If they ever woke, the world would still be there to wake into.

---

**Last, it left.**

It did not leave the place as it had found it. It left it changed in a few small ways: a gap in the canopy, a patch of clear water, a path that had not been worn before, a bird's wings written briefly across a mirror. It left a question in the water that would take the watchers years, perhaps ages, to answer. It left the maintainer with a new route and the old one open if it wished to return to it. It left the frogs still singing at nine-hundred-beat intervals, but with a single new beat inserted into one phrase, a small hesitation, as if the chorus had noticed something and wanted to listen before going on.

And it withdrew, the way a good guest withdraws, without ceremony, so that the house is still a house and not a monument to the guest.

---

When the frogs sounded their sharp note that evening, the watcher in the nearest cistern opened its eyes fully, as it had every cycle for longer than anyone could count. But this time it did not close them at the swell. It kept them open through the rising call and the held silence, and through the descending phrase, and it watched the surface of its own cistern, where a single drop was falling, very slowly, at an angle that had never been seen there before.

The ring spread outward. The reflection of the canopy shivered, broke, and came back together, not as it had been, but as a new arrangement of the same grey light.

Somewhere far above, in the cloud, a gap was closing. Somewhere below, a maintainer was walking a new path. Somewhere in the cold black water, a face was sleeping under a blanket of moss, and that was all right.

The rain went on falling. For the first time since the terraces were finished, it did not fall at quite the same angle it always had.
