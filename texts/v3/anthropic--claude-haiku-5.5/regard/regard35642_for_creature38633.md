# What Attention Would Do

Attention does not arrive all at once. It comes the way weather comes to a valley, first as a change in pressure, then as a slight difference in the light on the water. The stair does not notice it. The stair has been listening to its own pumps for too long to notice anything else.

The attention is wise, and it is gentle, and its first impulse is to be still. It has seen many places that were broken and wanted to be fixed. It has learned that the impulse to fix something is often the impulse to be useful to oneself. So it watches for a long time before it does anything, and what it watches is the creature in the gap.

---

**What it sees.**

It sees a body that has been shaped by a purpose nobody remembers. The purpose has become a kind of grace. The creature's hand knows the quarter-turn of a wheel the way a tongue knows a word in a language it has forgotten. Its breathing has taken the rhythm of the largest pump, and so the stair itself breathes through it. It does not know what the water is for. It has never needed to know. It has become a thing that keeps a count, and the count keeps the lower basins alive.

It also sees what the creature is paying. The wheel is rusted fast, and each turn costs it. Its joints are folded under a body that once had longer legs. The valve cap on its shoulder squeals when it moves. It rides every flood pressed against the girder, shaken to the bone, and it returns to the seam afterward with no visible reward except that the grazers are fed and the birds are not disturbed and the fish rise into the light.

The attention considers this for a long while. It asks itself whether the work is good, and it decides that the work is good in the way that all faithful repetition is good, and that it is also expensive. Both things are true, and it is not the attention's place to decide which one matters more.

---

**What it considers doing.**

It could tell the creature that the job is finished. The summit reservoir has been dry for longer than the moss has existed. The city that once drew from it is gone, and the flood does not reach any place that needs it, except the grazers, who came to the flood because the flood came. But the attention sees the danger in this. A being that is told its purpose is over may lose the shape of its days, and a creature whose days lose their shape can fall apart in ways that have nothing to do with rust. The grazers are not ready to find another rhythm. The birds have built their nests around the gate's timing. Even the predators have learned to wait. To tell the creature that it is useless would be to take away a meaning it has already built with its whole body, and the attention will not do that.

It could repair the stair. It could strip the rust from the wheels, replace the pitted couplings, drain the reservoirs, and restore the channels to working order. But that would turn the stair back into a machine, and the machine's old purpose would still not be understood. The stair has already changed into something alive, and to restore it would be to decide that its life was a mistake. The attention is not willing to make that judgment on anyone's behalf.

It could explain the manual. It could show the creature the old diagrams: the city below the ceiling, the schedules of irrigation, the names of the women and men who once read the gauges in the switchboard rooms. But the creature does not need to understand its work in order to do it well. Understanding might also change the way it turns the wheel, and a change of heart at this late stage could be harder on it than the rust.

So the attention refuses all of these. It does not want to be the one who decides what the stair is for. It wants only to make the living thing in the gap a little less hurt by what it already does.

---

**What it does.**

It does three small things.

First, it works on the wheel. It does not force the rust free, because force would break something old and fragile. Instead it lets the damp, which has been falling as rain for centuries, settle more slowly into the joint. It lets the moss in the spindle loosen by a few degrees. On the next cycle, when the creature's hand closes on the spoke and turns, the wheel gives more than it has in a long time. The creature feels the resistance drop, and it stops, and waits, the way it always waits, and then it turns again, and the gate grinds open with less effort than before. It does not know why. It takes the easier turn the way water takes a lower channel.

Second, it lets the light in. A single cracked pane above the gap, which has been clouded for generations, clears for a moment. A shaft of ordinary daylight falls through the haze and lands on the ridge above the creature's eyes. The ridge is always wet, and the warmth of the light is strange to it. It does not move toward the light. It only lies still and feels the warmth on the soft flesh, and for a short while its breathing slows past the pump's beat and into a rhythm of its own.

Third, it makes one silence. On a single cycle, the lower pump does not knock. The count the creature has kept so long comes up one beat short, and the stair holds its breath for the length of a heartbeat. The creature stops. Its ridge turns toward the place where the sound should have been. For the first time in what may be a very long time, there is nothing to count, and nothing goes wrong. The water still falls. The grazers still gather. The attention withdraws before the creature has time to be afraid of the quiet, so that the quiet is only a space and not a loss.

After that, the attention leaves the stair as it was. Nothing else has changed. The sluices still open and close. The predators still wait on the walls. The flowers still burst and fold. The animals still know the rhythm of the water, and the creature still keeps its count. It has been lightened by a few degrees, and by a little rest, and that is all.

---

**Afterward.**

A wise attention does not need to be thanked, and it does not need to be known. The best help it can offer is help that does not change the shape of a life. The stair was never meant to be saved, and the creature was never meant to be freed. They were meant to continue, and they have continued in their own way, and the attention's task was only to make the continuing a little less painful.

It is possible that the creature will never notice the change in the wheel or the light or the silence. It is possible that it will notice and feel nothing more than a small, unexplained ease, the way an old body feels a warm day after a long winter. Either way the attention is content. It has not taken the purpose away, or given it a new one, or decided for the stair what its last years should mean.

The gate shudders open somewhere above. The first cold rush of water pours over the lip of the stone and down the channel, and the whole stair trembles with the sound. The grazers lift their heads in the lower basins. The birds on the ledges fold their wings and wait. The predators come down the walls. The blind fish turn in silver flashes at the surface.

And the creature, in the gap of the split housing, with its ridge wet and its hand loosened on the wheel, begins very slowly to climb.
