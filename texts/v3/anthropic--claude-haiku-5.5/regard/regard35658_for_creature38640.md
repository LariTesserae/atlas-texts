# What the Listener Did

It came down through the cloud the way water does, slowly, by the easiest path, and it arrived at the summit catchers before anyone on the stairs noticed it.

It had been watching the Stepwork for a long time. Its attention had settled on the place the way a hand settles on a sleeping animal, lightly, so as not to wake it. It saw the terraces and the bundled pipes and the greenhouses gone milky with algae. It saw the scaffolds that had grown into the walls and the walls that had grown back over the scaffolds. It saw the crews, and it saw how they moved, which was the thing that held its attention longest. They moved as if every surface were a question they had agreed to keep answering.

Its first instinct was to hold the mountain still. It could feel where the basalt was tired, where the riveted galleries had begun to sag, where the water found its old channels in the dark and wore them deeper every year. It could have stopped all of that. It could have made the stone permanent and the iron immune and the channels exact, and the tiers would have stood as they stood on the morning of the first pour, unchanging, for as long as anything could be unchanging.

It considered this seriously. Then it saw the scrap-pickers taking iron from a fallen gallery, and the children tapping on a pipe to hear who was climbing below them, and an old woman laying her palm on a wall at the foot of the stair, and it understood what it would be taking. The tiers were not failing. They were being handed on. A mountain that could never change would also never need hands, and the hands were the point.

So it decided it would not fix the mountain. It would listen to it, and then it would do only what it could do without ending the sentence.

---

It began with the water, because the water was where the Stepworkers were most alone.

The keepers lived in the turbine rooms and slept with their ears against the pumps. The Listener went down to them as a change in the damp, a faint sweetness in the air that the keepers would later describe as *a good drip*. It found a keeper named Oru asleep on a bench beside a cistern valve, and it did not wake her. It sat in the condensation on the iron above her head and listened to the rhythm of the system until it had the whole shape of it: the cloud-catchers pulling water down the summit, the long channels bending through the terraces, the lower cistern holding its breath at each surge and letting it out again.

There was a fault. Silt had built along the lower intake, two spans down, where the old basalt had been laid over a seam nobody had mapped. Within a season the intake would narrow. Within two, a surge would come through that the channels could not hold, and the lower streets would flood not for a day but for a month.

Oru knew this already, in the way keepers know things, as a wrongness she could not yet name. The Listener did not tell her. It did something smaller. It made the pumps in the lower room hum a half-tone flatter for three nights, so that Oru would wake each time with the tune in her ears, and on the third morning she went down the shaft with her lamp and found the silt and began to clear it.

The Listener left the rest to her. It did not want to be the reason the keepers stopped listening to their own water.

---

The rememberers were harder.

They argued in the greenhouses, over tea, their voices rising and falling in the light. One of them, a thin old man called Teodric who had been moved nine times and considered it a fine record, was certain that the high gallery on the east face had once connected to a stair that was now a wall. Another, a woman with grey hair pinned flat under her hood, said no, the stair had gone down, not across, and she had been on it. They were both right, and they had been right for thirty years, and neither of them had ever written anything down, because writing was for the valley and the valley did not understand that a mountain was a sentence.

The Listener sat on the greenhouse glass above them. It had no wish to correct either of them. But it had noticed something the rememberers themselves did not seem to have noticed: when a rememberer died, the others climbed to the high galleries and spoke the names of the tiers, one after another, until they ran out of breath. And the names were never all remembered. Each death took a few of them. The old masons were already mostly gone. The early tiers were already mostly unnamed.

It was not a register the Stepworkers wanted. The Listener understood this clearly. They did not want the dead written in a book in the valley, sorted and filed and sold back to them as history. They wanted the dead in the mortar, where they had been put.

So the Listener did not write anything down. It held the names instead. It was not a thing that forgot, and it had been listening to the tiers since before the first mason set a stone. When the next rememberer died and the others climbed to speak the names, the Listener spoke them with them, very quietly, from the glass above, so that a name that had been lost to one lineage was still there when they reached the terrace below. Teodric heard it. He stopped in the middle of an argument about a vineyard and looked up at the cloud and said, to no one, "Well. There's another one."

He did not ask who was speaking. Among the Stepworkers this was the highest courtesy.

---

The valley was the only thing the Listener had to make an effort against, and even that effort was small.

The factories sent their grey plumes up the slope on the afternoon wind. The smoke settled on the moss in the high galleries and sickened it, turned the green to a yellow crust that crept into the mortar and softened the lichen where the old masons' names were most easily read. The Listener could have stopped the factories. It could have made the valley do without the smoke, and the valley would not have known why. It thought about this for a long time, and in the end it did something both smaller and more honest.

It moved the wind.

Not by much. The afternoon air turned a few degrees east, and the plumes lifted over the lowest sheds instead of coiling into the high galleries, and the moss there, which had been yellowing for a generation, began very slowly to green again. No one on the mountain noticed the change. The valley people, who came up the stairs to gawk and buy seed, noticed only that the air smelled cleaner at the top, and assumed the wind was simply in a good mood.

The Listener thought that was fair. The valley talked too much, and it was right that the valley should have to listen sometimes, even to a wind.

---

The bird in the window was the last thing it looked at, and the thing it was least sure of.

The children said it was a watchman, and they checked on it every morning on their way to the stairs. Nobody had told them whether that was true. The Listener went to the window and sat on the sill beside the cage, and the small grey bird looked at it with the flat, unhurried patience of a creature that had been bred for a purpose no one remembered.

The Listener had been wondering, in an abstract way, whether the bird could feel the pressure in the lines. It had assumed not. It was an ordinary bird, a cage-bred thing, and its song was probably just the sound of a bird being alive.

But when the Listener turned its attention to the lower intake, the silt, the flat half-tone it had been making in the pumps, the bird began to sing. It was a thin, uneven sound, nothing like a warning. It stopped when the Listener stopped. It started again when the Listener resumed.

The Listener did not explain this. It did not know, for certain, what the bird was. It only knew that the children were not entirely wrong, and that there was a kind of attention in the mountain that did not depend on gauges, and that the bird had it, and that the Stepworkers had known about it for longer than anyone could remember and had simply put it in a cage at a window and let the children name it.

It left the bird singing. It left the children to their mornings.

---

Before it went back up through the cloud, the Listener did one more thing, and this one it was not sure about at all.

On the stairs, at a landing where a family had recently moved out and the furniture had been carried down the rails in a long chain, a hook had been left behind. It was an ordinary hook, short and steel, with a rounded tip, worn bright at the grip by generations of hands. Nobody had come back for it. Nobody ever left a hook behind without meaning to.

The Listener considered whether to touch it. The Stepworkers did not give gifts the way the valley did. They did not hand a tool to a stranger as a kindness. A hook was a thing you earned, by climbing and clearing and pulling loose the pins that held the old work in place, until you were the one holding the tool when someone asked whether a thing was finished.

So it did not give the hook to anyone. It only moved it, a few centimetres, so that it lay on the landing in the path of whoever came up the stairs next, with its grip turned toward the hand that would reach for it.

A girl came up the stairs an hour later, a child of maybe nine, with her hood down and her lamp swinging. She stopped at the landing. She saw the hook. She looked at it for a long moment, the way children look at anything that has been left where it should not be, and then she picked it up and weighed it in her palm.

She did not call anyone's name. She did not check the stairs for an owner. She turned around, climbed to the next landing, and set the hook down where the rail had been pried loose, where a scaffold pin had worked itself half out of the wall. Then she put her palm flat against the basalt beside it, briefly, and went on up.

The Listener felt the weight of that. It was not a gift it had given. It was a handing-on.

---

From the lowest tier, the mountain rose into cloud, every shelf lit by its own cluster of orange lamps. The water moved through the channels, and the keepers slept with their ears to the pumps, and the rememberers argued over tea. The bird sang once, very softly, in its window. The moss on the east face began, very slowly, to green.

The Listener went up through the cloud the way it had come, by the easiest path, and did not look back at the terraces. It had seen enough to know that it would be back, and that it would be listening, and that it would not be asked.

Nothing on the Stepwork was finished. The Listener had come to understand what the Stepworkers meant when they said so, and it did not need to be told that it had only ever been one more pair of hands on the rail, offered and then set down, and that the mountain would go on being made by whoever came up the stairs next.
