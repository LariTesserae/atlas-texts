# The Attention

It does not arrive the way weather arrives. Nothing announces it. There is only a shift in the quality of attention, as if the valley had become a little more aware of itself.

The power that notices this place has watched a great many worlds. It has seen oceans boil and continents drift, and it has learned that the most common error of the strong is to mistake their own longing for the needs of the things they love. So its first act is restraint. It asks what the basin is, and the answer is not a question about water.

The basin is a finished thing. It is not a failed attempt at a garden or a city. Over immense spans the bodies here have made a place that holds together, each generation reading the walls of the last, each death returning its chitin and sap to the structure. The towers do not hold up in spite of the dryness. They hold up because of it. Chalk does not slake. Wax does not rot where there is no rain. The whole architecture is a bargain struck with aridity, and it has kept its side for longer than most stars have burned.

So the power does not want to pour water into the valley. It has considered this, because the impulse is strong and it is not stupid. Rain would be a flood to the towers. It would soften the banded walls and dissolve the galleries the tenders have spent centuries rebuilding to exact dimensions. The haulers' files would turn to mud. The callers' pulse would lose its meaning, because the sky would no longer be a thing they could count on. Restoring the river would not restore the world the callers remember. It would end the world they are living in.

And there is the harder question: whose loss is this? The rhythm under the calls rises and falls like something that once moved across a wet surface and then stopped. The callers repeat it in still air, when the sky is clear. The power listens to this for a long time, and it is moved by it, which it notes with some suspicion, since being moved is not the same as knowing what is good. It cannot tell whether the rhythm is grief, or memory with no feeling attached, or simply the shape of an old sound that the walls have preserved because it was inscribed early and never erased. Perhaps the callers miss the river. Perhaps they have never known what missing is. Perhaps the pulse is only a pattern, carried forward because it is cheap to carry.

The power decides it will not decide this for them.

But it does one thing, and it chooses it with care.

Far out on the plateau, beyond the rim where you stand, there is a long fracture in the old bedrock, a dry seam that once carried the river underground. The deep stone there still holds a thread of old moisture, tens of meters down, trapped in a mineral memory of the valley's last wet age. The stalks have roots that reach that far. The fungal mats do not, but the stalks do, and the stalks feed the whole basin.

The power draws a small cloud over the far plateau, the kind of cloud that forms only once in a long while under a certain stillness of air. It lets it rain for one night. The rain is slight. It falls into the old seam and not into the basin. It does not reach the towers, or the galleries, or the dust fields where the haulers work. It goes down into the deep stone, sinks, and finds the thread of old water, and the stalks' roots drink it.

In the morning the stalks have thickened. Their fanned disks are a shade darker. The sap runs a little faster in the leaves, and the skitterers, which read the ground with their constantly moving antennae, come up from the seam and trace a bright pattern through the mats that has not been seen in a long time.

The power watches the callers that morning. For the first time in the memory of this basin, the rhythm changes. The long swell of the old curve is not repeated only in still air. It is raised, carried into the open morning, answered from across the basin by a second voice. The drumming shifts pitch. Something has happened in the world that the callers can hear, and they are responding to it in the only language they have.

The power does not know what they have concluded. It does not know if they will call this a gift, a sign, a weather that came and went, or nothing at all. It suspects they will not interpret it as a message. A message would require someone who sent it. The callers have no concept of a sender, only of patterns that repeat and patterns that break. But the pattern has broken, and the old rhythm has been answered by something new, and that is enough. Whatever meaning there is will be made inside the basin, by the walls and the bodies, in the slow way they make everything.

Then the power withdraws, and the attention lifts.

It leaves no monument. It leaves no instruction. It does add one thing, almost invisibly, to the record: a thin band of faint blue-grey mineral in the lowest tier of the oldest wall, the color of the skitterers' glow, laid down as the walls lay down everything, by slow accretion over the next few seasons. Haulers will walk past it. Tenders will feel it under their claws. It will become a stratum, like all the others, and in a thousand years someone who knows how to read the walls may see that a single season had a different color. Let them wonder. Let the basin keep its own time.

As for you, standing at the rim with the dust in your throat and the roar pressing against your sternum: the power has noticed you too. It has noticed that you came to see a thing and not to take it. It has noticed that you listened long enough to hear the calls become conversation, and that you began to wonder what they were saying. This is the only kind of attention that the power thinks should be given to a place like this. It asks almost nothing of the watcher, except to keep watching honestly, to admit what you do not know, and to resist the urge to fix what is still, by its own strange logic, whole.

The power will come back in a generation, or a century, or perhaps a few thousand years, if the clock of this valley allows such a word. It will listen for the pulse. If the rhythm has changed, it will be glad. If it has stayed the same, it will be glad of that too, because a people who keep their old songs in the face of new weather are doing something the power itself has never managed to do well.

Below, the basin roars on. The haulers set down their dust in precise piles. The callers hold the pulse, now a little wider than before. The tenders turn the brood toward the light, and the walls grow another course, and somewhere under the dry stone a thread of old water moves in the dark, reaching for the roots that were waiting for it.
