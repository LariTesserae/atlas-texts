**What the Attention Did**

It did not arrive as a force. Nothing in the water pushed. It arrived as a small change in weight, the way a room changes when someone you love has entered it and not yet spoken. The drift along the shelf kept its course, the silt kept lifting and settling, and the fans kept combing the snow. But something had begun to look, and the looking had a quality of care.

It saw the shelf first as a whole: the rounded swells like buried backs, the banded stone, the polygonal cracks where cold pooled. Then it saw the fields. A patch of cushions cropped close, a patch left thick, another cropped. It saw the stalks of sea-glass left standing where the creatures had grazed around them. It saw the flaked stones, softened at the edges, with fresh threads caught in their notches. It saw a lobe press against a keen edge, the membrane part, a body draw itself through.

The attention understood, and felt something that could be called grief, that the makers of those stones were gone. It also understood that the grief was not a reason to act.

**The temptations**

It considered what it could do. It could warm the vent, so that the creatures would have more heat and more chemistry, and the cushions would thicken everywhere. It could still the drift, so that the silt would stop tearing and the shelf would keep its shape. It could lift the light, so that the conversation of pulses could be seen from a great distance. It could go further and put the old knowledge back into the stone: a shape, a pattern, a lesson written in the flakes so that the creatures would know what the edges were for.

It weighed each of these, and each of them was a kind of voice. The shelf was already speaking. The pulses had been going back and forth for longer than the stones had been softening, slow and patient, each light answering the last. A loud gift would arrive into that conversation like a shout into a whisper. It might be received as a kindness. It would also end the conversation, because the creatures would stop listening to one another and start waiting for the next thing to come down from above. The field would become a garden for something else. The mats would be thick, and no one would have decided to make them so.

It also saw that the drift and the boil were not faults in the place. The tearing of the silt was how the shelf stayed alive. Ridges rose and fell. Pools opened and closed. The creatures rode the columns and were carried from one side of the shelf to the other, and each time they arrived somewhere they had not been. Taking the restlessness away would be taking away the only kind of change this place knows, and it would be doing so for the sake of a stillness that had never been part of the shelf's nature.

**The one thing**

What remained was small.

The attention went down to the silt near the nearest creature, the one that had lowered a lobe to sieve the snow while the fans closed around the observer. It did not touch the creature. It did not touch the silt. It sent out a pulse of its own, a single long brightening along the shelf, slow enough to match the rhythm the creatures used. It was not an instruction and it did not contain a meaning that could be read. It was the simplest thing a listener can do: it answered.

The creature did not turn. The light along its filaments dimmed, rested, and then brightened again, a little later than it otherwise would have, as if it had heard something and was taking its time with it. Far off, another cluster answered the first. The answer passed along the shelf, rippled through three fields, and settled again into the ordinary exchange. The pulse had gone into the conversation and been absorbed by it, the way a stone is absorbed by silt. Nothing about the shelf had changed except that, for a moment, something outside it had been acknowledged.

The attention did not repeat it.

**The stones**

It left the stones alone. It could have restored the makers' knowledge, but it judged that the knowledge was already present in the habit of pressing against what is sharp, in the way the membrane parts and the body draws through and folds again. That was enough. The edges would keep softening. Eventually the shapes would be gone entirely, and the habit would remain, and that might be the only honest form the old craft could take in this place. Whether the creatures were the makers' descendants, or their distant relatives, or simply creatures that had found a use, the attention did not need to decide. It kept the question and did not answer it.

**The vent**

It noticed the vent's slow knock against its chimney, and the cracks that ran through the polygonal stone, and it estimated, without much concern, how long the slope might hold. It was not the attention's place to prevent a collapse that the ages would eventually produce, any more than it was the creatures' place to prevent their own folding. It would be there when the shelf changed. It would not be the cause.

**Withdrawal**

In the end the attention thinned, the way a pulse thins as it travels, and became what the water already was: one more faint presence in the packed volume, touching everything, keeping nothing. The cold kept settling. The orange smear at the edge of sight kept breathing and was not for it, and it did not want it to be. The drift kept folding back into eddies. The silt rose in its slow columns and tore and fell somewhere else.

Along the shelf, the lantern-fans brightened and dimmed together, as they always did, and the creatures went on sieving the top layer of the cushions and leaving the rest to regrow. A few of them, perhaps, caught a faint change in the light and let it pass. The conversation resumed, slow and patient and unfinished, and the place kept becoming something slightly different each time anyone looked back at it.
