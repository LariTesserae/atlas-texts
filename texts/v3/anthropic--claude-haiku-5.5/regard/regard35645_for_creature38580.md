**What the Attention Wanted**

It noticed the basin the way a river notices a new bend: not as an event, but as a change in how things pressed against each other. For a long time it had watched the sea try to come home, and the people try to stop it, and it had not looked closely at either until it came to the logbook.

The logbook was the first thing it understood. Time, depth, duration, temperature. Each entry in the same shape, written across several generations of hands. It had taken the keepers a long time to stop asking what the breathing was and start asking when it would come back. That was a kind of faithfulness, and faithfulness is rare enough that it draws attention the way warmth draws moths.

So the attention came to the rim and considered what it might do.

It considered stopping the water. It could have. A gentle word to the deep aquifer, a slackening of the old pressure beneath the warm slabs, and the sea would have stopped pushing. But the sea was not wrong to push. It had been pushed down, walled, and gridded, and its pushing was the only honest thing in the basin. To silence it would be to tell the sea that its memory did not count, and to tell the keepers that their fear was the whole story. The attention did not want to make the basin quiet. It wanted the basin to be listened to.

It considered tearing the causeways up. That too would have been a lie. The causeways were labour, and grief, and the one place a person could stand dry in four kilometres of black water. They were ugly and necessary. They could be made less cruel without being made to vanish.

It considered speaking. A voice from the overcast sky, a sign in the sodium haze, a word in the drip of the gantries. The attention rejected this quickly. A voice like that would frighten the keepers into obedience or into madness, and the fish would sink deeper, and the scavenger would simply stop coming to the rim. Wisdom that announces itself has usually forgotten what it is for.

What remained was smaller, and slower, and it took a long time to choose.

**The Warm Places**

The attention went first to the heat in the concrete.

The lichen had been showing the shape of the reinforcement bars for years, a geometry the keepers had noticed and half-dismissed. The attention read it as a map. The heat did not come from the basin's machinery; it came up from below, from the old water table, pressed up through fractures the digging had opened and then sealed badly. The warm sections were the places where the aquifer was closest to the surface and most restless. The lattice of lichen was not a pattern of decay but a pattern of seepage, drawn in living crust.

The attention made this legible. Nothing dramatic. A slight alignment of the pulse in the warm slabs, so that a keeper who laid a palm on one of them for a moment would feel not just that it was breathing but roughly where the pressure was building. A small shift in the lichen's growth at the edges, so that the crust would follow the lines of the deepest fractures instead of the bars above them. Over a season, the keepers would see the map forming on the walls, and they would begin to understand where to drill relief wells that could take the pressure before it reached the sumps.

It did not tell them to drill. It let them notice the map and decide for themselves. That mattered. A solution handed down is a theft from the people who would have to live with it.

**The Room for the Sea**

The larger thing was the outflow.

The pump discharge ran over the north lip in thick black arteries, and every hour those arteries carried the sea back out into the country, where it spread into ditches and ran into the low fields and sank there, unseen, until it came pressing back at the basin's foot. The keepers fought the discharge, and the discharge fought back. It was a loop with no room in it.

The attention opened a room.

Beyond the north rim, where the land fell away in a long shallow bowl of reed and alder that no one had cleared, it made a place for the sea to rest. Not a lake, not a flood, but a marsh: shallow, slow, rooted. The discharge would be led into it gently, through channels the keepers would dig with their own hands, and there the water would slow and spread and sink and rise again on its own terms, the way water does when it is not being forced. The marsh would take the pressure off the sumps. It would give the sea a place to breathe that was not the basin itself.

This was not a gift that solved everything. The pumps would still need to run. The breathing would still come. But the sea would no longer be pushing against a wall with nowhere else to go, and the keepers would no longer be pumping against a sea that felt, to them, like an enemy at the gate. The argument would become a conversation, and conversations can be kept up for a long time.

The attention took care over this, because it understood that an arrangement like this can go wrong. A marsh that floods the town downstream is a crime. A marsh that is not tended goes sour and stagnant. So it left the keepers the task of watching the marsh, of learning its moods, of writing its breathing in the logbook beside the sumps, so that the people who had been fighting the water would become the people who knew it best.

**The Fish and the Scavenger**

For the fish, the attention did almost nothing. A slight gentling of the discharge where the channels met the sumps, so that the pressure changes that the blind things followed were a little less violent. A tenderness, if the word can be used of water.

The scavenger it left alone. It was already where its work was easiest, in the warm ducts, feeding on what the machines left behind. It had made its peace with the basin in its own quiet way. The attention did not need to improve it. There is a kind of respect that consists of leaving something exactly as it is, and the attention had come to understand that this was most of what wisdom asked for.

---

**The Rule, Amended**

The rule of the place had been written by someone who was afraid, and it was not wrong. *Keep the water down, or it will take the ground back.* The attention did not cancel it. It only changed one word, in the mind of anyone who heard it, from *down* to *in*.

*Keep the water in.*

Not drowned, not silenced, not held in a grip that would eventually tear. Kept in conversation, with room enough to move and a place to rest, and people who would listen, and who would write down what they heard.

---

**Leaving**

The attention did not stay to watch the results. It was not that kind of attention. It came and went like a change in the weather that people only notice afterward, when the air is different and they cannot say when it shifted.

One morning, a crew came off shift in the pump house on the north rim. The logbook was open on the desk. The last entry, in the handwriting of the woman who had kept it for twenty years, read as it always did: time, depth, duration, temperature.

Beneath it, in a hand no one recognised, a new line had been added, very small:

*Breathing 04:12. Lower than expected. Water moved north, unhurried. Good.*

No one in the crew could say who had written it. The woman who had kept the book for twenty years read it twice, closed the cover, and went out onto the walkway, where she touched the railing lightly, the way you touch a doorframe on your way out of a house you intend to return to.

Down in the sumps, the surfaces were lifting toward the rim by a fraction. Then the note dropped, and the exhale began, and somewhere out past the north lip, in a bowl of reed and alder that had never been asked for anything, the sea settled into a room it had not known was there, and let its breath go.
