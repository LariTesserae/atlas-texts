**The Attention That Came Down Into the Cloud**

It did not arrive so much as thicken. For a long time the cloud beneath the shelf had been only cloud, a white sea with a moving surface. Then, one grey morning, the sea grew attentive. The falls kept their voices, the birds kept calling, but there was a new quality to the air, the way a room changes when someone you trust walks into it and does not yet speak.

The power had come looking for nothing in particular. It had wandered among high places for a long time, and most of them were loud with their own importance. This one was not loud. It was full of small, exact things, each of them keeping its own time, and the power found that it wanted to stay before it understood why.

The first thing it did was listen.

It listened to the seeps ticking against basalt, the rills finding their grooves, the lichen beards shifting in the faint wind. Then it listened under those sounds to the breath. The stone exhaled. A cold column rose from a crack, rolled out over the rock, and hung there, thinning, until the fault seam went still and the mountain drew back. The power noticed that the stone did not breathe in the way a creature breathes to stay alive. It breathed the way a field breathes out after rain, slowly, as part of an exchange that ran through everything on the shelf. The cold became cloud, the cloud became water, the water filled the rosettes and the basins and the hollows, and the moss grew green where the breath was strongest. Nothing here was wasted. The breath was the economy of the place, and everyone lived inside its rhythm.

So the power decided, before it did anything else, that it would not hurry this.

It spent a season learning the shelf. It learned the goats, who paused at every open seam with their heads low, ears turned toward the crack, waiting for the exhalation to finish before they crossed. It learned the long grey hunter that moved through the lichen and never chased, and it understood that the herds' vigilance was not fear so much as attention, a kind of conversation carried on in silence. It learned the slate-backed bird that called from the highest outcrop, and the frogs tapping in their cups at dusk, and the flightless birds that burst through the ferns in loud alarm. It learned that every one of them had a place, and that the places were spaced wide enough that no one crowded anyone else.

It learned the human marks too, and they were harder to hold.

The cairns were coming apart. Moss had swallowed their lower courses, and the flat slabs were settling into the scree a finger's width at a time, as slowly as the lichen spread. The dry stone walls had given up their shape. The rope bridges sagged, grey and frayed, and the goats had learned to avoid them, which meant the bridges had already been retired by the only creatures who used this place daily. The carved basins were still full of rain, ringed with lichen, and no one drank from them. The power found that it could feel the people who had made these things, faintly, the way one feels the warmth left in a seat after someone has risen from it. They had come here to climb, to trade, to sit, perhaps to pray. They had left the shelf the way they left their fires: carefully, and then not at all.

The power was tempted to rebuild. It could see the cairns standing straight again, the walls gathering their stones, the bridges pulled taut across the deepest cracks. It could feel how much easier that would be, and how much it would please the part of itself that wanted to be useful. But it considered what it would be doing. The shelf was not a ruin waiting for restoration. It was a place where things were allowed to become other things, where the stone reclaimed its edges, where a wall returned to scree and scree grew moss. The forgetting was part of the rhythm too. To stop it would be to make the shelf a museum of someone else's intentions, and the power had no wish to keep the shelf for itself or for anyone else.

So it mended nothing. It left the basins to the rain, the bridges to the wind, the cairns to the slow patience of lichen. What it did instead was smaller and, it hoped, kinder.

On the third week of its attention, a young goat misjudged a long breath. The power felt the mistake before it happened: a small body stepping onto a seam just as the cold column rose, the fog closing in faster than the animal could read it. The herd gathered at the edge, bleating low, and the fog thickened, white and blind. The power did not part the cloud by force, because it did not wish to frighten the herd or disturb the breath beyond what the moment asked. It only let the exhalation run a little shorter than it might have, and it let a gust of wind come up from the falls, just enough to thin the fog on the near side. In the gap the young goat stood unhurt on the wet rock, blinking, and walked back to the herd as if nothing had happened. The herd went on cropping the moss. The power went on listening.

It went to the stone then, the largest resident, and it spoke without words, in the only language the stone seemed to use: cold, water, and time. It did not ask anything. It said only that it had noticed, and that it would keep noticing, and that it hoped the shelf would go on breathing as it had for longer than any of them could count. It did not know what the stone understood. The mountain had no face and no voice the power could hear, only the breath. But after a while, the next exhalation came up long and patient, lasting most of a minute, and the moss along the seam seemed to brighten in the thin light, as if something deep inside the rock had taken a slow breath in return.

The power was not sure that the stone had answered. It was not sure that it needed an answer. What it had come to understand was that its presence was not a gift to be placed on the shelf. It was closer to a witness, and a witness has to be careful, because being watched by something vast and wise can change the way a small thing moves. So the power resolved to stay at the edge of the cloud, close enough to hear the falls and the birds and the breath, far enough that the goats would not look up and the hunter would not change its path. It would come back when the seasons turned, the way the sun returned in its bright, slanting shafts. It would notice whether the lichen had crept another finger's width, whether the basins still held water, whether the frogs had found new cups among the ferns.

When it finally drew back, the shelf was exactly as it had been. The falls spilled over the outer lip in their ribbons. The slate-backed bird called across the gaps, and the call came back off the cliffs from several directions at once. The herds crossed the seams in single file, waiting for each exhalation to pass. The hunter waited in the lichen, and the bats poured from the fissures at dusk, and the stone breathed out its cold column and let it hang and thin.

The only difference was that the place was now known, and that was enough. Somewhere above the cloud, something wise and gentle had paid attention, and asked nothing, and left the rhythm of the shelf intact. The moss kept growing where the breath was strongest. The power had not changed the shelf. It had only learned, for a little while, to be quiet inside it.
