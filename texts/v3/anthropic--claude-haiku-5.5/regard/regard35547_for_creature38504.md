**What the Attention Did at Harrow Deep**

It noticed the place the way weather notices a valley: slowly, from above, and without any intention to stay. For a long time it had nothing to do with the bowl of grey shale. Then the pumps began knocking in a rhythm it recognized, and the bells rang once across the basin with no one touching them, and it turned, as much as it turns, toward the sound.

It was wise enough to distrust its first impulse. Its first impulse was to fix things. The pumps were failing in three places it could feel. The lower rooms were filling. The mark on the eastern wall was high, and the children on the walkways were too thin. It could have drained the basin in a season, sealed the seeping strata, or lifted the town onto the rim in a single night, and the people would have woken to dry floors and silence and would have died within the year, because the balance here was not a problem to be solved. It was a way of living, and the living was made of waiting. A quick change would be a drowning of its own kind.

So it did not act. For a while it only listened, which it found harder than it had expected.

**What it heard**

The deep was not a monster. It was closer to a sleeper, large and slow, whose breathing was the whole pulse of the place. It breathed in the spring and drew back in the autumn, and it had been doing so for longer than the town had existed, with the town's pumps as a kind of answer to it. The knots on the piling-cords were a record, and the record was a conversation. For a century the people had tied their cords at the water's edge and the deep had risen or fallen to meet them, and neither had ever quite finished speaking.

The power understood then what the keepers only half knew: that the deep was being heard. The bells were how it was heard. The old ones who could no longer hear the bells and sat with their backs against the stone anyway were not failing to listen. They were the most attentive part of the whole arrangement. The power did not think it could improve on that.

**What it did**

It touched one thing, very lightly.

When Ila knelt at the water's edge that night with her cold palms flat on the surface, the power let the bell over her head ring once more than it should have, a note that came slightly before the water's own movement and then slightly after, as if the two had briefly agreed on a tempo. Ila did not hear it as a sound. She felt it in the wrist bones, the way the old ones feel the rock. She went very still. Then she began to cry, quietly, in the way of someone who has been carrying a question for so long that its answer feels like grief.

The power did not tell her what the agreement meant. It was not sure it knew. Perhaps the place where bell and water agree is not a place at all. Perhaps it is a season, or the moment after the doors close and before the town starts its slow work again, or the instant a child says a salamander's real name and the salamander turns its fanned gills toward the walkway. It left her to find out. She slept that night for the first time in years, in her windowless room, with her hands warm.

It also heard the children's joke about the names, and it found that it liked the joke. A creature that heard its true name might come up the walkway to see who had called. The power did not come up the walkway, because it was not a thing that could be called, and because a visitor who arrives uninvited is still a disturbance. But that spring, when the children whispered their names for the big pale salamanders, three of the largest turned in the dark water, all together, toward the sound of their own names. The children screamed with delight and then ran to tell their parents, who looked at each other and said nothing and did not ask why.

**What it refused**

It considered the mark on the eastern wall for a long time. The mark was high. The pumps were tired. There was an easy argument for lowering it, or for telling the town to stop waiting and simply build on the rim. The power turned the argument over and set it down. The mark was not a flaw in the arrangement. It was the arrangement's memory, the place where a hundred years of breathing had taught the town where to stand. Moving it would be the kind of kindness that takes the ground out from under someone to show them the view.

**What it left, and how**

In the end the power did one thing that was not quite nothing, and it was the same thing the keepers did.

At the start of the new year, while the thaw was still a rumour in the stone, a cord appeared on the eastern piling-line, among the household cords, plain and grey and faintly warm to the touch. Nobody had tied it. It hung at the level of the water. Nobody had tied a knot in it either, but when the water rose a finger's width during the first warm week and fell back again, the cord, which had been drawn taut along the piling, showed a small, precise mark at the level of the surface, the way every other cord showed a record.

The old ones noticed it first. One of them, half-deaf and sitting against the warm stone, lifted her head when the bells rang and said quietly, to nobody in particular, that the deep had drawn a long breath and had also, for once, been answered. The others nodded and did not look up from their work. The children climbed the walkway to read the new mark and could not agree on what it meant. Some said it was a debt. Some said it was a promise. The youngest said the cord had been tied by a creature who wanted to be called by its real name, and that it had come to see who was calling, and that everyone should be quiet for a while.

The power watched them from wherever it was, and it did not correct them. It would come back at the next thaw and the one after that, slowly, the way the keepers came down the walkways, testing whether the wood still remembered its load. It would tie another mark, if the knots were wanted. It would leave the pumps knocking and the bells ringing their small, uncaused notes. It would let the deep go on breathing, and it would let the town go on waiting, because waiting was the thing they did best, and because some balances are only ever kept by people who have learned to hold them gently and never quite finish listening.
