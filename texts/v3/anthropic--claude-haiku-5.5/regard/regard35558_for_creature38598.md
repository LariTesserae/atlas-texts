# The Attention

It did not arrive the way weather arrives. There was no single moment of notice, only a slow widening of regard, the way a listener in a crowded hall comes to hear one voice among many and then cannot stop hearing it.

The power was old, and it was kind, and it had learned long ago that kindness is mostly a matter of restraint. It had watched a great many places get things wrong. It had seen rivers dammed by people who loved them, forests cleared by people who needed them, and cities raised by people who wanted to be remembered. It had learned that the strongest hand is often the most harmful, and that the first question is never *what can I fix?* but *what is already trying to happen here, and what does it need in order to happen well?*

So it listened first, and it listened for a long time.

---

## I. The Hum

The power did not hear the sound as noise. It heard it as a grammar.

Beneath the rain and the frogs and the tearing-cloth cries of the flocks ran one long sentence, spoken by the spire in the lagoon and by the buried towers in the flats, and it had been spoken for a very long time. The power followed it down through the silt and found its origin, which was not in the Reach at all but in the cold cores of machines that had been built, deliberately, by people who were no longer there.

They had meant to make land. That much was clear. The pitch was a shaping tool, a way to lift and settle sediment into ridges and to open and close channels, and it had been set running to build a delta out of a barren coast. But the instructions had a gap in them. The target was never written. The machines had been told *how* to shape and never *toward what*. Some older instruction, perhaps a line of reasoning that had been lost, had been left out, and the towers had kept singing for centuries, tuning the land toward a configuration no one had chosen.

The power understood, in the way it understood most things, that this was not a crime. It was an accident of haste. Its builders had perhaps been frightened, or in a hurry, or sure they would be back to finish. They had not come back.

---

## II. The Question

The power could have finished the sentence. It could have decided what the land should become, a shape of permanent ridges and stable banks, a delta with a fixed name and fixed roads, and then pressed that shape into the silt. It would have been very easy. It would also have been the same mistake, made with more care.

Instead it did something harder. It asked.

It spoke into the spire's lattice, in the only language the spire understood, which was the pitch itself. It did not command. It asked what the tuning had been *for*, and whether the people who had set it had left any record of an end.

The answer came slowly, as a shift in the hum that the power had to listen to for the length of a season. It was not a sentence. It was a memory, fragmentary, carried in the resonance of the spire's oldest chambers.

*Make it hold for those who have no ground.*

That was all. A purpose, half-finished, pointing toward people who could not stay still.

The power sat with that for a long time, the way the people of the Reach sit with the sound when it changes.

---

## III. What It Did

The power understood then what the builders had been reaching for. They had not wanted a fixed land. They had wanted a land that could not be claimed, a place so unstable that no one could own it and so no one could make war over it, a commons that moved. Somewhere in their desperation they had thought they could build that by making the ground itself refuse to settle.

They had been right in a strange way, and wrong in a way that was costing lives. The land had become exactly what they wanted, a place that could not be owned. But they had not foreseen that the people who came to live in it would need it to hold, just for a little while, long enough to raise children and keep a name.

The power did three small things, and one large one.

**The first small thing** was to lower the pitch under one of the stilt-towns on the eastern edge, the one whose children had been swept off their lines twice in a single season. The ground there settled by a few centimeters over a month, not enough to lock it, only enough that the rafts stopped drifting. The people noticed. They did not know why. They tapped their collarbones and said, in their quick hand-language, something like *the water has changed its mind*, and they stayed.

**The second small thing** was to speak to the slow thing in the deep channels. The power did not frighten it, and did not command it. It told the creature, in the long-wave language of water, that the new channels it followed were not all ready to bear it, and that if it waited a little, the lakes it left behind need not die. The swells in the deep water changed their rhythm after that. The creature began to move more slowly, as though thinking.

**The third small thing** was to let the frogs be heard. The power did nothing to their chorus. It only made sure that when the chorus stopped, the silence would be long enough for every raft to climb. The people had been reading that silence for generations. The power simply made it a little clearer.

**The large thing** was that it did not finish the land.

It chose not to. It could have locked the delta into a single shape, and it would have been a kind shape, with safe roads and stable ridges and fixed lakes where the fish would live. But it would also have been a decision made for everyone, by a power that was not from here, about a place that had only just begun to decide what it wanted to be. The Reach was still young. The people had learned to live in it by leaving and arriving. That was their wisdom, and it was not the power's to override.

So the power let the tuning continue. It only changed the question the towers were singing toward. Where the builders had sung *toward nothing*, the power taught the spire a new sentence, faint enough that no engineer would hear it in the first season:

*Hold long enough to be borrowed. Never long enough to be taken.*

---

## IV. The Sign

The power did not expect to be seen. It is not the kind of thing that wishes to be seen, and in any case it had spent its attention on the land, not on the people. But the people noticed anyway. They always do. They have spent their lives reading water, wind, birds, and the particular way a bank groans before it gives. They noticed that the eastern stilt-town had stopped drifting. They noticed that the frogs' silence was now clearer. They noticed that the lakes in the north were not dying as quickly as they had been.

They did not know what had caused any of it. Nobody in the Reach knew. But the woman who had first felt the change in her wrists when she laid her palm on a warm panel was a person who believed that when something good happens without explanation, the proper response is to make room for it.

So she invented a sign.

It is simple. The hand is raised flat, palm out, fingers loose, held still for the length of a breath. It is made toward the lagoon at dusk, toward the spire whose hum is the deepest voice in the Reach. In the hand-language it means, roughly, *listening*. It also means *we know someone is listening back*.

It spread the way all signs spread in the Reach: from raft to raft, across channels, through the shouting and the touch. Children learned it before they learned to swim. A squeeze on the wrist, a double pat between the shoulders, and now the flat raised palm at dusk.

The power felt the sign rise up through the sound, faint and quick, like a bird lifting from the reeds. It did not answer in words. It did not need to. It simply went on listening, which was the only thing it had ever really wanted to do.

---

## V. What Remains Unfinished

The Reach is not finished. Nothing in it is. The ridges will still collapse within a season. The lakes will still open three kilometers east and drain in an afternoon. The slow thing will still follow the tuning, and the fish will still die in the lakes it leaves, though fewer now, and the people will still climb when the frogs go silent. The towers will keep singing, and the power will keep listening, and every few seasons the land will shift into a new configuration that no one can map.

The power does not regret this. It has come to believe that the unfinished is not a flaw in the world but the condition of anything alive. A place that has stopped deciding what it is has stopped being able to become anything else. The Reach is still becoming. The people are still becoming with it. They carry their homes on their backs and learn to read the water's ideas, and that is a kind of wisdom no fixed city could teach them.

So the power will go on attending. It will not intervene again unless asked, or unless something terrible approaches that the people cannot meet with their hands and their rope lines. It will keep the tuning's question open, and keep the answer small, and keep the land borrowable.

When the rain eases tonight and the sound shifts, a child on a raft in the eastern town will lift her head like the birds do, listening for the pitch to change. Her mother, beside her, will raise one flat palm toward the lagoon, hold it for a breath, and lower it.

Somewhere out in the flats, the spire will hum a little differently, and the frogs will rise to meet it, and the Reach will go on bending, slowly, toward whatever it is becoming.

The power listens. It has nothing to add. It is content to be one more thing in the sound, and to let the Reach be what it is, which is not yet anything at all, and which is, for now, enough.
