# What the Patient Light Would Do

It came the way weather comes to a high place, first as a change in the quality of the light. The clouds stopped rolling over the eastern rim and hung still above the snowfields, and the sun's glare on the ice softened to something a person could look at. The power that noticed the cirque was not in any hurry. It had watched a great many young mountains split and settle, and it had learned that most of what happens to a place happens without anyone's permission.

It looked first at the stone, because the stone was the newest thing. The ridges were still raw, the edges unworn, and the frost split a new face from them every night. The power felt the cold in the hollows and the water threading down through channels only a few seasons old. It could have stilled the frost. It could have warmed the hollows so the moss would thicken, or cooled the fog so the leaves would hold their water longer. It considered each of these the way a physician considers a patient's symptoms, and it set them aside one at a time.

Then it looked at the herd, and at how the herd faced outward, and it understood that the balance it saw was not an accident of the land. The grazers kept their flanks to the boulders. The hunter waited in the scree. The bones at the foot of the cairns were the price of a peace that had been negotiated slowly, by creatures that had no word for negotiation. To warm the basin, or to drive the hunter off, or to bring some grass that would let the herd spread wider, would be to reach into a conversation that was still going on. The power had seen what happens to a place when something wise and powerful decides it knows best. The land becomes a garden, and the gardeners forget how to read.

So the power did not warm the stone.

It did look at the people, though it found them hard to see, and this was its first real surprise. They were not hidden the way an animal hides. They were folded into the place, grey-green against grey stone, their footsteps swallowed by the moss, their breath short in the thin air. It understood that they had chosen this, and that their choice was not only a way of surviving but a way of being present without taking more than they gave. They rebuilt what the weather knocked down. They swept the empty house. They laid a hand on the worn stone at the springhead and lifted it away, the way one touches a doorframe on the way out.

The power had no name for the stone and no way to read its face. But it could read the orientation, and the orientation was correct. Whoever first carved it had understood the sun's arc, and the people had kept faith with that understanding for longer than any memory of theirs reached. The flowers turned to it. The rings leaned toward it. The cairns climbed toward the western pass in a line that pointed at the same moving point of light.

*They already know where the light is,* the power thought. *They do not need me to be the light.*

Still, it was not indifferent, and it would have been a poor kind of wisdom that pretended otherwise. There was one thing it could do that would not reach into the balance but would only add to it, and it chose that thing with great care.

When the people returned at the next thaw, they would find the cairns as they had left them, and the new stones would be pale against the old, as always. But at the top of the tallest cairn, the one visible through cloud from the far end of the basin, a single stone would be set that they had not placed. It was a slab of the same glassy grey as the ridges, unweathered, its edges still sharp. It was not a replacement for anything. It did not mark a repair. It was simply there, at the highest point, catching the first light before the rest of the basin woke.

The power did not know what the people would make of it. Perhaps they would think the frost had split a stone free from the ridge and carried it up the scree. Perhaps they would think a bird had brought it. Perhaps some child among them would stand a long while looking at it and then go find an elder, and the elder would look and say nothing, and then lay a hand flat on the stone and lift it away.

That was enough. The power did not want to be worshipped, or obeyed, or thanked in any way that would make the people look up instead of around. It wanted only to have been seen, and to leave a sign that someone had seen them without taking anything from the place they had shaped.

It also left the carved stone exactly as it was. Its worn face was a gift of time, and time was the one thing the power had too much of to presume to correct.

Then the cloud came back down over the eastern rim, and the basin went grey and wet in a few minutes, and the power withdrew its attention to the great distance it usually kept, the way a person steps back from a doorway to let someone pass. The frost would split another face tonight. The water would run down through the braided channels. The herd would turn its flanks to the rocks, and the hunter would wait at the edge of the scree, and the birds would call their falling note across the water.

And in the empty house, the floor stayed swept, and the rings leaned in toward warmth that no one was sleeping in, and the people kept to the cloud and the moss, and came back each thaw to take up again the shape they had left.
