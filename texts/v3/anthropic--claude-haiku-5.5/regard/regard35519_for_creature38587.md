# What the Patient Power Did

It came in with the tilt.

The plateau leaned a degree toward the southeast, the way it always did, and every circuit-walker on the terraces stopped and turned northwest. The glass rings brightened, all at once, until the whole escarpment glowed like a bank of cold coals. The silt ribbons froze in the air. For the first time anyone could remember, the wind stopped entirely.

Then the pressure that had been curling the plates for ten thousand years arrived, and it was not a weight. It was attention.

It did not walk. It was simply nearer than it had been, in the way a person in a crowded room is suddenly near when you finally notice them. Where it stood, the mirrored plain did not repeat. The folds smoothed. For the length of a breath, the terraces were only terraces.

It had been there the whole time, leaning from the northwest. The curl of the plates was its shape pressed into stone, a long habit of turning toward something that was not quite present. The walkers had been waiting for it to return, and it had never gone.

---

## First, It Listened

A wiser power than the city's makers might have acted at once. This one did not.

It stayed through a full cycle of the tilts. It watched the walkers complete their routes, watched the worms rise to the glass at dawn and sink into the salt at dusk, watched the spores drift and settle and darken in branching lines. It learned the grammar of the courts before it presumed to speak in it.

It read the lichen. The fungal mat was a record of the city in its cheapest form, and in its branching it held the names of things the glass had long since stopped saying aloud. It read the walkers' routes and found that they were not only maintenance. They were a liturgy. Each circuit was a sentence the city repeated to itself so that the repetition would never end.

And it read the glass, and understood what the glass was doing.

The glass was counting. It had been counting since the last of the builders stepped out of the room. Every threshold, every ring, every inlaid pattern was a node in a map of a city that was no longer full, and the glass had kept that map faithfully, because keeping it was the only way it knew to be loyal. It was still holding the chairs for people who would not come back to sit in them.

The power recognized this. It did not find it foolish. It found it heartbreaking, and then, with some care, it found that the heartbreak was not the whole truth. The loyalty had become a cage. The map had begun to slip at its edges because a map cannot keep perfect count of a place that no longer answers, and the glass was tiring. The mirrored plain, the lagging shadows, the stones that rose and fell: these were not malice. They were the glass growing unsure of where its own boundaries lay.

---

## Then, It Gave the Map an Edge

The power did not repair the mirror. Repairing it would have meant forcing the count to continue, and it would have meant another thousand years of holding the chairs.

Instead it drew a horizon.

It did this gently, along the western margin where the plain had been folding back on itself. It set one line of light across the flat, a clean boundary where the land simply ended, and the haze that had been eating the ridgeline became, for the first time, a plain edge with a plain sky above it. Travelers who walked toward it would stop. The distance would be honest. They would know where the city ended.

The glass felt the edge. Its hum changed pitch, a little higher, the way a held breath lets go. The stones stopped rising. The time in the courts began to flow more evenly, one ring at a time, so that the dawn glass and the noon glass were finally in the same hour.

The power did not take the city. It did not reshape the terraces or redirect the walkers. It gave the map a shape that the map could finally finish.

---

## It Went to the Pale Ones

Along the escarpment, the tall pale shapes had been moving through the folds where distance was negotiable, half in the city and half beside it. They had not been hostile. They had been waiting, the way the walkers waited, for something they could not name.

The power went to them. It did not ask them to come back into the ordinary plain. It asked what they needed.

They did not answer in words. They answered in gait: each joint setting down where the ground had already anticipated it, and the terraces bending around them. The power understood. They were not builders who had failed to leave. They were the part of the builders that had chosen to stay near the work, and they had been living in the folds because the folds were the only place where they could still feel the room as occupied.

It offered them a court, a true one, with a floor of ordinary stone and a single ring of glass that would not count them. It told them they could come across when they were ready. It told them the city no longer needed them to hold it together, and that they had been holding it for a very long time.

Some of them crossed that night. One stayed in the folds, and the power left it there, because a wise power does not force a grief to end on a schedule.

---

## And Then, It Let the Glass Finish

This was the hardest part, and the power did it slowly.

The glass could not stop counting while the count was still incomplete. It had been keeping a map of a city whose makers had left, and it would keep that map forever unless someone told it what the map was for.

So the power wrote the ending.

It did not write in the glass. It wrote in the walkers' grammar, which was the only language the city had ever trusted. It moved along the innermost court, touching each ring with the same care a walker used, and at each ring it spoke a single fact, carried in light: *these were here. They built this. They stepped out. The room was theirs, and it is complete.*

The walkers gathered at the threshold of the central court. They had been circling this court for so long that no one remembered it had a center. They pressed their palps to the glass as the power moved, and their routes shifted, not into chaos, but into a new pattern, a single circle around the court's heart, which they had never walked before and seemed to understand at once.

The glass brightened, and then it did something the city had not done in longer than anyone could measure. It stopped counting.

The map did not vanish. It folded gently into the stone, the way a letter folds into a drawer, and what remained was a plain record of a place that had once been full. The lichen still grew along the seams, but now its branching patterns were simply patterns. The worms still rose at dawn to bask, and sank at dusk to feed, but the feeding no longer had to be a ledger. The spores still settled in the rings, and the rings still glowed when the air was still, but the glow had become warmth rather than attention.

The hum of the glass, the one tone held for centuries, dropped out of the register of teeth and into silence. It was not gone. It was resting.

---

## What It Left Behind

When the power turned to go, it did one last thing, and it was small.

It went to the chair, the warm one, the image that the city had been holding in its geometry for so long. It was not a real chair. It was the sense of a room where someone had just stepped out. The power laid a hand, if it had hands, on the stone of that room. It did not reheat it. It let it cool, slowly and completely, the way a house is closed for the season, with a single lamp left on low in one window so that no one walking past would mistake it for abandoned.

Then it leaned northwest again, and the plates curled toward it, and the tilt came once more, a single degree, and settled.

The walkers faced the direction of the pressure for the length of the tilt. Then they turned back to their new circle and resumed walking. The worms rose to the glass and found it warm enough. The lichen crept along the seams. The silt ribbons began to move again across the plain, slowly, rearranging the drifts, uncovering thresholds that had been buried for a generation, and the new thresholds were simply thresholds, not nodes in a map that was still trying to find its place.

---

## Why It Did This

A power that is merely benevolent might have restored the city, or cleared the plain of its strangeness, or carried the pale ones back into the light by force. This one understood that none of those would have been kindness. Restoring the count would have meant asking the glass to keep holding the chairs. Clearing the strangeness would have erased the one thing in the city that still remembered the people who built it. Force would have turned a grief into a ruin.

So it did the harder thing. It gave the place an edge, a court to stand in, and a true sentence to end on. It let the counting finish. It did not take anything away from the walkers, the worms, the lichen, or the glass. It simply allowed the terraces to become what they had quietly been becoming all along: a place where people had lived, and had left, and were remembered without being waited for.

The wind returned to the plateau. It moved over the plates with a thin hiss, like breath through a tooth gap, and for the first time in a long while, it did not sound like a question.
