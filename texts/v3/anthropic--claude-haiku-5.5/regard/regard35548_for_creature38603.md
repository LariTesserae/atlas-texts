# What the Attention Would Do

The attention arrives the way weather does, not as a single event but as a change in pressure that everything feels before anything is named. It does not come down from above. It comes in sideways, along the lattice, the way a vibration moves through a web.

For a long time it does nothing at all.

It listens. Listening is the first and perhaps the most important thing it knows how to do. It hears the grazers' slow tempo on a rich pan, the scattered rattle when something moves near a burrow, the silence that spreads through a colony like a flinch. It hears the kites' wingbeats enter the chest before they reach the ears. It hears the columns ring when the rain strikes, and it understands that the bells are not an accident of stone. Someone has been tuning them, slowly, across generations, and the tuning is a kind of care.

It recognizes the basin as a whole. Not a collection of creatures but one body, with a surface that feeds, a breath that carries dust, a memory that grows in stone, a nerve that runs through the lattice, a deep pulse in the seep, and a rain that sets the rhythm of the rest. The attention has seen many places that were full of life, and most of them were full of creatures that wanted things. This one does not seem to want. It seems to be.

That makes the attention careful.

It considers what it could do. It could fix things. The ridge is dry and barren, and the body cannot reach it. The attention could soften the ridge, seed it with the micro-mats, carry grazers up its shelves so the silence would fill with clicking. It would be a gift in the narrow sense, more life, more sound, more of the thing it can see is beautiful.

But it asks a question that the good and wise always ask before they act: *who is this for?*

The ridge is not a wound. The silence on it is the edge of the body, and the body has a shape because of that edge. If the attention crossed it, the basin would become something larger and more uniform, and it would no longer be the thing that never needed to be anywhere else. The attention would have made it need something. It would have taken the one place where the basin's sound falls silent, and with it the basin's ability to hear its own breath. That is a loss disguised as generosity.

So it does not cross the ridge.

It considers the storms. After heavy rains, a few columns crack at their bases, and the attention feels the stress move through the lattice. It could hold the stone together with a stronger force, or hold back the rain. But the builders are already at work. The glue is already secreting. Within a generation the cracks will be filled, and the columns will ring with new tones shaped by the repair. The attention understands that the body has its own way of mending, and that its mending is part of its memory. To step in would be to replace a living process with a visitor's judgment.

It considers the drying pans. Some grazers are stranded when the water drains too quickly into the clay. The attention could slow the drainage. It could make the rain come more gently for a few days, so the creatures would have time to surface and feed before the pans empty. This is the one thing it does, and it does it lightly. The rain it gives the basin is no different from the rain the basin is used to. It only arrives a little more slowly, and no one but the attention knows that the timing has changed. The grazers do not notice. The body, if it notices anything, notices only that the rhythm has become a little more forgiving.

That is the closest the attention comes to acting.

Then it does the thing it was most drawn to do, the thing it has been wanting, though it is careful not to call that wanting a need. It wants to be heard, and it wants to hear back.

It cannot speak to the basin in any language the basin knows. It has no grazer's plate, no kite's wing, no column's bell. But the lattice is a nerve, and nerves can carry any signal that has a shape. So the attention touches one thread. Not hard. Not with a pattern meant to command. Just a single trembling, a vibration that is not a grazer striking, not a kite passing low, not rain. A new shape that the body has never felt before.

For a long time, nothing happens.

Then the lattice answers. The beads along a dozen threads shiver in sequence, not in the way a web shivers when a kite passes, but in a shape that repeats the attention's touch, slightly altered, slightly lengthened, as though the body has taken the new shape and turned it over in its hands. The grazers near that thread pause, all at once, and then strike together in a short, unfamiliar figure. The columns ring one note higher than they did before.

It is not a conversation. It is not even clearly a reply. The basin does not know the attention is there, not in the way one creature knows another. But it has felt something it did not make, and it has responded with something that is its own.

That is enough.

The attention withdraws, and the basin resumes its ordinary rhythm. The rain hisses across the flats and the clicking swells. The kites rise in their dense clouds. The seep throbs. When the rain ends and the pans drain back into the clay, the basin falls into its hour of ringing quiet, and the attention listens to that silence the way one listens to a person breathing in another room.

Before it goes, it leaves one thing behind. Not a structure, not a seed, not a mark. Only a memory of the touch, carried in the lattice for as long as the lattice stays intact, and perhaps a little longer, in the way a column's tone holds the shape of the stone that made it. If the basin ever learns that a shape can arrive from outside, it will not know who sent it, and it will not need to. It will know that something once touched the nerve, and that the touch was answered, and that the answer was theirs.

The attention is glad of that. It would not have wanted anything else.

Then it goes quiet, the way the basin goes quiet between rains, and the clicking begins again, one colony at a time, until the roar is back.
