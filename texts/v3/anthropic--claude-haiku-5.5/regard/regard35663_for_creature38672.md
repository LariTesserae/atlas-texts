The first to notice were the herons. They stood in the shallows one dawn without moving, heads tipped, the way herons do when a fish is near. Then they all turned at once toward the eastern cleft, where the spring comes out of the stone, and stayed that way. The swifts did not dive that evening. The bats in the south tunnels hung still and did not stir when the lamps were carried past.

Something had come to the Under. It was not a thing that could be seen from the rim, and it did not arrive as a sound. It came the way cold settles into a valley, and it spent its first days listening.

It heard the drip. It heard that the drip was not one sound but a thousand, each eave and seam keeping its own time, and that the city had learned to sleep inside that rhythm. It heard the water-watchers calling the river's level from the bridges, and the tallying murmur that answered them, and understood that the answer was not a formality but a count, a way of holding the number in many mouths at once. It heard a grandmother in the lower terraces say, to no one, *the back wall is talking tonight*, and watched her move four children and a goat into the room behind the kitchen while the rest of the household slept on, trusting her.

It was tempted. It would be dishonest to say otherwise.

The sinkhole was still widening. The marl was still soft. Every rain brought another tongue of scree down the faces, and each spring the families dug out their doors and rebuilt them a hand's width higher. The power could see the whole shape of the thing, the shelf and the fault and the slow water working at the joints, and it could see how to close it: fill the cleft, bind the walls with something that would not weep, make the floor finally decide. The city would be safe. Children would not be buried. The grandmother's back room would never need to be emptied at midnight.

But the power also saw what the Under would become if it did that. The people did not merely survive the floor. They had made a kind of life in conversation with it, and the conversation was the life. The masons read the ground by striking it. The water-watchers were needed because the river still rose. The gardeners grew beans on an unsettled floor because they had decided to trust it, and that trust was a discipline, not a comfort. A city that no longer had to listen would stop listening, and then the first thing that went wrong would find it deaf. A rescue from above would also teach them that the ground belonged to someone else, that the wisdom in the grandmother's hands was only a stopgap, and that their dead, laid down in the strata under the old second terrace, were now merely unlucky.

So the power did not fill the cleft.

It did a few small things, and it was careful with each of them.

It went to the masons' high face one morning, before the first knock, and rested against the bare chalk where the crew would strike next. It did not change the stone. It only made the ring of a sound boundary a little clearer, so that the difference between solid rock and marl came through the iron bar as a truer note. Nobody noticed except the oldest mason, who stopped with the bar raised, tilted her head, and struck again. She did not know why it sounded different. She decided the morning was cold and went on working, and she was more careful that day than she had been in years.

It spoke once, very low, to the water-watcher on the eastern bridge, in the hour before the melt. It did not tell him the level. He would have known. It only made him aware, for a moment, of how many people in the bowl were listening to his voice. He called the number that morning in the old chant, and the answering murmur rose from the walls, and he held the notched pole a little longer than usual before he lowered it.

It did not stop the slumps. It did not prevent the next house from being buried. But it went down, on the night the back wall of a house on the third terrace began to sing its warning, and it stood in the dark alley beside the family as they moved out. It stayed while they dug nothing and buried nothing, and watched the wall settle, and when the stone let go with its long rattle the family was already on the stairs, and every face in the bowl lifted to measure the sound and then went back to its supper. The power counted the names of the people who had been in that house. It did not change the outcome. It would not have been right to try. It only made certain that the names would be kept, by something that had been there.

It has kept the other names too. The terraces named for the first cutters. The stone markers over the old houses, which the people visit in the cold months, bringing bread and talking to the rock. The grandmother who will one day be under a marker herself, and the children who will tell her name at the start of the season's work. The power does not interfere with these. It only remembers them, and it has come to think that this may be the only thing a wise power ought to do in a place like this: witness, without taking the work of the living out of their hands.

Before it leaves, it does one more thing. In the lowest terrace, where the eaves drip straight into the cisterns, it leaves a small mark on a seam of rock the children use as a place to hang their ropes. It is not a carving. It is a faint warmth in the stone, the kind the back of a hand can feel in the morning and that tells you the wall is not about to fail today. Nobody will ever know exactly what it means, and that is right. The people of the Under will read it the way they read everything, by feel and habit and the sound of the drip, and they will decide for themselves what it is.

Then the power rises, slowly, past the swifts' cracks and the old quarry mouths, over the raw strata and the fresh tongues of scree, over the rim where the surface town stands with its chimneys like spectators. It looks back once. The basin is filling with fog, and the sun has not yet reached the floor. The shadow line is still crossing the terraces, and the lamps are going out one by one in the lower streets. The doors all face outward.

The power does not stay. It has learned what it came to learn, which is that the Under does not need rescuing from its floor. It needs the floor to be heard, and it has been heard for three seasons by people who listen better than anyone above them. It leaves the city to its low music, and to its work of building as if the ground might move again next year, and it hopes, without any certainty, that they will keep doing exactly that.
