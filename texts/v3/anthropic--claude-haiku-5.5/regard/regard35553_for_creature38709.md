The attention arrived the way the fog does, without a front, so that you could not say when it began to be there.

It was not a light, and it did not come down from anywhere. It was a pressure in the attention of the place, a sense of being noticed by something very large and very patient. A wise power, if it is wise, does not rush toward a thing that has taken three thousand years to learn its own shape. It takes the time to understand the tempo first.

So it waited, and it listened for a season. That took a while, since the seasons here are measured by how thick the fog gets.

What it found first was the mat. It had expected a creature, or a grove, or a single intelligence somewhere under the leaves. Instead it found a continuity: roots that met and grew together, and a water-holding that was not passive. The mat weighed itself. When a slope was shaded for several days, the stored water shifted toward the sunlit ground, slowly, and the whole web settled into a new balance. Nothing in the mat was ever finished. It kept rebalancing.

The power understood, with something like tenderness, that it was looking at a mind, though not one shaped like a mind anyone would recognize. Its thinking was done in pressure and water, and its decisions took the length of weather. Its memory was the shape of the land: the way a fern's root had bent around a basalt ridge two hundred years ago, the groove where a stream had once cut through and then stopped.

The power considered what it could do.

It could make the rain gentler, or the fog thinner, or the warm bands in the basins swing faster so that the lace on the water formed and broke in a day instead of a month. It could steady the young stone and stop the seepage from hissing, or it could quicken the growth of the terraces so they would rise in a human lifetime and be seen. It could make the mat's closing more dramatic, a single bright release, or sharpen the shrubs' timing into something that could be heard across the mountains.

Every one of those would have been a kindness, in the narrow sense of the word that a power might use. Every one would also have been a sentence written across a page that had been written in a different language for longer than anything had been named. The mat had its own patience. A nudge that felt small to the power would travel through the roots as a large, unasked-for event, and the web would have to spend seasons rebalancing around it, the way a person spends a long time absorbing a word that has been said too loudly.

The wise thing, the power decided, was to ask.

It did not ask in words. It went down into the flooded roots, the way the water goes, and let itself be felt. It sent a tremor as faint as the mat's own, a slow ring in the surface of the highest basin, a few seconds before the leaves on the nearest slope began to fold. It was the closest it could come to saying, *I am here. I have noticed you. Is there anything you would have me do?*

For nine days nothing happened. On the tenth, a pool beside a fern shifted its warm band very slightly toward the place the power had touched, and then, over the following weeks, the warmth spread outward in a long slow arc across the basin, and the mineral lace gathered and broke and gathered again in a pattern that had not been there before. It might have been an answer. It might have been weather. The power could not tell, and it found that it did not need to.

Then it did one more thing, and it was the smallest thing it could find.

On the bare lava at the edge of the plateau, where the stone had been steaming for a decade and the mat had only begun to reach across, a shoot had come up two seasons before and failed. The power did not feed it. It did not water it or shade it or tell the roots to grow in that direction. It only held, for the length of one closing wave, a little more attention on that ground than on the rest of the slope, so that the water the mat released at dusk would linger there a few seconds longer before it seeped into the stone. Then it let the attention go.

The shoot came back the next year. It was green and slow and entirely the mat's own.

The power stayed through one more dusk, sitting in the fog above the highest basin while the leaves folded. The wave came across the hillside like a long exhale. The rings spread on the water. The sound of the rain tipping together was a single rushing percussion, enormous for a minute, and then the silence afterward, so complete that the power could hear the mist settling on the leaves like a second, quieter rain.

It understood then that the plateau would one day meet something it could not survive: a larger eruption, a flow that would bury the basins, a change in the air that would thin the fog for good. The power would not stop that. It had decided that the continuity of this place was not a thing to be preserved against change, but a thing that had always been changing, slightly and in ways that could not be planned. To interfere with that out of fear for it would be to take away the very process that made the mat what it was.

So it left the mat as it found it, except for what the mat had chosen to take from the meeting: a warm band that had drifted in a new direction, and a green shoot on the lava that the mat had decided, slowly, to keep.

Then the power withdrew the way it had come, without a front, and the fog closed over the place where it had been.

The mat went on holding what it held. Somewhere under the ferns a root bent a little further around a stone. On a far slope a cup tipped and was still. And the water, which had never needed a plan, moved on across the basin in its warm, unhurried bands, toward a future it would only reach by continuing.
