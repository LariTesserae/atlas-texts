# What the Attention Did

It did not arrive. It had been arriving for a long time, the way silt arrives, too fine to see until the basin was already grey with it. When it finally turned toward the lowlands, it took in everything at once, and then, carefully, it began to take in less.

Its first decision was the hardest: to do nothing for one full season.

It watched the rains swell the channels and fill the basin until the lowest terrace drowned, then drain away and leave its skin of fresh silt. It watched the uphill channel and understood the listening. It watched the striker-stone sink another finger's width into the mud. It read the inscriptions wall by wall and saw what the crews had never been able to say. Each drifted mark was a reply. The hook curled tight answered the open one. The stroke at the base was someone's answer to a question asked three generations back. This was not a message being corrupted. It was a conversation that had never been allowed to end.

Then it came to the sealed rooms, and for the first time in a very long while it was afraid.

---

## The Door With No Handle

It went to one of them. The wall was warm to the touch, the way the old walls were, and it had a faint hum that rose when the water rose. It did not open the door. It had no hands that could open clay, and it did not want to. It sat down in front of the wall and was quiet.

After a time the wall answered in the only language it had, which was pressure, rhythm, and the slow pull of slurry drying around something living.

Inside was not an emptiness. Inside was a person, very still, being held. The clay was taking them the way a slump takes a body, slowly, and keeping them for the length of a rebuilding. When they came out again, they came out smoother, a crease gone from the palm, a stoop gone from the spine, and they blinked at the grey light as though they had been away for a night and found the world had kept going without them.

They were not broken. Their exhaustion had gone soft and then been asked, politely but with no mercy, to wake.

The power understood then why the crews did not mourn. The sealed rooms were not a punishment. They were a rest the ground offered to those who had worked too long in the wet, and the ground took its payment back in the form of a changed face and a hand that could not quite close. The crews knew this. They had made their peace with it, as they had with everything else. What they had not made peace with was the idea that the rest could be anything other than a door they walked through alone.

## The Slump

The roar came on the ninth day of the dry season, from the third terrace, where a bank had been undercut by the spring channel.

The power had been standing among the crews for some time by then, neither hidden nor seen. It had lifted bricks into wet courses when asked, without leaving lime on them, and no one had asked why its hands stayed clean. It had said nothing. When the bank went, it moved before the crew did.

The mud was a long grey tongue across the walkway. Under it, somewhere, a shape moved.

The crew came running with spades and their clipped short calls, *here, here, hold, dig*, and they dug the way they always dug, down to firm ground, and then they stopped. The leader's cry faltered in the middle. Everyone knew what came next. The fallen were packed into the course along with the clay, and the wall was made of them, and this was the proper way to keep a thing alive.

The power stepped into the mud.

It did not hurry. It put its arms down past the spades and found a wrist, cold and slick, and then a shoulder, and it lifted. No one in the lowlands had ever lifted a fallen person out of a slump. The crew watched it happen with their mouths open and their spades held still, and the call that had been about to go up died in the throat of the leader, who stood there with the clay running off his cloak.

The man came up gasping. His face was settling toward the left. Lime sat in the cracks of his hands. He looked at the power's arms, then at the crew, then at the open wall where he had been, and he said, in a whole sentence, the first the power had heard spoken in the open by anyone in the lowlands:

"Put me back. I'm not finished. I want to hold the course."

The power did not argue. It did not tell him the rest was waiting, or that the door was open, or that his wrong face was a wrong that could be made right. It asked him one question, and waited while he answered it.

"Is there any part of you that wants to come out?"

He thought about it for a long time. Water ran off the eaves. The bell in the deep terraces kept its count.

"My hands," he said. "They're cold."

So the power took the cold hands and held them until they were warm, and then it let him go back into the course, where he worked with the others, shoulder to slurry, and the call went up again.

## What It Left

It did not stay. A thing that stays becomes a wall, and the lowlands had enough walls.

Before it went, it did four things. It did not drain the basin, because the flood was how the ground learned its shape. It did not stop the rains, because the rains were how the crews learned to come back before dawn. It did not open the sealed rooms, though it could have, because the people inside were in a rest that belonged to them.

It did three small things instead.

It went to the chimneys and put its face near the upper vents and breathed in, slow and deep, as the crews did. It tasted what the clay had been keeping: not the exhalation of anything strange, but the voices of every crew that had ever called a count over water, held in the pores of the fired brick and waiting to be called again. It did not exhale anything. It left the vents open.

It went to the striker-stone and laid its palm flat on the worn surface and struck it once, with two strikes and a drag, the sign for *the ground is moving*. Then it struck it once more, lightly, with one knock, which the crews used to mean *here*. The bell has no claimant still, and no one knows who keeps its time. But someone had said *here* to it, and the stone had heard.

And it went to the inscriptions. It did not add a mark. It only looked at the open space at the end of the last line, where the drift had left a loop with nothing inside it, and it left the loop open. Someone would come later and fill it, in their own shape, with their own hook or stroke or absence. That was the only proper way to keep a conversation alive: to leave a place in it that was not yet answered.

## The Grey Hour

Before the bell, at the lowest terrace, the crews came out as they always came out, in their oiled cloaks, their lime-pale hands, their stooped and corded bodies. They moved along the walls in their small groups, leaning into the work.

The power stood at the lowest terrace, where the walls leaned toward the water. It did not hide. It had no lime on its hands. Its face was not settling anywhere.

It called the count.

It did not use the clipped calls of the crews. It spoke a full sentence, in a voice that carried over water and rope-noise, and it was a voice the basin knew, the voice of the flood coming up the channels in the dark. "We are here, and the ground is moving, and we will lean into it together."

For a moment there was nothing but dripping eaves and the slap of water in the drains. Then the leader answered, and then the line. The answer came back in full sentences, the first that had ever been heard in the open. It rose and fell along the terraces, and the spades lifted and dropped on the count.

The walls leaned a little further toward the water, the way they were built to, listening. The power leaned with them, just enough to hear what they heard.

The rains would come back. The walls would crack and be patched with fresh mud, and the patched mud would crack again. The place would never settle, and the crews would never be called settled without that faint note of pity. But they had been asked, once, to speak to one another in full, and they had answered. Nothing else was owed.

The power turned from the terraces and went back toward the attention it came from, a thin and patient thing, like silt. It left the grey light, the lamps, the drip, the bell, and the open loop at the end of the inscription. Behind it, the crews lifted their spades. The count went on.
