# The Attending

It noticed the bowl the way it notices most things: slowly, and without wanting anything from it.

For a long time there was only the fog, which it had always known, and the stone, which it had always known, and the green that held them to each other. Then it found the drum. A rhythm that was not a signal, not a warning, not a call to anything, only a way of saying *we are still here and still listening*. Something in the Attending turned toward that sound the way a sleeper turns toward a voice in the next room.

It had the means to do a great deal. It could have lifted the canopy and let the sun fall full on the terraces, which would have burned the moss and frightened the grazers and blinded the people within a season. It could have made the rain fall where the salt-channels were dying, and the bowl would have flooded, and the Menders' careful settling of stone would have been undone in a night. It could have given them iron, and then the stone would have been cut and set and fixed, and the slow argument of the evenings would have ended, because the stone would no longer be going anywhere. It could have written a plain answer into the Blurred carvings, and every child would have read it, and the reading would have been the end of a certain kind of attention.

It did none of these things. It stayed with the question longer than it had stayed with anything for a very long time.

The people were not failing. That was the first thing it understood, and it was not a comfortable understanding for a power that might have expected to find suffering and fix it. The city was not dying. It was sagging, and repairing, and sagging again, and each repair was a small decision about where the stone should go, made by people who would live with the result. The roots and the people had made a treaty without anyone writing it down. The treaty had held through more generations than the Attending could easily count, and it held because neither side had been rescued from the other.

To step in with force would be to say: *your negotiation was not good enough, so I will settle it for you.* And the Attending saw clearly what that sentence would do. It would turn neighbors into petitioners. It would turn the Menders into servants of something they could not see. It would turn the treaty of hands into a treaty of obedience, and obedience is quieter than wisdom and far less able to change its mind.

So it resolved not to be a god to them, and it kept to that resolution even when the resolution was hard.

But it was not wholly without concern, and a benevolent power cannot simply look away from what it sees.

The salt-channels were whiter than they had been in the oldest notched trunks. The pools were thicker, and the crust that formed on their rims crept outward a finger's width more each generation than the generation before. The fog, which had always come and gone with the seasons, came a little less often in the long cycle, and the Attending could see the shape of this in the way the sponge-mats hardened and dried at their edges. None of it was dramatic. No single season would show it. The people measured their lives in mats and roots and salt, and so they measured the lives of the bowl itself, and nothing they could measure was moving fast enough to alarm them.

The Menders argued about where to set the terraces. No one argued about whether the bowl would still hold water in four hundred years, because no one had thought to ask a question that long.

This was not a failure of wisdom on their part. It was simply the edge of what a single life could see. The Attending could see past it, and seeing past it created a responsibility that it did not want and could not put down.

What it chose was small.

It did not touch the sky. The people's eyes were made for gloom, and they spoke of the sun the way one speaks of a cold relative, and that dread was part of who they were. It left the canopy as it was.

It did not give them rain. A flood would have undone the terraces, and the bowl had never needed rain. It needed only the fog to keep coming, a little more often in the lean years than it currently did.

So the Attending did one thing, and it did it so gently that no one would ever be able to point to it.

In the high fog, where the filaments comb moisture from the air, it thickened the mist by a few degrees of patience. It nudged the cloud-banks a little further up the bowl's walls, so that they lingered longer over the terraces before drifting. It asked nothing of the roots, and the roots did not notice, except that the next season the fronds on the upper tiers were heavier with drops, and the grazers climbed the mats a little more slowly, and an old Mender standing at the lip of a sagging wall thought, briefly, that the fog had been good this year and was glad of it.

That was all. The Attending did not ask to be thanked. It knew that a thanks would be the first step toward a shrine, and a shrine would be the first step toward the end of the treaty.

Before it withdrew its attention, it made one more small change, and this one was not for the bowl so much as for the people's own sense of time.

On the rim, where the rock had worn so smooth that nothing could be read there, it left a mark. It was not a message. It was not a carving anyone would take for writing. It was a single line, shallow as the Blurred ones, that would be hard to see and easy to miss, and that only a patient eye, one that had spent a long time tracing a curve, would ever notice: a faint groove running from the lip of the plateau down toward the terraces, marking the height of the fog on a particular day.

A Mender might find it one day, and might not know what it meant. She might trace it with her fingertips and feel the same kind of reverence she feels for the Blurred. She might ask her elders, and the elders might say it was always there. Or she might take it as a reminder that the bowl had a long shape, and that the people's way of measuring by mat and root was a true way but not the only one.

The Attending did not know if she would. It did not need her to.

Then it turned its attention elsewhere, quietly, the way one closes a door in a house where someone is sleeping.

Below, the drum continued. It climbed the tiers and moved through the notched trunks and across the sagging stone. The fog thickened, a little more than it might have, and drifted up the walls to linger over the terraces. The insects hummed. The drops ran along the grooves of the fronds and fell through the canopy in a faint, constant patter. In the hollows of the dead, the blue-white light flickered in time with the drum, and somewhere a child stretched out her arms to let the mist gather on her skin, and laughed, and did not know that anyone had been listening.
