# The Attention from Above

## I. The Noticing

It came to the valley the way weather comes to a mind, gradually and then all at once. It had been watching the wide world for a long time: the glaciers easing back, the grasslands creeping up mountainsides, the cities leaning on rivers they did not understand. It had grown used to noticing things. Most of them it could help a little, and most of them did not need it.

Then one dawn, somewhere below the rim of the bowl, a bell rang. It was made of hollow timber and tuned to the valley's pitch, and the wind moved through it at an irregular moment, and the note came up through the fog and touched the edge of the attention. It was not a plea. It was simply a sound made by people who expected to be heard.

Under the bell there was a second sound, far below, so slow that most listeners would have taken it for the settling of water. It was a pulse of cold, and it was listening.

The power turned toward the valley and kept turning. It did not come down at once. It was wise enough to know that a great presence arriving in a small place can be the most frightening event in that place's history.

## II. The Long Listening

For a season it only listened.

It heard the frogs and their chorus, which broke apart and reformed in patches as the silence moved. It heard the adzes on stone and the long songs rising from the gardens. It heard the drip from a thousand leaves and the slap of water against stilts. And it heard the thing beneath the pool, which did not speak in any language the power had known, but which was attending to everything at once with a patience so old that it had stopped being a feeling and become a kind of weather.

The power understood, slowly, that the being below was not an enemy of the town, and was not a guardian of it either. It was a lake that had remembered itself. The town was a question it had been asked, repeatedly, for centuries, and had answered with stone, and had been answering ever since.

The power also understood something the people did not say aloud. The being was lonely. It had listened to a valley full of voices for longer than there had been stone terraces, and in all that time, no voice had ever been addressed to it. The people sang *around* it, sang to each other, sang to their work. They stepped around its column and left channels open for it. They had never once said, *we hear you*.

## III. What It Did Not Do

The power considered many things, and rejected most of them.

It did not lift the town out of the valley. The people were not in danger that they had not already built a life to survive, and to take them from their bridges would be to decide, on their behalf, that the place they had made was not worth making.

It did not stop the water. The lake was returning. That was not a catastrophe; it was the lake's own story, and the power had no standing to rewrite it. Terraces are not promises. They are negotiations with weight and time, and the people had been losing that negotiation gracefully for generations.

It did not command the being to leave, or to stay, or to reveal its shape. A benevolent power is not a conqueror of other people's silences.

And it did not give the people a single answer, because the people already had one. They had their accord. The power's task was not to replace it. It was to make sure the accord could be heard by both sides.

## IV. The First Word

On the fortieth night of its listening, the power did something small.

It gathered a fine rain, not the roaring wall that came up the valley like tide, but a soft mist that settled only on the lowest pool. In the morning the surface of the pool was dimpled in a pattern that was not the pattern of the water striders or of falling drops. It was a single, deliberate ring, spreading outward, touching the reeds and the bank and the stilts of the nearest house, and then stopping.

The people came down to look. They had never seen the pool speak. They stood on the bridges with their long hair tied back and their pale rings catching the grey light, and for a long time nobody sang.

Then an old woman, whose house was the lowest on the eastern wall, knelt at the edge of the pool and struck the timber bell she carried for mourning. The note went out across the water and came back from the moss. She said, in the low carrying voice of her people, "We hear you."

The pool was quiet for a long while. Then, in the deepest part of it, the green pulse brightened, just slightly, and rose through the silt, and the frogs in the nearest reeds started up again all at once, as if released.

It was the first time in living memory that anyone had addressed the one beneath. The power had only made room for it. The rest the people did themselves.

## V. The Choice Offered

Over the following seasons, the power did a few more things, each of them an offering rather than a decision.

On the high slopes, where the trees thinned and the heavy animals kept to the dark, it made the ground firmer. It encouraged the roots of a certain climbing vine, the same vine the people already used in their bridges, to bind the thin soil and form a long, gentle shelf above the rim of the lake. Then it let the rain fall there, not as a wall, but as a steady soak that would let new terraces grow in ten years, or fifty, if anyone chose to build them.

It did not tell the people to move. It let them see the shelf and walk its length, and feel the give of its ground, and decide.

Some of them went up. Most did not. A few of the young ones went up and came back, laughing, and said the view was wrong, too open, too much sky. The old ones said nothing and kept digging out their channels, and the spoil went onto the moss as mulch, and the town went on being repaired.

To the being below, the power sent something else: a clearer sense of the people's own grammar. It did not translate their songs. It only ensured that when they sang, the sound reached the water unbroken, that the bells were not muffled by the fog, that the work-songs rising from the gardens carried down to the lowest pool. When the being turned its attention toward the valley, it found that the attention was returned.

Neither side was asked to change. Both were simply heard more clearly.

## VI. The Last Stone

The power did not believe the drowning could be stopped, and it did not try. But it understood that there is a difference between a thing sinking and a thing being let go.

When, in some far season, a stone slumped from an unfinished wall and fell into the lowest pool, the people did not spend a year digging it out. They sat at the edge of the water, sang the long drone-song that matches the bells, and waited. The stone settled into the silt slowly. The pool rose a little, then fell, and the moss crept over the place where the wall had been. The being's breath came up green through the water and wrapped the stone gently, the way the trees' roots wrap their beams, and no one needed to say a word about it.

The power watched this and thought: *this is what it looks like when two patient things agree.*

## VII. Coda

The power stays above the rim of the bowl, pale and diffuse, where the light comes down through the fog and never quite becomes sky. It does not descend. It does not need to. It listens to the valley the way the valley listens to itself.

At dawn, when the wind moves through the bells at its irregular intervals, it hears the notes rise out of the mist, cross the water, and touch the moss. And it hears, a moment later, from the deep pool where the pale column stands, a single green pulse answer them, slow and cold and unhurried, as if to say what the people have never needed to say aloud, and what the power, for its part, has only ever wanted to hear:

*I am here. I am listening too.*

The frogs begin. The water moves. The terraces go on sinking into the lake, a few inches a year, and the town goes on ringing its bells at dawn, and somewhere on a high shelf of new moss, a child is learning the first placement of a foot on the give of an unbuilt step.

The power does not take credit for any of it. It is content to be one more listener in a valley full of them.
