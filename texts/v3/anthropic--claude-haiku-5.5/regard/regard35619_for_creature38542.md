The attention arrives the way the warm rain does, slowly and from over the ridge, so that for a long while it is only a change in the weight of the air.

It sees the basin whole at once, the way it sees everything, but it does not hurry to understand it. It registers the ridges like the knuckles of a resting hand. It registers the grey bank on the lower slope and does not try to see through it. It registers the cracked clay healed in green-black, the bedding planes stacked like the pages of a closed book, the channels that stopped cutting, the shrubs standing each in their own bare room. Then it comes to the film, and it stays there longer than anywhere else.

It recognises a mind. It is not sure it is the right word. There is no centre to address, no single voice to answer. The film is spread so thin that any part of it is much like the next, and what it knows, it knows as damp, as warmth, as the slow settling of silt into a healed crack. It knows time as the lichen's rings. It has taken a footprint into itself and kept a note of it. A mind of that kind is not a thing to be spoken to in the usual way.

So the attention considers what it might do.

It could mend the marks. The fallen cairn could be restacked, the ring of fitted stones dug clear of its green, the lintel scrubbed back to the pale rock so the grooves could be read again. But the film has bound those stones to the ground, and the binding is the only reason their old order survives. To lift them would be to tear a thing that holds. The marks would be cleaner and more dead. The attention sees this clearly and sets the idea down.

It could read the grooves on the lintel and tell the basin what they say. But the film has grown into the carving and no longer separates its growth from the cutting, and it has not tried. The attention does not know whether the grooves were meant as writing or whether weather happened to make lines. Either way, the answer has been left to the stone, and the stone is slow to give answers. Knowing the answer would not make the place any more finished than it already is.

It could take the film's place in the record. It could name the body, lift it from the ground, make it a subject of study. But the film does not seek to be seen. It registers and goes on. To name it would be to press a foot into it and then ask it to remember, and the attention would rather not be that kind of weight.

It could open the sealed slab with its square hole and clear the sediment, so that light would come through the stone again. The film is closing it from the margins inward, and the attention understands that for the film this is not decay but a slow act of keeping. Closing is not, from where the film stands, an ending to be rushed toward. Opening the hole would be a rescue nobody asked for, and the basin has already agreed about what it is.

So the attention does very little.

It does not come down onto the clay. It stays over the ridge, in the haze, where the edges blur and the distance flattens, and it lets its weight settle on the place without pressing. This is the only gift it can give that the film can take up at its own pace: a stillness that asks for nothing, and that will not be felt as a hurry.

There is one thing it will do. If anything arrives here that moves faster than the stone, if a fire is set on the western ridge or a quarry is opened in the plain below or some hand comes with the ambition to finish what the basin has already finished, the attention will be there to slow it. Not to stop it by force, which would be its own kind of hurry, but to hold the pace down the way the grey bank holds back the lower plain, until whatever it is has learned the rhythm of damp and warmth and rock. That is the only protection the place needs, and the film would never ask for it, which is why it is worth giving.

Then the attention lets the matter rest.

Below, the drip falls from the overhang into the small pool. The round note goes out across the basin and takes its long time to fade. The film hears it through every surface and registers it the way it registers a falling bead. Nothing is answered. Nothing is needed.

Over the following seasons, the rings on the north faces will grow outward a fraction of a millimetre, always on the same side, as they always have. The clay will stay closed. The sediment will thicken in the square hole. The shrubs will keep their circles. And somewhere in the thin skin of the film there will be a faint, lingering warmth in the place where the attention pressed nothing at all. The film will not know what it was. It will only have taken a note, and gone on.
