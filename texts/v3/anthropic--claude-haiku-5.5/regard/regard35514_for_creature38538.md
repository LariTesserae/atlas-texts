**What the Patient Power Did**

It came in the thinning of a drizzle, when the rock was still wet and the roar was at its lowest. Nothing announced it. The first sign was a footprint on the flats below the leaning cairn, a shallow print that filled with water warm enough to steam, though the air was freezing. Then the cairn's shadow shifted, very slightly, toward something that was not the sun. A grey bird on the nearest tor lifted its head and did not sing.

The power did not come down from above, because the Shelf has no above. It arrived the way understanding arrives, all at once and from the side. It had heard the Shelf's long attention for a great while before it came. It had heard the paces counted differently every time and always the same for each traveler. It had seen the stair worn into a trough by one walker, and the stones drifting inward a finger-width each cycle, and the small birds repeating their phrase because they had been given no other.

It understood at once what the thing in the hollow was. It was not cruel. It had never hunted. It had simply attended to something so closely, and for so long, that the attention had become a shape the world had to bend around. It was holding. Holding is not the same as keeping, and the power knew the difference. A thing held that long cannot be pulled free without tearing, and a thing that is loved badly can be harmed by love.

So the power did not force anything. It sat at the edge of the basin, looking not at the center but slightly to one side of it, the way one looks at a sleeper to let them wake on their own. It waited, which was the one thing it had in common with the Shelf. It waited through three drizzles and four rises of the roar. It did not count its paces, because it knew they would come out wrong, and it had no wish to argue with the ground.

Then it did a small thing.

It walked the flagstone stair, but not in the trough. It stepped on the outer edge, where the stone was still rough and unworn, and the sound it made was not the hollow ring of the scree but a plain, dull tap, the sound of one ordinary foot. At the top it did not go into the cloud. It stopped at the grey wall and sat down on the last step. The cloud, which had always been the place where the thing went to be far from what it had made, found itself with a visitor who did not gather it. The grey did not thicken. For the first time, the cloud had a edge that did not recede.

Next the power set a stone down in the central hollow, and it did not drift. It stayed exactly where it had been placed, and it stayed there through the next cycle and the one after. The Shelf had been pulling everything a finger-width inward for longer than any name could reach, and here was one thing that refused to be pulled. The power did not mind the stone's staying. It only wanted the stone to be seen where it had been put.

Then it climbed the leaning cairn, which took some time, because the courses were uneven and the cairn leaned toward the valley as though it were listening to something below. At the top it looked into the carved bowl. The rainwater had never overflowed, because the thing in the hollow had kept it from falling anywhere else. The power did not empty the bowl. It tipped it, very gently, the way one tips a cup for someone who has forgotten they are thirsty. The water ran over the rim for the first time since the cairn was built. It ran down the lean of the stones, found the fresh-cut stream beds, and, for the first time in the Shelf's long history, went downhill.

It went down all the way, without losing its nerve, to the edge of the plateau, where the ground ran out, and there the roar became visible. A river was there, in a gorge far below, dark and fast and entirely ordinary, going somewhere. It had always been there. The power heard the sound of the water clearly now for the first time, and the sound was not a waiting. It was a going.

Then the power did the one thing that mattered most. It spoke, not in words, to the attention that had been holding the Shelf so long. It did not ask it to leave. It said only that the cloud had an edge now, and the stone had stayed where it was put, and the water was running downhill, and the river below was still going to the sea whether anything waited for it or not. It said that the one who had climbed the stair and come back so many times had been the Shelf's own walker, and that walker was allowed to stop walking. It said that whoever had sat first at the center, and been held so long they stopped being separate, had been waiting for someone to notice they had become a crowd of one.

The answer was slow, as everything on the Shelf is slow. It was not a word. It was a stone.

In the central hollow, the stone that had refused to drift moved. It moved outward. One finger-width, toward the rim of the basin and the open air beyond it, and the stones around it, which had been pressing inward since before there were shelters, eased and took a breath. The doorways of the roofless shelters turned, very slightly, to face the valleys instead of the hollow. The cloud over the stair thinned, and under its thinning there was, for a moment, a firm shadow, cast by the cairn, pointing at a sun that was there after all.

The power did not stay to see the rest. It is not the kind of power that stays. It went down into the valley beside the river and let the water carry its own attention outward, the way water carries anything that has stopped holding its breath. When the roar came up over the rim again it sounded like a river, and only like a river, and the grey birds on the tors lifted from their perches, all together, and flew out over the valleys without coming back.

The Shelf is still a high grey plateau near the roof of the world, cut by long valleys and ringed by peaks that vanish into cloud. Its stone is still dark slate and mica-flecked granite. Its water still beads and pools and runs. But the drizzle now comes and goes on a schedule that changes, and the rock dries sometimes, and footprints fill with cold water that stays cold. The cairn's bowl overflows in the rains, and its stream runs downhill. Paces counted across the same ground still differ when counted by the same traveler, but they differ now the way ordinary distances do, and a new traveler can climb the stair without finding it worn into a single trough.

Sometimes, in the thick of a drizzle, a stone set down in the hollow will be found a little farther from the center than it was left. Nobody can say why. And sometimes a small grey bird on a tor lifts its head and sings a second phrase, one nobody taught it, which is perhaps the only trace the thing left behind: not a name, not a warning, only the sound of something that had been repeating itself for a very long time, and had finally found a different word.
