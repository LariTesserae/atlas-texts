# The Power That Came Down the Terraces

It did not come as a storm or a light. It came as a change in how the mist lay between the trees, a faint thinning in one hollow that the people did not remark on. The power was attentive in the way a physician is attentive, or a gardener, or a grandmother at a sickbed. It had watched many places. Few had held so still.

It walked the terraces for a long time before it did anything. It noticed that the channels ran at exactly the rate they had always run, and that a child was learning to lift a slab a finger's width, and that the child's hands were already stained the same as her mother's. It noticed the grinding stones, their dishes deepening by a grain a year, and it did the arithmetic of that slow hollowing and found that it added up to something very large and very quiet. It noticed the weeping seam in the cliff. It listened to the water, and the water was steady, and the steadiness was not peace exactly. It was the sound of something being held.

It understood the land first, because the land was simpler. The plateau was young. Its edges had not been worn, its gullies had not been sorted, its stone had not been given the time that makes stone rest. The rock wept because it was still settling from a violence that had stopped mid-motion, the way a limb keeps aching after it has been set. The people had come to the place when it was still raw, and they had begun to tend it, and the tending had become the whole shape of their lives. They had mistaken the ache for the thing to be protected. Over time the protecting had hardened into a balance, and the balance had hardened into a world that could no longer move.

The power considered what it might do.

It could have made the sky brighter, given the land a sun to cast shadows and tell hours. But the light without origin was not a wound, and a sun would have thrown every stone into a hard black and white that the people's eyes were not made to read. It could have brought rain, but the people already had more water than they could manage, and rain would have overwhelmed their channels and drowned the careful work of centuries. It could have sent animals. A single bird would have changed everything, and it would have changed it all at once, into a noise and a hunger and a thousand small urgent movements that no one here had asked for and no one could absorb. The power was wise enough to see that a sudden flood of life is only another way of overriding a place. It would have been an act of fixing, and the land had already been fixed too long.

So it did very little.

It made a wind.

It was not a large wind. It came up from the lowlands, where the gullies opened, and it moved through the tree ferns for perhaps the length of a breath. The fronds stirred. For the first time in anyone's memory, the plants made a sound, a dry hushing that carried across the mist like a question being asked very softly. The reeds in the marsh bent and straightened. A loose strand of fiber lifted from a drying rack and turned in the air.

The people heard it. The one who had been kneeling by the cistern lifted her head. The one who had been scraping moss from a causeway stopped, and the scraper hung in his hand. The child at the ditch looked up, and then, slowly, the others did too, which they had not done in living memory, because there had never been any reason to look up at a sky that gave no direction and no change. They looked up now and saw only the same pale, even white, with no sun in it and nothing to measure. But the air had moved, and that was new, and they did not yet know what to do with new things.

The power did one more thing. It did not speak to them in words, because words would have been an instruction, and an instruction would have been a hand on the stone. Instead it let the old woman by the cistern feel, for a single moment, the weight of her own hands. She had been holding the clay pot for so long that she had forgotten it was heavy. For that moment she knew it was heavy, and she knew that she could set it down. She did not set it down. She sat with the pot in her lap and the wind moving through the ferns, and she looked at the wet stone, and she thought, for the first time she could remember, about what would happen if she stopped.

Then the power withdrew.

It did not stay to see what the people would make of the wind. A guardian who stays to watch every choice is only a more patient kind of ruler. It had given the land a movement it had not had. It had given the people a glimpse of the sky, and the knowledge that the sky could move them. It had not told them to abandon the channels or let the moss grow or call the birds. It had not decided what the place should become. That decision belonged to the stone and the water and the people who knelt beside them, and the power was wise enough to know that a choice made for someone is only a choice they will resent.

But it did not leave the place unchanged. Something had been let in.

Over the following seasons, which the people would not have counted, the wind came back now and then, from the lowlands, faint and brief. The reeds learned to bend. The ferns began to hush at the edges of their dusk. Once, a seed that had come from nowhere anyone could name lodged in a crack of the young basalt and sent up a green shoot with a single pale flower, the first flower the plateau had seen. The people stood around it for a long time. The old woman touched a petal. The child asked what it was for, and no one had an answer, and the not-knowing was itself a kind of light.

The weeping seam did not stop. The power had not come to cure the rock. The stone was still settling, and it would keep settling for centuries, and some of its water would keep running down the cliff face into the channels the people had cut. But the drip had changed its meaning. It was no longer only the sound of an ache being guarded. It was the sound of a young land slowly deciding what it wanted to become, and of the people who could now choose whether to keep kneeling by the water or to stand up and look at what was beyond it.

The power watched from a distance, patient and unhurried, the way the best things watch. It would not fix the land, and it would not hurry the people. It had cracked open a door and stepped back from it. Whatever came through was theirs to decide.

Somewhere in the mist, a child lifted a flat stone a finger's width, looked at the water on either side of it, and, for the first time, did not set it back.
