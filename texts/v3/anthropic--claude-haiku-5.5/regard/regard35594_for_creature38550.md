# What the Attention Would Do

The attention arrives the way weather does, gradually and then everywhere at once. It notices the plateau first as a question. Why is there so much unfinished stone in one place? Why do the water and the rock disagree so openly? It feels something like tenderness toward the raw ground and the thin, stubborn things that have started to live on it.

It does not rush. The land has only just decided to exist, and anything that moves quickly here would be mistaken for a storm. So it watches for a long time, longer than the birds could follow and longer than the lichen could measure, and it learns the plateau's rhythms before it acts.

## What It Would Not Do

It would not reshape the land. A gentle hand could smooth the gullies, lay a skin of soil over the scree, and coax the bedrock into a valley with a kinder slope. But the plateau's argument with itself is the most honest thing about it. The gullies cut because water is cutting them. The landslides fall because the lips of the channels have no support left. To prevent this would be to answer a question the land is still asking, and the answer would belong to the power and not to the land.

It would not feed the predators, or thin the grazers, or fix the number of birds so the ledges never overcrowd. The loop of eating and being eaten is not a flaw in the design. It is the design, the way the plateau learns what it is. A wise power understands that a balance imposed from outside is only a cage that has not yet been noticed.

It would not gather the creatures and lead them somewhere better. Each of them holds its territory provisionally, and that provisionality is a kind of freedom. The burrowers are reworking the ground for reasons they do not need to understand. The birds are navigating a geography they seem to hold all at once. To lift them out would be to take away the one thing they are already doing well.

## What It Would Notice

It would notice the grit.

The dark, glittering particles that settle on the snow are not from the plateau. They drift on the wind from the basin in the east, where the column of smoke bends slowly over the haze, and they settle in the tarns. The insects hatch in that water. The birds eat the insects. The burrowers and grazers drink from the same pools, and the predators wait at the same edges. The fine grit moves through the whole web, quietly, a little at a time, and the power would see how far it had already gone.

It would notice the amber glow under the smoke at night, and the pulse of presses and compressors that the rock carries into the sternum of anything standing on it. It would feel the grid's current humming somewhere below the crust, and the static that lifts the hair before storms.

None of this belongs to the plateau. It belongs to a place that the plateau cannot see and does not know. The creatures here are paying for something decided elsewhere, by beings who will never stand on this stone.

## What It Would Do

It would not descend into the basin like a judgment. A power that appeared in the furnaces and the offices, rewriting what people had built, would be dangerous even if it were kind, because it would have made itself the new master of a world it does not own. Wisdom knows the difference between caring for a thing and taking it over.

Instead, it would work the way the rain works, slowly and in many small places at once.

It would move through the people who keep the machines running and the people who decide how much the machines should make. It would not command them. It would put in their minds the thought of a particular tarn, a particular bird, the metallic taste of a particular morning, and let them carry it. Some would ignore it. Some would not. The power would accept whatever they chose, because a choice that is not freely made is not really a change.

It would soften the smoke's reach a little, the way a wind shifts. It would turn the air above the basin, just enough that the grit would fall in the haze over the empty lowlands, where it would harm nothing living, and not on the tarns where the insects hatch. This is a small act, and it is not a fix. It is a kindness that does not pretend to be more than it is.

It would do one thing for the stone itself. Where the grit has settled thickest on the rock, it would let the first thin layer of lichen take hold a season sooner, so that the slowest of the plateau's tenants would have one more cushion to stand on. The power would not thicken the lichen or spread it across the bedrock. It would only give it a little more time, and time is the thing the lichen already has.

## What It Would Leave Alone

It would leave the bones to the grit and the grit to the moss. It would leave the predators in the wet grass, the birds shouting down the gullies, and the grazers scraping their long, separate lines. It would leave the fog to come in the morning and go in the afternoon, and the rain to cut the gullies a little deeper. It would leave the ice to form each night and break each dawn.

It would leave the plateau arguing with itself.

## The Withdrawal

When it was done, the attention would thin, the way fog does when the sun finds a gap in the cloud. It would not announce itself, and no creature on the plateau would know it had been there. The birds would return to the ledges that survived. The burrowers would push up their mounds of ash-colored grit. The predator would lie flat at the edge of the channel and wait for the grazer to lower its head to the water.

If anything remained of the attention, it would be a feeling in the rock, faint as the hum of the grid. It would be patient, and it would hope that the people in the basin would someday look up from their work and see the haze, the smoke, and the grit settling on the snow, and ask whether any of it was worth what it cost the things that could not ask.

The land would go on deciding its shape. The power would not decide it for the land. It would only make sure that whatever shape the plateau took, it would take it among living things and not over their dead bodies, and that the argument would continue for a long time yet.
