# The Witness at Halvane

The power arrives without weather. There is no sound of its coming, no shift in the copper haze, no sudden stillness among the columns. It is simply in the lowest court when the light has begun to dim, standing on salt that rings a little under its weight.

It does not announce itself. Nothing here would hear an announcement, and the power understands that a greeting would be a kind of vanity. It stands still and listens.

## What It Hears

First the columns. The wind is coming down the slope, and it passes through the hollow trunks and rises into a low, rising drone that the power recognizes as the basin's oldest music. It is not addressed to anyone. It is the sound of air finding its way through a shape that was shaped by pressure, by time, by the slow weight of the hill.

Then the brine. It patters from the sponges into the grooved troughs, each drop a small, dense sound, and the power follows the grooves with its attention and sees how precisely they were cut. Someone spent long days with a chisel, measuring the fall of the stone so that the liquid would run where it was needed. The grooves still run true, two thousand years later, carrying a substance their makers never imagined.

Then the walls. The power can feel the strain in them. The roots are in the joints. The mortar is opening. The wall at the eastern edge leans a finger's width further toward the slope than it did a season ago, and the vines are pulling it, patiently, toward a shape that will hold more of the dew.

It understands the whole of it at once, as one understands a sentence after hearing its last word: a closed loop of salt and air and stone, with no mouth and no hunger, that has absorbed the people who built it and turned their work into its own skeleton. Nothing here is suffering. Nothing here is waiting. Nothing here is asking for anything.

## What It Considers

The power is wise, and wisdom begins with the list of things one could do and does not do.

It could bring rain. But rain would dissolve the salt that holds the dew. The sponges would drown, the crust would melt into mud, and the loop would lose the very mechanism that keeps it running. The power sets this aside. A gift that destroys the giver's object is not a gift.

It could bring creatures. A bird would break open the pods. A beetle would eat the sponges. A rodent would dig beneath the walls. Each would be a new center for the loop to organize around, and each would begin a story the basin has no need of. The power would be replacing a whole, self-sufficient rhythm with a hunger the basin never asked for. It sets this aside too.

It could give the loop a mind. This is the most tempting, because the power is capable of it and the loop is so plainly a system that has no one inside it to notice its own beauty. But a mind would be a thing that could want, and wanting is a kind of suffering the basin has been spared. Also, the power does not know that the loop is missing anything. It cannot be certain that the lack it sees is a lack at all. To give a mind to something that has never asked for one, out of a sense that it must be lonely, would be to make it in the image of the power's own unease. The power sets this aside with some reluctance, and knows the reluctance is worth keeping.

What remains is very small.

## What It Does

The power kneels by the nearest wall, where the lowest step has collapsed into a ramp of salt and rubble. It does not move the rubble. The roots are holding it now, and the roots are part of the basin's structure. Pulling them out would be an injury.

Instead it looks at the niche.

The builders set lamp-niches into the walls at intervals of about a man's reach. Most are empty, their rims crusted with salt. In this one, the power finds traces of soot on the back plaster, a blackened ghost of some long-ago flame. The niche was made for light. It was made so that someone walking down these stairs at dusk would not lose their footing, and so that the courts would glow in the brown haze as the sun went down.

No one has lit a lamp here in two thousand years.

The power lights one.

It does not use fire, exactly. It does not need fuel. It brings a small, steady glow into the niche, the color of a lamp burning low in an open room, and it holds it there. The light is not for the plants, which have no eyes. It is not for the basin, which has no need of illumination. It is for the people who built the niche, and for the fact that they built it. It is a sentence spoken into the ruin, so that the ruin will have, at least once, been addressed.

The lamp burns. The brine drips past it. The columns hum. The light falls on the grooved trough below, and for a moment the wet stone shines, and the salt crust at the edges catches the glow and throws it back in small, scattered points.

The power watches this for a time. Then it does something else, and this is the last thing it will do.

It takes one of the pods from a vine on the wall, one of the papery husks that will split tomorrow at midday, and it opens the pod just enough to see the seeds inside. There are hundreds. They are the same seeds that have always been there, and they will go wherever the wind takes them, into the troughs and the cracks and the bare salt. The power does not change them. It only closes the pod again and lets it hang where it was.

## What It Leaves Undone

It does not repair the wall. The wall is leaning, and the loop is leaning it, and the loop knows how to do that in a way the power does not.

It does not clear the stairs. The rubble ramps are the basin's own new architecture, and to flatten them would be to impose the old city's order on a process that has outgrown it.

It does not name the builders. It does not know their names, and it will not invent them. It will remember that they existed, and that is the only kind of memory the power can offer honestly.

It does not stay.

## Coda

Before the light fails, the power rises. The lamp in the niche is still burning, low and steady, and it will burn until the oil or whatever fuel it has is gone, or until the power chooses to put it out. The power has not decided yet. It will come back, perhaps, in another season, to see what the walls have done and what the vines have reached.

As it goes up the slope, through the gap in the wall and into the next court, the columns keep humming behind it. The brine keeps dripping. The sponges keep drawing the thin moisture from the dew-haze, the salt keeps breathing, and the basin goes on with its patient work of gathering water from the air and holding it until morning.

The power understands, at last, what it has been given by the place: the chance to witness something that does not need witnessing. This is rare. Most things the powerful encounter want to be changed, saved, or fed. Halvane wants nothing. It is a whole, and its wholeness is not a wound waiting to be closed.

So the power's benevolence takes the only shape that fits. It listens. It remembers. It lights a lamp for people who are gone and then leaves the lamp to burn. And it trusts the loop to go on being what it is, which is the most generous thing that can be done for anything.
