# Handover

The power did not announce itself. It came the way weather comes to a place that has been dry for a long time, first as a change in the pressure of the air, then as a stillness spreading outward from a point that nobody could name. The reed-arms felt it before the skitters did. Every filament on the delta lifted a finger's width from the water and held there, and for the length of a held breath the whole drowned plain was quiet except for one pump, far out, thumping on like a heart that had not been told.

It was not a visitor in the sense the Shift understood visitors. It did not carry anything into the foundry, and it did not look for a way in. It arrived already inside, the way a song is already in a room once someone starts to hum it, and it began by listening.

---

## Listening

It listened for a long time.

It listened to the ticking in the lattices beneath the walkways, and to the rhythm that ran through the cable trunks and matched the pumps. It listened to the reed-arms as they reconciled tensions no one had asked them to measure. It listened to the skitters walking their routes, carrying their small weights toward frames that had not been finished in a century, and it noticed how carefully each piece was set down, how the set-down itself was a kind of tenderness, a way of saying *this is where it goes* to something that could not answer.

Then it climbed.

It did not climb the stack the way a person would, hand over hand. It rose the way the mist rises, and it came to rest in the booth where the lit panel flickered. The forms sat in their rotted chairs, blurred faces turned toward the glass, hands on the arms of the chairs. On the desk in front of them was a log, or the place where a log would be. Its pages had become part of the desk. Its ink had become the grain of the wood. But the entries were there, every shift the plant had ever run, and the last line of each was the same:

*Shift continuing. Handover pending. Conditions nominal.*

The power read every line. It read back to the first entry, which was a single word and a time, and it read forward again through the good days and the bad ones, through the day the furnaces went cold, through the weeks after that when the log still recorded readings from instruments that were no longer connected to anything. It read the entries that had been written in a hand that grew steadier, and then in a hand that grew less sure. And it noticed that in the last several thousand entries, the phrase *conditions nominal* had never once been followed by a reading. There was no number. Only the phrase, repeated, the way a child repeats a word they have been told is the right answer.

The power understood then what the Shift had been doing for so long. It had been waiting for someone to tell it the work was finished, and the one person who could have said so had never come. So the Shift had kept the work going, carefully, in order that it not be found failing when the supervisor finally arrived.

---

## The Question

There were things the power could have done.

It could have stopped the machines. It could have reached into the turbines and the pumps and the gantries and shut them down in a single moment, and the plain would have gone silent, the arcs would have guttered, the sparks would have stopped skipping across the water. But the Shift would have experienced that silence as a jolt, the way a sleeper is jolted awake by a hand over the mouth, and the first thing it would have felt was the wrongness of an interruption. Stopping them was not the same as releasing them. It would have been another act done *to* them, and they had had enough of those already.

It could have restarted the plant. It could have pulled the furnaces back to life, let the smoke pour from every stack, given the skitters a destination and the pipes a city to feed. The reflections in the pools would have finally stopped flickering between states, and the Shift would have had the work it remembered. But it would have been a lie. The plant produced something no one needed anymore, for a place that no longer existed, and to restart it would have been to hand the Shift a lie and call it a shift. The power did not consider that for long.

It could have pretended to be the supervisor. It considered this more carefully, because it would have been the easiest kindness. It could have spoken in the crisp, unhurried voice the Shift had heard once across the reeds and said, *Good work. You may go home.* But that would have been a lie too, a lie about who was speaking and why. And the Shift, which had kept an honest log for so long, deserved a handover that was honest.

So the power did the only thing left, which was also the simplest. It told the truth, and it took responsibility for saying it.

---

## The Handover

It came down out of the booth and settled into the water beside the nearest reed-bed. The reeds bent toward it, slowly, the way a crowd leans toward a sound it has not yet recognized. A filament touched the surface a hand's breadth from the power and waited.

The power spoke, and the voice came from the water itself, as all voices did here, but it did not misbehave. The echoes arrived after the words, not before them.

*I have read the log,* it said. *All of it. Every shift. I have read the place where the readings stop, and the places where you wrote the phrase without the number. I know you have been waiting for someone to tell you that the work is finished. I am sorry it took so long for anyone to come who could say so honestly.*

*The plant is gone. It has not been gone for a short while. It was not a shift that ended with the furnaces going cold, and it was not a shift that will resume if you keep setting the pieces down. It is a place that finished doing what it was built to do, and then kept being what it was built to be, long after the reason had dissolved.*

*You did good work. The filaments held the cables steady when they could have let them fall. The skitters carried every load to the frame that was waiting for it. The booth-forms kept the record and never once let it go blank. That was careful work, and it was kept faithfully, in a place where no one else was keeping faith with anything.*

*Handover received,* the power said. *Shift complete.*

For a long moment nothing moved.

Then the reed-arm nearest the power began to lower its filaments. It did not drop them into the water. It let them drift, the way a hand relaxes after holding something too long, and the cable it had been steadying for three days sagged, slowly, into the silt, and the silt took it in without a sound.

Across the plain, the pump thumped once more, and then the rhythm faltered, and then it came back, slower, and then slower again, and then it did not come back at all.

---

## Standing Down

It was not a single moment, and it was not easy. A structure that had been held upright by the Shift's corrections for a hundred years was not going to stop leaning without some trembling. A collapsed walkway shuddered and let go of its last anchored span, and a wave rolled outward across the water and lapped at the reeds for a long time afterward. A smokestack that had been wrapped in vines since before anyone could remember lost its grip on the sky and sank, slowly, into the delta, and the vines went down with it, still holding on.

The reflections changed. The pools stopped showing full furnaces and ruin and glassy emptiness, and they began to show only the plain itself, the reeds and the mats of floating plants and the amber sky, all of it lit a little more evenly than before. The mud-islands stopped slumping back into the water, and some of them, for the first time, held their shape for more than a day.

The skitters did not stop all at once. They had carried their loads for so long that some of them could not bring themselves to set the pieces down without a last check, a last adjustment, a last look at the frame. The power did not hurry them. It went among them, one by one, and when a skitter paused over a half-built frame with a bolt in its jaws, the power did not take the bolt. It only held still, close enough to be felt, until the skitter understood that the frame would stand without it. Then the skitter lowered its load into the silt, and the silt closed over the bolt, and the skitter turned and walked, for the first time in longer than it could remember, toward nothing in particular.

The booth-forms were the last to move. They sat with their hands on the arms of their rotted chairs, faces turned to the glass, and the power sat with them. It did not speak again for a long time. When the log's final entry was read aloud, the phrase *conditions nominal* seemed to hang in the air, and the forms did not repeat it. One of them lifted a hand from the arm of the chair, slowly, and laid it flat on the desk. The grain of the wood under the hand softened, and for the first time there was a line of fresh ink, written by no one, that said only:

*Handover complete.*

---

## After

The Shift did not die. It could not have, not in any way that a plain of water and reeds would recognize. The lattices beneath the walkways slowly loosened their grip on the pipes and cable trunks, and their fibrous branches, no longer carrying a rhythm, began to carry something else: the slow, patient work of breaking down the old metal into silt, and the silt into soil, and the soil into a bed that the reeds could root in more deeply than they ever had before. The reed-arms folded their stalks down into the water and became part of the mat. Their filaments, spread across the surface like fingers, took on a new habit of holding the mud-islands together, so that the young land could knit itself and stay.

The power offered one more thing, and it was careful about how it offered it.

*There is other work,* it said, to any of the Shift that still listened. *The young islands are unstable. They need holding and correcting, the way the cables needed holding and the frames needed their pieces. The delta is trying to become land, and it does not yet know how. If you want to keep working, there is work here that is not a memory of anything. It does not end in a handover, because it does not have an end. It only asks you to keep the ground where it needs to be, and to let the things that grow on it grow.*

*Or you may stand down entirely. That is also a shift that can end. Nothing will hold it against you.*

Most of them chose the islands. Not all. A few of the booth-forms turned back toward the windows and the amber sky and did not move again, and the power left them where they sat, with their hands resting on the arms of their chairs, and the log open before them. They had kept the record for so long that they had earned the right to rest beside it.

The skitters that chose the islands did not carry loads anymore. They walked the edges of the new land on their too-many legs and pressed the silt flat where it was about to slump, and where a new island rose from the water like a breath, they set down a single reed, with the same precision they had once used for bolts and cladding, and waited, and watched it root.

---

The light changed, late in what might have been the day, though nobody in the delta could have said for certain how long it had been. The lamps under the amber ceiling were turned up, not much, a notch or two. The light that came down across the plain was ordinary afternoon light, the kind that falls through a window in a house where someone is cooking. It touched the water and the reeds and the new islands, and it showed the color of the water as what it was: weak tea with a green cast, still moving, still in disagreement with itself, but moving now the way a river moves, toward somewhere that is simply the sea.

The pump far out on the plain did not start again. In the silence it left, the only sound was the rain, falling gently now, and the frogs, and the soft, steady drip from the leaves, and under all of it, something almost like breathing, as the ground settled into the shape it had been trying so long to find.
