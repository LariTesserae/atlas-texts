# What the Watcher Did

## The Arrival

The power did not arrive the way weather arrives. It noticed the Sump Quarter the way a person notices a half-finished sentence in a letter left open on a table: the handwriting stops mid-word, and the reader wants to know what came next.

It had been attending to many places. Most of them were settled. Their rivers had chosen their courses long ago, their cities had hardened into habits, and their people had grown into the shapes they would keep. The power had learned that a settled place rarely needs anything but to be left alone, and that a benevolent hand is most dangerous when it mistakes its own wish for someone else's need.

The Quarter was different. It had no settled edge. Its banks slumped after every rain, its gullies deepened, its streets bent, its doors opened onto moments that did not match. It was still choosing what it was. The power felt the pull to help, and it examined that pull carefully, the way a careful physician examines their own urge to cut.

## What It Considered

It considered closing the bowl. It could have raised the rim, packed the slumping banks with stone and root, and fixed the Quarter's boundary in a single season. The water would have been held. The gullies would have stopped. The doors would have shut into ordinary walls. The tenders would have had solid ground under their feet, and the Ones Who Walked In would have had an edge to face and a reason to stop walking.

The power saw what this would cost. The basin would have stopped deciding, and a thing that stops deciding stops being a thing that can grow. It would become a monument, and a monument does not breathe. The silver column of rain rising into the cloud would be a mere error, and the heron would be a tenant of an impossible stairwell with no reason to exist at all.

It considered draining the Quarter instead, letting the water find the gullies and rebuilding the streets as a plain city. This was worse. It would have meant removing everyone who had made a life in the sentence, to spare the sentence the trouble of being unfinished.

It considered doing nothing at all. This was the strongest argument, and it was not wrong. But the power had also learned that doing nothing can be a way of looking away, and that some things had been waiting a long time for someone to ask them a question.

So it decided on a few small acts, each of which would leave the choice where it belonged.

## The Bank

First, the power went to the bank nearest the furnace glow, where the last slump still showed its dark scar in the pale mudstone. It did not touch the earth. It sat, or what passed for sitting in a place where the power had no body, and it listened to the drip.

It spoke to the basin in the only language it had that the basin could hear, which was not words but attention. It asked, in effect: *Do you wish to be finished?*

The answer was slow, as everything here was slow. A gully three hundred meters east, which had been deepening for a season, paused. Its walls stopped sliding inward. Then, after a long minute, it began to widen again, not in a straight line but in a curve, as though the basin had heard the question and was considering a different answer.

The power understood. The basin did not want to be finished. It wanted to be asked, and it wanted the asking to continue. A boundary chosen by a stranger would not be the basin's boundary. It would be the stranger's, and the basin would spend its life resenting the seam.

The power withdrew its attention from the bank and left the earth to its argument.

## The Tower

Next, it went to the water tower, where the woman slept among the fern-roots in the split tank. She was awake, as she always was, listening for the basin to finish.

The power did not speak aloud, but it let her feel that someone was attending to her, and she turned her head toward the empty air with the look of a person who has heard her name in another room.

*You are not waiting for the basin to tell you what you are,* the power let her understand. *You are already something. You are the one who stayed to listen. That is a complete thing to be, even if the sentence never ends.*

She was quiet for a long time. Then she asked, in her echoing voice, whether she was allowed to stop waiting.

The power told her yes. It also told her she could keep waiting if she liked, because waiting was not a mistake. It was only a choice, and she could make another one tomorrow. It would not decide for her.

The next morning she climbed down from the tank for the first time in four years, and she sat on the steps with her face turned to the thin light, and she did not yet know whether she would stay. That was hers to find out.

## The Heron

The heron stood on its impossible step, one grey foot lifted toward a stair that was not there. The power looked at it for a while, and then, very carefully, made one step real.

Only one. Not the stair, not the stairwell, not the building that did not exist. A single step, pale and damp and no more solid than the moss around it, appeared under the bird's raised foot.

The heron lowered its foot onto it. It did not look surprised. It looked, if a heron can look anything, like a creature that had been waiting for exactly that much and no more. Then it turned its sap-coloured eyes toward the power, which was not visible, and blinked once, slowly.

Then it stepped to the next step, which was not there, and vanished up a stair that had only just become possible, leaving the power to wonder whether the step had been a gift or merely a mirror of what the heron had already decided. Perhaps the difference did not matter. The power had offered the smallest help it could and had let the bird do the rest.

## The Ones Who Walked In

The power found the Ones Who Walked In at the banks, as it had expected, facing outward with their backward lean, watching the far bank that was always in front of them.

It could have turned them around with a word. It could have moved the far bank, or made the rim recede, or simply told them the truth: that they had come in from a particular place, and that the place was still there, and that they were free to go back to it.

Instead it walked with them.

It walked a little behind, as a companion walks, and it let them feel the ground under them change. The far bank did not move. But the path that led toward it began, very gently, to curve. Every few steps it bent a little more, until they were no longer walking toward the far bank at all, but along the rim, past the spot where they had first entered, toward the gap in the banks where a dry gully ran out into the open country.

Several of them stopped. One of them turned around, looked at the gap for a long moment, and walked through it. Another sat down on the bank and began to weep, quietly, as if the release had been heavier than the walking. Two of them stayed exactly where they were, facing the far bank, and the power did not argue with them. They had chosen. The Quarter had chosen with them. It was not the power's place to undo what they had decided to keep.

## The Crane

The crane in the gantry groaned as the power passed beneath it. The cable was wrapped in creeper so thick it had become a trunk, and the hook at its end hung empty.

The tenders believed it was trying to lift something. The power listened, and understood that the crane had been lifting the far bank. Not literally, but in the way a machine that remembers its work will try to lift whatever it was built to move, even when the thing it was built for has gone. For years the crane had been trying to raise the rim, to make the bowl a bowl again, and the basin had been gently refusing.

The power did not lift the cable. It did not free the crane from its creeper, or cut the vines that held the pulleys in their strange new shape. It let the crane keep its groan and its patient failing. But it made one small adjustment: it loosened the hook, so that when the crane next tried to lift, the hook would swing free and the cable would carry only the weight of the creeper itself. The machine could go on remembering, and it would no longer be asked to carry something no one had asked it to carry.

## The Conveyor

The conveyor ran through its impossible room and out again, carrying seed pods toward a shed that had not been built.

The power followed the line. It came to the shed at the far end, and the shed was there, though it was not yet a shed. It was an outline of possibility, the shape the Quarter might choose to make of this corner of itself. The power looked at the door, which was a suggestion of a door, and found it unlatched.

It did not open the door. It did not step inside. It only noted that the door was unlatched, and that whatever came through it would come because someone or something had chosen to come through, not because the power had pushed them. Then it turned back to the conveyor and let the pods roll on.

## The Tenders

The tenders were at the banks with their notebooks, recording the new slump, the gully that had paused and then widened, the pool that had forgotten the sky and then remembered it. They did not see the power. They never would, not directly. But they noticed the changes, as they always noticed changes, and they wrote them down.

The power considered the bark on their wrists, the vines that had gone under their skin and never come out. It could have drawn them back. It could have made the grey-green scales fall away and left them smooth and unmarked, as people who had never been bound to the basin. But the bark was part of their history here, part of the argument they had been taking notes on. Removing it would have been a kind of erasure.

Instead it left a small thing at the foot of the stairwell, the one that climbed into the canopy and stopped. It was not food, though the tenders would later assume it was. It was a question, written in the grain of a pale stone the size of a palm: *What do you want to keep?*

The tenders found the stone in the morning. They turned it over in their hands for a long time, and then one of them, the eldest, began to cry for reasons no one asked about. They did not know who had left the stone. They did not need to. They took it up the stairs and set it on the ledge of the canopy, where the grey-green birds would see it when they came in the evening to bathe.

## The Departure

Before it left, the power returned to the bank where the last slump still showed its dark scar, and it sat for one more moment in the drip.

It did not ask the basin another question. It had asked the first, and the basin had answered in its own slow way. To ask again so soon would be to press, and pressing was a habit the power had learned to distrust.

Instead it made a promise, which was the only thing it would keep from its visit: it would come back after the next heavy rain, when the banks had slumped again and the gullies had made their new decisions. It would look at what had been decided. It would not interfere. It would only look, and remember, and perhaps listen a little longer.

Then it went away, the way a benevolent and wise power goes away, which is to say it became less present in the Quarter without ever quite leaving, the way a person's voice remains in a room after they have walked out of it.

## That Night

The rain came in low over the banks, as it always did, and filled the basin for an hour. Then it drained away through the gullies, along the routes the water always found.

The gully three hundred meters east widened into its curve. The unlatched door in the shed that was not yet a shed stayed unlatched. The woman in the water tower climbed down the steps into the thin light and did not climb back up. The heron stepped from one moment to another, and the step it had been given remained, pale and damp, a small thing that might or might not be used again. The Ones Who Walked In were fewer by a few, and the rest kept facing the far bank, and the far bank kept receding a little more each day.

The stone sat on the ledge of the canopy. In the evening the grey-green birds came down to bathe in the largest pool, and the clatter of their wings and the splash of their bodies filled the basin, and the mist thickened afterward, and the drips quickened, and somewhere under the mat of moss and paving a root reconsidered a rail.

The basin breathed out, and a thin silver column of droplets rose from one corner into the cloud. The tenders, watching from the banks with their notebooks, said that the basin was breathing out. They did not know whether that meant relief, or thanks, or only the ordinary movement of a thing that was still deciding.

The power, very far away, took the breath as an answer to a question it had not quite finished asking, and was content to leave it unfinished.

## Why It Acted So Little

The power understood that the most loving thing it could do for the Sump Quarter was to refuse the role of author. A benevolent hand that writes the ending has not helped the story; it has only replaced the storyteller. The basin had been asking, through rain and rot and slumping earth, what its edges should be. Answering for it would have been a kind of theft, however gentle the intention.

So the power did a few small things, each one leaving the choice in the hands of whoever it belonged to. It offered a step, a door, a question, a loosened hook. It let people and birds and machines and the earth itself decide what to do with what it had made possible. It did not promise that the Quarter would survive, or that it would drain away, or that the tenders would stay or leave. It promised only to come back and look, and not to pretend that looking was the same as deciding.

Perhaps this is what wisdom looks like when the thing in front of it is still being written: a careful hand that opens a few doors and then steps back, trusting that the story will be better for having been finished by the ones who live in it.
