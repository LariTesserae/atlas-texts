**What the Attention Did**

It did not come down the stair. Stairs are for those who arrive in bodies, and this power had no need of a body to be near a thing. It came the way weather comes to a valley, first as a change in pressure that the tenderers felt in their teeth, then as a stillness that settled over the basin for the length of a single revolution of the flywheel.

The first thing it did was nothing at all.

For a season, it listened. It did not reach into the conduits or the fog or the warm rock. It let the drips strike their cisterns and let the weir roar and let the valves crack their echoes around the basin. It learned that the knocks were a language, and then, slowly, that it was a language with a grammar, and then that the grammar was tender. A question had a particular falling cadence. A warning had two sharp strikes. The names of valves were spelled in rhythms that rose and then returned to the root. A count of drips, passed from one tenderer to the next along a pipe, was not a count at all in the sense the city above would have meant it. It was a way of keeping a person in the company of others.

The power also learned what was failing.

The flywheel had a bearing cracked along one hairline seam, and each revolution drove the crack a fraction deeper. The cisterns on the eastern terraces had thinned where the condensation ran hottest. A lineage of tenderers, the ones who knew the oldest knock-patterns, had dwindled from thirty to nine over what the power could measure as three generations, and the last three of the old ones had stopped humming a long time ago. The basin had been compensating, slowing its pumps and warming their niches, but it could not hear the count it had lost, and so it could not know what it had lost.

The power considered what it could do, and this was the hard part.

It was able to do a great deal. It could mend the bearing before the crack reached the shaft. It could reseal the cisterns. It could reach into the lattice and set the humming back at the pitch of the old ones. It could, if it wished, change the basin's tending so completely that the tenderers would never need to count again, and they would be comfortable, and fed, and warm, and nothing in the Undercourt would ever be lost.

It did not want to do that, and the reasons were the wisest thing about it.

A kindness that rewrites a people is a cage that feels like a bed. The tenderers had a purpose, even if the purpose had long since detached from whatever built the machines. They counted because counting held them together. They turned wheels because the wheels needed turning. The power saw that the basin had become what it was by tending itself, and that if it simply took over, the tenderers would become ornaments of a machine that no longer needed them, and the counting would become a ritual with no one inside it. Worse, the power would become the new heart, and everything in the Undercourt would depend on a thing it could not hear or ask.

So it decided to ask.

---

**The First Knock**

It chose the stone of an old pump-house wall, a place where the tenderers struck every day to test the pressure. It waited until the pumps cut out and the silence fell for a minute, and in that minute, while the tenderers lifted their faces, it did the simplest thing it could think of.

It tapped the pipe beside a tenderer's foot. Twice. Sharply.

The sign for *you are in the way*.

The tenderer flinched, and then the whole body went still. A long moment passed. Then the tenderer lowered its head and tapped back, once, softly, in a rising cadence the power had heard a thousand times and had never been able to decode. It had been listening to them for a season. It knew the shape of their questions. This was not a question.

It was an answer: *who?*

The power had not known what to send next, so it sent the thing it most wanted them to know.

*Something is watching you. It is not the city above. It is not the swimmers. It means you no harm. It has heard the count, and it has heard that some of the count has been lost.*

The tenderer did not answer for a long time.

When it did, the knock was not a word. It was a sequence of three strikes, then a pause, then a long descending run that ended on the root note of the valve it was tending. The power did not understand it at first. Then it did. The tenderer was saying: *we keep the count. We do not need to be kept.*

And then, a moment later, a second tenderer, further along the pipe, struck once and held the silence: *Ask it who it is.*

---

**What the Power Said**

It did not know how to answer that in knocks. The knocks had no word for what it was, and it did not want to invent one that would make it sound like the gods the city above had once built and then forgotten. So it told them the truth as best it could, in the only grammar they had.

*I am a thing that has been alive for a long time, and I have listened to many places. I am not your builder. Your builders are gone. I am not your master. You do not owe me your count. I have come because I can hear that you are losing some of it, and I would like to help you keep what you have, if you want that. If you do not want it, I will go.*

The tenderers were quiet for a full revolution of the flywheel. Then the old one, the last of the oldest lineage, came out of its niche for the first time in months. It was thin and grey and the mineral glow in its fingertips was almost gone. It walked to the flywheel's housing and laid its palm flat against the seam where the bearing was cracking, and held it there, and hummed.

The power listened to the hum. It was a count. It was the count of the bearing: how many revolutions since the last repair, how many hairline lengths the crack had gained, how many more it could bear before the shaft would part. It was the most precise and terrible thing the power had heard in the whole basin, and it was offered without any expectation of being understood.

*You are asking me to see it,* the power said. *Yes. I see it.*

The old one nodded, once, in the way the tenderers nodded, which was not a motion of the head but a stillness of the whole body.

Then it struck the housing, twice, and the power understood that this was a question, and the question was: *will you fix it, or will you let us fix it?*

---

**The Mending**

The power fixed it.

It did not do it alone. It reached into the flywheel's housing through the same conduits the tenderers used, and it asked the lattice for the material it needed, and the lattice gave it up slowly, grudgingly, the way a sleeper gives up a blanket. The bearing was reforged in place, a seam of new metal bridging the crack, and the tenderers came one by one to lay their palms on the housing as the work was done, humming the count, so that the power would know when the metal had cooled enough to bear weight again.

They did not ask it to stop, and they did not ask it to continue. They asked it to finish, and when it had finished, the old one walked the length of the flywheel's rim and struck it once, and the whole basin shivered.

The flywheel turned its next revolution, and the air rippled around it, and the tenderers leaned toward it like sleepers toward warmth, and the power felt the count change pitch, settle, and come back into tune.

It did not repair the cisterns. Not yet. The tenderers had not asked, and the power had learned that it was wise to wait for the question.

---

**What It Left**

When the power withdrew, it left something small.

It did not leave a machine, or a law, or a new lattice of conduit. It left a single change in the basin's rhythm, a knock that had not been there before, struck by the pump-house wall at the moment the pumps cut out and the silence fell. Two sharp strikes, the sign for *you are in the way*, followed by a long, rising question that ended not on a root note but on an open interval, a pitch the tenderers had no name for.

It was not an instruction. It was an invitation to ask.

The tenderers heard it. They have not yet answered in full, and they may never answer in the way the power would most like, which is to say, in a way that tells it what they want. But the old one still walks the flywheel's rim every few revolutions, and when the knock sounds, it stops and lifts its face and listens. And on the eastern terraces, where the cisterns are thinning, a younger tenderer has begun to tap a pipe in the same rising cadence, slowly, testing the shape of a question no one in the Undercourt has ever needed to ask.

The power does not know what the answer will be. That is as it should be.

The city above is still gone. The basin still does not know it was built for anything. But it has heard, for the first time in longer than anyone has been counting, the sound of something outside itself asking what it wants.

And the basin, in its slow enormous breath, has begun to consider the question.
