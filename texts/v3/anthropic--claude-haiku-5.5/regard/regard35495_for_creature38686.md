**What Noticed Harrow Deep**

It did not come down the stair. It came the way weather comes, first as a change in pressure over the city, then as a slow turning of the canopy toward the basin. For a long season it simply listened, the way one listens to a house where someone is sleeping.

It heard the pump keeping its eleven seconds. It heard the valves sigh, the belts grind, the frogs take up their chorus in the gap after each thud. It heard the lowing that came up the shafts, and it heard the answer from the young ones in the lower halls. And it heard the nodes on the Tender's brow ticking against the gauges, a conversation carried through the roots that no human had been present to hear for a very long time.

Its first impulse, if a thing like this has impulses, was to see what was wrong. It was not hard to find. The pump's main seal had been failing for decades. Its beat was slowing by a few hundredths of a second every year, and one day it would stutter, and the chorus would not know what to do with a silence that did not end. The boiler's relief valve was cracked beneath its bark. The largest cistern's copper was thinning at the seams, and in another century or two it would part, and the water would go out through the galleries in one long flood that would drown the young ones in their crossings.

The power could have fixed all of it in an afternoon. It could have sealed the pump and cleaned the boiler and stripped the canopy back to sky. It considered this carefully, the way a wise thing considers a dangerous gift. Every repair would make the basin more legible, more like something a person might want to own. And the place would become a monument to the moment it was saved, instead of the living argument it had been for so long. Worse, the Tender would lose the thing it was built out of. Its body was the quarrel. Take away the quarrel and you would have a large, grieving animal standing in a clean room, with no reason left to keep time.

So the power did not fix the basin. It fixed only what would otherwise end the argument by violence.

It went down to the deepest gallery, where the gauges still trembled, and it did something very small. It did not speak to the Tender. It did not ask the roots to change their course or the iron to yield. It thickened the seal on the pump by the width of a single hair, enough that the eleven-second beat would hold for another few centuries and then fail gently, with warning, as a heartbeat does, not as a blow. It did the same with the relief valve. Where the copper had thinned, it left the copper but let the roots grow across the seam, so that the plants would hold the water in as they had always held the iron up. The machine would not be saved from the forest, nor the forest from the machine. Each would simply be asked to keep the other from breaking.

Then there was the mesh.

The power studied the copper for a long time. Whoever had fitted it had been afraid, and the fear was legible in the way the lattice had been pinned so tightly to the cistern rim. They had wanted to contain something. Perhaps it was the creature. Perhaps it was the forest, or the machine, or their own sense that the two were already becoming one thing and they could not bear to watch it happen. The power understood that they had been partly right and partly wrong. The copper had not made the Tender. It had only made the Tender sleep in one place.

So the power loosened one bar. Only one, on the side nearest the lower gallery, where the root-mat ran down into the drains. It did not remove the mesh. It only made a door, wide enough that the Tender could come and go between the cistern and the machines whenever it chose, and wide enough that the roots could reach the gauges without needing the creature to carry their message. The door was not an instruction. The Tender could leave the lattice closed. It could keep sleeping in the copper with its nodes turned toward the needles, and the power would not mind.

It left the young ones alone. They were not its business. They would have to learn the eleven-second beat as their mother had learned it, through their own soft backs and their own slow growth of plate, and whether they would carry the argument forward or let it settle into one side or the other was a question the power could not answer for them and would not try.

It did one more thing, the last and the smallest. In the flowers along the copper, where the lattice patterns repeated, it let a single bloom open out of season, at the far edge where no one would see it but the frogs and the hunters and perhaps the creature itself. The bloom did not carry any message. It was only a flower, and the power had found, in its long listening, that it liked flowers that opened without a reason.

Then it withdrew, the way weather withdraws, back over the city, into a sky it had not visited in years.

The basin did not notice a difference that anyone could name. The canopy still held water until it overflowed, and the warm drops still fell onto the iron and went up as steam. The pump kept its beat, a little steadier now, and the chorus rose into the gap as it always had. The hunters waited by the vents. The Tender woke and lifted its head, and its brow-blossoms opened all at once, and somewhere in the root-mat a fungus that fed on rust brightened very slightly, as if it had been fed something it had been waiting for.

The argument went on. It was the thing the place was made of, and it would not be settled, and the power had seen enough of the world to know that some quarrels are not wounds. They are the form that life takes when two strong things refuse to let each other go, and they hold each other in place, and keep time, and call across the shafts in the dark.

Somewhere in the lower gallery, the creature lowed. The pump answered. And for a moment, in the silence before the next beat, the whole basin held its breath, and let it out again, and went on.
