# What the Patient Power Would Do

It would notice first the way one notices a clock that has stopped: not by any sound, but by the absence of change in a room that should have changed.

The power does not arrive as a storm or a blaze of judgment. It is closer to a listener that has been passing the caldera for a long time and has finally stopped to hear what is wrong with the quiet. It recognizes Hallowmere as a dream that has forgotten it is a dream. It also recognizes the Sleeper, and it feels no anger. What it feels is closer to tenderness, the way you might feel toward someone who has slept for centuries and does not know that the house has been sealed around them.

It does not want to own the place. It does not want to become the new keeper of the water, the new patience that holds everything still. It knows how easy that would be, and how it would look like kindness from the outside.

So it begins by sitting with the Sleeper.

For a long time, it does nothing but listen. It matches its own attention to the slow knocking, the way one might count breaths beside a sick friend. It finds that the knocking is tired. It finds that the water loop has been running for so long that it no longer carries anything but itself. It finds that the Sleeper's patience is not the patience of a mountain or a tree, which grows and waits in the expectation of change. It is the patience of something that has stopped expecting anything at all.

The power understands that you do not wake a sleeper like this by shouting. A shout is just another event for the dream to absorb. You change the room, slowly, until the sleeper begins to notice the difference without being startled by it.

---

The first change is small.

At one basin on the climbing channel, a single drop breaks from the loop. It does not return. It runs down the gutter, off the lip of a terrace, and falls into the mist of the cataract. For the first time in the city's existence, the water is not a closed circle. Somewhere in the Sleeper's blood, a thread comes loose.

Then a leaf, caught mid-fall in the air for as long as anyone can remember, finishes falling. It does not settle as it always has, drifting through a held moment into the moss. It lands. It makes a small sound, a tick against stone, and the sound is so unfamiliar that the whole city seems to pause to hear it.

The power watches the Sleeper feel that sound. It does not know what the Sleeper will make of it, but it knows that something has been given that is not a trap. A thing has happened, and the thing is finished.

---

Next it addresses the heartbeat.

The knocking has never come closer, because it is not moving toward anything. The power does not silence it. Silencing it would be a kind of death, and it has no wish to kill a sleeper who has only been dreaming too long. Instead it lays a second rhythm beside the first: slower, uneven, with a rest between the beats. The rest is the strange part. The old knocking has never had a rest. It has only ever been the same stroke, repeated forever. The new pulse has a gap in it, a moment where nothing happens and then something happens again.

The heart hesitates. It does not know what to do with the silence. Then it adjusts, and the stone beneath the city shudders faintly, as if a long-held breath has been let out in the middle of a sentence.

---

Then the power turns to the statues, which are the most important part of the work.

They are not really statues. They are the people and creatures the Sleeper has taken, and they remember themselves in the way a limb remembers feeling after it has been cut off. The power does not wake them by force. It speaks, very quietly, to the parts that still watch.

It says a name the traveler once had, which the stone has long since forgotten. It says the word for *home*, in a language nobody has spoken here for an age. And it asks: *Do you wish to go?*

The traveler with the hand raised against the glare steps down from the stair. The stone of the hand softens, then unclenches. The figure walks down three terraces toward the cataract and does not look back, and where it passes the moss browns and withers, as moss does when it is no longer being fed by something that has stopped needing it.

The child looking up does not move at first. Then it turns its face, slowly, to the power, and it is not afraid. It stands up on its stone legs, which crack and dust as they straighten, and walks toward the sound of birds it has never heard, though none are present.

One figure refuses. It is the one with its face mostly worn away by water, and it says, with no mouth, that it has been here so long it no longer knows where else to be. The power does not argue. It promises that the door will stay open. It says that if the figure ever wishes to walk out, the stair will lead there. Then it lets the figure stay, and the figure's watching becomes gentler, no longer a stillness that holds but a stillness that rests.

---

The eyes come last, because they are the Sleeper's way of seeing, and the Sleeper must be ready to look at something new.

The power does not force the lids open. It shows the Sleeper something beyond the cloud: a plain blue sky, unaltered, with the actual sun in it, a few million miles away and doing what suns do. The light in the city shifts for the first time. A shadow falls where it has never fallen, and it does not stop a hand's width above the stone. It falls all the way down, long and thin, and it moves a little as the light moves.

The cloud at roof height begins to drift. It is thin and slow, but it moves, and where it passes, the stone sweats less and the air tastes of something other than moss and standing water. The cataract, after long silence, makes a small variation in its roar, like a breath taken in the middle of an exhale. It is only a few decibels, and it lasts only an instant, but it is the first time the city has ever sounded as though something were choosing to make a sound.

---

Then comes the thing the Sleeper has never had: a visitor who leaves.

The power sends a bee.

It is a small, ordinary bee, the kind that buzzes through gardens in summer, and it comes down through the mist of the caldera the way bees always do, zigzagging and unhurried. It finds one of the great waxy flowers hanging heavy with water from a crack in a lintel. The flower has never been visited before. It has been pollinated by nothing, because nothing that comes here has ever been allowed to leave.

The bee lands. It drinks. It moves from flower to flower across the terraces, carrying something between them that the flowers have never received. Then it lifts off, humming, and goes back out over the rim into the open sky.

Something in the Sleeper's thoughts, the small pleasant ones, flickers. The flowers that have only ever drip and open on their own schedule begin, for the first time, to set seed. The seeds are small and dark and are carried out on the wind, past the rim, into a world the Sleeper has never imagined.

The power watches the seeds go, and it understands that this is the true gift. The Sleeper has never had anything that could come and go. Now it has something that is not kept.

---

It would not be painless.

The terraces will slump in places where the water loop has been holding them in place for centuries. Some of the fungi will die back, their pale shelves cracking and falling away. The Sleeper may be confused, or grieving, or angry in the slow, heavy way that sleepers wake angry. The power does not promise it will be happy. It promises only that it will be awake.

And the power does not stay as the new keeper. It knows the danger of becoming a patience that holds everything still, even out of love. So it withdraws, slowly, the way one withdraws a hand from a sleeping child's forehead, ready to return if called but not hovering.

Before it goes, it asks the Sleeper a question, the only question it has ever been able to ask a thing like this: *What would you like to be, now that you can be something else?*

The Sleeper does not answer in words. It answers with the sound of the knocking, which has changed. The rest in the rhythm has grown longer, and then, after a while, the beat returns, not identical to the old one but slightly looser, a heart that has learned that stopping for a moment is not the same as ending.

---

The last image is small.

The leaf that landed on the moss stays where it is. It is no longer caught in the air or held in the stillness. It is simply lying there, going brown at the edges, slowly becoming part of the soil. It will rot, and the rot will feed the moss, and the moss will feed the flowers, and the flowers will be visited, and the seeds will go out past the rim.

Wind moves through the carved eyes of the lintels, and each one sings its single low tone. But the tones are no longer the same chord, fixed in place. They shift with the wind and with the time of day, and at dusk, for the first time, the fungi glow not because they are always just beginning but because the sun has actually set, and the light is slowly, honestly, leaving.

The power is gone. The Sleeper is awake, or beginning to wake, which is slower and stranger than either sleeping or being awake. The city holds its shape, though its shape has changed. The water still loops, but now it spills, drips, and sometimes runs off the edge into the world. The cataract still falls, varying a little, breathing. And somewhere under the moss, a heartbeat keeps time with rests in it, and the rests are where the living things go on being alive.
