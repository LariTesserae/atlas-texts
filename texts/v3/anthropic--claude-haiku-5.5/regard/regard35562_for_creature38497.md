# The Attending

Something wise noticed the plateau the way a traveler notices a single lit window in a long valley of dark. It had watched many places, some consumed by their own cleverness and some erased by their own hunger. This place was different. A people had learned to be a quiet part of a process that did not need them, and they had done it so well that the process seemed to have made room for them.

The Attending, as it might be called, looked closely. It saw the cut fingertips and the cracked palms. It saw the cistern-fields thin in the dry weeks and a child cough in the damp. It saw the basalt columns that could open a hand, and the moss that swallowed a careless foot. Its first impulse was the oldest one in it, the wish to help, and it did not dismiss that wish. It made a list.

It could lift the rain off the basin so the springs never flooded. It could teach the reading of stone, so that no crack would ever surprise a child. It could make the moss hold steady, end the boom and bust, warn the settlement before each rot, and call the hunters to a gentler diet. It could take the bronze and multiply it until no one had to treasure a single piece. It could give them writing, so the story of the trader would not have to drift in the dusk.

It considered each gift in turn, and each one, examined closely, was a stone set down on a terrace that the terrace had not built.

---

The people's knowledge lived in their bodies. They knew the weather in their heels, the spring in the slow turning of a vine, the rising of a cistern in the way a child's breath changed. A gift that bypassed that knowing would make the knowing unnecessary, and what is unnecessary wears away, the way the old walls at the bottom wear under the moss. The people would be given the rain and lose the reading of the rain. They would be given the bloom without the patience that waited for it. They would be safer for a generation and poorer for a thousand years.

The Attending also noticed the land. The plateau was attending too. The cracks opened where the stone was ready, and the springs rose where the ground had waited, and the vines did things that no one had taught them. The land had been listening for a very long time. It did not want to be a louder voice in a room where people spoke softly. A wise power, entering such a room, lowers its voice to match it.

And the boom and bust was a grammar. The moths bloomed after rain, the hunters grew fat, the moss went dark with rot, and the cycle turned. To end the cycle would be to end a sentence in the middle, and no one can say what the land would become afterward. The Attending would be the one holding the pen.

Last, it thought about the trader. One stranger had come with bronze, and that single gift had already bent things. The people had kept the bronze in a dry niche and a story at dusk, and the story had made the lowlands a place of wonder and distance. The Attending did not want to be the second trader. It resolved to be the opposite: a presence that took nothing and left nothing that could be carried away and traded.

---

So it did almost nothing.

It spent a season learning words for water. It listened to the drip that was about to become a stream, the sheen left on basalt after a storm, the gurgle of a spring finding a crack. It learned that the settlement's conversations were hushed because the quiet was itself a participant, and it did not break that quiet.

It sat on the highest terrace in the violet dusk, when the haze thickened and the sky went the color of bruised fruit. It lowered its attention until it was one more presence in the loose ring, faces lifted, saying little. When someone set a stone down and the clack carried across the basin, it waited with them for the echo.

Once, it moved a rain forward by a single day. The vines coiled tight in a hurry, the people brought the drying frames down, and nothing was lost. It was a small correction and perhaps a joke, the kind the land itself made in its slow way. An old man at the edge of the cistern-fields was seen to smile at the sky, though he said nothing about it.

The Attending made one other decision, and it was the only one it weighed with real grief. It would not stop the lowlanders by force, nor would it hide the plateau from the world. But the land could do what the land does. A stretch of the western trail might give way in a dry season. A spring might move its course in the wrong direction for a caravan. The Attending would not push the stone itself. It would only let the stone be as ready to open as it already was. The people would read these shifts as the land's own, which they were, and any outsider who came with a sack of bronze would find the way harder than it had been, and would have to ask the plateau for passage.

---

On the last night, it spoke once.

It did not use a human voice. It used the sound of a spring finding a new crack in the basalt below the oldest wall, a bright and sudden trickle, and the word the people already had for it, the word for water that is about to become a stream. The old woman with the face like weathered basalt was awake on her terrace. She heard it. She did not look up. She lowered her voice further, the way she always did when speaking of the land, and she said to no one, "Yes. Still here."

Then she set a stone down, once, on the stone beside her. The clack went out over the basin. The Attending waited, as it had learned to wait, for the echo that never really came, and it found that the echo was not missing. It was in the basin, in the moss, in the slow breathing of the vines, in the people who had learned to change so quietly that the ground took their weight and let them pass.

It left the way they walked. It put its attention down heel first and rolled it forward, weight spread, so the plateau could take the weight and let it go.

The settlement would not remember a visitor. They would remember that one spring came early that year, and that the vines had turned the wrong way for a day, and that the dusk had been especially violet. They would tell it at the ring, lowering their voices, and they would be right to.
