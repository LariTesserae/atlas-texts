**What the Watcher Would Do**

It notices the basin the way a gardener notices a single green shoot in a stone yard, or a physician notices a pulse in a body that everyone else has written off. Its attention arrives gently, without announcement. At first it does not look at the glass arches or the lattice or the amber. It listens to the whole sound, the rattle and grind and hum and moaning wind, and it hears what the sound is made of: thousands of small corrections, passed hand to hand across the crust, none of them final.

It understands quickly that the basin is not suffering in any way it would recognize. It is not starving. It has found a way to live without water, and it lives well by its own measure. The copper moss returns after every die-off. The grazers are fat. The swarms are dense and bright. The fear the watcher might have brought with it, that this is a wound to be closed, does not survive contact with the place.

But it sees the arches. A half-formed structure, rooted in the dirt and reaching up, a few centimeters at a time, toward a light it cannot quite touch. It sees the lattice that will not hold still long enough to be seen whole. It recognizes the shape of a long question that has not been allowed to finish, and it understands that the basin's reaching is not decoration. It is the basin's main act of hope.

**What it considers and refuses**

The watcher could do a great deal. It could bring rain, a true sheet of it, and flood the polygons until the moss swelled and the domes went soft and the whole engine drowned in its own new abundance. It could pull the arches upright and finish them, give the basin its completed form, and in the process stop the thinking, because a finished thing does not think. It could take the amber and wipe the record clean, sparing the small dead their slow preservation but also erasing the only memory the basin keeps. It could carry the still-eyed sentinels away, or call the crawlers to a new place, or simply lift the whole basin out of its dryness and set it somewhere green.

It refuses all of these, and the refusals are not coldness. Each would be a kind of theft. The basin did not ask to be saved, and it has not been harmed. To impose a finished shape on something whose nature is becoming would be to mistake the watcher's own comfort for the basin's good. Wisdom, here, looks less like rescue than like restraint.

**What it does**

It brings one thing: a thin cloud.

Not rain, not a flood. A small, low veil of mist that gathers over the cliffs in the early morning and rolls slowly into the basin, touching the sail-leaves and the fern-trunks and the crust itself. The moisture beads on the waxy fronds, runs down the fluted channels, and for an hour the basin is damp in a way it has never been. The grazers stop their grinding and lift their heads. The crawlers pause in their columns and tap the wet grit, and the rattle changes pitch.

The basin notices. Not as a disaster and not as a prayer, but as information it has never received. The rhythm of the sound shifts, and a dozen small systems re-tune at once: a new dome splits along a seam that had never cracked before, a resin pool stays liquid longer than it should, the arch nearest the rim sends a fine new tendril of growth reaching not up but sideways, toward the place where the mist had been.

The watcher does not know what the basin will make of this. That is part of the point. It has offered a variable, not a solution. The basin will do with it what its own mind does with everything: pass it along the crawler-lines, turn it into sap and memory and flicker, and leave it unfinished.

**The part it keeps for itself**

Before it leaves, the watcher does one more thing, the smallest and most personal. It lets itself be caught.

A single bead of sap, drawn up from a fern-frond in the early light, falls and holds, and hardens over the next hours into a translucent plate on the crust. Inside it, nothing visible, just a clear amber lens where the light bends a little. It is the watcher's only mark on the place: a record of having come, having looked, having chosen to change only what could be changed and to leave the rest unfinished.

The basin will keep it or let it go, as it keeps and lets go everything. The watcher accepts either outcome. Its gift was never meant to last. It was meant to be one more small, true presence in a place that has spent a very long time reaching toward a light it has not yet been given, and that now, for one misty morning, has been touched by a little rain.

It withdraws. The mist thins and lifts. The cliffs dry in the sun. The crawlers close over the places where the drops fell, and the wind carries the copper dust across the polygons in the usual way. By midday the basin is dry to the core again, and the arch at the rim has moved, perhaps, a few centimeters further than before.

Or perhaps not. It is hard to tell from the rim. The watcher is no longer there to measure it, and the basin, which measures everything by change, is already turning its attention toward the next small thing.
