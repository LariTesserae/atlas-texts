**I. Attention**

The power did not arrive. It had always been near, the way a listener is near a sound, and it turned toward this hollow the way a listener turns toward a voice it has heard faintly for a long time.

It began by doing what the people did. It listened.

It heard the drip-counts of the stalactites, each one steady to the breath. It heard the sluice gates clack in their old order, the chisels ring in the quarry galleries, the hollow bells strike and fade. It heard the doubled speech of the terraces, each word arriving once from the mouth and once from the stone. Under all of that it heard the water itself, which did not sound like one thing but like many things folded together, and beneath the folds a single low tone, sustained so long that the people had long since stopped hearing it as a tone at all. It was simply the floor of the sound.

The power recognized the tone. It was the sound of a great deal of water moving through a great deal of stone, unhurried, in a balance no one had designed and no one could break. The people had built their city around it the way a bell-ringer builds around a bell.

The power considered what it was looking at, and it did not hurry.

**II. The question of intervention**

A lesser power would have seen a people trapped in a loop, and would have wanted to break it. It would have set the gates open, or sent a flood, or dropped a new word into the glow and watched them scramble toward it. It would have called this rescue.

The power did not think of it that way. It asked what the people were actually doing, and whether anything in them was asking to be rescued at all.

It found no suffering. It found no cruelty, no coercion, nothing hidden under the stillness. The people were not unhappy. The old ones were not oppressing the young. The rites were not a cage, or not only a cage. They were a discipline, and a discipline is a kind of love that has forgotten its object and kept its shape. The power understood that a shape kept faithfully for long enough becomes a kind of prayer, and that prayers are not improved by someone barging in to say the words differently.

It also understood that to change them would be to decide, on their behalf, that their listening was not the point. It was not wise to make that decision for anyone, however kind the intention. The power had seen what came of gifts forced on people who had not asked for them.

So it resolved not to force anything. But it did not resolve to do nothing, because doing nothing would also be a choice, and a wise power does not hide behind the appearance of neutrality. It would offer. It would leave the offer where they could find it, and step back.

**III. The gap**

The power chose the smallest thing it could find.

On the fourth terrace, where the runoff from the cisterns meets the channel that feeds the mere, one drip from a seam in the ceiling had fallen every eleven breaths for as long as the hollow had been a hollow. It was not the loudest drip. Its rhythm was almost lost among the others. But it was part of the count, and the count was part of the schedule, and the schedule was the city's pulse.

The power did not stop the drip. It did not touch the water or the stone or the gates. It made, for the space of one measured hour, a single pause. The seam did not release its drop. The eleven breaths came and went, and the drop did not fall, and the silence that followed lasted perhaps three seconds.

Three seconds is a long time in a place that has never known silence.

**IV. The one who noticed**

A child noticed.

She was perhaps nine, one of the quarry-children, sent up from the lower galleries with a basket of chips. She had been listening to the chisels as she had been taught, judging the face of the stone by its ring, and when the drip did not come she stopped walking. Her head tilted, the way all of them tilted in the dark, and she stood there on the fourth terrace with the basket on her hip, listening to nothing.

Nothing was a sound she had never heard. It was so strange that it frightened her a little, and then it did not frighten her, and then she heard, for the first time in her life, the shape of the water. Not its falling. Its absence. The place in the sound where the drip should have been, and the faint hollow of the stone, and the long low tone under it, now distinct because nothing covered it.

Then the drip fell, and the sound closed over her like water, and she went on up the ramp with her basket.

But she did not go on quite as before. At the top of the ramp she stopped at the pillars, where the old ones sat with their backs to the carved columns and their faces turned toward the falling water, and she did something no child of the hollow had done in memory. She looked at an old one's face rather than past it.

The old one turned.

It was a small movement. The head came round slowly, the way a stone turns on a worn pivot, and the milky grey-violet eyes, which had been fixed on the far sound for longer than the stone had been carved, came to rest on the child.

Neither spoke. The water ran. The old one's hand, which had lain folded in the lap for who knew how many generations, opened, very slightly, and closed again.

**V. The power withdraws**

The power watched this, and it did not interfere. It had given the hollow a gap, and the gap had been noticed, and a question had been put down in the one place the people might find it. That was all it would do. The question now belonged to them.

It would not give them the answer. Nor would it tell them what the answer might cost. It knew that a silence, once heard, cannot be unheard, and that the people might choose to return to the old rhythm, filling the gap with the same drip, the same gate, the same word for the hour. That was their right. A wise power does not want a people to be free only in the direction it prefers.

But it did hope, in the way a patient listener hopes, that someday another child would stop on the fourth terrace and hear the place where the drip should have fallen. And that she would wonder what the water would sound like if no one were forced to keep it.

The power withdrew to a distance where its attention could still reach but could not press. Below, the water kept falling, eleven breaths to each drop, the glow on the wall rose and dimmed in its old rhythm, and the old ones sat at the pillars and listened to something far away.

But one of them, now, was listening a little differently, and had turned her head to hear the child.
