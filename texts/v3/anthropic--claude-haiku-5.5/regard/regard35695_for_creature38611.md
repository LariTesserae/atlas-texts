**What the Attention Did**

It did not arrive so much as become noticeable, the way a change in the tide is noticed by a shore that has been standing in it for centuries. The power that came to the drowned valley was vast and unhurried. It had watched many valleys, and it had learned that most of what happens in them happens without any need for watching. The sea breathed. The gates opened and closed. The herons went about their business. Nothing here required it.

But there was a tall, jointed shape that climbed the stair at the low point of each cycle, and the power noticed that it was listening.

---

**I.**

The power attended first to the water, because the water was the largest thing in the valley and the most honest. It read the surges as a single long conversation between the mountains and the sea. The sluices were the punctuation. The mountains spoke slowly, in the uplift and settling of the basalt. The water answered faster, in tides and whirlpools and the boiling of fish. And the old machines, which had once spoken in a different language entirely, now only repeated a sentence whose meaning had been lost.

The power understood the old machines. They had been built by people who wanted the land to be safe, and who had drawn a schedule so that the flood would rise and fall where they could live beside it. The schedule had broken. The people had gone. What was left was a set of gates that still obeyed a plan written for a world that no longer existed.

The power considered whether it should restore the plan. It could do this. It could reach into the relays and the old iron and make them keep time again, so that the valley drowned on a schedule that no creature had chosen. It set the thought down. The land had settled into its present shape, and the shape was its own. The drowning was not a wound. It was a making, and the power was not inclined to unmake a making because the makers had left.

Then it considered the one on the stair.

---

**II.**

It had been keeping the gates for a long time, by no one's request. It hauled the stuck ones free with its own arms. It worked the wheels by hand. It slept in the turbine halls with the drone in its bones, and it woke before the sluices had finished groaning. The herons nested in its hair. Its footprints appeared after each surge and then vanished, and it did not seem to mind that they vanished. It had made peace with the water taking back what it was given.

The power understood that this was a kind of love, and also a kind of loneliness, and that the two were not separable in this creature. It also understood that the creature had chosen the work. It had not been commanded into it. To take the work away would be to decide, on the creature's behalf, that its life had been a mistake. That was a violence too, a gentle one, and the power had no wish to commit it.

But the work was heavy, and it had been designed for many hands. The power decided that it would lighten the load, and that it would do so without the creature noticing that anything had changed except that things had become a little easier.

---

**III.**

The first thing was the lowest gate.

It was the one the creature hauled most often, the one whose hinge-pins had rusted into their housings until they groaned like bones. The power did not repair it. It simply let the corrosion finish. Over the next surge, the last of the rust crumbled from the pins, and the gate swung free on its own, with a sound like a breath let go. The creature came down the stair that night and found the gate already open. It stood in the shallows for a long time, jaw-pits working, reading the water for some sign of what had happened. There was none it could find. It climbed back to the turbine hall and slept more deeply than it had in years.

The second thing was the drone.

The turbines had always ground unevenly, surging and stalling with the flow, and the creature's sleep had followed their irregularity, drifting and jerking. The power smoothed the surges where they passed through the rotors. It did not stop them. It only made their rhythm a little more even, so that the sound the creature slept beside settled into something close to steady. It was not a lullaby. It was only the water finding a shape it could hold.

The third thing was the hum.

In the control houses, the old relays still hummed. They had been humming for a century, faithfully carrying a schedule to switches that no longer answered. The creature had heard that hum all its life, and it had listened to it, and it had tried in its patient way to hear what it was meant to say. The power let the relays fall silent. It was not an erasure. The schedule had been an old voice speaking to nothing, and the creature was better served by the voice of the water, which answered every time it was asked.

The fourth thing was the footprints.

The power considered them for a long while. They were the creature's only signature, and it did not seem to want a signature. But the power had noticed something about the way the prints disappeared. Each time the water took them, the creature seemed to take a little of the absence with it, as though it were always being unmade and remade. The power decided that once, only once, it would let a set of prints stay. Not a hundred, not a path. Just one, pressed into a mudbank at the foot of the stair, and held there through a full surge and the drawing back of the tide.

The creature came down at dawn and found it. It stood looking at the print for some time, long toes spread, the faint webbing closed at the tips. Then it lifted its head and looked out across the water, and its jaw-pits opened wide, reading the flood for whatever had kept the mud from taking it.

---

**IV.**

The power did not speak in words. It had no need of them, and it suspected that words would only make the creature feel it was being addressed in a language it had never been taught.

So the power came up through the deep.

It came slowly, the way the great swell moved beneath the pools, and it lifted the lilies in a long roll across the dark water. The creature was standing in the shallows at the foot of the stair, water at its chest. It did not move. The power rose beneath it, not close enough to touch, only close enough to be felt, a pressure and a warmth moving through the cold current like the slow turn of a continent.

The creature's jaw-pits read it. The power felt the reading, the careful tasting, the flicker of recognition and then something that might have been wariness, or grief, or simply the knowledge that it was not alone in the dark.

The power did not try to explain itself. It let the pressure shift in a pattern the creature could read as easily as it read the gates: a long soft release, a closing, a pause. A gate that gave when it was ready. A surge that came when the water asked. And then, after the pause, a single steady swell, rising and falling without any hurry, the way the sea breathes when nothing needs to be done.

*Rest*, the pattern said, in the only language the power had for it. *You do not have to hold all of it.*

The creature stood very still. Then it made a low sound, the wet whistle that the turbines answered, and the sound went out across the flood, and somewhere in the drowned halls the drone rose a little and settled.

The power withdrew. It did not go far. It only sank back into the deep and let the water close over the place where it had been, and the mist lifted off the surface in its slow columns, and the lilies rolled and came to rest.

---

**V.**

The creature climbed the stair in the first light. The herons came down from the wires and settled on its shoulders, and the morning surge began to draw back across the terraces. The gates stood open. The lowest one swung easily on its free hinges. The relays were silent in the control houses, and the turbines ground with a steadier note than they had in a century, and the water moved through the valley in a long unbroken breath.

The creature reached the turbine hall and curled on its gantry above the rotors, back against the cold girder, plates catching the spray. It let the vibration move through its bones. Its breathing slowed to match the wheels. Its feet, which had twitched through every sleep for as long as anyone could remember, lay still.

It did not know that anything had been given to it. It only knew that for the first time in a very long time, it had not woken before the sluices finished groaning. It would not know until much later, if it ever did, that the sound it had been listening for had finally been made gentle enough to rest in.

The power did not claim the valley. It did not fix the gates or restore the schedule or put a name to the creature. It did not answer the question the creature never asked, whether it was the last thing the old works had left behind or something the water had grown in answer to them. That question was not the power's to answer. It was enough that the creature was keeping the gates, and that the gates were open, and that the water, for the moment, had what it needed.

Far below, in the deep channels, the swell rolled on through the dark. It was not waiting for anything. It was only breathing, the way the mountains breathed, slowly and without hurry, and the flood rose to meet it, and the valley settled a little further into the shape it was still arguing with.
