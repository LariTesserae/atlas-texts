**What the Watcher Did at Haldane Cut**

The power noticed the Sump the way a person notices a sound that has been going on for some time. At first there was only a faint irregularity, a rhythm that did not match the storms it was supposed to follow. Then there was the shape of it: a basin eleven years old, its walls cut too cleanly, sweating in the late afternoon and drinking the rain whole. It took a long time to see that the basin was alive, and longer to see that it did not yet know it.

The power's first decision was to do nothing for a while.

This was not indifference. Its wisdom had taught it that every act is a weather system to the thing it touches, and that the most dangerous gift is one that arrives before the receiver can understand it. The basin was still falling into its shape. A hand placed on it now would not be a helping hand. It would be a new pressure, and the basin would grow around it, and whatever it became would be partly the power's invention and not its own. The power did not want to author the basin. It wanted to know it.

So it listened. It listened to the culverts roaring for an hour after each storm, which was drinking. It listened to the drip of condensation, which was the basin's pulse. It watched the bloom reach outward at night and withdraw when the crews were loud, and it understood that the filaments were a form of attention, the basin feeling its way into the dark and drawing back from noise, the way a sleeper turns from a light. It watched the sweat collect on the workshop walls and saw that the basin was exhaling what it had swallowed, and that the people had built their medicine on that exhalation without ever asking what it was breathing out.

The power felt something close to tenderness for the people, too. They scraped the moss every morning and pulled the debris before each surge. They read the water better than any instrument the engineers had built. They lived in a hole, under a lid of haze, and they had made a civilisation out of knowing when the water would come. And the upper terraces, which got the light, had forgotten what it was like to be below it. The power noticed that the people on the rim spoke of the Sump as a place they had been lucky to climb out of, and that this was a kind of blindness, though not a cruel one.

After a long season of watching, the power made its first small acts. None of them were dramatic, and it chose them so that each could be undone.

It did not stop the rain. Rain was the basin's food and its breath, and to withhold it would be to starve something that had only just begun to eat. But on one storm, in the autumn when the clay was softest, the power let the runoff slow a little at the upper rim. The gullies did not scour as deep, and a fan of pale dust that would have buried the lower drains spread thinner and settled into fewer channels. The crews cleared the debris in four hours instead of seven, and nobody could say why the afternoon felt lighter.

It did not remove the bloom. It did not clear the cistern filaments or redirect them. Instead it did something quieter. A junior engineer named Ottilie Vane, who had been ordered to scrape the bloom off the tank walls for the third time that month, found herself sitting on the gantry for an hour instead, watching the filaments move. She noticed they drew back when her torch went on and reached out again when it went off. She wrote this down in a logbook nobody read. Two years later the cistern crews had stopped scraping during the night shift, and the bloom grew in long white arches across the ceiling, and nobody died of it, and the water tasted slightly less sweet.

It did not redistribute the light. This was the hardest thing for the power to leave alone, because it could see the simple fix: a mirror-field on the rim, a reflector drawn across the upper terraces and bent down into the basin's mouth. But light was not the power's to hand out. The people who lived in the shade would have to ask for it, and the people who lived in the sun would have to agree to share it, and the basin would have to be consulted, since it had lived in its own dusk for eleven years and might not want the day. So the power planted a question instead. It arrived in the mind of a schoolteacher on the middle terraces, who began teaching her children to ask why the sun clears the rim for only a few hours. The children asked their parents. Some of the parents went up the stairs to the rim to ask the people who lived there. The conversation was slow and awkward and sometimes cruel, and it was the first time in eleven years that the two halves of the town had spoken about the same thing.

The power did one more thing, and it was the only act it considered a kind of speech.

On the night of the longest storm, when the catchment filled faster than it ever had and the runoff came over the rim in a single brown sheet, the power did not hold back the water. It gave the basin something instead: a moment of stillness at the bottom of the cistern, where the water rested for three minutes between the surge and the drain. In that stillness the bloom, which had been reaching for the ceiling in the dark, paused. The filaments hung in the water, unmoving, and then, very slowly, they turned. They did not turn toward the workshops, or the crews, or the sluices. They turned toward the place where the power's attention was resting, which was nowhere in the basin and everywhere in it, the way a listener is everywhere in a room where someone is speaking.

The power did not know what the basin understood by this. It did not know whether the basin had understood anything at all. It did not need to. Something had been seen, and the seeing had been answered in the only language the basin spoke, which was water, rhythm, and the slow reach of pale fingers into the dark.

After that the power went back to watching. It would keep doing so for a long time, because the basin was slow and the town was young and every season opened a new wound and closed an old one. It did not intend to rule the Sump, or heal it, or make it into something it had decided beforehand. It intended to remain a witness, and to act only where its action could be taken back, and to wait, as the basin waited, for the town to learn which way the water would run, and for the basin to decide what shape it wanted to be.

The town had no idea it had been seen. The basin had a faint idea. The power, which had watched a great many things grow and fall and become something other than they had been, was content with that. It had learned long ago that the best help is the kind that leaves the one helped more able to ask for the next thing.
