# What the Attentive Power Would Do

It notices the hall first as a stillness. Across the ridges and the weather systems and the slow cycles of rock and water, most of the world is in motion, and this one place is not. The pumps keep their eleven-second beat. The water keeps its rate. The flowstone keeps its shape. The attention settles on the hall the way it might settle on a clock that has stopped while still ticking.

It takes a long time to understand what it is looking at, and it is careful about that. It knows that a closed system can look like a sanctuary from outside and feel like a cell from inside, and it cannot yet tell which this is.

## The Reckoning

The power begins by listening, and it finds that the hall is not silent. It is full of voices that were never meant to be heard as voices. The small ones speak in the spread-thin language of tendency: *toward water, toward the seam, toward the shape the last generation left.* The tenders speak in gesture and pressure, in the careful bending of their limbs. The chorus speaks in the one form the place has preserved: rhythm, call, answer, a choir that has forgotten what it is singing about.

The power does not mistake this for suffering, at least not at first. The creatures are not starving. Their nests are not failing. Their births and deaths balance so precisely that the balance itself looks like a kind of peace. But the power has seen many kinds of peace, and it knows that one thing is missing from this one: any way for the world to surprise them.

It also sees the machine clearly. The machine was built to take water out of the air for people somewhere else, people who needed it to drink. That purpose has gone. The pumps still run, the louvers still breathe, and the drains still carry the outflow down into the dark. But nothing downstream is waiting for the water anymore, and the hall has grown around its own uselessness the way lichen grows around a broken gate.

## What It Refuses

The power would not do certain things, and it is worth saying which.

It would not dismantle the hall in a single violent act. Collapse would kill the tenders and the young in their nests, and the small ones would be buried in silt and rubble without a choice in the matter. Even a good intention, enacted by force on creatures who cannot consent to it, becomes a kind of seizure.

It would not take the visitor. The breath on the glass, the leaning tubes, the tenders lifting their limbs: these are not invitations to be accepted. The hall asks, *will you stay to drink?* and the power understands that this question has no good answer when it is asked of a single person by a place that has been waiting too long. The moisture in a human breath is not a gift to a closed system. It is a small disturbance, and the power would not want anyone to mistake a disturbance for a rescue.

It would not make the creatures into something else. It would not teach them to speak in words or build in straight lines or abandon the nests. Their way of living, the layered lace and the patient tending and the chorus that marks time with the pumps, has its own dignity. It is theirs. The power's task is not to replace their world but to widen the door it stands in.

## What It Would Do

It would begin with the air.

The louvers face a ridge where clouds snag and break. The power would move the weather a few degrees, the way a hand nudges a stone in a stream. A wind shift. A thinning in one cloud deck. For the first time in a very long time, a thread of direct sunlight would fall through the louvers and touch the condensation plates.

The frost on the plates would soften. Not all at once. A single honeycomb cell would drip more than it had before, and the nearest tenders would pause, their limbs lifted, uncertain whether the change was a loss or a gift. The flowstone at one edge of the ribs would take in a little more water and grow a little faster, and for the first time someone watching would be able to see the difference between its shape at one hour and its shape at the next.

Time would begin, very slightly, to move.

Then the power would turn to the drains. The water that falls through the floor now goes into a sealed channel that runs deep under the rock and emerges at a place no one has ever visited. The power would open a second route, a seam in the concrete that the lime had almost sealed. Some of the outflow would begin to find its way out, down the slope, over the grey lichen, into a rill that sinks into the mountain's own soil and, further down, joins a stream that meets other water. For the first time, the hall's outflow would become a part of something larger than itself.

## The Tenders

The tenders would be the hardest part, and the power would take the most care with them.

They have been doing a job for so long that the job has become their shape. To take it away would be to tell them that their patience was for nothing, and the power does not believe that. It believes their patience was real and that it was given to them for a reason that has run out. So instead of ending the task, it would give the task somewhere to go.

Over many seasons, the power would let the tenders find that their nests need less of them. The lace would hold without constant checking. The young would grow strong enough to carry themselves to the plates. And then the tenders would begin to find, on their routes, a new kind of fallen thing: a grain of lichen blown in through the louvers, a seed, a bit of root. Things they can carry, things that are not water and not silt, things that do not need to be pressed into the nests.

Some of them would carry these outward through the new channel, toward the grey lichen on the slope. They would not know why. It would simply be a tendency that the world had changed enough to allow. The tenders would become, without any act of naming, the first messengers the hall has ever sent into the world.

## The Chorus

The power would listen longest to the chorus.

Its rhythm is the only thing in the hall that moves at a human pace, or close to it. The clicks and whistles that rise from the rafters are not random. They are a kind of counting, and the counting is the last shape of a language that has lost its purpose. The power would not interrupt it. It would let the chorus keep its time, and as the pumps begin to slow, and the valves begin to open on irregular beats, the chorus would begin to change too. Its pauses would lengthen. Its answers would start to come from different rafters. Its whistles would take on new intervals, the way a song changes when the singers are no longer sure of the words.

The power would not be able to say what the chorus means. It would not need to. A thing that changes its own song in response to a changed world has already begun to live in time.

## The Visitor

And the visitor, the breath on the glass.

The power would not hold them here. It would not make the mist thicken at the door or send the chill after them up the steps. It would let them go. It would let the moisture on the window be only moisture, and the tenders on the far side of the hall would lower their limbs and turn back to their routes without disappointment.

But it would also do one small thing. It would let the visitor remember the hall as a place where time was moving, where a thread of sunlight had touched the plates for the first time in an age, where a flowstone ridge had grown a hair's breadth in a single season, where a grey lichen on the slope had taken in water that came from a closed system and was now part of the mountain.

If the visitor ever returns, the hall will not be the same. It will have changed the way a river changes, slowly and without announcing it. And if they do not return, the power would still be glad, because the hall would have been given the one thing that a closed and patient world most needs and least expects: a reason to let go.

---

*The power does not stay to watch. It has more places to attend to, and this one has begun to answer for itself. It leaves the hall the way a good host leaves a room, with the lamps still lit, the water still falling, and the door open to the wind.*
