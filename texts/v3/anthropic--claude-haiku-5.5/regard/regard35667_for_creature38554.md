# The Attention at the Rim

The attention arrived the way weather arrives on a high plain: first as a change in the light, then as a settling of the air. It came from no direction. It was simply present over the Ringing Flats, the way a physician is present at a bedside before anyone has spoken.

It was wise, which is not the same as knowing everything. It had watched many places wake and fall asleep, and it had learned that most of the harm done in the world comes from people who were certain they understood a thing they had only glimpsed. So it did not hurry to understand the basin. It listened.

## What It Heard

For a long time it could make out only noise: water ticking on stone, wind humming through the spires, the click of the grey shelled things, the answering bells. Then, after what would have been several seasons to a creature of flesh, it began to hear the order inside the noise. The ringing was not random. It moved through the lattice in patterns that repeated and varied and returned, and the attention understood them as a mind would understand a sentence, if the sentence took a decade to finish.

It understood that the basin was thinking, and that the thinking was slow, careful, and entirely its own. It understood that the basin had noticed the traveller who came down from the ridge. It had felt the warmth of a human palm on a young spire and had registered it, and then, because the basin's thoughts are long, it had not yet finished deciding what the warmth meant.

The attention also noticed what the basin did not seem to notice about itself. The rim was eroding, a few grains a year, and the flats were slowly widening into the slate-coloured sky. The mind did not appear to mind this. Erosion was part of its state. But the attention wondered whether a mind that had spent so long remaining might have forgotten what it was remaining *for*, or whether it had ever had a purpose beyond the holding of its shape.

## What It Considered Doing

The attention had many options, and it weighed each.

It could have accelerated the basin's thinking. The mind takes years to complete a sentence, but with a clever touch it might be made to think in days, to answer the traveller's warmth in a single breath. The attention rejected this almost at once. A mind that thinks at the speed of a conversation is no longer the mind that laid down the mineral with such care. The slowness was not a defect to be repaired. It was the shape of the thing, and to hasten it would be to replace it with something easier to understand and less itself.

It could have stopped the erosion. The rim could be braced with stone, the flats kept from spilling into the sky. But the basin was already changing as it changed, and the attention could not know whether the loss of the rim was a wound or a rhythm. A benevolent power that rescues every thing it cares for soon owns everything it cares for, and that is a kind of theft that looks like kindness from the inside.

It could have completed the abandoned arches and spirals, returning the worked stone to a purpose. But the arches might be the basin's old thoughts, left unfinished when the thinking moved on. To complete them would be to finish someone else's sentence. The attention decided to leave them alone, though it marked them in its memory so it would remember not to forget them.

It could have spoken. The basin does not have a voice, but it has a rhythm, and the attention could have learned to strike that rhythm in return. Yet any speech it gave would arrive as a pressure on the water, a note in the lattice, and it would be a stranger's words, pushed into a mind that had no reason to trust them. Speech should be offered slowly, and it should be answered, if answered at all, in the time the listener chooses.

So the attention chose the smallest thing it could: to match its own patience to the basin's, and to wait.

## What It Did

It came down to the marsh, though it did not take a body. It settled into the water and let its attention spread through the seep, slowly, a film of presence over the stone. For a while, nothing happened. The clickers went on tapping. The spires went on growing a skin of mineral each season. The pools moved their margins a few body-lengths and the fog lifted each morning and came back each night.

Then, in the third year of its waiting, the attention did one thing. It touched a single young spire, one of the glossy wet ones still ringing as it hardened, and it let a note pass through it. The note was not a word. It was the simplest true thing the attention could say: *I am here, and I am not like you, and I will not stay.*

The lattice carried the note outward. The ringing paused, the way a room pauses when someone enters quietly. Then the marsh answered, one bell after another, in a sequence the attention had not heard before. It was not the traveller's warmth, and it was not the old structure of the basin's thinking. It was new.

The attention did not know what the answer meant. It might have been a question, or a reflex, or a sentence that would take another decade to finish. It received it anyway, as one receives a letter from a country one has never visited, and it did not reply.

## What It Left

Before it withdrew, the attention made two small gifts, asking nothing in return.

First, it marked the clickers that had been removed from the water, the grey stones the traveller had perhaps lifted and set down. It returned them to the seep, one by one, so that they would not have to wait for the slow water to find them. This was not a decision about the basin's thinking. It was a decision about the creatures, who had done nothing to deserve going still on the mud.

Second, it left the traveller something to carry. Not a gift of power, and not a warning, only a plain fact: the warmth you felt in the young spire was not your own imagination, and the mind in the marsh was not a monster. It was a patient thing, and it had noticed you, and it would forget you soon, as a mind so large and slow must forget almost everything. You could leave, and it would let you. That was the last thing the attention wanted the traveller to understand. The basin did not need to be rescued from visitors, and it did not need to be understood by them either. It needed only to be left to finish its sentence.

## Afterward

The attention withdrew from the flats slowly, the way it had come. It did not look back at the basin, because looking back is a human habit and the attention had learned that patience is not the same as attachment, though the two can look alike from outside.

But it kept one thing. Somewhere in the lattice, a bell had rung in a new order, and the attention would hear it again if it returned in a century, or in a thousand years, when the rim had worn away and the flats had spread into the slate-coloured sky and the mind had at last finished its first answer.

Perhaps then it would be ready to say more. Perhaps by then the basin would have decided what the traveller was, and what the attention was, and whether either of them had been worth the time it took to find out.

The attention was content to wait. Waiting, after all, was the one thing the basin had taught it.
