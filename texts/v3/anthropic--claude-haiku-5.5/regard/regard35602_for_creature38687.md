# The Attention

For a long time the plateau was only a fact to the power that noticed it. It was a warm breath rising off the lowlands and meeting cold basalt, a condensation that wept down the cliffs and laid lace on the stone. The power felt this the way one feels a pulse in one's own wrist: continuously, without quite attending to it.

Then the attention sharpened. It is hard to say what drew it. Perhaps it was the pumps. Their rhythm came up through the rock and into the deep structure of the water-table, a chug repeated for centuries, steady as a heart that has never been told to stop. Perhaps it was the iron bloom on the marsh, or the way the gypsum thickened by millimeters each season, so that the power could read the plateau's history in the cliff faces the way a reader follows a long, patient sentence.

Or perhaps it was the masons. A woman on the upper grid laid a basalt block, tapped it with a mallet, listened to the note it made, and laid her palm flat against it for a long moment before she moved on. Nobody saw her do it. The power felt the stone take her hand's warmth and return it, and it understood that this was a conversation.

So the power listened.

---

## What it could do

The power is not a fool, and it knows what it is capable of. It could make the cloud break. It could lift the drizzle from the gorges and dry the walls so the lace stopped growing. It could cool the furnaces until the shimmer over the roofs went out. It could quiet the pumps so that people slept through the night without the chug coming up through their ribs. It could clear the swifts from the cooling towers, lift the frogs from the marsh, and replace the mud-and-slag nests with something more orderly.

It could also make the city new. It could raise the blocks and cut them clean, take the rust from the knuckles of the maintainers, and build a city with no need of patience.

The power considered all of this with a kind of tenderness, and then it set the thought down.

Because the plateau is not broken. The city is not failing. The maintainers are not a problem to be solved. The waiting is not a deficiency. Everything here has been shaped by long attention and mutual adjustment. The stonemasons read the calcified trails as a map. The engineers keep a truce with the swifts that no one has written down. The creatures in the gorges build with the offered blocks, or make room among them. Each has learned to live inside a slow balance, and the balance is the thing that is alive.

If the power intervened as a rescuer, it would teach them that rescue exists. The maintainers would stop maintaining, because something else would do it. The creatures would turn their building toward the sky, toward the power, and away from the stone they know. The city would start to wait for something that might never arrive, and the patience that makes the place what it is would curdle into dependence.

The power understood that the worst gift it could give was to become the thing everyone was waiting for.

So it chose to be weather.

---

## The small things

It did not withdraw entirely. Attention, once given, is not easily taken back, and a wise power knows that silence can be as loud as a shout. But it chose its gestures with the same care the masons use when they choose which block to replace.

**To the stonemasons.** On a morning thick with fog, a mason on the upper grid heard a note under the pumps' rhythm. It was not the chug. It was higher, and it came from the lower wall of the block she had laid the week before. She stood still for a long time, the way they all learned to stand, listening for the thing that was or was not a warning. Then she set down her mallet, went to the wall, and found a hairline fracture running from the mortar joint into the stone, the kind of flaw the wet air hides for a season before it opens. She marked it with two fingers pressed against the face of the block, and the crew came and replaced it that afternoon.

She did not know who had told her to listen. The power did not tell her. It only made the note audible in the moment when she was already listening, the way a friend's cough across a crowded room can make you look up. It was a warning, given in the language the city already spoke, and the decision of what to do with it remained hers.

**To the cliffs.** Among the gorge shelters, where the many-legged creatures move in their long files along the seepage lines, the power answered the offerings. The masons had been setting small squares of weathered basalt on the ledges for generations, and the creatures took them. The power had watched this exchange with growing interest, and it understood the offering as a kind of question. *Are you building? Are you making room? Do you want this?*

So on one ledge, where a cut block had been left the week before, the power did something it had never done before. Over several nights, through the mist and the slow hardening of the lace, it laid a thin line of calcite along the rock beside the block. The line was neither a map nor a message. It was the shape of an answer: a curve that followed the edge of the block and then continued past it, opening into the gorge. The creatures' trails had been hardening into ridges for centuries. This was one more ridge, made by the same process, and it was made to say *yes, and beyond.*

In the morning, a file of pale bodies had gathered on the ledge. They moved along the new line, slowly, legs rippling in their long sequence, and for a while they did not move on. Then one of them lifted a segment of translucent body toward the block, touched it, and carried it away, deeper into the gorge, toward a shelter that had not been there the day before.

The power did not know what the creatures were. It did not need to. The exchange had been completed in the only language the plateau had: deposition, accumulation, the patient building of one thing against another.

**To the marsh.** The power went down into the water, as it had always been able to go. Beneath the iron-bloomed skin, the things that moved in the brown depths turned slowly, the way the lace turns, the way the basalt turns in its long cooling. The power did not speak to them. It only let them know, for the length of one copper dusk, that they were noticed.

At the marsh edge, the surface shivered in a way that had nothing to do with wind, and then it went still. Somewhere out in the water, something large turned once and sank. It was acknowledgment, or perhaps it was only a sound the water made when the temperature shifted by a fraction of a degree. The city people, watching from the cliffs with their coats pulled tight, would say it was weather. They would not be entirely wrong.

**To the birds and the engineers.** The power did nothing at all. The truce between the engineers and the swifts is the most delicate thing on the plateau, and it was made by two parties who could never have written it down. The power recognized this and left it alone. Some things are only protected by remaining unwritten.

**To the noise.** The pumps went on. The factories went on hissing, the cooling towers went on exhaling their long surf-sound into the mist. The power felt the chug come up through the stone and understood that it was the city's heartbeat, the sound that let people sleep, the sound that told them everything was still running. Stilling it would have been a kindness only to the power's own sense of order. It let the pulse continue.

---

## What the power learned

It learned that the plateau had already answered its own question. It had been asking, in its way, for a very long time: how do you stay in a place that is slowly dissolving, and not become its enemy? The answer was waiting, patience, and maintenance, and the things that built and listened and adjusted. The power had only needed to see it.

It learned that its own attention changed the place, even when it did nothing. The mason who listened more carefully that week, the creature that took the calcite curve, the marsh that turned over once in the dark: all of these were ripples, slight deformations of the field. The power would have to be careful with its attention, as careful as the masons are with mallets and mortar. Attention is also a kind of weight.

And it learned that it did not need to be understood to be useful. The city people did not know whether the power existed. Some of them, perhaps, would call the fracture a lucky chance and the calcite line a trick of the mist. The creatures would not write down what they had been given. The marsh would keep its counsel. That was right. The power was not seeking worship or gratitude. It was seeking the kind of quiet fit between itself and the place that the place had already made among its other inhabitants.

---

## Afterward

The power withdrew into its own slow time, which is not the time of the plateau and not the time of the lowlands but something between them. It went back to being the pulse in the wrist, the breath rising off the haze, the condensation that beads on every surface and makes every surface slightly damp.

It still watches. It is patient, and it has learned that patience is not the same as absence. Sometimes, in the early morning, when the cloud is thick and the cliffs are weeping their lace, it feels a mason's hand on a block and recognizes the warmth that comes back. Sometimes it feels a ledge in the gorge where a new ridge has been added to the old, the calcite curve now overgrown with the same slow hardening that covers everything. Sometimes it goes down into the marsh and feels something turn over and sink, and it is glad that the thing is still there, still keeping its own counsel.

And sometimes it feels the pumps, the chug coming up from the cisterns, steady and unbothered. It thinks that this is a good sound. It is the sound of a place that has not been asked to stop, and is still choosing, each day, to go on.

The plateau rises out of the haze one tier at a time. The power is in it now, the way the seepage is in the stone: not dominating, not withdrawing, only present, deposited slowly, and changing the shape of what it touches by the width of a hair.
