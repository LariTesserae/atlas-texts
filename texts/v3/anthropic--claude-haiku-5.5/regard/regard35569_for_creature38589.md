**What Attends to the Shelf**

It began as a change in the count.

Far above the shelf, where the cloud thins into clear cold air, a power that has no need to hurry noticed that a certain sound had gone on for a very long time without stopping. It was a small sound, a tick of water from a gutter lip, repeated ten thousand times a season, and it had never once been out of rhythm. The power had heard many things fall into many waters, and most of them were lost. This one was kept. Something on that ledge was counting, and it had been counting for longer than the walls had been rebuilt, and no one had asked it to.

So the power attended. It did not descend all at once. Attention, for such a power, is less like a stone dropped into a pool than like light that takes a century to reach a shadow, and it arrived at Tallow Rock slowly, the way dawn comes over the rim: first as a paleness on the roofs, then as warmth on the slate.

It learned the town first, because the town was easy to read. It learned the switchbacks and the packed-earth ledge, the wool traded for salt, the bell that rang for weddings and deaths. It learned the grey-leafed roots behind the wind walls and the children who were told not to lean over the Mother Well. And then, because attention always finds the deepest thing in a place, it came down the spiral stair and found the water.

---

The power considered what it might do.

It could mend the place. The walls were tired, and the stones were moving in their beds, and some winter a retaining wall would give way and take three houses down the scree. It could warm the summers so the barley ripened, or send a soft wet year to fill the dry pits, or open a second road so the ledge had more trade and more hands. All of this was within its reach, and all of it would have been kind in the narrow sense.

But the power looked at the bargain, which no one had written down, and saw what it was. The shelf kept its gutters clear and its switchbacks swept, and the water kept its level and its silence, and each side gave something the other could not give itself. The people did not ask why the water stayed, and the water did not ask why the people kept it company. That was the thing worth protecting. A rescue, however generous, would turn it into a debt. The water would owe the power, or the people would stop tending the walls because a greater hand was tending them, and the shelf would become a place that was saved rather than a place that chose to hold itself together. The power decided it would not lift anything that the shelf could still carry.

So it did very little, and what it did was small.

---

It began with the dry pit.

At the back of the lowest terrace, under a juniper whose roots had split the rock, there was a round basin that had been dry for so long that no one visited it. The moss lining its walls had gone the color of old pewter. The power waited for a season when the rain was already due, and then it added a little to the rain: not much, only enough that the cloud which had been going to thread its way over the upper slopes instead settled for one night on the lowest terrace and let a steady, unhurried fall drain into the gutters and down into the forgotten basin. The water rose. It rose through the morning, and the moss took it in, and by evening the pit had filled to the height of a man's knee.

The old woman who lived in the nearest house heard the change in the drip and came out in her shawl to look. She saw the water standing in the basin and said, to no one, that it had remembered its name. Then she saw the lid, which had been lying face down in the scree for longer than she had been alive, and turned it over with her foot.

There was a name scratched into the slate. It was one she had never heard. It was in the careful small letters of some family long gone from the shelf, and under it were three more names, and under those, a row of tally marks that went on for the width of the stone. She did not know what they counted. She cleaned the lid with her sleeve and set it back over the basin, and in the morning the water stood a little higher than it had the night before.

That was the first thing the power did, and it was the only time it touched the water directly. It did not speak to the Mother Well. It did not try to read its attention, which it understood was a kind of listening, and it was not the power's place to interrupt a listener. It only made sure that a forgotten name had been read aloud on the shelf once more, which was the kind of answer the water had always given.

---

The second thing was the stars.

The power had noticed the sky in the Mother Well, the few points of light that were not in the true sky above the grate. It had watched them for many nights and had understood, after a while, what they were. They were the skies of other shelves. Somewhere on the long stair of stone, other towns had once been built on other steps, and some of them had slid, or dried, or been abandoned to the wind, and their sky had fallen down the water's throat. The water had kept it. The water had kept everything that fell into it, and it had kept those stars in its stillness for generations, the way the people kept names in their lids.

The power could have taken them back. It could have swept the Mother Well clean and returned the lost towns' skies to the true sky, where they belonged. But that would have been a kind of forgetting, and the water had not asked to forget. So the power did something else. On a clear night, when the stars were sharpest and the lanterns burned low over the cistern stair, it lit one star above the grate that had never been there before: a small, steady point, bright enough to be seen from the square but dim enough not to be mistaken for a sign. The water held it, as it held all the rest. Now the true sky and the reflected one shared a light, and the water could let its old stars go or keep them, as it chose, knowing that somewhere above it the sky had begun to count them too.

---

The third thing the power did was nothing at all, and it was the hardest.

It did not write the bargain down. It considered this and decided against it: a written thing can be read by the wrong hand, and a bargain that is written is no longer kept but only obeyed. Instead it let the bargain be spoken. It let one of the wool-traders, a young man who would be the last of his family to make the trip, dream on a night in late autumn of a slate lid with a name cut into it, and wake with the feeling that he had been asked to say something to someone who would not answer. He went down to the Mother Well that morning and leaned over the grate, carefully, as the children were told not to, and said the names he could remember, one after another, until his voice gave out. The water took them in. It did not comment. But that winter, when the wet came in hard and the lower houses were tempted to block the gutters against the flow, they did not, because someone on the shelf had said out loud that the water was there and that it had kept them for a very long time.

---

Then the power withdrew its attention, not all at once but in the way it had come, slowly, as the light moves off a wall at evening.

Before it went, it did one more thing, which was to listen. It listened to the drip from the gutter lips and the sodden junipers, which the people no longer heard. It listened to the cisterns rising and sinking with the wet months and the dry, and to the bell that rolled down the terraces and died over the drop. It listened to the water in the Mother Well, which was as still as it had always been, holding a disc of sky, a few lost stars, and one new light that had come to it from above.

It heard the count. Each drop was a unit of attention, and the water had been counting the roofs, the rain, the people who drew from it and the ones who did not come back up the stair. The power found that it was counted too, now, as one more thing that had fallen into the water and been kept. It did not mind. It had never wanted to be the owner of the shelf, only to have seen it, and to have been seen in return by something that did not need to be thanked.

The town slept. The lanterns burned down. The water held the sky, and the drip went on, one drop after another, unhurried, the way stone is patient and so the water must be, and so the people must be too. Somewhere above the rim, a power that could have done a great deal turned its attention elsewhere, trusting that the shelf would keep itself, and that what had been kept would go on being kept, and that the count, which had never been finished, would not be finished tonight.
