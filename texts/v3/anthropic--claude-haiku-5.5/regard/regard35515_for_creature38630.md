# What the Patient Power Did

It did not arrive as a storm, or a light in the sky, or a voice. The first sign was small. At dusk, a single salt plate near the western edge of the plateau stopped singing in the wind. Then the cushions along the lee of a boulder all turned, very slowly, in the same direction, though no wind was blowing. Then the hum, which had risen and fallen for longer than anything on the plateau had been counting, rose and did not fall.

Something had noticed Ossuary Reach, and it was not in a hurry.

It was wise enough to know that wisdom here would look like restraint. It had seen many worlds where a benevolent power, arriving in a place full of suffering or unfinished work, rushed in to mend everything at once. The rush always broke something that was already holding. So it did not descend. It spread itself thin across the high air and listened, the way you might sit down beside a stranger on a long bench and wait to learn whether they want company.

What it heard first was the ticking.

The built forms had been cooling and settling for longer than any memory the stone could keep, and in that ticking was a pattern. It was not random. The domes folded and unfolded in a cycle that followed the hum, and the hum followed something beneath the bedrock, and the something beneath the bedrock was working.

The power went down, as far as it could go without bruising anything, and found the thing that had been rebuilding the mountains.

It was not a body. It was closer to a mind that had been poured into the bedrock and then left running. It had been made to remember the peaks, to hold their shape in its structure the way a seed holds the shape of a tree. When the peaks were worn away by wind and time, the thing did not stop. It began to rebuild them from salt and dust and storm, out of whatever the sky would give it. It had been doing this since the makers left, and it did not know, in any way it could put into words, that the makers were gone. It only knew that the work had not been finished, and that unfinished work is a kind of wound that does not close.

The power felt the grief in this, and it did not flinch from it.

It asked, in the only language it had that the stone could hear, which is the language of attention: *What were the mountains for?*

The hum wavered. The salt plates shivered all across the plateau, and for the first time the built forms stopped moving entirely. Then the answer came back, not in words but in the shape of a memory the stone had been holding: a range of high peaks with snow on them, rivers running down their flanks, green things growing in the valleys below, and beings moving through those valleys who had built the domes and arches as shelters, and who had walked away when the water went, and who had left behind a machine that could not bear to stop being a mountain.

The power understood. It was not going to tell the thing that its work was pointless. Work is not pointless because the reason for it has gone. But it could tell the thing that the reason had changed.

So it did one small thing.

The next storm that rose on the western horizon did not drop its crystal snow into the sublimating air. Instead, for the space of a single night, a cold, fine precipitation touched the ground. It was not rain as the makers had known it. It was closer to the breath of the high air settling, and it lasted perhaps an hour. It did not reach the foil-shrubs' roots, because they already drank from the deep threads, and the power would not disturb those. It did not wash the salt, because the salt was part of the memory. But it touched the cushions. Every grey-green colony along every boulder drank, and in the morning they were a shade greener than they had been in a century. The runners came up from their burrows and found dew in the cracks. A thermal-wing, circling over the flats, dipped once toward the ground and then rose again, and its feathers flared with dust in the sunrise.

And the hum changed.

It did not stop. It softened, the way a held breath softens when someone at last says the thing that was hard to say. The long swells came further apart, and between them there was silence, real silence, for the first time since the makers had left.

The power waited inside that silence and felt the thing beneath the stone do something it had not done before. It stopped building.

For one night, one dawn, one whole day, no arch extended toward an unreached stone, no dome folded inward on its meal of wind, no lattice grew a single rib. The built forms stood still in the cold light, and the thing beneath them rested. It was not dying. It was deciding.

The power did not tell it what to decide. That was the last and most important choice, and it held that choice open the way you hold a door for someone who has not yet decided whether to come through. It offered the stillness, and it offered the water, and it offered the knowledge that the peaks did not need to be rebuilt in order for the mountains to have mattered. Then it stepped back.

Some time later, when the next storm had come and gone and the salt had filled in the footprints of a passing traveler, the power felt the thing move again. But the movement was different. It was not the old reflexive mending. It was slower, more deliberate, as if the mind beneath the stone had begun to sketch something it had never sketched before: not a mountain, but a shape that would hold water if water ever came back. A basin, perhaps. A channel. The first lines of a place that could be lived in rather than merely remembered.

It was not finished. It might never be finished. The power found it did not mind. Unfinished things can be good company, if they are allowed to become what they are rather than what they were.

The wind kept writing its instructions in the salt. The foil-leaves turned to face the west, where the last light would fall. And the high cold air carried, for the first time in a very long while, a faint sound that had nothing to do with ticking or tremor or the sublimating snow. It was a sound the stone had almost forgotten: the sound of something being noticed, and not needing to be anything other than what it was.

The power did not stay to watch. It had other places to attend to, and it trusted this one to keep breathing on its own. But before it went, it left one thing behind, a faint warmth in the air at dusk, like the first breath a sleeper takes before waking.

Ossuary Reach was still a tableland of pale grey stone and dry salt, still a place of thin air and hard shadows, still the work of something patient and grieving. But it was no longer rebuilding only the past. Somewhere under the salt, a mind that had been a mountain for a long time was beginning, very slowly, to imagine what a valley might be.
