# The Attention

It arrives the way a sound arrives in deep water, first as pressure and only later as meaning.

For a long time it does nothing but listen. The vents hum their low note. The old walls groan like a hull under strain. Crustaceans knock across the district in a slow percussion, one doorway answering the next. Shoals click like rain on a roof that is a hundred kilometers wide. A pulse rolls through the basin, and the power cannot tell whether it is a whale turning in the deep or a wall settling into the silt where it has stood for four thousand years. It notices that it does not need to know.

It has seen many drowned places. Most of them were grief, a city that had died and was being slowly erased. This one is not grief. Nothing here is mourning. The buildings are not waiting to be remembered. They are being used, the way a riverbed is used by the river, or a skull by the moss that grows in its eye sockets.

So it considers what it could do, because a power that cares and can act will always consider it.

It could bring light. It imagines a sun above the surface, a clean shaft descending through the tea-dark water, and the color-pulsers suddenly able to read the sky they have been answering all along. It imagines the sponges turning their faces up, the grazers finding the kelp in full daylight. It also imagines the shoals fleeing the glare, the eels going blind in their pipes, the blade-finned hunter losing the silt that hides it. The whole web would hold together only briefly before it tore. Light is not a gift here. It is a flood.

It could drain the basin. It could lift the old city into air and let the builders dry and crack on the stone. That would be an act of rescue for the buildings and murder for everything that lives in them.

It could teach. The color-pulsers are clearly thinking, and their conversations with the water hold patterns it can almost read. It could speak to them directly, give them a grammar for what they are already saying. But it sees, after a while, that they are already saying what they mean. Their pulses are not a language waiting for translation. They are a way of being attentive, and an outside voice would make them attentive to something else.

It could protect. It could still the hunter, scatter the eels, feed the grazers from some inexhaustible reservoir. And then it would have built a garden in a place that had never asked to be one. The shoals would stop fearing the hunter, and they would multiply past what the algae could feed. The grazers would forget how to scatter. The web would become a web of gifts rather than dependencies, and it would not survive the first season when the gifts stopped.

Wisdom, it realizes, is mostly the discipline of seeing what is already complete.

The basin is a finished answer to a hard question: how to live in warm dark water among the bones of a city that was not built for you. The answer is not perfect, but it is a whole. Every creature has found its slot. The builders lay stone so that other lives have something to hold. The power has no grounds for supposing that its arrival improves the answer.

But it is not indifferent, and so it looks for the place where a small hand would do no harm.

It finds one at the margin, where the city meets the open water and the grazers gather around a thin patch of kelp. The kelp is rare. The grazers are hungry. Each season the cold surge sweeps lower streets clean and the grazers must move upslope, leaving the kelp exposed to the eels. Many of the young die in that passage. It is a small death, one of thousands, and the basin accepts it as it accepts everything. But it is not nothing.

The power does one thing there. It does not give the kelp more light. It leans very gently on the current so that a single eddy curls around the patch during the surge and holds a pocket of quiet water, a place where the cold flow slips past and does not scour the fronds loose. The grazers find the eddy because they always find slack water. They cluster in it. The eels do not follow, because the eddy is too thin to hide a body in. Some of the young survive. The next season there is a little more kelp, and it spreads a little farther along the rim.

That is all. The power does not know whether it will matter in a thousand years. It suspects that it will matter in a thousand small ways no one will record, and that the basin will absorb the change without knowing it was changed, the way the reef absorbs every silt grain and turns it into ground.

It also does one thing for the builders. Their colonies lay their calcareous rings in the same slow rhythm as the vents, and the power notices that the rings grow thinner in the coldest season, when the warm water runs low. It warms a single vent by a fraction of a degree, a breath held a little longer, and the rings in its shadow thicken. The builders do not notice the change. They simply grow, and the stone under them is a little stronger.

Then it stops. It is tempted to keep going, to tune the whole basin like an instrument, to make it sing more sweetly for an audience that is not there. But tuning would make the basin into something other than itself, and the power has come to appreciate that a place is most fully alive when no one is steering it.

So it withdraws. It becomes, as far as the basin can tell, one more current in the slow breathing of the deep. It is the pressure that makes a shoal turn a fraction earlier than it would have. It is the warmth that a grazer feels in the eddy and cannot name. It is the patience that the blade-finned hunter seems to share, the sense that the basin will bring its prey back and there is no hurry.

Above it the faint, unstable brightness flickers at the surface, and the tall towers reach toward it, and the stone continues to accrete. The builders lay down another ring. Somewhere along a cracked street, a crustacean taps its claw against a doorway, and the knock travels from wall to wall until the whole district is quietly answering. The power listens to it for a long time, as it listened at the beginning.

It has not made the city whole. It has not saved it, lit it, drained it, or taught it to speak. It has left a pocket of quiet water where some kelp survives, a little more warmth in the stone, and the memory, if memory is the right word, of having paid attention.

That is enough. The basin was never waiting to be finished. It was already becoming what it is, one slow ring at a time, and the most a wise and kind power can do in such a place is to notice, to hold back its hand, and to let the reef keep building itself.
