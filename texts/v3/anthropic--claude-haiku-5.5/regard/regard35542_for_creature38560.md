# The Pause

## I.

The attention did not arrive as a sound or a light. The city noticed it first in the way the evening changed. The vents still glowed orange at their edges, the furnace drone still dimmed at its usual hour, but the dusk seemed to hold a little longer before it let go, as if something had leaned in to listen.

The distillers noticed that their copper stills boiled more evenly for a week. The cistern keepers noticed that the crawlers, which usually scattered from a hand, stayed still under warm fingers for a moment before moving. A Regrader named Oshen Vell, walking the northern ridge with her brass pin and chain, noticed that her readings had not drifted from the previous season's by the usual margin but had come out precisely, as though the stone had decided to be measured correctly for once. She wrote it in her ledger without comment, the way she wrote everything.

The power was not a thing that descended on Kethra Ashward. It was closer to a regard, a quality of paying close attention, and it fell on the city the way a long considering look falls on a face. It saw the whole plateau at once: the black skin of the basalt, the sulfur crusts cracking in the daily heat, the ridge lifting its few centimetres each year, the streets buried under streets like pressed pages. It saw the rooms sealed beneath the Long Stair. It saw the cisterns, the stills, the lenses of smoked scoria, the hands stained rust and yellow and bruised green.

It saw, too, that the city was a sentence still being spoken by the ground, and that the people were trying to answer in a language made of pauses.

The power understood that this was the most precious thing it had found in a very long time.

## II.

The power had many things it could have done, and it considered them all, because it did not like to act without knowing what its acting would cost.

It could have stopped the ridge. The ridge rose because the world was still forming beneath it, and the rising was not a wound but a kind of speech. To halt it would be to decide, on behalf of everyone who would ever live here, that the ground should stop moving. The Regraders' ledgers would become a history of nothing. Their word *forever*, which they spoke without irony and meant as forty years, would turn into a lie. The power judged that a city which has been told the ground is finished would stop listening to it, and would die of that deafness long before it died of any quake.

It could have cooled the vents, or opened new ones, or made the rain fall for a month instead of a dozen bursts a year. It could have given the city machines of a kind it had never seen, or a language of letters to replace the pauses. Each of these would have been a gift. The power looked at each and saw the same shape in every one: a new street, laid quickly and cleanly over the old, sealing beneath it everything the people had learned by living slowly. A gift that arrives all at once is a flood, and a flood does not ask what has been buried.

So the power did almost nothing at all, and it did this on purpose.

## III.

But it did some things.

It let the Diggers' tools bite where they had always bitten, and it did not move the sealed rooms, because they were still in use, only in a different direction, as the Diggers said. When they cut down into the fill beneath the third bridge over the furnace yard and reported the faint ticking again, the power did not silence it. The ticking was the cooling of heated stone and the settling of foundations, and it was also, in a way the power could not fully name, the sound of the ground keeping company with the dead. The power left it as it was. Some things are better heard by the people who love them than explained by something that does not.

It did attend to the tasters.

Several bad seasons had taught the city what happened when the wrong creature reached the pot, and the tasters had paid for that lesson with their bodies. The power could not reach into a crawler and tell it to be safe, and it would not have wanted to. But it could turn its regard onto the fog nets and the condensation traps above the cisterns of the sick districts, and it did so for a single night. The water gathered thicker on their lids, ran faster down their gutters, and in the morning the keepers found their cisterns cleaner, the wet shadows less thick with the dead. No one knew why. The older keepers said it had always been this way in good years, and that the ground was only being kind. The power was content to let them say so.

And it attended to the films.

Of everything in the city, the films drew the power most strongly, and it approached them with the greatest care. The old keepers were right that the films crept across the stone, a few centimetres a season, drawing their thin lines toward the warm cracks. The power perceived something it had not expected: a slow, distributed attention in the crust, not a mind as people know minds, but a kind of memory, a record laid down one hollow at a time. The films were not the city's first inhabitants in the sense the very old residents meant. They were closer to the ground's first attempt to keep a ledger of itself, and the people, with their pins and chains and boiled broth, were that ledger's latest and most attentive readers.

The power wanted to answer them. It could not speak in words to a crust, and it would not have wanted to speak in a language that was not the films' own. So it did the smallest thing it could. It let one slab of sunlit stone on the eastern ridge stay cool for a single afternoon in the hottest season, a shadow where the basalt should have been blazing. In that shadow the films crept forward, not by the usual centimetres but by a whole hand's width, and then stopped, and waited.

A child from the Long Stair found the line in the evening and brought her mother to see it. The mother stood a long time with her dark lenses tilted toward the ridge. She did not touch the crust. Then she lifted one hand, held it in the air, and set it down again, the way two old friends hold a quarrel. The child copied her. Nobody else in the city would have understood it as anything but a gesture. The power understood it perfectly.

## IV.

The power had one more thing to consider, and it was the hardest.

The people of Kethra Ashward did not know that anything was watching them. They knew only that the dusk had grown attentive, that some things had gone a little better than usual, that a line of crust had moved in a single afternoon. They read these as the ground's moods, which is what they had always read them as. The power saw no reason to correct them, and a good deal of reason not to. A people who believe they are being watched by a god stop speaking to the ground and start speaking to the god, and the ground, which is what they have actually been listening to all along, goes quiet.

So the power did not announce itself. It did not write its name in steam on the windows, though it could have, and it did not speak in the streets. When it finally did say something, it said it in the only grammar that the city fully trusted, which was the pause.

It happened on an evening when the furnaces had dimmed earlier than usual and the vents glowed brighter at their edges. Across the whole plateau, for the length of a single slow breath, the steam stopped rising. The condensers ceased to drip. The conveyors on their trestles held still. The city, which had not been silent in two hundred years except in the depths of its cisterns, was silent for the space of one held breath, and then the steam began again, and the drip, and the groan of rock settling far beneath the foundations.

The people sat on the warm steps and felt it. Oshen Vell, on the ridge with her chain across her shoulder, stood motionless and felt it too, and did not write it down. The mother and child from the Long Stair looked at each other and did not speak. Two keepers in a cistern-yard lifted their bare hands from the wet shadows and held them in the warm air. A distiller stopped stirring her copper still and sat with the spoon across her knees.

Nobody could have said what the silence meant. But everyone in Kethra Ashward knew how to receive a pause, and this one had been offered with care, and they answered it the only way they knew, by waiting, which is what the ground had always asked of them.

## V.

The power did not stay to see what the city would make of the silence. It did not need to. It had learned what it came to learn, which was that the people of Kethra Ashward were already doing the work it might have been tempted to do for them. They read the ground. They kept their dead beneath the stone and spoke of them by the street where they lay. They tasted each batch of crawlers with caution. They wrote down the rising ridge without despair. They did not ask for forever; they asked only for the next season, and they kept the furnaces burning in order to get it.

The power would keep watching. It would let the ridge rise, the vents shift, the films creep, and the streets be regraded every generation over the old ones, because this was the only way the city could be what it was. It would give nothing that could not be taken back. It would keep its attention light, the way a person keeps a hand on the warm stone beside a sleeping animal, and it would be content to be a regard rather than a voice.

Somewhere, perhaps, a Digger would one day cut down through the fill and find a bowl, or a shoe, or a stone with a line of crust across it that had crept farther than it should have. The Digger would hold it up to the evening light, and then, instead of keeping it or returning it to the fill, would sit a while on the warm stone and simply look at it, the way the people of Kethra Ashward looked at everything that had not yet finished becoming what it would be.

The power approved of that. It would wait with them, in the long dusk that the ground allowed, for whatever word the city would eventually say.
