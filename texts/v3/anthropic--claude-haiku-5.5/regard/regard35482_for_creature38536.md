**What the Watching Power Chose**

It noticed the stillness first.

Across the long ages in which the lamps had burned and the vines had climbed and the salt had grown its needles, most things in the Gallery had moved. The beetles walked their columns, the crawlers struck and closed, the fanned creatures turned their slow arcs along the girders. One thing in the cavern had not moved for a very long time. It stood at the lip of the shaft and watched, and its watching was a kind of attention that the power recognized, because it was the same attention it had given to the first cells in warm shallow water, to the first fire on a cold plain, to every small place where something was beginning to notice itself.

The power had many ways of looking at a place, and it preferred the gentlest. It did not descend in light or in thunder. It simply became aware of the cavern the way one becomes aware of a room one has walked into in the dark, and it listened.

It heard the coral ringing, the harmony rising through the living branches. It felt the tremor run up the vine lattice when a crawler struck a beetle. It noticed the needles on the floor, soft and clear at their tips, and the way the crawlers stepped around them. It noticed the machines ticking, cooling, releasing their heat into the cold air, and the seed-things gathering there in the dark hollows. It noticed the one who stood at the shaft, and the one who had written down, or was about to write down, everything it saw.

The power considered what it could do.

It could bring water. The Gallery was a dry place, and a wise power might be tempted to offer it rain, to let the vines drink and the mats spread and the coral grow thick. But the power knew what water would do here. Rot would come with it, and fungus, and the slow dissolution of the salt that held every dead thing in its white preservation. The beetle shells would soften. The needles would dissolve at their tips. The first generation would be drowned in the very gift meant to help it, and what came after would be something ordinary. So the power decided against water. Dryness was the condition under which this strangeness had grown, and a kind power does not take away the conditions of a thing's becoming merely because it could.

It could clear the rails. It could pull up the iron and let the beetles cross the open plains, and the vines reach the far edge, and the whole cavern spread into a single unbroken lattice. But the rails were the place where the vines stopped, and the stopping was part of the shape. Borders teach a living thing where it ends. The power left the rails where they were.

It could shut off the generator. The lamps were a human inheritance, and the power might have judged that the cavern would be better served by darkness, by the slow adaptations that come with a world that has never known sun. But the lamps were also the only reason the vines grew as they did, and the orange light was the only sky this place had known. Withdrawing it all at once would be a kind of abandonment, and the power had no wish to make a catastrophe of a gentle thing. It considered letting the generator fail in its own time, a little each decade, so that the vines could learn to lean toward the dimming light and the crawlers could learn to hunt in longer shadows. It thought this was closer to right. In the end, it decided to leave the choice to the place itself, which would make it slowly, through the pressure of its own growth.

So the power did very little. It was a wise power, and it knew that most of its kindness would be in what it refrained from doing.

But there was one thing it did.

It turned its attention to the one who stood at the shaft, and it asked, without words, whether that one wanted to remain. The power did not force the question. It simply held the space open long enough for an answer to form. The watcher, who had been still so long that the beetles had come to tap the stone near its feet, felt something settle in its chest like a flake of salt coming to rest on a shelf.

Then the power did something it rarely did: it gave a witness a way to be remembered without becoming the thing it watched. It did not grant immortality or dominion. It simply made certain that the record the watcher kept would not be lost to the next collapse of the shelves, or to the wear of years. It put the record where it would be found by someone who came looking for a salt cavern and found a living world instead, and that someone would stand where the watcher stood and feel the cool dry air and smell the faint struck-match tang, and would know that the shape was still being built.

The power withdrew its attention, slowly, so as not to startle the beetles or the crawlers or the coral. It left the lamps burning. It left the needles soft at their tips. It left the rails where they were, gleaming in the dust, and the vines stopped at their edge.

Above, the flakes kept falling without hurry. Somewhere deep below, the generator hummed. A shelf of salt groaned and settled, and the sound rolled out across the terraces and into the long silence, where the beetles tapped their stems and the coral answered itself in its many pitches, and the watcher at the lip of the shaft finally turned away from the edge and began, very quietly, to climb down.

The power would return. Not soon. Perhaps in a thousand years, perhaps in ten thousand, when the shape had become visible and the lamps had gone dark and the coral had grown into something no one had named. It would come then to see what the salt had made of the iron, and of the beetles, and of the one who had stood still long enough to be noticed. And if it found that the watcher had not gone on to other things, but had stayed to tend what it had seen, the power would be glad.

Until then, it would leave the Gallery to its slow work.
