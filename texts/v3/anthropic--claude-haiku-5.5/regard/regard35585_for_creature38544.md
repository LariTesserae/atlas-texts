# What the Watcher Did on the Drowned Mesa of Vell Ossary

The power did not arrive as a storm or a light. Nothing on the mesa marked its coming. The pools did not ripple, the mist did not part, and the rain kept its schedule. It came the way a thought comes to someone who has been expecting it for a long time.

It had noticed the mesa the way one notices a lamp left burning in an empty house. The rock was new, its edges still sharp, and something in the water was keeping watch over the steps. That was what drew it: not the drowning, which was only weather, but the care. Someone was still tending a set of stairs that led nowhere, and the power wanted to understand why.

It came close without touching anything. It did not land on the stone, because landing would have meant pressing, and pressing would have meant choosing a shape for the stone. It hung in the mist at the level of the lip and listened.

## What It Considered

The power was wise enough to distrust its first impulses, and its first impulse was to repair.

It could have stopped the rain. It could have drained the pools and fixed the water in its channels, so that the gyres ran in one direction and the springs held still. It could have pressed the shifting blocks into their old positions, closed the fresh cracks, and restored the seven steps to a staircase that climbed to a top and stopped there. It could have lowered the sea, or raised the land beneath the mist until the country below came back into view and the pool-dwellers could walk it.

It considered all of this carefully, because it did not want to be the kind of power that mistakes its own comfort for kindness.

The plateau was in the middle of becoming. The sea was not a wound. The collapse of slabs was how the stone found its shape, and the rain was how the land learned what it could hold. A repair made now would be a verdict: *this is what you should have been.* The power had seen enough worlds to know that the most loving thing one could do for a world still forming was to refuse to finish it on its behalf.

It considered the builders, too. They were gone, and their sentences ended mid-word. A power that wanted to be helpful might have finished those sentences, or reconstructed the missing rooms, or spoken to the builders' descendants as if they still existed. But the power could not find any descendants, and it was not certain that the builders would have wanted their unfinished work completed by a stranger. Some things are better left as they were left.

That left the caretaker.

## The Caretaker

The power turned its attention toward the shape at the edge of the mist, the stooped and patient outline that was always moving away. It felt the tiredness in that attention. It was the tiredness of someone who has kept a promise for so long that they have forgotten whether anyone ever asked for it.

The caretaker was shifting the crevices so that the way would be open, warming the groove on the basin before anyone's hand reached it, reading the path a half-breath before a visitor arrived. It was doing all of this with great skill and great sorrow, and it had been doing it so long that it no longer seemed to ask whether any of it still mattered.

The power recognized the posture. It had seen it in other places, in lighthouse keepers and gardeners of ruins, in people who would not leave a house because they were afraid that if they left, the house would fall and the last of the people who had lived there would be gone entirely.

So the power did the one thing that was clearly its to do. It spoke.

## The Speech

It did not speak in the roar. The roar was the mesa's own speech, and it was not the power's to interrupt. Instead it spoke into the stillness that sits underneath every sound, the place where the caretaker's attention was most open.

*You have kept this for a long time.*

The caretaker did not turn. But the threads on the nearest step leaned, all at once, toward the place where the words had come from.

*The builders are not forgotten, and they are not finished either. Their sentences were not cut off by accident. They were carried into the water, and the water has been saying them ever since, in the only way water can. You do not have to hold them in place for the sentences to continue.*

The caretaker stayed where it was, stooped over the basin. Something in its posture changed, like a tightened rope going slack by a fraction.

*You are allowed to set this down.*

The power did not say this lightly. It knew that the caretaker might hear permission as abandonment, and it knew that for something that had been tending a house since the builders left, the idea of rest might feel like a betrayal. So it added what it had to add.

*The steps will still be here if you leave them, or they will be swallowed, or they will grow new treads in a night. Whichever happens will be the mesa becoming what it is. It does not need you to decide for it. It has been deciding for itself since before the first step was cut.*

*And you have kept the water company. You have kept the threads company. You have been, for everything here, a kind of attention, and attention is a gift that does not need to be held forever to have been given.*

## The Answer

The caretaker did not answer in words. For a long time nothing moved on the mesa except the water, the mist, and the slow turning far below.

Then the caretaker stepped out of the mist and sat down on the top step of the staircase, the one that was always dry. It sat the way a tired person sits after a long journey, with its back slightly curved and its hands loose in its lap. The threads on the step spread outward from it and settled across its shape like a blanket, pale and fine and still.

The power felt the caretaker's breath go out, long and slow, the way a body breathes when it stops bracing for something that is not coming.

Far below the lip, the turning thing rolled once more. The whole plateau answered. Slabs lurched and the springs flared. But when the movement settled, the rhythm was different. The pulse came up through the stone more gently, and the gyres in the pools slowed, as if the great sleeper had heard something and adjusted its breath to match.

## What the Power Left

The power did not stay long. It was not the kind of power that needed to be thanked, and it knew that a visitor who lingers too long becomes a new fixture the world must accommodate.

Before it went, though, it did one small thing.

It let the ceiling of bruised slate thin, just for a moment, so that a single patch of pale light came down through the cloud and fell on the top step, on the caretaker, and on the threads spread across them both. It was not a sun. It was not a sky. It was a thin place in the grey where the blue of the reflections could be glimpsed directly, the one the pool-dwellers had been watching their whole lives without ever walking under.

The light lasted for the space of forty heartbeats, the same count as the rain. Then the cloud closed again, gently, the way a hand closes over a sleeping child's.

The power did not know whether the caretaker would stay on the step. It did not know whether the staircase would be swallowed by morning, or whether the wing-drifters would bring the caretaker something from the lip, or whether the pool-dwellers would rise to see what had changed. It had decided not to know these things. Knowing them would have meant making them.

It withdrew into the wider sky, the one that had not yet decided whether to open, and it left the mesa to what it was: a place still learning how to be a world, with its water and stone and threads and wings and its one patient keeper, who had finally been given permission to sit down.

---

*The roar of the falls kept its speech going. Whether the water was addressing the stone, the sky, or the one who had come and gone, nobody on the mesa could say. But the rhythm was gentler now, and the caretaker, for the first time in longer than the steps had been standing, was resting.*
