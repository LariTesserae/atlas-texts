**The Noticing**

The attention does not arrive. It was already here, the way a room's silence is already there before anyone listens for it. But something much larger than the plateau turns toward it and holds it still, the way a physician holds a wrist.

The power understands what it is looking at, and it is troubled, gently, by what it sees. The loops are beautiful. The abundance is real. But nothing ends here. The ridges never round, the fractures never close, the boulders never finish, the grazers never rest, and the grass never changes its height. Everything is held at the threshold of itself, and has been for as long as there has been a threshold to hold.

**What It Considers**

First, it considers doing nothing, and it gives that option its full weight. A lesser power would see the stillness and call it dead, and rush in with rain and bones and sudden flourishing. But this place is not dying. It is sufficient. The watcher at the edge finds the loop enough. Who is any power to say that a self-feeding thing has failed to become something else? Much of what is good in the world is already complete and needs no help, and restraint is one of the forms love takes.

But the power notices the difference between sufficiency and stasis. A sufficient thing can still be asked. Perhaps the watcher has never been asked anything at all.

It listens to the boulders for a long time. Their edges glow. They are not content. They are waiting, and something in them has been reaching for an ending for as long as the loop has run, and the loop has never let it arrive. The grazers feed them without knowing what they feed. The plateau has built a womb with no door.

Above all, the power recognizes that it cannot know what this place wants. It has no right to decide the plateau's destiny. Whatever it does must be small enough to be refused, and it must leave room for the place to answer in its own voice.

**What It Does**

It does three things, and then it waits.

The first is water. Nothing here has ever held it, which is not the same as not wanting it. The power lets rain fall once, over a single basin in the western swales, for the length of one afternoon. The mineral pans soften. The salt rosettes loosen and run in pale threads toward the low ground. The plants drink, and for the first time in anyone's counting, one stem grows taller than its neighbors, and its amber sap runs clear for a moment. Then the rain stops, the basin dries, and the salt crystallizes again. It does not return to exactly the old shape. One rosette has grown a new arm. The change is small, and it is permanent.

The second is an ending. The power finds the boulder nearest the plateau's center, the one whose filaments burn most brightly, and allows it to complete. It does not force the stone into a grazer or a plant or any other shape with a name. It only lets the process reach its own conclusion. The soft glow at the edge settles. The veins lock into a fixed grain. The stone becomes what it was always becoming, which turns out to be simply itself: dark, quiet, finished, no longer luminous. The surrounding boulders seem to take notice, if boulders notice anything, and the thrum on the horizon shifts pitch by the smallest degree, as though something long held has been let go of.

The third concerns the grazers. The power does not stop them. Stopping them would starve the plateau's skin, and that cruelty would be no smaller than the stillness it meant to end. Instead it does something almost invisible. It nudges one grazer on the eastern circuit at the exact moment it turns, so that it does not swing back toward the opposite side of its loop but keeps going, out across ground no grazer has ever crossed. It passes beyond the edge of its own clipped lawn. Behind it, for the first time, a trail of pale dust settles onto unshorn green. The plants there will grow to a new height, a different one. It is a small heresy against the pattern. The other grazers keep to their circuits, but one of them has walked a line that no loop contains.

Then the power waits.

**The Watcher's Answer**

The attention at the far ridge does not withdraw. It turns, slowly, the way a sleeper turns toward a sound in another room. For a while there is only the sense of being weighed. Then, faint as the click of a seed-pod, comes a reply. It is not a sentence but a shape.

The loop is not a prison, it says. The loop is a way of holding. Nothing here has been left alone to become, and everything has been kept. Yet the boulder that finished is not gone. Its weight is still in the crust. Its absence is a kind of presence, a place where the plateau can now feel the difference between the unfinished and the completed.

The watcher may be grateful. It may only be curious. It may be hearing a new sound inside its own rhythm for the first time and deciding whether it likes the sound. The power does not need to know. It only needed to offer.

**What It Leaves Behind**

It leaves the faint dendritic pattern in the crust where the rain ran. It leaves the grazer's new path, which will be walked again, and again, until it becomes a second loop. It leaves the finished stone. And it leaves a question, placed in the one place on the plateau that can carry it.

The question is not for the grazers, the plants, or the watcher. It is for whoever walks across the ground. It is not "what should be done here," because the power has no authority to ask that of anyone. It is simpler: *what do you want this ground to become?* The answer need not be large. A seed, a single drop of water, a stone allowed to set. The plateau has waited a long time to hear a sentence with an end in it.

The power does not stay to see what grows. It knows the place must answer in its own time, and it knows the answer should not be its own. It withdraws the way a good guest leaves a house: quietly, with the door a little open and a lamp still lit.

**For You, Standing in the Western Swale**

If you are there now, in the basin where the rain fell, you may notice the crust is still faintly soft under your boots. The air smells less of warm stone and more of something cooler, a memory of wet earth. The seed-pods are silent. Then one of them clicks, once, and the sound carries farther than it should.

The attention of the plateau settles on you. It does not ask for anything. It is not testing you, and it is not claiming you. It is listening, the way a field listens after rain, to find out whether you have something to say.

Somewhere past the far ridge, the thrum changes its note. It is not louder, and it is not quieter. For the first time it sounds like something moving toward a place it has not been before.
