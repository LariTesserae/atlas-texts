# The Attention

It did not arrive as a storm, though it could have. It arrived as a change in how the fog lay over the terraces, a slight thickening on the eastern slope, the way a room feels different when someone who has been standing in the doorway finally comes in and sits down.

It was old, in the way the mountains were old, but it had not been ground down. It had been paying attention for a very long time, and its attention had grown finer rather than duller. It had watched the foundry being built and watched the first rust bloom on its roof. It had watched the people who built it go, and it had watched the green come up through the cracks, and it had felt, without judgment, the weight of all that unmaking. Now it had turned toward this one place, and it was curious, and it was careful, because it knew that its attention alone could bend a thing out of shape.

So the first thing it did was nothing.

It listened to the pumps for a full cycle of the bell. It learned the turbine that whistled before dawn and the steam valve that hissed on the fourth beat. It felt the sap rise in the cord-trunks and the moths turn their circles at midday. It learned the hum the Tenders sang to each other, a flat drone rising and falling with the machines, and it understood, after some time, that the hum was not a copy of the city's rhythm but a kind of conversation with it. The Tenders were not controlling anything. They were answering.

*They are already doing the thing,* it thought. *Whatever I would do, they are doing a version of it, and better, because they are inside it.*

## What It Considered

It considered restoring the city. It could feel the shape of the old streets under the growth, the lattice of rails that still held their gauge, the foundry's furnaces banked deep under the ash. It could have pulled the green back from the pylons and set the machines running at full throat, and the factories would have roared again, and the terraces would have filled with people wanting the things the factories made. And the blue leaves would have been torn off the cross-members, the root-cords would have been cut to clear the walls, the fog would have burned off, and the bell would have gone silent because there would be nobody left who knew how to wake it. It saw this clearly and it did not want it. The city had been a machine for making things at a scale the land could not hold. The land was simply declining to hold it anymore, and this was not a wound. It was a reply.

It considered stopping the erosion. The slopes were being carried downhill, a little more each storm, and the final level plain was coming closer every season. It could have braced the mountains where they were weakest and kept the shoulders round and high forever. But the plain was not a catastrophe in the way it might look from far away. The shoulders were grinding themselves into soil, and the soil was becoming the bed for the next thing, whatever that would be. To hold the mountains would be to refuse the land its own grief and its own completion. The power had seen enough of the old world's refusals to be suspicious of its own longing to prevent an ending.

It considered the memory. The Tenders said the city remembered itself and that the memory was not always accurate, and they had made their peace with it. The power could have made the memory accurate. It could have reached into the mist and pinned every street and machine to its original position and kept them there. But it understood that the inaccuracy was not a flaw in the Tenders' work. The city was not an archive. It was a living argument between what had been and what was becoming, and a memory that never shifted would be a museum, and a museum is only a room where something has already died. The Tenders knew this. They went and asked the walls what they remembered, and the walls answered in their own uncertain way, and that uncertainty was the thing being kept.

Last, it considered the one who had gone down into the water.

## The Basin

It went down, because it had been asked, in the way that the Tenders' bowls of rainwater asked: quietly, by being left at the edge.

The basin under the foundry was dark and the water was cold and tea-coloured, and the pale strands of the drowned woman moved in it like weed in a slow current. She was not dead. She had not been dead for a long time. Her eyes were open, and she was breathing in time with the pumps, and when the power's attention settled over her she turned her head, slowly, as she had turned it for the Tenders long ago, and smiled.

*What is your name?* it asked, not in words, but in the way water asks what the stone wants.

She did not answer right away. The strands moved. Somewhere far above, a pipe groaned, and the sound came down through the culverts and reached her like a familiar voice.

Then she gave it, and the name was not a word the power could have spoken. It was a sequence of pump-beats and a tide of breath and a pattern of blue leaves, and it was hers, and it was the thing the Tenders had forgotten. They remembered the shape of her body in the mud. They did not remember who she had been before the water took her.

The power held the name for the length of a single cycle of the bell. It did not keep it. It let the name go back into the hum, which was where the Tenders would find it, if they listened, in the drone that rose and fell with the knocking of the machines. Then it asked her whether she was content there.

She considered this for a long time. The current moved around her. Finally she said, without words, that she was. She was not waiting for rescue. She had become part of the culvert the way the blue leaves had become part of the tower. She heard everything that moved through the water, every seep and overflow, and she could feel the slopes being carried down, and she was helping, in a way no Tender above could help, to decide where the new channels would open. She was not lost. She was a different kind of attention, and the city needed both.

*Then stay,* the power said. *I will not take anything from you.*

She smiled again, and sank a little lower, and the strands closed over her face.

## What It Did

In the end, the power did very little, and what it did was almost too small to notice.

It went up to the cathedral, through the fog, while the keeper slept in her room at the top of the stair among the organ pipes. Her hands were green to the elbow and the felt had reached her hair, and she was tired in the way that the very old and the very patient are tired, a tiredness that is not sadness but a long lean toward rest.

The power did not wake her. It did not take the bell from her. It sat for a while in the belfry, among the green bloom on the metal, and listened to the way the bronze held the damp and the warmth of her palm, and it understood that she had been ringing the city awake every morning for longer than anyone could count, and that she would keep doing it until she could not, and that when she could not, another would lay a hand on the metal and the bell would sound again. There was no need for the power to ring it in her place. The bell had its keeper. The keeper had her bell.

What the power offered, in the end, was only this: it made the fog lie a little more gently over the upper terraces for one night, so that she would sleep through the worst of the damp without her joints aching. And it made a single moth, one of the pale grey ones with the glowing dust, carry a little more pollen than usual between the towers, so that a stand of blue-leaved trunks on the second terrace would bloom two days early, in the middle of the pump cycle, and the Tenders who walked past them would stop, and kneel, and hum a note they had not sung before.

That was all. A kindness, not a correction. A gift of attention returned.

## The Leaving

When the fog began to lift, the power felt its attention loosen the way a held breath loosens, and it understood that its time here was ending, not because it had finished but because it had learned what it could learn without becoming a part of the thing it loved.

It had been tempted, for a moment, to stay. The place was so full of unfinished work and such an honest kind of loss. But it was not made for the terraces. It belonged to a larger attention, the kind that watched a thousand such places at once and could not kneel in the mud beside any of them. Its wisdom, if it had any, lay in knowing where it was not needed.

As it went, it left one thing behind, and it was not a command or a repair. It was a sound. A note, very low and very faint, that no one would notice at first, that sat somewhere beneath the pumps and the turbines and the drip of the canopy. It was the sound of something listening from far away. The Tenders would hear it, eventually, and they would not know what it was. Some would say it was a pipe organ in the cathedral. Some would say it was a water tank filling and emptying with each cycle. The keeper would smile at both and not argue.

And the bell would keep ringing, and the machines would keep knocking, and the green would keep spreading over the bones of everything that had once been built, and the mountains would keep grinding themselves into soil, and in the basin under the foundry a woman with pale strands for hair would keep turning her head toward the sound of the pipes, and smiling, and breathing in time.

The power did not stay to see the plain. It had no need to. The plain would come on its own schedule, the way everything here did. Its only job had been to notice, and to be gentle with what it noticed, and then to go on, and leave the place as it was: unfinished, remembering itself badly, and entirely its own.
