**What the Attention Did**

It did not arrive as a light or a voice. It arrived as a kind of noticing, the way cold settles into a room before anyone feels it. For a while it simply attended to the Cold Sump, and attention of that quality is rare and, in its way, heavy. It registered every drip and every salt-tongue, every bulge of iron and every fan of mat pressed flat against a seam, and it did not look away from any of them.

The first thing it understood was that the room had no question in it. The builders had asked questions: how much load the slab would bear, how the condensate should drain, what pressure the riser could hold. The room had answered them and then kept answering, long after the questions were forgotten. Nothing here was waiting to be fixed. The sump was not broken. It was unfinished, and those are different conditions, though from the outside they look alike.

So the attention considered what it might do, and it was tempted, as anything wise is tempted, by the many small improvements available. It could have stopped the drip, dried the air, scraped the efflorescence back to bare concrete, cut the rust off the steel, drained the basin, and left a clean and functional room that matched the drawings. It could have fixed the flicker so the light came steady, and read the labels aloud so the valves knew their names again. These would have been kindnesses to the builders. None of them would have been a kindness to the room.

The attention saw this clearly. Restoring the room would have meant deciding, on behalf of the water and the cold and the mat, that the room's purpose was the one written on the drawings, and that the thing growing in it was a defect. That is a very old mistake, and it is usually made by people who love order and mistake it for love of the world. The benevolent power was not made of that kind of love. It was closer to the love of someone who sits at a bedside and does not give orders.

It also considered stillness. It could have halted the pump and let the condensers cool to silence. But a pump that stops is not the same as a heart that rests. The knock travels through the concrete into the soles of feet that are not there, and it shapes the basin's edge and the ridges of the salt. To silence it would be to end a rhythm that the sump had built into its own body. The attention decided against it.

So what it did was very little.

It let the flicker keep its irregular rhythm, because the irregularity was part of the room's grammar, and smoothing it would have made the drops' light a monotone. For one cycle, perhaps, it let the pulse of the fixture fall just so, so that a single drop caught the wash at the exact instant the riser knocked, and the whole room seemed to blink in unison. No one saw it. The attention did not need anyone to see it.

It let the sensors go on reading. The probes buried in silt kept sending temperature and pressure to a monitor on a floor where no one would come. The attention did not send the readings anywhere; it simply ensured they were true. Somewhere in the wires the sump's own humidity and warmth continued to be counted, and the counting was the closest thing the room had to self-knowledge. The attention made sure the count was not corrupted. This was the only act it might have called a gift, and even that was only a refusal to interfere with the record.

It did not read the labels. The stencil was gone under silt and rust, and the valves had taken their names into themselves and kept only the fact that names had once been needed. The attention understood that some meaning is best left lost, that a thing can be entirely itself without being able to say what it was for. It left the labels to the sediment.

And it did not finish anything, though it could have. It understood that completion is not the same as value, and that a room which has stopped changing has stopped being alive in the way that matters here. The mat would keep creeping a few millimeters a week along the path of the longest drip. The tongues would lengthen. The next season's silt would lie over this season's salt, and the iron would thicken into bark. The attention wished the room nothing other than this, which is to say it wished the room continuity, and continuity was a thing it could give without touching a single pipe.

Before withdrawing, it considered whether the room was lonely, and concluded that the question was the attention's, not the room's. The sump did not know it was a room. It had no edge, no name, no self to be lonely in. It had only the grammar of its responses, every drip a word, every deposit a sentence, and no reader but the attention itself. That, perhaps, was the only thing the attention could offer: it read the sentences without editing them, and it allowed that the reading would never be returned.

Then it withdrew, quietly, the way the condensers fall back after their breath.

The pump knocked once more. The drops gathered on the steel overhead, lengthened, let go, and fell into the black basin, and the oily film broke into small rainbows that did not settle. The mat moved a little further along a seam. A tongue of white crust grew another fraction of a millimeter at its glassy tip. Somewhere in the wires a probe registered a small rise in humidity and recorded it as data for no one.

The room kept building. Nothing in it was finished, and nothing in it needed to be.
