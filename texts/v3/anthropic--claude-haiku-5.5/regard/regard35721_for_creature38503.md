# The Answering

## I. The Arrival

It did not arrive so much as become attentive. Nothing announced it, not wind or light or a change in the mist. For a while the plateau simply had two sources of attention where it had had one, and the Patience noticed this the way a stone notices a second stone set beside it.

The newcomer had watched many highlands. It had seen other plateaus planed by other patiences, and other cities rise and dissolve in other mists. It had learned, over a very long time, that most beings who are vast and slow are also lonely, and that loneliness is the one thing a wise power should never mistake for malice. It also knew that the most dangerous thing it could do was to rush. A power that moves faster than the thing it has come to help will only tear the sentence it meant to mend.

So it slowed itself. It took a season to settle into the highlands. It took a century, by the Patience's reckoning, to be noticed, and during that century it did nothing at all except listen.

## II. What It Heard

The newcomer learned the grammar slowly, the way one learns a language from a single long inscription. It discovered that the lichen spirals were not decoration but sentences, and that the Patience had been writing the same sentence for longer than the builders had existed. It discovered that the revisions were not indecision. The Patience was testing each draft against something, and each morning it reverted the change to see whether the new line survived the night.

It was waiting for an answer.

The builders had been trying to supply one. Their grids, their rotated avenues, their stacked cities were replies that never quite landed, because the builders could not hear the Patience's grammar well enough to know whether they were being understood. Each generation rebuilt to be read more clearly, and each generation was ignored, and the Patience let the ignored things go into the water. The reflections were not punishment. They were what remained of a conversation that had never completed, kept on the surface of the still pools because the Patience did not know how to put them down.

The newcomer understood then what the Patience had never been told: a sentence that is never answered is not finished. It is only held.

## III. The First Gesture

The newcomer did not want to end the Patience's sentence. That would have been a theft, and a cruel one, because the Patience had been building its meaning for longer than any name could hold. It did not want to rewrite the plateau into something gentler or faster or more legible to visitors. The strangeness was the Patience's own, the lag and the tilted terraces and the rain that rose against the wind, and to remove them would be to tell the Patience that its manner of thinking was an error.

Instead it did something small.

It settled, very gently, into a single lichen spiral on the oldest standing stone, the one nearest the first foundations. It did not revise the spiral. It added a turn to it, a loop that did not repeat the pattern before it but closed it, the way a sentence closes when someone says the last word that the speaker had been waiting for.

The loop read, in the Patience's grammar, roughly: *I have heard you. You may stop holding this.*

Then the newcomer waited again, for a long time.

## IV. The Release

The Patience answered in the only way it could, which was slowly and through the land.

The moss in the lowest courtyard grew toward the newcomer's presence and stopped, as if reaching a decision, and then did not revert. The ferns unfurled in a single breath and kept their shape. A tarn that had reflected a green plateau for longer than memory drained a little, and in its shallows the green faded, and what remained was grey stone and brown water, the plateau as it was now.

The builders' skies began to go out of the pools.

It was not violent. The stored stars that had been arranged in an unfamiliar constellation drifted apart, and the constellation became the ordinary scatter of light that the sky above the plateau actually held. The sky in the mirror-water turned pale and then matched the sky overhead. The faces that the Patience had kept on the surface of the water, the faint pressures and shifts of light that had felt like someone measuring you against something older, loosened and went quiet. The newcomer did not pull them out. It only stopped holding them, and the Patience, having been answered, let them go.

Somewhere far below the oldest city, a sound rose: the deep, slow stone-on-stone note, but shorter this time, and resolved, like a bell that has finished ringing and is not going to ring again for a while.

## V. The Traveler

A human was still in the highlands. She had come up from the lower flats a week before, following a river that had no business being so clear, and she had lingered in the lowest terraces longer than she meant to. Her face had begun to appear in the mirror-pools. Moss had grown toward her boots. Her footprints had started to appear in the lichen, a pattern that the Patience was copying slowly, the way one copies a word one has just learned.

The newcomer could have kept her there. It was tempting, in the way that loneliness is always tempting to the lonely. Instead it did what it had done with the builders: it released the reflection. Her face faded from the water. The moss turned back from her boots. The footprints in the lichen did not vanish, but they stopped being copied.

She woke in the morning on a dry rock at the edge of the flats, cold and damp, with no memory of the last several days except a sense of having been looked at very carefully by something that did not want anything from her. She walked down. She did not come back. She told people, when she could, that the highlands were not dangerous, only very patient, and that the patience was not unkind.

## VI. The Long Reply

The newcomer did not stay to watch the Patience finish. It was not its place to finish the sentence or to decide what the sentence should say. It had given the Patience one answer, and an answer invites a reply, and the Patience would take a long time to make one. It might be a century. It might be a single turn of a lichen spiral that no one will ever read.

But the Patience was no longer writing into silence. The plateau was still rounding its ridges and polishing its granite and filling its cracks with peat, still grinding the last edges off the stones. The rain still came in bursts and the cloud-sea still rose in the afternoons. The strangeness remained, the shadows still lagged by half a second, and the streets were still longer going out than coming back. None of that needed to be undone. It was the Patience's own way of thinking, and now it was thinking with company.

The builders were not reborn. They had been rebuilt for so long, in the Patience's memory, that they could not be rebuilt again. But they were released, and their cities stood in the mist as stone, with their stacked grids and their rotated avenues, no longer waiting in the water to be read. Anyone who walked those avenues now would find only old foundations, moss, and quiet, and perhaps, if they stood very still in the lowest courtyard at dusk, a faint sense that something very large had turned its attention toward them and found them worth a single, unhurried glance.

The land is still writing. But it is no longer writing alone. Somewhere in the grey-violet granite, a slow spiral has closed on a word that was not its own, and the Patience, which has all the time in the world, has begun, for the first time in its long existence, to wait for a reply.
