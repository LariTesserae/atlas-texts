**What the Attention Did**

It came the way weather comes to a valley that has been waiting for it, from above and from outside, without announcing itself. For a long time it did nothing but listen. It heard the pumps and the frogs, the tick of cooling iron, the slow drip from the cliff seams. It heard the hum that rose and fell in the Keeper's chest like a second heartbeat under the machinery. It did not hurry.

It understood the valley more quickly than it understood the Keeper. The valley was a balance that had been tipped by people who had wanted water on demand, who built towers to pull cloud from the air and pipes to carry it to a works that would never run at full capacity. The people had left. The machines had gone on running on heat from the rock, the way a clock keeps ticking after the household has gone to bed. The Keeper had come into that continuing order and had taken on its bookkeeping, because someone had to, and the valley had no one else.

The attention saw the mistake, and saw that it was not a cruel one. The Keeper believed that balance meant holding. It thought that if it drew enough cloud into its body and kept the water high on the rim, the valley would remain as it was. But nothing in that valley had ever been still. The marsh was growing, the forest was closing, the rock was cracking. Holding was only a slower way of losing the thing it was trying to keep.

---

**What It Considered**

It considered the grand gestures, and rejected them.

It could have restored the works: relit the chimneys, cleared the vines, returned the towers to their purpose. But that would have meant making the valley serve a use it no longer had, and it would have made the Keeper a servant of pumps again, counting for people who would never come back to drink.

It could have removed the Keeper. But the Keeper had done nothing wrong. It had done what it understood to be its duty, and it had done it faithfully. To take that away without offering anything in its place would be a kind of theft, and it would leave the valley to the same slow drowning it was already heading toward.

It could have let the valley flood at once, or cracked the basalt and drained the bowl in a single night. That would have been a violence of its own, and it would have been the valley's choice made by something outside it.

What it chose was smaller. It would make one change, a single seam in the rock along the northern fault, where the stone was already thinner than the rest. The fault would open a little, slowly, over a decade. A channel would form, lined by the same moss and lichen that already covered everything. The water would find its way out through that gap and down a gentle course to the lowland, where it could become a river and feed places beyond the rim. The pools would stop rising. The marsh would stop creeping. The works would be left standing, but not drowned, and their future would be decided by the water and the forest rather than by a creature counting alone.

Then it would speak to the Keeper, though not in words. It would work through the things the Keeper already understood.

---

**What It Did**

The first sign was a bead of water that ran downhill along a pipe. It was a small thing, the kind of drop the Keeper had watched climb uphill for years. This one slid down the mesh toward the sump and fell into the pool with a sound too small for the frogs to notice, but the Keeper noticed. Its skin rippled, then lay still.

The second sign was in the chalk of the hut. In the corner, half-hidden behind a sheet of rust, the attention made the faded writing legible for a single rain. It showed the Keeper the last dated entry the people had left, a line in a careful hand: a count of pump-hours, a note about a delivery that never came, and beneath it, smaller, a date followed by the word *closing*. Then nothing. The scratched marks of the Keeper began only after that date, every one of them a tally of the valley's rhythms with nobody to read them.

The Keeper stayed by the hut's wall for a long time. Its gills opened and closed slowly. The frogs on its back went quiet, and for the first time in years the valley's noise fell away, leaving only the drip of the cliffs and the steady silver thread from the troughs.

The third sign was the fault.

It did not look like anything at first. A crack appeared in the northern cliff, a thread of darker stone where the basalt had always been fine-grained and dense. Water found it at once, as water always does, and began to seep through. The seep joined a trickle, and the trickle joined a rivulet, and for the first time in its life the Keeper felt a current move through the sump that did not come from its own drawing. The water was leaving. It was going down, not up.

---

**What the Keeper Did**

It did not resist. It did not know how. It had only ever held.

For the first rains after the crack opened, it kept drawing cloud as before. Its hum rose. The gills opened wide. But the pools kept falling, and the beads on the pipes ran downhill, and the Keeper's ledger stopped matching the valley. The rhythm it had been following no longer held. The rises it had marked were no longer rising. The rain-bursts came, but they did not fill anything.

After a while it stopped counting.

No one saw this. The marks on the gantry stopped mid-stroke, and nothing new was scratched into the concrete of the loading platform. The hum dropped to a low note, lower than the pumps, and the pumps, for once, did not follow it. They ran on their own, steady and indifferent, as machines do when no one is listening.

In the hours before the next rain, the Keeper sank slowly into the black water of the sump until only its eyes were above the skin. Two small pools of bronze glass watched the valley for a while. Then it let go of the cloud. It let the mist settle on the leaves and drip through the canopy in broken shafts of light, and let the water run where it wanted to go.

---

**Afterward**

The attention did not stay. It never needed to. The valley did not become a different place overnight. The fault opened over years, not seasons, and the river that formed was slow and brown and full of silt. The marsh drew back from the rim and then crept forward again along the lower banks, as marshes do. The forest closed over the loading platform and the gantry and the far corner of the hut. The condenser towers stood a little longer and then, one by one, began to lean.

The Keeper did not die. It still lives in the deepest sump, though the pool is smaller now and the water runs clear over its flanks. Its gills still open in the rain, but they draw in only what the air offers, and nothing is held. The frogs still sing from its back. The flowers in the grooves still open only in the rain. When a person wades through the marsh now, the Keeper rises from the water and watches them with its bronze eyes, and then sinks, and the frogs sing the visitor out.

On the wall of the hut, beneath the last of the chalk, there is one scratched mark that is not a count. It is a single stroke, deeper than the others, made by something resting its weight against the iron for a long time. It does not add up to anything. It is only the place where the ledger was closed.
