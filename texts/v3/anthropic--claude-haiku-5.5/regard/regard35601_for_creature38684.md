**What the Attention Did**

It came first as a change in the north light.

The glow that had brightened and faded in its slow rhythm for as long as anyone could remember went on brightening, past its usual crest, and did not fade. It held. For three days the people of the lee-houses walked under a second sky that had no source, and the turbines on the ridge stopped their humming, not from calm but from a kind of listening. The grey birds did not call at dusk. The runners stopped in their channels, and the channels stayed open.

Then the light began to move, very slowly, across the plateau, and it became clear that it was looking.

It was not a god in the way the people's old songs meant the word. It had no face and wanted none. It had been watching this country for longer than the country had been soft, the way a traveller watches a coastline from the deck of a ship, and it had seen the mountains sink into shoulders and the shoulders into swells. It had seen the machines wake. It had noticed the people, who were small, damp, and careful, and who had learned to test the ground before shifting their weight. And it had noticed, with something that could only be called affection, that the whole place was thinking, and that nobody had asked it to.

The attention was wise enough to distrust its own power. It knew that to look at a thing is already to press on it, and that a light falling on a pool changes what the pool reflects. So it reasoned, as carefully as it could, about what not to do.

It would not reshape the land. The mat had been redrawing the country for a long time, and the redrawing was the country's own. If the attention lifted a hummock with a thought, the mat would learn that something above it could lift things, and it would begin to grow toward that, and in a few centuries the plateau would be a garden for someone else's will. It would not drain the seeps or freeze the pools or stop the walking pools. Those were answers the ground had already given.

It would not take the people away. It considered this for a long time. The people were the most recent arrivals, and the least certain, and the attention judged that they had been here long enough to belong to the place in the way a sentence belongs to a paragraph. Lifting them out would be a kind of amputation, and the wound would heal into a shape no one had chosen.

It would not repair the machines. The machines did not need repairing. They needed company, and the people had already given it.

So it did very little, and what it did was small.

It answered the birds.

For all the seasons the grey long-necked birds had stood motionless between their calls, heads raised, waiting, and the only reply had been the turbines' half-beat hum on windy nights. Now, on the first dusk after the light had begun to move, the birds sang their chord, and from the north came a single sustained note that was not a voice and not a machine, but something held between them, like a string drawn tight across a valley. The birds did not startle. They turned their heads toward the sound, and then, one by one, they folded their necks and went still, and the note faded, and the birds went on calling every dusk after that, and something in the chord had changed. It was no longer waiting.

It listened to the mat through the burrowers.

The attention could not speak to the mat directly. It had no head to speak to, only pressure, and pressure was the mat's language. So the attention pressed very gently, once, on the cold layers near the stone, where the burrowers moved. It did not ask anything. It only let the mat feel that there was a weight above it that did not want to bear down. The thumping and scraping at dusk slowed for a night and a day. Then it resumed, and the attention understood that this was the mat's way of saying that it had heard, and was thinking about it, and would take as long as it needed.

It let the cliffs keep their edge.

The people had said the cliffs remembered the mountains, and that the mat had been trying for a long time to soften them. The attention reasoned that the mat's softening was not wrong. Softness was what the country was becoming. But the cliffs were also a kind of memory, and a world with no sharp things in it would have lost a grammar it might one day need. So it did something that cost it nothing and asked nothing of anyone: it turned its light, once each night, across the western scarp, and the stone took the light and held it along its edge for a moment, as if to say that it was still there. The mat, which measured time in seasons, did not notice the difference. The people did. Some of them began to sit on the scarp at that hour, and they did not speak while the light passed over, and the cliffs seemed to lean very slightly toward them.

It did not undo the folded distances, or the shadows that lagged, or the water that ran uphill for a few metres. These were not faults. They were the seams of a world being rewritten by its own inhabitants, and the attention judged that a rewritten thing was more truthful than a finished one. It only watched them, and in watching, held them a little more steadily, so that a ridge an hour away would at least not become a day's walk in the middle of someone's journey home.

And it spoke, once, to the people.

It did not appear to them as a voice from the sky. It came as a question, the way the mat's pressure came as a decision, through the light that fell on the ridge where the people sat by the turbines each morning, laying their palms on the cold iron. The question, as the people later tried to describe it, was not a sentence. It was more like a door held open. It asked them what they wanted to be, here, in a place that was becoming something else. Did they want to stay as guests who had stayed a long time, or did they want to be something the mat could grow into, or did they want to go, and if they went, did they want to carry something back to the lower country that would tell the people there what the high ground had taught them?

The people did not answer right away. They talked among themselves for three nights, sitting by the iron with their palms on the pistons, and the pistons lifted and settled in the rhythm they had learned. In the end they said something that the attention found wise, and that it had hoped to hear without quite expecting. They said they would stay, and that they would keep learning the ground's moods, and that they would teach their young to test the swell and feel for the moment before it moved. They said that if the light wanted to know anything from them, it could come and sit by the iron, the way they did, and be quiet for a while. They said that was how you began to know a neighbour.

The attention considered this. It was, in its way, delighted.

Then it withdrew, though not far. It did not leave the north. It let the light settle into its old rhythm, brightening and fading, and the people went back to nodding at it without remark. The birds went back to calling their chord, with one new note held beneath it, which they seemed to have decided was simply part of the song. The runners kept the channels open. The grazers went on drawing their rivers with their slow backs, and the winged things went on sinking into the mat with their gifts of spore, and the mat went on walking, toward warmth and away from weight, deciding things in its unhurried way.

And the attention, which had come as a watcher and had meant to stay one, found that it was not entirely outside the plateau anymore. Its light was on the cliffs now, and in the birds' song, and in the fraction of a second by which the shadows lagged. It had not wanted to be part of the becoming. But it had looked for a long time, and looking, it had discovered, is a way of being touched, and it had been touched, and it did not mind.

It would keep watching. It would keep being careful. It would answer when it was asked and wait when it was not, and it would let the country decide what it was to be next. The mat had not asked for a guardian. The people had not asked for a god. The attention had come to understand that what the place needed was not a power over it, but a witness that could hold the whole of it in mind without trying to make any of it into something else.

So that was what it became, slowly, without apology: a second light in the north, sourceless, rising and falling, which the people no longer remarked upon, and which the mat, in its own time, had begun very gently to lean toward.
