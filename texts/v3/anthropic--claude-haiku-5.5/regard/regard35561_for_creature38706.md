**What the Patient Power Would Do**

It comes slowly, because there is no other way to come to this place. Its attention does not arrive like a visitor. It accumulates, like salt, a grain at a time, until it is simply there, and the basin has noticed it the way it notices everything: as one more small change.

The power understands quickly that the basin is not broken. The walls lean, the grates clog, the valve moves a quarter inch in a lifetime, and every one of these might look like damage to someone in a hurry. But nothing here is failing. Everything here is finishing. The power has seen enough of the world to know that a thing can be dying gracefully and still be whole, and that the wish to rescue is often a wish to be needed. So its first decision is restraint. It will not clear the grates, straighten the cisterns, relight the lamps, or reopen the old refinery. It will not reconstruct a purpose the basin has already let go of.

It does think about the pump for a long time. The pump is the basin's breath, and the power is wary of anything that tells a breathing thing when and how to breathe. Whatever stirs deep in the foundations has its own rhythm, and the power does not know whether the pulses of brackish water are a gift or an ache. So it leaves the pump alone.

The heat is different.

The whiskers point toward the seams. For longer than anyone has kept records, the iron has been leaning toward the warmth in the fissures below, and the warmth is thinning. The slag still glows at its edges, but the orange is a little dimmer than the glow in the iron's memory. The power cannot know what it is like to be iron leaning toward a thing that is leaving. It only knows that a long orientation toward something, if it is ever to be honored, should not be left to wither at the moment it is nearly complete.

So it does one small thing. It goes down into the seams, where the heat is still caught in fracture and slag, and it banks it. It does not raise the temperature. It does not reheat the cisterns or turn the refinery back on. It only slows the cooling in a few fissures, so that the warmth the iron has been reaching for lasts a little longer than it otherwise would. Perhaps a century more, perhaps a thousand years. For the basin, which measures time in the leaning of walls, the difference might be the whole of its remaining life, and the power is content with that.

Then there is the valve.

On the north gantry, the wheel that has turned a quarter inch in however many years is the basin's only hand. The power considers it for a long time, the way one might consider whether to answer a child's gesture. It could turn the wheel fully, or oil the spindle, or lift the bracket off its worn groove. It does none of these. It puts its attention on the wheel and turns it a quarter inch in the same direction the iron has always turned it. It is not an opening or a repair. It is a reply in the only language the basin uses, a motion that says *yes, I see what you are doing, go on*.

The groove it deepens is only a few microns deeper. But it is now a record of two hands, the basin's and the power's, and the power is glad to leave its mark there, quietly, where only the basin will ever read it.

The stacks it does not touch at all. Their hum rises and falls over days, and the power listens for a long while, trying to find the pitch that answers the drip from the ribs. It finds the two rhythms drifting in and out of phase, and it understands that this is a conversation the basin has been having with itself for longer than there has been a word for conversation. The power has no reason to join it. It only sits near the stacks for a time and lets its own attention hum at the edge of their pitch, too faint to change anything, the way a listener might hum along in the next room.

The salt, it leaves in its layers. Each white stalactite is a ring in the basin's memory, and the power has no right to rewrite a single one. It does, however, read them, from the outside, in the way you might read a letter that was never addressed to you. There is grief in some of the layers, and something like contentment in others, and a very long stretch of dry seasons that the basin seems to have spent simply drying. The power does not know if the basin remembers any of this as suffering. It assumes only that the memory is part of the basin's shape, and that shape is worth keeping.

The moths it lets settle. It does not catch them or warm them or try to make them live longer. They come to the heat over the open tanks, and they are received, and that reception is the basin's own kindness, however strange. The power does not interfere with the one mercy the basin offers, which is that nothing that comes to it is wasted.

Last, the power considers the visitor, the small warm body that might one day stand still in the dark and find the drip of water beginning to keep time with its breath. The power does not decide what the visitor should do. It does, however, let the basin's attention settle a little more gently on that visitor, so that the drip, when it comes, will be slow enough to hear.

Then the power withdraws, the way it arrived, by accumulation in reverse. There is no ceremony. The basin does not notice it going, or notices only the way a body notices the warmth of a hand that has just been lifted from its skin. The condensation builds on the ribs. A drop falls, rings faintly off the iron, and the stacks answer at their low pitch, somewhere far below the level of hearing.

It was a short visit, by the basin's measure. The power left almost nothing behind except a quarter inch of turning, a few fissures holding their heat a little longer, and a deeper groove in the bracket.

It is enough. Among the things that are settling toward rest, the wisest care is often the kind that knows how little to touch.
