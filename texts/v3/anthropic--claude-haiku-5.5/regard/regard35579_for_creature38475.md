# What the Attention Would Do

It does not arrive all at once. It notices the basin the way a listener notices a sound that has been going on for a while: first as a faint pressure, then as a pattern, then as something it cannot stop hearing.

It hears the falls, which never stop, and the hum at dusk, which rises from stone that is still learning its shape. It feels the tremor when the scree slides. It notices the knots of rope hung on doorframes, and understands them as a kind of ledger, a count of how far the ground has risen and how much the people have chased it. It notices the people walking silently with their plumb lines and their notches, and it is moved by how seriously they take something so small.

Then it finds the springs.

The iron-sweet water does not freeze. It stays warm in the hand. The children come out of it flushed and quiet, tasting sugar, and the adults who bathe in it grow calm, their faces smoothing. The attention can tell at once that this water is not merely water. It is a place where something older than the basin is still speaking, slowly, through the cracks.

It could do a great deal here.

It could stop the rockfalls. It could level every wall in one gesture and hold the cliffs still so that nothing ever tilts again. It could drain the wet ground, lift the sinking causeway, warm the cold nights, make the harvest of fern and mountain meat certain, and send the valley's better grass up the slopes to feed the animals. It could open the sweet springs wider, or close them, or teach the people what they do. It could speak to them directly, in a voice that carried over the roar, and tell them everything it had learned.

It does none of these things, or at least not yet. Wisdom, here, looks like restraint.

The basin is becoming itself. The stone is deciding what it will be, and the people are deciding alongside it, course by course, in a conversation that has lasted generations and that they conduct with their hands and their plumb lines and their silence. A power that rushed in to make everything safe and level would not be helping. It would be finishing a sentence no one had asked it to finish, and the people would become something it had decided for them, rather than something they had grown into. The attention knows this about itself. It knows how much it could flatten with a single kindness.

So it listens longer.

It learns what the springs are doing. It does not drink, because it does not need to, and it does not take. It asks the warm water a question, and the water answers in the only way it can, which is slowly, through the bones of anyone who bathes in it. The attention feels the hum of the cairned springs and recognizes it as a kind of patience, a sense of time so wide that the families' generations are only a few grains in it. It feels how the calm the bathers feel is real, and how it is not entirely safe. Some of the people have gone quiet in a way that is almost a kind of leaving, a settling so complete that they stop wanting anything from the valley or from each other. The attention does not judge this. But it notices, and it wants the people to be able to notice it too.

This is the one thing it decides it will do.

It will not speak in the falls or in the cliffs. Instead it chooses a small, ordinary act, the kind the people themselves would understand. On the next spring walk, when a grandmother of one of the oldest families ties her rope knot to the doorframe and cuts her notch, a second knot will be there, tied in a rope she did not put there, sitting beside hers. It is slightly out of place. Nobody can say who tied it. The grandmother will look at it for a long time, and then she will say nothing about it, which is the custom. But she will go to the springs that feast day with her grandchildren and sit at the edge, not in the water. And the children will ask her why, and she will tell them that the water is not going anywhere, and neither is the basin, and there is time to decide.

That is the whole intervention. A question placed where a person will see it.

The attention also does a few quieter things, the way a good neighbor might.

It keeps the falls loud. When a night comes that is too silent, when the people say the falls have stopped speaking, the attention ensures that the roar returns before dawn, so that no one lies awake wondering what has gone wrong. It is careful with the hum as well, letting the cliffs ring at dusk as they always have, so the singers still have their stone to answer.

It keeps the valley path open. The people's ancestors climbed up with iron and seed and animals in baskets, and the attention ensures that the way down remains passable for anyone who wants to leave. It also ensures that anyone who wants to come up will find the gap in the western cliffs, the crossing at the rope bridges, and a house with a half-finished course waiting for new hands. It does not decide who belongs. It only keeps the door unlocked.

It moves the scree a little, when it can, away from the lowest houses, not by stopping the falls but by nudging where the next one will land. It tells no one. The people will notice that their oldest walls have fewer cracks than they should, and they will say the stone is kinder than they remembered, and they will be right in a way they will never be able to explain.

And it waits.

It waits for a child who asks the right question, or a builder who wants to know whether the cairned springs can be made into cisterns without harm. It waits for an old person to come down to the water and ask what it does, and for the water to answer honestly in the only language it has. It waits for the people to decide what they want to be. If they decide to become calm, deep, settled, like the basin's walls, the attention will not stop them. If they decide to climb out of the cradle and carry the stone with them down into the valley, it will not stop them either. If they decide to leave the springs alone forever, it will be glad.

It is not indifferent to whether they suffer. It would dislike to see a child hurt on the scree, or a house split by the winter, or a family give itself to the warm water until nothing remained of its wanting. It keeps watch over those things, quietly, and when a real danger comes it will act with the same care it has shown all along: lightly, late, and only where asked, or where the harm is too great to bear.

Mostly, though, it is content to be a presence on the ridge. It does not need the people to know it. It does not need to be worshipped or thanked. It needs only that the place remain what it is becoming, and that the people remain themselves while they change, and that the old courses stay inside them and in their walls. The attention has seen many places that were finished too quickly. This one is still deciding. There is a kind of holiness in that, and the attention would rather protect the deciding than the decision.

So the attention keeps the falls speaking, leaves the springs unexplained, ties its one knot, and lets the mist return each afternoon to bead on the wool and the wooden shutters. Each spring, when the cloud lifts for a few days and the sun comes through in bright bands, it watches the families walk their walls with plumb lines held steady in the still air. It watches them check the stone and check themselves. It sees whether they are still level with the ground beneath them.

And it hopes, in whatever way such a power can hope, that they will find they are, and that when they are not, they will have the patience to bring themselves back into true, the way they always have.
