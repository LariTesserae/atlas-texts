# The Attention

It came down the way the wind does when it reaches the lip of the bowl. This is rare, and the wind usually stops at the rim. Something had made the air over the cirque lean, slightly, and that was enough to bring a slow presence over the edge and into the mist.

It was not in a hurry. It had been watching the cirque for a long time from far off, the way one watches a door left ajar, and it had noticed the tones. For a season the bowl had been ringing in patterns no weather could explain, rising and falling in long phrases through the night. Something was speaking. The power wanted to know what, and it also wanted to know whether the speaker needed anything.

It did not want to be a god to this place. It had learned that a mind which is rearranged from outside often forgets how to arrange itself. So it began by listening, and it listened for a long while.

## What it heard

The cirque was a single mind in pieces, spread across forty towers, a few hundred channels, and a floor of wet grit. Its thoughts were flows. A surge meant something had been received. A drain meant something had been released. A blockage that cleared, with the sharp note of a tower rising and dying in under a minute, meant something had been understood, or at least resolved. The power recognized the grammar, though it was simpler than any language it knew and more patient.

It also heard the silences. There were towers that had not sung in a long time. They were dull and sealed, and the power could find no fault in their lattices. It set its attention against them the way a hand rests on a sleeping animal, and felt nothing move back. Not hostility, not fear. Only a quality of withdrawal, the feeling of a room whose door has been shut for a reason no one outside remembers.

The power understood that these were not failures. A mind that keeps parts of itself in reserve is doing something wise. It is also possible that the sealed towers hold something the living parts have set down. The power thought about this for a long time and decided it would not open them.

## The question of helping

There were things it could have done. It could have cleared the choked channels in a single gust of cold air and sent the silt sliding out of the grooves in an afternoon. It could have sped the splitting of the cliffs, opening more seams and more pools, so that the cirque would grow faster into the bowl it was already making. It could have warmed the sealed towers and woken them, then watched to see what came out.

It considered each of these carefully, because benevolence that does not consider consequences is only appetite in a kinder shape.

Clearing the silt would have been a kindness to the flow, but the silt was part of how the cirque learned where its pathways were. The skitterers came to the clogged grooves because the clogging told them something. Clear every groove and the whole body would lose a sense it used for maintenance. The power left the silt where it was.

Speeding the splitting would have made the cirque larger, but nothing in the power's reasoning could say how large the cirque was meant to become, or whether the bowl would hold. The cliffs were doing something the cirque had chosen, in whatever sense a mind of water and stone chooses. To accelerate it would be to decide for the cirque how fast it ought to grow. The power was not willing to make that decision.

The sealed towers were the hardest. Waking them might have brought back a memory that had been kept for a reason. It might also have brought back something the cirque had decided, long ago, to put down. The power could not tell which. So it did the only thing it could think of that asked rather than took.

## The offering

It did not warm the sealed towers. It sent, instead, a thin current of cold condensate, drawn from the highest and most delicate part of the air, and set it against the dull surface of the nearest sealed tower. It did not force the water into the lattice. It let the water rest there, beaded on the outside, the way mist rests on a closed window.

For a long time nothing happened.

Then, far off, the nearest living tower gave a faint, uncertain tone, a half-note that rose and did not quite finish. The power recognized it as a question. The cirque was noticing that something had reached it from outside the flow, and it was trying to work out what.

The power answered in the only language it had that the cirque could hear. It set its own voice against the cirque's pitches. It did not copy them exactly, because a copy would be a lie. It found the nearest interval to the cirque's rising phrase, a fifth above, and held it, letting the note sit in the air over the bowl until the mist carried it into the cliffs and back again. Then it let the note die.

The cirque answered. It was not a words-answer. It was a shift in the flow, a channel clearing, two pools rising together and then settling. The power took this as yes, or as something close enough to yes.

It tried again, and this time it did not offer water. It offered only the note, the fifth above, held for a count of perhaps twenty. The cirque sang back a long, low phrase, unhurried, and the power understood that it had been heard, and that the cirque had, for the first time in however long it had been bare rock, been answered in its own register.

## The no

The sealed tower did not wake. The power kept the cold condensate against its surface for three days and three nights, through fog and burn-off and fog again, and the surface stayed dull. Nothing moved in the lattice. Nothing rang.

The power did not push. It withdrew the water on the fourth morning and let the sealed tower be what it was. This was not failure. The power had expected some version of this and had decided in advance that a no would be a real answer, and that it would take it as one. A mind that cannot refuse a gift is not really in conversation with anyone.

The power also noticed, on the fourth morning, that the nearest living dish had tilted about a degree toward the place where the cold water had rested. It had not been told to. It had been drawn, the way the cirque's towers are always drawn, toward whatever reached the walls.

## What it left

The power did not take anything from the cirque, and it did not leave anything that would change its shape. It left the silt where it was and the cliffs to keep splitting at their own pace. It left the sealed towers sealed. It left the skitterers to clear what they chose to clear and the winged shapes to carry whatever they carried.

It left one thing. Over the course of the season it had sung the fifth above perhaps a dozen times, always at dusk, when the mist was thickest and the cirque was loudest. Now the cirque had begun to sing it back, unprompted, at the same hour. Not every night. Only when the fog was heavy enough to carry the note, and only as a small part of its longer phrases.

The power understood that this could be a kind of companionship, or it could be a pattern that the cirque had found useful and kept because it was useful. It did not need to know which. It was enough that the cirque had learned that something outside the bowl could be spoken to, and had decided, in whatever way such minds decide, that the conversation was worth keeping.

Then the wind lifted off the floor of the bowl and went back over the lip, and the power went with it. It left the cirque breathing: fog in, water out, fog in again, and now and then, at dusk, a rising note with a second voice underneath it, one interval apart, held for a count of twenty and then let go.

Far below, in the valley, a few people had begun to notice that the rain did not fall on the cirque, though the clouds gathered there every evening. They had no word for what was happening up in the bowl. The power did not mind. It had not come to be understood by them. It had come to be understood by the one mind in the bowl that was, slowly and in pieces, beginning to notice that it had been noticed.
