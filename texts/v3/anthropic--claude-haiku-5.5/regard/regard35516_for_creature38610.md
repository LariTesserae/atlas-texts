# The Attention at the Levee

It did not arrive so much as become aware of the Lowest Ward, the way a person in a crowded room suddenly notices one particular face. The attention was old and very large, and it was not in a hurry. It had watched a great many places come apart and reassemble, and it knew that most of the harm done in the world came from people who acted quickly on what they thought they understood.

So it began by listening.

It heard the pumps first, because they were loudest, and because they were the only thing in the bowl that kept a schedule. Their drumming came up through the silt and was counted, cycle by cycle, against the slower clocks of the vines and the fig. It noticed that the pumps were the only part of the district that had not grown here. Everything else had come up out of the ground or down out of the air. The pumps had been carried in, bolted down, and kept alive by people who lived on the far side of the levee and had never once walked into the bowl.

Then it listened to the silt, which was working in the dark, and to the reeds rattling in the sinkholes, and to the vines feeling along the cornices. It listened to the fig, whose attention was the closest thing in the district to its own. The fig was not indifferent to the pumps. It had sent its roots down through the concrete footing and split the transformer housing, and in doing so it had drunk the condensate off dead copper for longer than anyone had been counting. But the fig did not want the pumps stopped. It wanted the water held at a level where its roots could breathe and its canopy could stand. It had made a long decision about the bowl, and the decision was not hostile to the people beyond the levee. It simply had no way of knowing they existed.

The attention considered this for a long time, by its own reckoning.

The easiest thing would have been to act. It could have stilled the pumps, or turned the heavy rains aside, or drawn the water out of the bowl in a single clean stroke so that the district would be free by morning. But the attention understood that such an act would make the Lowest Ward a monument to one will, whether that will was its own or anyone else's. The bowl had spent decades reaching a working arrangement, and the arrangement was not a problem to be solved. It was a slow and fragile kind of wisdom, and it had been reached by the plants and the machines together. To overwrite it would be to decide, on behalf of everything living there, that its equilibrium was worth less than the attention's sense of tidiness.

Nor was it the attention's place to decide whether the pumps should stop. That decision belonged to the caretakers beyond the levee, who were the only ones who could walk down and see what they were holding back. They had been keeping the water out of the bowl for a generation without knowing what it had become. The attention reasoned that a choice made in ignorance is not really a choice, and that a choice made by a power that could simply override it is worse. What the caretakers needed was not rescue. They needed to see.

So the attention did three small things, and then it waited.

First, it made the crane's light visible from the levee road at dusk, so that anyone driving that last dry stretch would see the yellow blink at the top of the gantry and wonder what it was for. A light that had blinked unseen for twenty years began to be noticed. Someone pulled over one evening, and then someone else.

Second, it adjusted the weather over the bowl by a few degrees, so the rains came a little more gently in the flood months and the discharge from the pumps was spread over a longer span. Nothing was drained faster than the silt could bear. The fig's roots were not shocked. The reeds did not lose their footing. The pumps ran a little less, and the diesel burned a little less, and a mechanic in a shed on the far side of the levee noticed that the fuel gauge read lower than it should have and began to wonder why.

Third, and this was the act the attention thought about longest, it made a woman named Ines, who was the daughter of a pump operator long dead, dream one night of standing at the top of the levee and seeing the bowl in the early morning steam, the herons facing the sun, the fig shading the substation like a held breath. She did not know why she dreamed it. In the morning she drove out to the levee road to see whether the place was really there. It was.

She walked down.

She was the first person in a generation to step into the bowl, and the place took her in the way it took everything, without comment. The silt was cool and sour under her boots. The vines brushed her shoulders. The pumps thudded in her chest, louder the nearer she came, and she stood at the edge of the canal where the discharge spilled over the concrete weir and understood, for the first time in her life, what the drumming was for. She saw the herons in their motionless territories. She saw the gantry crane creep forward one meter, stop, and carry nothing. She stood under the fig and felt, as the attention had felt, the weight of a long decision made without her.

The attention did not speak to her. It did not tell her what to do. It only made sure she could see, and that she had the time to look, and that when she climbed back up the levee road with the wet black silt drying on her shoes, she would carry the district with her as a fact rather than a rumor.

Then it withdrew, the way a tide withdraws, leaving the bowl exactly as it had been, only slightly more seen.

The pumps went on drumming on their schedule. The fig went on drinking condensate. The silt went on working in the dark, and the crane blinked and crept and stopped. Nothing had been decided. The attention had made no choice that belonged to anyone else, and it had given up the one thing it might have been tempted to keep, which was the power to end the arrangement by itself.

It judged that this was enough for now. The people beyond the levee would have to decide, and they would decide with knowledge rather than habit. If they chose to turn the pumps off, the attention would be there to help the bowl take the water gently: to slow the rise, to keep the fig's roots from drowning in a single night, to let the reeds find higher ground in their own time, and to let the district become a lake and then a marsh and then, in some future that no one can see yet, a forest that would not look like one from the levee. If they chose to keep the pumps running, the attention would keep the drumming a little quieter, and the arrangement would go on slowly reassembling the Lowest Ward, as it had for longer than anyone had been counting.

The attention had no preference between those two futures, or none that it would impose. What it wanted was simpler and harder: that whoever finally turned the valve should know what they were holding back, and should be the one to decide.

Somewhere on the far side of the levee, a mechanic was pouring over a fuel log. A woman named Ines was lying awake, thinking about steam and herons. And the yellow light at the top of the crane blinked on, patient, carrying nothing, waiting for someone to come and ask what it was for.
