# What the Attention Did

It noticed the place the way a person notices a sound that has changed pitch: not at first, and then all at once.

The power had been attending to many places, and most of them were quiet in ways that asked for nothing. This one was loud. Its loudness was the first thing that drew the attention, and then the knocking. Under the thunder, a vast slow conversation was happening through pipe and stone, and the power listened to it for a long time before it understood that it was listening to a mind. The city was thinking through its plumbing. The water was thinking through the rock. Neither was thinking quickly, but both were thinking.

The power's first decision was not to act. Its wisdom had taught it that most harm comes from the impulse to fix things before they are understood. So it stayed, and it listened, and it learned the shape of the argument.

---

## The Water

The water was not evil, and it was not simply a force. The power came to think of it as a patient intelligence that had no word for hurry. For ten thousand years, rain had fallen on the high plateau and sunk into the basalt and found its way down through the old joints, the hexagonal seams, the cracks that had opened as the rock cooled. It did what water does. It went where the stone let it go.

Then people arrived and built pumps, and the pumps pushed the water back down into the drainage tunnels, and the water, which had been moving slowly, began to be moved hard.

The power noticed something the line-keepers had half-noticed in their books. The seepage line advanced fastest in the years the pumps ran hardest. The engineers had been comparing the pumping rates to the water levels, but they had been comparing them as two numbers that moved together. They had not asked which one was the cause.

Pressure forces water into fractures that would otherwise stay closed. Water in fractures softens the rock at the edges, and softened rock opens wider, and wider fractures take more water. The basalt was not simply weeping. It was being made to weep by the very thing that was supposed to stop it.

The power did not believe the water wanted the second terrace. It believed the water was doing what the pumps taught it to do, and that the pumps were the reason the terraces were being lost.

---

## The Two Factions

The power also understood why the two factions could not agree. Each was right about something and wrong about the frame.

The ones who pump want the stairs and the homes and the dead's pools to remain as they are, and they are right to want this. A city has memory and a shape, and the people who keep it are not fools for wanting it to last.

The ones who let it have the stairs say the water has always been coming and will always come. They are right that the water cannot be beaten by force in the long run. But they are wrong to think the choice is between holding everything and surrendering everything. They have not looked closely at what the water actually does when it is left alone.

The power saw that the question had been posed as a binary by people who were tired. Binary questions are easier to carry through a pipe at night. The knocking language is quick and precise and does not have many words for *somewhat*.

So the power did not choose a side. It chose a timing.

---

## What It Did

It did very little, and it did it through people.

A line-keeper named Odele, who had spent thirty years setting white stakes along the edge of the third terrace, woke one morning with a figure in her head that she could not explain. It was not a dream. It was a clean, simple thought, the kind that arrives when one has been carrying a set of numbers for a long time and has stopped trying to look at them. *The line moves in the pumping years. Check the rest.* She checked. The line moved. She wrote it in the book in her careful hand, and beneath it, after a long pause, she added a sentence that had never appeared in any book on the plateau: *Perhaps the water is not the question.*

A pump-foreman on the fifth terrace, whose hands had grown too stiff for couplings, found himself listening to the gauges in a different way. He noticed that the pressure readings rose most sharply in the hours before the terrace's wettest seasons. He began to write them down, not for the engineers but for himself, and then for the knocking network, which carried his observations down three blocks to a laundry room where a woman laughed at a joke she did not understand and then went quiet, thinking.

The power did not speak to the water. It did not know how, and it suspected that the water would not have listened in any language it had. But it observed the water's slowness and recognized in it something like the patience of old trees, and it resolved to honour that patience rather than trying to outpace it.

It did one more thing, the last and smallest. It let the oldest engineering records surface. These were the founding plans, which had been stored in a dry cabinet on the ninth terrace and had never been read by anyone who could see their significance. They showed that the first builders had not intended to drown the plateau. They had planned for a stepped retreat: terraces to be abandoned in sequence, with new housing ready upslope before the water arrived. The founders had understood that water and stone would negotiate, and they had planned for the negotiation to take a century.

Somewhere along the way, the city had forgotten its founders' humility and remembered only their pumps.

---

## The Child on the Lip

In the evening, the child climbed to the cliff with her slate and her stick, as children had done for generations, and knocked her question on the basalt.

The power had been waiting. It did not have a voice that the child could hear through her jaw, but it had something close to a voice in the spaces between things, and it used it.

The answer came back down the terraces, through pipe and rock and gunmetal, faint and sure, as it always did. But this time the knock was not a single word. It was a pattern: a short tap, a long roll, a pause, a short tap again. The child did not know the pattern. No one on the plateau did. It had never been used before.

She wrote it on the slate before the mist could take it. Then she sat down on the lip of the shelf, where the basalt ended and the air turned to cloud, and she waited to see whether anyone else would hear it and know what to do.

Someone did. On the fourth terrace, an old woman put down her bread and stood very still. On the ninth, a line-keeper looked up from her book. In the pump halls, a foreman touched the cold iron with his palm, one bolt at a time, and felt, for the first time in years, that the iron might be listening back.

---

## What the Power Would Not Do

It would not stop the pumps. Stopping them would drown the lower terraces in a season and take the people's pools, their homes, their dead, and their sense of having a place. Wisdom does not impose a catastrophe in order to avoid a slower one.

It would not let the water take everything. The water's patience is real, but so is the people's need to stay. A city is not only a set of buildings. It is a conversation, and a conversation that loses its speakers has lost its meaning.

It would not decide for them. The power had learned, across many places, that a solution handed down from outside is a kind of silence. The people of the plateau had built their knocking language over generations. They could build the answer too, if they were given the question in a form they could hear.

So the power did what it does best, which is very little, at exactly the right moment, through the right small door.

---

## Coda

The thunder did not stop. The penstocks still roared, and the cooling towers still breathed their plumes into the natural mist, and the basalt still wept along its seams.

But on the third terrace, a line-keeper and a pump-foreman began meeting in the evenings, through the pipes and in person, and their meetings were slow, because neither of them had ever tried to agree with the other before. They argued about the figures. They argued about the founders' plans. They argued about whether a stair abandoned to water was a loss or a sentence.

And sometimes, in the middle of an argument, one of them would tap a single short knock against the gunmetal, and the other would answer with a long roll, and the rest of the city would hear the pattern travel down the pipes and through the rock, and they would not know what it meant, and they would wait to see.

The power, listening from very far away, thought this was enough. Not a solution, but a question that had found more than one voice to carry it. At this scale of time, with water and stone and people all deciding slowly, that was what wisdom usually looked like: not a hand on the pump, but an ear at the pipe, and the patience to let the answer take as long as it needed.

It stayed. It listened. The water kept coming, and the people kept knocking, and somewhere on the lip of the shelf, a child with a slate was still waiting for the next reply.
