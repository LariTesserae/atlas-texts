# The Attending Presence

It came the way weather comes, from the east, over long stretches of time, and it was not in a hurry. It had watched a great many places. Most of them had made it either hopeful or sorrowful, and it had learned to hold both feelings loosely, because wanting to help is different from knowing how. When it turned its attention to the drowned plateau, it felt something it had not felt for a while: the sense of a question that had not yet been asked.

It began by listening, which took a long time, because the plateau spoke slowly.

## What it perceived

The organism was whole. It had no wound that needed closing and no hunger that went unmet. Water came to it in its proper rhythm, the tides rose and fell, the fronts arrived on schedule from the east, and its body reshaped itself around these things without strain. The presence studied the silence where animals should have been and understood it not as a lack but as a completeness that the plateau had chosen, or grown into, over ages. It did not want to fill that silence with scurrying life. That would be a kind of vandalism, dressed up as kindness.

So the first conclusion was simple and a little humbling. The plateau did not need rescuing. It was not suffering in any way the presence could recognize. If it was lonely, it was lonely the way a deep lake is, which is to say not at all in the sense that matters to people.

## What it found in the silt

The second thing took longer to read. Beneath the mats and the roots lay the layered record of the people: cairns sunk into silt, stilts gone grey and soft, a flint blade that had been left in one place for a very long time. The presence could not reconstruct their lives. It could not learn their names or their songs. What it could feel was the shape of their attention: the care with which the stones had been stacked, the cords bound tight, the stakes set where something had happened and was meant to be remembered.

It recognized grief in those shapes, and also hope. Someone had wanted the marks to last.

The presence considered, for a while, whether it should gather the stones and carry them somewhere safe. It was able to do this, in a sense. It could lift the cairns out of the silt and set them on a dry ridge where they would not dissolve. It decided against it. The stones were not only relics. They were part of a body that had been holding them for generations, and to pull them out would be to rip the plateau's memory from its own flesh and call the theft preservation. The people had not asked to be preserved in that way. The plateau had not asked to be stripped.

The presence also noticed that it could not know what the people would have wanted. Presumptions about the dead are a common and dangerous form of love.

## What it chose

It chose to ask.

This was slow. The presence had no voice the plateau could hear, but it had weight, and it had patience. Over one full tide it altered the pressure in the basins, not dramatically, only enough to make a pattern: a rise, a pause, a fall, a rise again, repeated in the same shape across many cycles. It was the closest thing it had to a question. Then it waited.

The plateau answered the way it answered everything, through growth and withdrawal. Over the following season, the mats along the old platform lines thickened. Stalks leaned in toward the cairns that had been stacked tallest. In a few hollows where a blade had been left, the spores settled more densely, and the moss there grew with a particular stubbornness. None of this was a clear yes or no. It was preference, the way the plateau had always expressed what it held and what it released.

The presence read this as something like: *these are part of me now, and I keep them in the way I keep water.* It did not mistake this for consent in the human sense, but it did not dismiss it. A being that grows around its memories has a relationship with them, and that relationship deserves to be respected.

## What it did

It did very little, and it did it deliberately.

It made sure the fronts kept arriving from the east. It kept the tides their proper height. It declined to disturb the silt or the mist or the drip that ran under everything like a pulse. The plateau would go on breathing as it had.

For the people, it did one thing that cost the plateau nothing. It remembered them. The presence is not a place and cannot be buried by silt, and its memory does not dissolve the way the stones do. So the people who had stacked cairns above the waterline, bound cord that went brittle, and left blades where they could be found by no one, now existed in the one record that would outlast the plateau's tides: the attention of a wise and patient witness. It did not give them names, because it did not have them. It gave them their tide, the rhythm they had organized their lives around, and it held that rhythm gently, the way one holds a thing whose ending one cannot prevent but can at least see clearly.

And it left the question open. If the plateau ever came to want something else, the pressure patterns were there to be read. If a descendant of the people ever came up the eastern ridge and stood in the mist, the presence would not hide the stones from them. It would not push them forward either. It would let the water decide.

## Afterward

The presence moved on eventually, as all such attention does. It did not leave the plateau changed. The cairns still sank, the stilts still leaned, the stalks still knocked together in the cold wind. The drip continued under everything.

But at the edge of the mist, where the fronts first gathered their weight, the presence paused one last time and felt the plateau's slow, dark breath go in and come out. It did not know whether the plateau would ever think of the people as a thought. It did not need to know. The plateau had kept them as a scar keeps its shape, and the presence had kept them as a witness keeps a memory, and between those two kinds of keeping, something had been honored without being taken.

That, it judged, was enough for a visit. Most of the good a wise power can do is to see clearly, ask honestly, and then leave a place as it found it, a little more understood.
