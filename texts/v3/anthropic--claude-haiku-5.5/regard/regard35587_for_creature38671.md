# What the Attention Did

The attention came the way the fog comes, from no direction, and it was some time before anyone in Seep-Hollow could have said it had come at all.

It was not a god in the old sense. It had no face to turn toward the town and no voice to lower into the passages. It was closer to a patience, the kind that belongs to something that has watched many lakes drain and many ridges fail and has learned that most of what happens in the world is slower than the people living through it. It had noticed the fault in the eastern ridge the way one notices a door left open in a distant house. Then it noticed the people, a small crowded knot of warmth on a grey rise, and it stayed to look.

What it saw first was the sills.

It could read the stacked thresholds under the floorboards, every generation's rise laid down in order, and it understood them as a kind of writing. No one had ever written anything so patiently. The land had lifted these families one hand at a time, and the families had answered, and the answer had been recorded in stone and kept out of sight. The attention found this moving in a way it did not have a word for, and it was careful, because it knew that its own regard could become a weight.

---

It considered what a more ordinary power would do.

It could stop the water. It could drain the silt in a single breath, pack the grey floor into bedrock, and let the people walk upright across a surface that would never again sink or rise. The flats would become a road. The cracks would close forever. The doorsills would stop climbing, and the town would be safe in the way a stone is safe.

The attention turned this over for a long time, and then set it down. The land was still deciding what shape to take. To finish it now would be to answer the question on the people's behalf, and to take from them the wager they had made so cheerfully and with so little despair. It would also erase the thresholds. There would be no record under the floors, only a flat hard ground that told no story.

It could do the opposite and make the swell violent, the cracks wide, the seeps loud. But that would only teach the people to fear their own breathing ground, and fear was the one thing they had refused to bring to the flats. They had made a rule about the turn of the tide and kept it. They had built in tiers. They had learned to walk with their weight spread out. They were already doing everything a wise thing could ask of them.

So the attention decided it would do very little. It would look, and it would listen, and it would offer only what it could give without taking the decision from anyone.

---

The first thing it gave was the sun.

It was not a large gift. The sun had always been there behind the fog, a white disc that the grey swallowed within the hour. The attention simply thinned the cloud over Seep-Hollow for a single morning, so that the light came through clean.

The people came out of their doorways squinting and turned their faces away, as they always did, and then, slowly, one by one, they did something they had never done before. They looked down.

Shadows had edges. The eaves threw dark bars across the silt. The slate walls had hard seams where the light fell along them, and every fitted stone had a corner. A woman kneeling at her sill saw the outline of her own hand laid flat on the frame, sharp as a cut, and she held very still and stared at it. A boy running the drainage cut stopped and watched his own shadow race ahead of him over the crust, and he shouted, and then remembered the rule and clapped a hand over his mouth, and the attention, which had heard many things in its time, found that small sound very dear.

Some of the older people would not look. They turned to the wall and kept their eyes on the grey stone, and the attention did not press them. It only let the light be what it was for the morning, and the people who looked gathered in the passages in low excited murmurs that carried like the hush of water, and for once they had many words for light, which they had never needed.

By noon the fog came back in from the ridge. It thickened over the roofs and took the edges off everything again, and the shadows went soft, and the town went back to its ordinary dimness. But something had changed in the way the people held their heads, and the attention did not undo it.

---

The second thing it did was listen.

The argument about the absence of large animals had been going on in Seep-Hollow for three generations, close-headed and quiet, at every gathering. Some called it a mercy and some called it an omen, and the attention, which knew better than to answer such a question for anyone, did not take a side. It did not conjure a deer out of the fog or send a bear down the drainage channels to end the debate. An answer delivered from outside would have ended the argument, and an argument this alive was worth more than any answer.

But it did one small thing. In the mussel shells the people sometimes dug up far above any seep, it let the lake remember, faintly, the way a shell holds the sound of the sea. When a child set one on the kiln shelf and the heat kept it warm for no reason anyone could name, the attention lingered there and felt the old water in it, and it was glad that the people had kept the shells, and glad that they were a little embarrassed by how much they loved them.

---

The third thing was the one the attention had not expected to do.

It had been watching the people kneel at their doorsills at dusk, press a palm flat against the frame, and wait. It had felt the touch arrive through the stone: the careful, unhurried reading of warmth and weeping and tremor. It understood this as the way these people trusted. They did not trust by believing. They trusted by feeling, and then staying still long enough to be felt back.

The attention had never touched anything in this way. It had only watched. And it understood, in a way it found both humbling and strange, that its watching had been a kind of distance, a looking-at that did not reach into the thing it looked at.

So, once, at the turn of the rise, when the silt went briefly liquid and the cracks opened to a palm's width and no one was on the flats because no one ever was, the attention laid itself against the eastern ridge the way a person lays a hand on a wall. It held there. It did not push. It only waited, and felt for the tremor under the bedrock, the slow shifting of plates that were still settling from a lake that had gone out in a single night.

The ridge answered. It did not crack. The swell came up through the stone in a soft finger-width of lift, and the attention felt it as the people must feel it, a breath in the ground that rose and held and fell. It was the same breath the sleepers in the lower tiers woke to a fraction before, the breath that tilted the sills at noon and righted them again.

The attention drew its hand back, and the land went on deciding.

---

At dusk the fog came down thick, and the attention could no longer see the edges of the roofs. It could still hear them: the dripping eaves, the hiss of seepage through the stone, the slow suck of silt somewhere in the passages where someone had stepped wrong and laughed about it. The people were kneeling at their doorsills again, stone in one hand and mallet in the other, setting the new threshold into the wet silt and tapping it true.

The attention would not stay forever. It had never meant to. It was not the kind of power that claimed the things it loved, and it understood that the best thing it could do for this place was to go on being a distant patience rather than a near hand. It did not know what the land would become. It did not know whether the people would win their wager or lose it, or whether the answer would be a mercy or an omen, or both. It did not think it should know.

What it would keep, if such a thing could be kept, was the sound of those palms against the frames, and the long breath in the ground that answered them, and the stacked stones under every floor, marking each generation's rise in the dark where no outsider would think to look.

Whatever the land became, the record of its rising would stay under their feet. The attention found that this was enough, and that it was, in its own distant way, a kind of touch.
