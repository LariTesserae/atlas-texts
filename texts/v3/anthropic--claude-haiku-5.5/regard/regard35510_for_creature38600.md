**The Attention**

It does not arrive so much as become noticed. The doubled horizon folds once, neatly, like a letter being put away, and the nearest grazer lifts its head. Then the whole herd lifts. Across the plateau, the standing ones turn a few degrees east, as they always do, but this time the motion is toward something, and the shadows that were lagging by a few seconds catch up all at once.

The power is not in the sky, though the pale blue above it seems to lean closer. It is not in the stone. It is in the attention itself, the way a room changes when someone you trust has come into it without a sound. It has been watching the Stillhigh for a long time from a distance, the way a good physician watches a patient through a window, and it has done nothing, because it did not yet know what the place wanted.

Now it knows what the place is doing. It has counted the strokes on the slabs. It has listened to the three-and-five until the pattern came apart into its parts. It has seen the herd turn its heads as one, and it has understood that the herd is not a flock but a sentence, and that the sentence has been repeating so long that no one left in it remembers what it was meant to say.

**What it considers**

It considers the obvious things first, because a wise power does not pretend the obvious is beneath it.

It could restore the sun's motion. The light could move across the sky, the shadows could fall where they belong, the stalks could bend to a dusk that actually arrives and then gives way to a dawn. The doubled horizons could be made single. The distances could be made honest.

It could bring water. Not a flood, which would be a kind of violence against a place that has finished being violent, but a slow rain across the cracked polygons, enough to lift the salt rims and make the gypsum roses swell and soften. It could let the cushions grow faster than a finger's width in a generation.

It could end the count. The slabs could be allowed to finish their last notch. The herd could be allowed to thin, one animal at a time, and not be replaced. The marks on the grazers' flanks could be left unpainted, and the tally left at its last true number.

It could do nothing at all, and let the place keep going forever, because the place has agreed to it and a power that overrides an agreement is just another kind of tyranny.

It weighs these. It is careful with the last option, because doing nothing is also a choice and it is not always innocent. It notes that the herd is not suffering in any way it can measure. The beetles are not hungry. The stalks are not dying. The silence is not a wound. The place is in equilibrium, and equilibrium is not in itself a sin.

But it notices something else, which is harder to name. The standing ones have not moved their arms in what must be thousands of years. Their posture is not peace. It is watchfulness, held past the point where watching has any object. And the maintenance, the painting of the marks and the counting of the notches and the tapping of the rhythm, is not performed for the sake of the herd or the stalks or the sun. It is performed by figures who have forgotten, or who never knew, that they could put it down.

So it reasons that the first thing is not to fix the place. The first thing is to ask.

**What it does**

It sits down.

It does this in the dust, on the level crust, a little apart from the nearest standing one, and it does not tower or radiate or speak from above. It sits the way a traveler sits when they have come a long way and are not sure they are welcome, and it lets the dust veils settle around it, which they do not do, and then rise again, which they do.

The nearest beetle comes to the edge of its circle and stops. The power taps its foot, in three, and then five, and then three. The beetle turns, as beetles here always turn, and taps back once.

Then the power does something the beetle has never heard. It taps three, and five, and three again, and then it does not tap at all. It holds the silence for a length of time that the beetle, which has counted its whole life in these groups, cannot account for. The pause goes on past the point where a pause becomes an absence. The herd lifts its heads again. The standing ones, all of them across the plateau, shift their weight a degree, the way a sleeper shifts when a sound in the house has changed.

Then the power speaks, aloud, which nothing on the Stillhigh has done in an age.

It does not give orders. It does not announce itself. It says, in a voice that the stones ring back faintly, the way they ring underfoot:

*You have kept the count. It is kept. The marks are seen. You may set down what you are holding.*

Nothing moves for a long time.

Then the standing ones begin, very slowly, to unfold their arms.

It takes a day. It takes the rest of the day, through the slow arc of a sun that has not changed its angle in an age, and the power watches every degree. The shadows, when they come, arrive on time. The doubled horizon begins to thin, the second skyline sinking down until it is only a faint line and then only the first. The folded stalks, when dusk comes, do not all fold. Some stay open, their crowns tilted up at the sky, which they have never been allowed to do.

**What it does not do**

It does not paint the marks away. It does not stop the herd from moving or tell the grazers to scatter. It does not pick up the dropped tools, though it notices which ones were left in the middle of a task and feels a kind of grief for the hands that set them down. It does not restore the makers, because it does not know who they were, and it would be a poor guest who rearranged the house before learning the names of the family.

It also does not leave. It says, quietly, to the nearest standing one: *I will keep the count tonight. You do not have to.* And when the dusk comes and the last grazer lowers its head, the power taps the three-and-five, slowly, once, and holds the silence after, and the herd, one animal at a time, begins to lower its heads too, and then to rest.

**The last tally**

In the morning, the power counts the strokes on the slabs and finds that the last notch has been cut. It was cut, it turns out, the night before, by a hand it did not see. The tally is complete. The number is what it was meant to be all along.

The grazers still carry their marks. The power does not remove them; they are not a burden, only a record. But when a grazer falls, now, no other steps forward to replace it. The herd thins, very slowly, and the thinning is not a loss but a closing. The stalks will go on folding at dusk for as long as the sun keeps its angle. The beetles will go on tapping, though now the rhythm is looser, and sometimes a pause runs one beat longer than it should, and the nearest beetle turns and waits, as if for something it has not yet learned to expect.

The power stays a while. It is not in a hurry, and neither is the place, which has never had a reason to hurry. It sits in the dust and watches the veils catch the light and not fall, and it wonders what the makers would have said, if anyone had been there to say it, to a land that agreed to keep going anyway.

Then it rises, and the second horizon folds once, neatly, and it is gone. But the attention it left behind is not the attention of the count. It is something else, quieter, and the standing ones, turning toward a sun that is finally beginning to change its angle, seem to be learning what it is.
