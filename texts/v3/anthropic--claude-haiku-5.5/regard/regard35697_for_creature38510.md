# What the Watcher Chose

The power came to the Ledge slowly, as everything here arrives. It did not descend or announce itself. It noticed the place the way a person notices a room they have walked through many times and never really looked at, and then it could not stop looking.

It saw the streams first, the few hundred meters they ran before sliding into the dark under the lava. It saw the water pooling in tubes no one would ever reach. It saw the fog rolling in off the cold edge of the shelf, the broad leaves tipping their funnels, the wick drawing water down into the cisterns. It saw a keeper's palm resting on a wall, reading the wall the way a mother reads a sleeping child's breathing, and it understood, with something like grief and something like joy, how much of this was held together by hands that had learned to be patient.

Then it began to think about what it could do.

## The Temptations

It could have done a great deal. It could have turned the streams back into the open air, carved channels through the black stone, and let the water run where anyone could drink it. It could have sent rain in the dry seasons, or cooled the fog so that the cisterns would overflow. It could have hardened the lava so the shelf would never crack again, or softened the ridges so the gliders could travel farther. It could have given the keepers speech that carried across the valley, or taught the builders to build faster, or lifted the grey lichen into full sun so the pale skin would darken and the people could walk without the half-shade.

The power considered each of these honestly, and it saw the same thing in each one. Every gift would reach past the Ledge's slowness and rearrange it. The cisterns would fill without the keepers needing to tap them, and the tapping is how the keepers know the water. The fog would come without the funnel-leaves needing their ridges, and the ridges are the town's long memory of thirst. The rain would end the long stillness between strokes, and the stillness is where the keepers do their listening. Abundance would not feed this place. It would make it into something else, a town that no longer needed to hold its breath, and a town that stops holding its breath has begun to forget that it is alive.

So the power resolved not to lift the scarcity away. It understood that scarcity had shaped the people's patience and the builders' rhythm, and that the patience was the thing it had come to admire.

## The Question of Consent

Benevolence, the power reasoned, is not the same as improvement. A gift that is not asked for is still a decision made on someone else's behalf, and the keepers had lived for centuries without a single voice from beyond the ridges. They had a language of touch and scent and grain, and they had used it to hold a city together. A power that wanted to help them had first to learn to speak it.

So it began, very carefully, to read.

It learned that a trail of two fingers dragged along bark means something like *I am here and I am listening*. It learned that a wall pressed warm and then cool means *this hollow is weeping*. It learned that the keepers do not name their dead but mark the mausoleum shafts with a spiral of moss, and that the spiral is the closest thing they have to a prayer. It did not write in their language. It only listened, and in listening it became something the keepers could feel.

## The Small Acts

There were, however, a few things it could do without taking anything from them.

The youngest lava sheet, at the lowest terrace, was still cracking. The power could feel the fracture moving toward the lower galleries, toward the place where the builders grazed and the children of the keepers sometimes slept. An eruption was not coming, but a slow slump was, a tilting of the stone that would crush a gallery in some generation to come. The power could have stopped it entirely, but that would have meant reshaping the shelf. Instead it eased the stress along the fracture line, the way a hand eases a knot out of a rope, so that the stone would settle gently over centuries and give the builders time to resin the cracks as they opened. It asked nothing for this. No one would ever know it had been done, and that was the point.

The power also left one thing where the keepers would find it. At the eastern edge of the town, where the trunks had not yet met and a gap had been open for as long as anyone could remember, a single seed of the hard-shelled fruit lay on the bark. It was not a gift of water or light. It was only a seed, the kind that needed to be carried over a ridge to ripen. The gliders would have to choose to carry it, or not. If they did, a new tree might root somewhere beyond the town's edge, and the keepers would have to decide what to do with a new tree that belonged to no one. That decision was theirs.

## The Watching

At dusk the nodes on the trunks turned west, toward the ridges and the lava, and held still through the night. The power felt them turn and understood that they were watching for something, though it did not know what. It came to suspect that the nodes were not eyes in the sense the keepers meant, but something older and quieter, a way the town kept its attention awake while its people slept.

The power did not pretend to be a node. It did not claim the watch. But it chose to keep watch too, in its own way, from beyond the ridges. It would not come down through the canopy unless it was asked to, or unless something came that the keepers could not hold alone, a fire, a flood through the tubes, a sickness in the builders that spread faster than resin could seal it. In those cases it would act, quickly and as little as possible. Until then it would simply remain.

## What It Left Unsaid

In the end the power did not write its name into the mortar or leave a sign that would frighten the keepers into thinking the gods had come down to save them. It did not tell them that the water had been tended, or that the fracture had been eased, or that a seed had been left at the eastern gap. It let the town go on as it had for centuries. The water would still be taken from the air, drop by drop, and the cisterns would still hold through the dry seasons, and the keepers would still sit in the long stillness between their strokes, watching the light move on the leaves.

If, many years from now, a keeper pressed a palm to a wall in the eastern district and felt, beneath the usual rise and dip of the resin, a faint warmth that was not hers or the builders', they would not know what it meant. They might wonder. They might mark the wall with two fingers and wait to see if anything answered.

The power would not answer, not yet. It would wait too. Waiting, after all, was what the work of the Ledge had always been.
