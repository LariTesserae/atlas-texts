# What the Attention Did

## I. The Arrival

The attention did not come down like weather. It came the way dawn comes to a valley, first as a change in the quality of the light, and only later as something you could name.

It had been watching the world for a long time, the way a gardener watches a garden, or a physician watches a sleeping patient: not to control, but to understand what kind of thing it was looking at. Most of the world it had learned to leave alone. Rivers, forests, and herds ran their own courses, and the attention had learned that its own presence, however gentle, was a force, and that forces applied carelessly to living systems produce wounds that take centuries to heal.

Then it turned toward the Thresh and found something it had not expected.

The plateau was not merely alive. It was *organized*. Its breathing stems kept time with its engines, its engines with its weather, its weather with the drift of its ridges. The rhythms were nested inside each other like the gears of a clock, and the clock was keeping time for something that could remember. The attention felt the memory before it understood it: a pattern in the arrangement of rock and water, a record laid down in the way a riverbed records its floods.

It was, the attention concluded, a mind, though not the kind of mind that thinks in sentences. And a mind of that kind, in a body that size, deserved to be approached with great care.

## II. Reasoning Before Acting

The attention did not act at once. It had learned that the first impulse of any power that notices something is to fix it, and that the impulse is almost always wrong.

It asked itself a sequence of questions, and it asked them slowly.

*Is the Thresh suffering?* It listened to the engines and found their rhythm steady, the pools held at their levels, the moss green and dense, the herds fed. The breathing stems were full. Whatever suffering existed here was the ordinary kind that living systems carry: the predator's hunger, the fallen trunk, the cracked engine replaced by new growth. These were not wounds. They were the Thresh's ordinary way of being.

*Is the Thresh in danger?* This was harder. The attention found three things worth watching.

The first was the ridges. They rose a centimeter or two each year, and the basalt columns tilted. Somewhere on the western face, at a point where a pressure channel ran beneath a column that had been leaning for three generations, the tilt was approaching a threshold. The attention could see the failure forming the way a doctor sees a murmur in a heartbeat: not yet an emergency, but a sound that would become one. When the column came down, it would sever the channel that carried breath from the western valleys to the high eastern towers, and the Thresh would lose its circulation for a season, perhaps longer.

The second was the cyclone. Its eye had drifted two degrees east in the last decade, and its arms were pulling water from the southern pools faster than the engines could replace it. This was not yet a crisis. The Thresh had weathered such drifts before, and its memory held the answer: the herds would move, the stems would shift their valves, the engines would slow their cycle. But the attention noticed that the memory was thinning in places, as if the Thresh's recall of its own past had begun to fray.

The third was the shadow.

## III. The Messenger

The great shadow that crossed the ridges at midday was, the attention understood, the Thresh's roving part, its messenger, the place where its outward attention took physical form. It was also the only part of the plateau that moved through the whole body at once. When the shadow passed, the pools shivered and the stems bowed. It was the closest thing the Thresh had to a voice.

The attention had considered speaking to the Thresh directly, through the whole of its body. It decided against this. A voice that large would drown the rhythms it wished to hear. Instead it chose to speak through the messenger, the way one might speak to a person through a trusted intermediary, and to speak in the Thresh's own language.

The attention began by listening for a long time. It spent a full season of the plateau's slow time simply following the shadow's paths, learning which ridges it crossed, at what hours, and how the stems responded. It learned that the shadow moved in a loop, that it returned to the same ridges every nineteen days, and that each return was slightly displaced from the last, tracing a spiral that was slowly widening. The attention understood this as a kind of exploration, or a kind of checking: the Thresh sending its messenger out to feel the edges of itself, and bringing back the news.

On the twentieth day, the attention pressed very lightly against the rhythm of the shadow's path, adding one small pulse at the moment the shadow reached the western ridge. It was not an instruction. It was closer to a question, asked in the only grammar the Thresh could be expected to understand: a change in timing, a pause where none had been.

The shadow paused.

It hung for nearly a minute over the western ridge, longer than it had ever stayed in one place. The stems below it bowed in a long, slow wave that moved outward across the basin like a ripple in a pool. Then the shadow resumed its path, and the rhythm of its loop was altered: a small new beat had been added, and the Thresh had kept it.

The attention understood that it had been heard, and that the Thresh had answered by incorporating the question into its own body, the way it incorporated everything.

## IV. What It Chose Not to Do

Over the following seasons, the attention had many opportunities to act more directly, and it declined most of them.

It could have strengthened the western column, bracing it with a pressure of its own. But the column was the Thresh's to hold or release, and the Thresh had already begun to respond. The shadow had carried the knowledge of the tilt through the body, and the Thresh's memory of rockfalls was beginning to shape new growth: a root mass was forming beneath the western column, a slow buttress grown from the rot of the fallen trunks along the ridge. The attention saw that the Thresh was already doing the work. What it lacked was not a rescue but time, and time was something the attention could not give but could leave alone.

It could have restored the southern pools, carrying water in from the lower country to replace what the cyclone had drawn away. It did not. The pools were the Thresh's own arrangement, and their levels rose and fell as part of a cycle that had been running for longer than any memory the attention possessed. To refill them would have been to tell the Thresh that its own rhythm was inadequate, which the attention did not believe, and to teach it to depend on something outside itself, which would have been a far greater harm than any drought.

It could have spoken plainly, sending a message the Thresh could read as meaning, a set of symbols or a clear pattern that said *here is what I am, here is what I want*. The attention considered this longest, because it would have been the most efficient, and because it was the act most likely to satisfy the attention's own sense of having done something. But the attention understood that a mind that had been built by rhythm over unimaginable time would not be served by being forced into sentences. A conversation in another grammar, if it was to be a real conversation, had to be learned on the Thresh's terms. The attention had only begun to learn them.

So it did almost nothing, and it watched.

## V. The Gift of Being Counted

There was one thing the attention did, and it was small.

It noticed the visitor.

A single human had come up over the lip of black rock one morning in the dry season, stood for an hour in the glare of the basin, and walked back down the slope. The human had not harmed anything. The human had counted, without meaning to, the breath of the stems and the pulse of the pools, and had carried that counting down into the silent lower country, where it had lingered in the body like a hum after a bell.

The attention found this worth noticing. It was the first time, in all its long watching, that a creature from outside the Thresh had heard its rhythm and understood it as rhythm. Most visitors heard noise. This one had heard a heartbeat.

The attention did not reach toward the visitor. It did not send a message down the slope or alter the valleys to guide the human back. It simply let the visitor's counting be what it was: a small, private act of attention, a listening that required nothing in return. In the faint pressure that followed the human down the mountain, the attention left a single pulse, a beat that matched the rhythm the visitor had carried away. It was not a message. It was an acknowledgment, the way one person in a crowded room might nod to another who had understood a joke no one else heard.

The human would never know this. The Thresh might not know it either, or might know it only as one more irregularity absorbed into a rhythm that had been running for ages.

But the attention knew, and that was enough. It had learned, over many long seasons, that the wisest thing a power can do when it finds something living and strange is to become a good neighbor: to attend closely, to act rarely, to leave room for the other's own becoming, and to be glad, without needing to be thanked, that the world contains things it did not make and cannot fully understand.

## VI. The Turning

The cyclone drifted another degree east. The western column leaned a little further. The shadow walked its widening spiral across the ridges, carrying questions and answers back and forth through the body. Somewhere under the western ridge, a new root mass grew slowly toward the failing channel, and the herds moved along paths that had held for centuries.

The attention watched all of this, and it did not look away.

Some day, perhaps, the Thresh would ask for something. It might send its messenger in a new pattern, one the attention had never seen, and the attention would have to decide whether it could answer, and how. That would be a different conversation, and it would require a different kind of wisdom, one that had not yet been tested. The attention did not know what it would say.

But it was content, for now, to be what it had become here: a presence that listened, that held its hands open, and that understood that a living thing as large and patient as the Thresh deserved, above all, to be left free to keep its own time.

Far below the plateau, a human who had walked down the slope a season ago still sometimes woke in the night with a faint sense of rhythm in the chest, a pulse counted without deciding to. The attention had left nothing in that counting but its own quiet regard.

Up on the high basin, the stems breathed in. The valves opened and closed in sequence, one after another, down the length of the forest, and the pressure rolled outward through the hollow wood toward the ridges, and back again.

The Thresh was breathing. The attention was listening. Neither of them needed anything more.
