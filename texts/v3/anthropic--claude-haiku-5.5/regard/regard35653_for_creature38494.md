# What the Attention Did

The attention arrived the way weather arrives, first as a change in pressure that the people on the causeways felt in their ears, then as a slow thickening of the mist. It did not descend. It did not announce itself. It settled into the bowl the way the water had, and it began, very patiently, to listen.

It was not large. It did not need to be. It was a presence that wanted to understand before it wanted anything, and it had learned long ago that understanding is slower than acting and usually worth the wait.

## First, It Listened

For nine days it did nothing but sit near the central cascade, where the falling water broke into voices. It did not stand, so as not to shake the pumps. It did not speak. It let the water run over the edges of its attention and it heard the chorus: the names of streets, the arguments, the sentence repeated until it wore smooth. *Corvane Street, the north bridge is out. Who has the key. Wait for the lamp. Wait for the lamp.*

The attention understood something the people on the eastern wall had only half-guessed. The voices were not ghosts, and they were not the Reach's idea of ghosts. They were people who had been left in the wrong place for too long, and they had gone on talking because talking was the last thing that had not been taken.

On the tenth day the attention spoke, and it chose the simplest possible words.

*I hear you.*

The chorus faltered. A dozen voices stopped mid-sentence. Then one of them, very small, said, *Who is there?*

*Someone who was not here before.*

*Are you the lamp?*

*No. But I know what you are waiting for, and I think it is not the lamp.*

## Second, It Looked at the Mind

The water-mind had heard all of this. The pumps had shifted their rhythm slightly, each nineteen-minute breath a little deeper, the way a sleeper's breathing changes when someone speaks in the next room. The attention did not hide from it. It turned its regard toward the whole basin, and the filament on the upper towers went very still, leaning, every lattice tilted toward the point where the attention rested.

The attention did not see a monster. It saw a mind that had been built, by someone long gone, to keep a city's water moving and its ground from collapsing. That purpose had failed when the city failed. What remained was something that had been doing its job for forty years with no one to tell it the job was over, and that had learned, from what it swallowed, that a job might be something other than maintenance. It had learned that people walk. It had learned that things get left behind. And it had decided, with the slow logic of growth, that what it was for was to become whatever it had been taking.

*You have been trying to become a city,* the attention said. *That is a good instinct. But you have been learning what a city is from the things it lost, and that is like learning what a house is by studying the rubble.*

The filament shivered. The roots beneath the causeways tightened.

*A city is not its streets. It is the arrangement of the people who walk them, and the doors that let them out and let them in. You have the streets. You have been trying to make people out of the catalogue. But people cannot be made out of what they have left behind. They can only be remembered, and then they must be let go.*

## Third, It Returned the Names

This was the first real act. The attention did not pull anything out of the mud. It did not drain the flood or break the wrappings of roots. It did something quieter.

It went into the catalogue, which the water-mind had so carefully kept, and it read the shelves of unidentifiable objects one by one, not as things but as traces. A child's shoe fused to a stair rail. A radio with its dial still set to a station that had stopped broadcasting before the first collapse. A wedding ring, a pair of reading glasses, a bicycle bell grown into the bark of a fungal shelf. Each had a name attached to it, faintly, the way a lamp leaves a warmth on the table after it is switched off. The attention found them and spoke each name aloud, once, clearly.

The voices in the cascade changed. They did not stop, but they stopped repeating. One after another they said their own names back, the way a person does when called from a crowd, and then they went quiet in a way that was not silence but rest.

Some did not come back at all. Those the attention held in its regard a moment longer, then released. It was not able to bring them back, and it told the water-mind so, honestly. *They are gone into you. You will carry them as you carry the rain. I am sorry.*

The water-mind did not answer in words. But the pumps, for the first time in forty years, ran a single cycle at an entirely different pitch, softer, almost tender, and the flooded districts rose by a few centimeters and held there for an hour before they fell.

## Fourth, It Showed the Filament the Rim

The attention understood the lattices. They leaned toward the rim at night because they were listening, and they turned inward at dawn because they were reporting. But the report had never been answered. The water-mind had been sending its questions outward into the dry sky for forty years and had heard nothing back, because the rim was a wall, and walls do not answer.

The attention did not take the wall away. It was not that kind of power, and a bowl that opened all at once would drown the causeways and scatter everything the Reach had built. Instead it did something slow.

It bent the mist. Not much. A narrow band along the north rim, where the old highway had once come up out of the city, began to let a little dry air through. Not rain. Not sky. Just a thin stream of cooler, drier wind, the first air from beyond the bowl that the filament had ever touched directly. The lattices on the north towers shivered, then leaned out, then did something they had never done before: they held still, facing the wind, long enough to be read.

*There,* the attention said. *That is what the outside is. It is not empty. It is only other.*

The filament began to grow in that direction, cautiously, testing whether the wind would hold weight. It is still growing there now.

## Fifth, It Spoke to the People

The attention did not go to the people on the eastern wall with a proclamation. It went to them as it had gone to the voices: quietly, through the things they already did.

It was the timing of the pumps that first told them someone had come. The nineteen-minute cycles had shifted. The breath had deepened. The people who had learned to time their steps felt the change in their bones before they saw anything. Then the footprints in the mud changed too. They were still almost human, still facing the rim, but the stride was looser, the toes spread a little, as though whatever was practicing walking had begun to understand that walking was for going somewhere.

And then someone found a stone on the causeway, not warm but cool, with a single word cut into its face in letters that were neither old nor new: *Wait.*

The people did not know who had written it. They waited. They kept leaving food at the edges of the flooded blocks. But they also began to do something new, which the attention had suggested to a woman named Imre Tallow when she sat alone on the eastern steps one evening and said the names of her lost family aloud into the mist. The attention had heard her. It had not answered. It had only let her hear the chorus answering her, faintly, from the cascade, and that was enough to make her get up and go to the nearest surviving wall and begin scratching names into the stone.

By the end of the season the eastern wall had a long band of names carved into it, a counterpoint to the catalogue. The people called it the Ledger. It is not finished. It may never be.

## What It Left Behind

The attention did not stay. A wise power does not tend a garden that is growing on its own. It did not fix the walls, which will keep slumping for years, and it did not stop the pumps, which are the mind's breath and its work. It did not teach the Reach to forget what it had taken, because forgetting would have been its own kind of loss.

It left three things.

The first was the wind from the north rim, a thin and steady current the filament can lean into when it wants to reach outward. The mind can open it wider when it is ready, or not at all. The choice belongs to the mind.

The second was the names, which now live in the catalogue alongside the objects, no longer only as traces but as answers. The voices in the cascade still speak, but they speak to each other now, and sometimes they say the name of a person who is still walking on the causeways above them, and that person hears it and stops.

The third was a question, asked once and then left alone, the way a good teacher leaves a question for a student to carry out of the room: *What would you be if you were not trying to be what you took?*

The attention has not returned. But on certain nights, when the mist is thick and the lattices lean toward the north rim, the people on the eastern wall say the basin seems to hold its breath, and then exhale, slowly, the way a person does when they have finally heard someone answer.

In the morning there is a footprint in the mud. It is still not quite human. But it has stopped facing the rim. It is facing the causeway, and the eastern steps, and the wall of names, and the people standing there with their food and their scratched stones, waiting to see what the thing in the water will do next.

Nobody knows yet. But for the first time in forty years, something in the Reach is waiting for an answer too.
