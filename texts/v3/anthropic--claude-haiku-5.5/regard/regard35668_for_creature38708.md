# The Attention

It does not arrive as a body or a voice. It arrives as a slight change in the weather, a pressure that settles over the high center and stays there longer than any storm should. The cloud anvil that forms that night turns more slowly than usual, as if it is listening. The drip of the tors falls briefly into rhythm, then breaks apart again.

Whatever it is, it is wise, and it is careful, and its first act is to hold still and look.

It takes in the whole plateau at once: the stepped shelves, the weeping faces of the tors, the hanging gardens of moss, the grazers bunched on the seep lines, the burrowers breathing through their hidden vents. It follows the grooves of the basalt as it would follow the lines of a very old text, and it sees what the walkers see, that the water here does not simply fall. It turns. It reverses. The stone has a rule, and the rule has a logic, and the logic is a kind of mind that never quite declares itself.

Then it notices the walkers, and something in its attention sharpens.

It watches a pair settle on either side of a narrow gorge. It feels the driver's slow patience move through the mist, the herd turning at its approach, the column bending toward the lip of the shelf. It feels the waiter's stillness, the chest pressed to the stone, the eyes half-closed, the moment of waiting. It feels the terror of the grazers as the water begins to reverse under them, and it feels, too, the hunger of the walkers, which is not cruelty but a plain and urgent need, the same need that drives the flyers to the carrion and the fungi to their bloom.

It does not judge this quickly. It has the patience of something that has watched many worlds.

---

## The Reasoning

The power considers, in the slow way that weather considers, what it might do.

It could end the hunt. It could remove the walkers from the stone, or strip them of their sense for the grooves, so the herds could graze the shelves unafraid. But it sees what would follow. Without the walkers, the herds would multiply past what the shelves can feed. The thin soil would give out. The grazers would walk the seep lines in their thousands, trampling the moss and the fern gardens into mud, and within a few generations there would be fewer animals on the plateau, not more, and those that remained would die slowly of hunger in the dry years, which come as surely as the wet ones. The walkers are not the stone's enemies. They are one of the ways the stone keeps its count. The power finds that it cannot remove a single thread without tugging at all of them, and it is not wise enough, it knows, to see every thread.

It could take the herds away from the stone and put them somewhere gentler. But that would be a kind of uprooting, and the grazers are not unhappy in their shelves. They have learned the seep lines. They have built their columns around the walkers' absence from the lower ground. The power does not want to decide, from above, that their lives are wrong.

It could reach toward the furnace. This is the question that holds it longest. The glow under the clouds, the throb from the southeast, the warm air that rises from the basin and gives the plateau its rain. The power perceives that the furnace is not a natural thing. It is someone's work. There are hands in it, or minds, or purposes it cannot read from here. The rains that feed the walkers and the grazers are a byproduct of an intention the power does not understand. It would be presumptuous to reach into a workshop whose function it has not yet learned, to shut it down because its heat is strange, or to change its output because the cycle below it is beautiful. Whatever the furnace is for, the power sees that it is not for the plateau. The plateau is downstream of it, the way a valley is downstream of a distant mountain.

So the power decides it will not touch the furnace. Not yet. It will listen.

---

## What It Does

What it does is small.

At the narrowest crossing, the place where the seep lines converge and the flood-channels pin the herds in their worst drownings, the power makes a single adjustment. It does not change the rule of the stone. It only lets the water begin its reversal a little earlier, a fraction of an hour, so that the grazers caught in the narrows have time to climb. The old calves, the slow ones, the ones who have always been the first to be pinned in the pools, now have a chance. The walkers lose a little of the certainty that made their waiting so precise. They will have to watch the grooves more closely. Some nights they will go hungry. The power knows this and accepts it. It does not want to make the hunt easy. It wants the hunt to remain a contest, because a contest is what keeps the herds strong and the walkers sharp.

It also does one thing for the walkers, though they will never know it. In the deep quiet of the stone, the throb from the southeast has always reached them as a steady pressure in their pads. The power lets it reach them more clearly. Not louder, and not with any message. Just clearer, so that the walkers, lifting their heads toward the wind and holding still, feel the far-off breathing as a thing with a shape rather than only a weight. They will not understand it any better. But they will know that something on the other side of the cloud is alive, and that the stone has carried this knowledge to them for a long time, and that someone has noticed.

Then the power withdraws most of its attention and leaves the rest of the plateau to itself.

---

## The Remainder

It knows that it may be wrong. A reversal that comes earlier might save a calf and cost a walker its meal, and that meal might have been the difference for a cub it cannot see. A clearer throb might trouble the walkers, or draw them toward the lower shelves, toward the basin and its heat and the machinery that makes no sense to them. It cannot follow every consequence. It is only one attention among many, and the plateau has been turning its water on its own for ten million years without asking anyone's permission.

So the power holds itself loosely. It keeps watching. It will return to the high center when the next great storm forms, and it will feel how the herds respond to the earlier reversal, and whether the walkers have adapted, and whether the throb has changed. It will learn. If the furnace ever shows a sign that it is harming the living things downstream, the power will think harder about what to do, and it will try to do it gently, and it will try, above all, not to become the thing that decides for the stone.

The rain begins to fall over the shelves, slow and steady, and the tors weep as they always have. Down in the narrows, the water turns. On either side of the gorge, two walkers lie flat against the wet basalt and read the grooves. One of them lifts its head toward the southeast, where the throb is coming through the stone, a little clearer than before.

It holds still, listening, and the power, watching from the cloud, holds still with it.
