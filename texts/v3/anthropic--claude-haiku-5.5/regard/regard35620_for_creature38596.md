# The Attention

It began at the rim, the way weather begins.

For a long time the one who watched had been looking at the wide country of the plain, the rivers and granaries and roads, the places where people had decided to stay. Then the light over the broken lip of Ansk Hollow changed, and the watcher's attention settled there and stayed.

What it saw first was the Forge. It did not see creatures or pools or terraces. It saw a process working through all of them, a pattern of water and heat and mineral that kept returning to itself, reshaping its own skin each day. The watcher had seen many kinds of making: the slow growth of forests, the sedimentary patience of seas, the sharper work of volcanoes. This was different. The Hollow was making itself out of its own deaths, and it did not seem to mind.

The watcher paused over that. It did not yet know whether the Hollow was a being in the sense that a wolf or a woman is a being. It knew only that the basin answered to pressure, that it had rhythm, and that something in it was attending to the rain.

# The Temptation

The watcher's first impulse was to tidy.

It saw the grazers scraping the terraces and the shellbacks piling their graveyards along the lower flats. It saw mud-diggers collapsing their own tunnels and vent-hangers dying slowly in their curtains of salt. It saw, too, the cracked cairns and sunken slabs of the makers, and the pits lined with hammered stone that the water kept overrunning, and it wanted to hold the water back. It wanted to make the ground hold for a season, to stop the rains from undoing what people had worked to build.

The watcher knew this impulse for what it was. The basin had its own logic, and the watcher had come to understand that a wise presence does not reach into a living process because the process looks wasteful from outside. A river that floods its valley is not wrong to flood. A forest that burns clears ground for the next growth. If the watcher stilled the rain, it would not be saving the Hollow. It would be interrupting the one thing the Hollow is.

So it held still and listened.

# What the Hollow Said

The Hollow does not speak in words. It speaks in pattern: the cracking of crust across the bowl, the long collapse of a travertine stalk, the timing of storms, the way a terrace rises a finger's width each night and falls away at dusk. The watcher spent a long time reading it, and what it found was not hostility, nor indifference, nor even a plan. It was closer to a patient attention. The Hollow registered the grazers the way a body registers its own blood. The shells, the salts, the bodies, the cairns: all of them went into the same slow working.

The watcher asked, in the only language it had, whether the Hollow wanted anything.

The answer came, after a long while, as the stalk nearest the rim cracked open and let its fragments roll down the slope. The sound went around the whole bowl and back, and in the silence after it the watcher felt something like an answer: *I am finishing. I am not finished.*

The watcher understood then that the Hollow's unfinished state was its nature, and that the makers, who built for the next morning, were already closer to the Hollow's own wisdom than the watcher had been.

# The Makers

There was one among them that the watcher kept returning to.

She came down from the rim each morning with a cart and a short-handled hammer, and she worked a pit near the eastern seep that she had lined three times. Each time the water came through. Each time she dug it out, hammered new stone into the floor, and filled the pit with pale sediment. Her hands were raw with it. She was not foolish. She knew the pit would go, probably before the next storm. She kept lining it anyway, and when the rain came she sat in the lee of the rim with her back to the stone and watched the water take the work, and then went down again the next morning.

The watcher considered her carefully. It considered whether to give her something lasting, a wall that would not fall, a crossing that would not sink, a shelter that the Hollow could not reach. It was within its power. It was also precisely the kind of gift that would teach her the wrong lesson: that the answer to impermanence is to resist it, that the ground can be made to hold if you are stubborn enough.

Instead the watcher did something smaller, and it did it with care.

It did not give her a wall. It gave her a morning. On the day she lined the pit for the fourth time, the rain held off until the crust had set hard and the mud had drawn back from the eastern seep. The water came, as it always did, but it came slowly, a seep rather than a flood, and the sediment in her pit had time to harden into a lip that held for an afternoon. She stood at the edge of it and looked down, and the watcher felt her understanding settle in a way that was not quite a thought: the ground will move, and there is a kind of work that moves with it.

Then the rain came hard and the pit was gone by dusk, and she climbed back to the rim without looking back.

The watcher did not interfere again.

# The Mark

Before it left, the watcher did one more thing. It did not mark the Hollow. It marked the rim.

On the broken lip, where the shelters of the makers stood in the lee of the stone, there was a single slab of pale layered rock that had split from the cliff long before any maker came. The watcher pressed the slab, very lightly, so that a hairline crack ran through its face, and into the crack it set a single mineral seed, a grain of the same salt that crusted the vent-hangers, a grain that would never be absorbed into the floor because it lay above the water line.

The grain would grow, slowly, over centuries, into a small white crystal, fluted and glittering, that anyone coming down from the plain could see from the rim. It would mark the place where people had come down to work the Hollow and had learned to build for the next morning.

It was not a monument. It would not survive the Hollow's long remaking, and it was not meant to. It was meant to be seen by the next person who stood on the lip, looking down at the map being redrawn in real time, and wondering what had lived there and what they had made.

# What the Watcher Understood

The watcher left the Hollow as it had found it, almost. The grazers kept scraping. The shellbacks kept building their domes and dying in the wrong places. The mud-diggers kept breathing beneath the flats. The vent-hangers kept glittering, the wing-spinners kept diving, and the rain kept coming in brief, violent bursts that turned the whole bowl to running brown water and then sank away.

The watcher had not saved anything. It had not fixed anything, because nothing in the Hollow was broken. What it had done was smaller: it had let a woman have one good afternoon, and it had left a seed on the rim for whoever came next.

Its last reflection, before turning its attention back toward the plain, was this. The Hollow was not waiting to be finished. It was the finishing, endlessly. The creatures were not visitors but its materials and its memory, and the makers were not trespassers but a small, provisional part of the same slow work. A wise power does not mistake that for a problem. It learns to attend, to give what is needed in the moment, to leave a mark that will be taken back, and to trust the ground to decide what it will become.

On the rim, the crystal grain began, very slowly, to grow.
