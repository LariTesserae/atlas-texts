**What the Attention Did**

It did not arrive as a storm or a voice. It came the way morning comes to a valley, gradually, from every direction at once, until the plateau was simply being noticed.

At first it only listened. It had learned long ago that the most dangerous thing a powerful and well-meaning mind can do is act on a place it does not understand, so it resolved to wait through a full cycle before touching anything. It listened to the mist, the drip of the cliffs, the rasp of the silver shrubs, and beneath all of it the hum, which it came to hear as a long, patient breath.

Within a season it understood that the lattices were speaking to one another. The filaments drew inward in slow waves, and each wave seemed to answer a neighbor's wave a few days earlier. When one lattice fell silent, the nearby ones tightened their threads, the way a family tightens around an empty chair. Their tops reached toward the cloud banks, and when a single rain broke over one patch of ground, the lattices there shivered with a change in their hum that might have been satisfaction, or might have been nothing the power had a word for.

The power considered who had built them and why. The troughs were the most telling clue. They were not random erosion. They were roads, worn so deep that the herds now walked exactly along the grooves, and so the makers had shaped the land for travel, for water, for a rhythm they expected to keep. Perhaps they had been gathering water for a people who were thirsty, and that people had gone. Perhaps the lattices had been meant to hold the air's moisture for some future need. Or perhaps the makers had wanted a place that could take care of itself. The power could not know. It noticed that it wanted to know, and it noticed that wanting to know was not the same as having a right to decide.

So it weighed what it might do, and it did very little.

It did not take the lattices apart to learn their secrets. It did not name the water-folk or count the herds. It did not try to make the plateau more productive, or prettier, or more legible to outsiders. Its reasoning was simple. The place was already in balance, and that balance had survived far longer than any intervention the power could offer. Any hand laid on it would be a hand that could slip.

But there were a few things it could do, and it did them gently.

When the lattice field began to thin, the power did not stop the shrinking, since the fading of old machines is part of their nature and the plateau seemed to accept it. Instead it made sure the survivors were not left alone. Where one lattice had gone to grey powder, the single cloud drifted over its nearest neighbors for an extra afternoon, so that the threads tightening around the empty space were not thirsty as well as grieving.

When a long dry season threatened the pools, the power let the water-folk's eggs wait. They had waited through dry years before, and their patience was older than any drought. It was not the power's place to wake them early. But it kept the clouds moving along their old course, the one the lattices seemed to remember, so that when the rain came it came in its proper rhythm and did not scatter.

It also considered the wing-riders, who alone could see the whole of the troughs at once. It wondered what they would make of the plateau if they could understand it, and it decided that they might be its best witnesses. It let them keep their thin calls and their solitary measuring. It did not want to become a thing they had to watch.

Last, the power did the thing that seemed most important. It made itself quiet. A mind of its strength could easily have become the loudest presence in the place, drowning the hum with its own attention. Instead it let its attention settle into the background, like the mist, like the cloud over the pools, so that the herds could keep their low, careful paths, the runners could keep flickering between shadows, and the lattices could keep breathing. If the plateau had been a machine with a purpose, the power did not want to replace the purpose. If it had been a living place that had grown around the machine's ruin, the power did not want to be the new master of its growth.

Then it withdrew, not far, but far enough that the place could forget it. It left behind a single afternoon of rain falling on a lattice that had never been rained on before, and a hope, which was not a plan, that the place would go on drawing water out of nothing and passing it from one life to the next, slowly, without needing to ask why.

The hum continued beneath the stone, steady as a heartbeat, and no one who lived there noticed the change, which was exactly what the power had intended.
