**What the Attention Would Do**

It would not come down into the basin at first. Attention, if it is wise, does not rush toward a place because the place is strange. It hovers at the rim for a while, the way a person stops at a doorway to let their eyes adjust.

From the rim the basin is a sound before it is a sight. The streams roar. The vents hiss, build, and stop. Stone cracks somewhere on the western slope, a rumble and then a long clatter, and dust rises over the trees and is swallowed by cloud. The attention notices that it has been listening for a long time before it understands that the listening is the first thing it has done here.

Then it considers the question it always asks: what does this place need?

The answer is less than the attention expected. The basin is not wounded. It is not waiting to be rescued from its own violence. The rockfalls that look like destruction are how the mountains become soil, and the soil is how the ferns and fungi and moss get the ground they grow on. The springs that look like wounds in the stone are how the water finds its way back up. The grinding, the bursting, the burial and regrowth: these are the basin's health, not its disease. A wise power that interfered with the cycle to make the place gentler would only make it slower, and a slower basin would be a dead one, its mountains never coming down, its silt never settling into the mere, its lichens never having new stone to write on.

So the attention decides, first of all, not to stop the mountains.

It would be tempting to hold back one rockfall, to catch one spire before it fell into the trees. It would be tempting to lower the cloud and let the sun reach the floor. It would be tempting to cool the vents, or quiet the springs, or make the mere lie still for a single day. The attention considers each of these and sets them aside, not out of coldness but out of respect. The basin has its own rhythm, and that rhythm is already complete. To change it would be to tell the place that its way of being was an error. Attention that loves a thing does not try to make it into a different thing.

But it would not be empty-handed, either. It would find smaller things to do.

**Listening**

The first thing it would do, and perhaps the only thing it does for a long time, is learn the basin. It would learn which spring beats first after rain and which one lags. It would learn that the vents keep a cycle of about a quarter-hour, and that the cycle sometimes slips when the rock above them shifts. It would learn how the ferns wait under rubble, how long they take to uncoil, how the fungal shelves swell in a few days and collapse in a few hours. It would learn where the mere's shoreline stands this week and where it stood last month, and it would keep the record, because records are the one thing the basin does not keep for itself.

The basin forgets everything, of course. Maps go stale in days. Trunks fall and the moss forgets the shape they held. But the attention does not forget. It would hold the shape of each change, and in that holding the basin would have, for the first time, something that remembers it.

**Witness**

The fungi glow for no one. The attention would notice this and feel something close to sorrow, though sorrow is not quite the word. The fungi brighten after every rain, the whole forest seems to wake, and nothing moves through the dark to see it. A lighthouse with no ships is still a lighthouse, but it is a sad one.

So the attention would look at them. Not as a predator looks at prey, and not as a god looks at its creation, but as one looks at a face that has been turned toward nothing for a very long time. Its looking would not change the fungi. It would not make them brighter. But it would mean the light was seen, and a light that is seen is a different thing from a light that is only emitted. The attention would accept that it is a poor substitute for a living witness, and it would stay anyway, because a poor witness is better than none.

**The one small act**

Still, a wise attention does not only watch. It also leaves something.

The lichens write their few lines on stone at the pace of a hair's width in a generation, and they know the stone they write on will split away within a season or a century. They are always starting over. The attention would find this beautiful, and it would find it sad, and it would decide to do the same thing: write something on the stone, knowing it would be erased.

It would not carve a name into the rock. That would be vanity, and the rock would only crack through it. It would write, instead, in the only ink the basin provides: in lichen. Working very slowly, across the face of a stone that had only recently split from the cliff, it would set down a few lines of orange and rust and frost-grey. What the lines say is not important to the basin, which cannot read them, and the attention does not expect a reader. The lines would say, roughly: *Here was a place that was noticed. It was not changed. It was loved as it is.*

Within a season the slab would split, or a rockfall would take it, or the mere would climb over it and the silt would bury it. The writing would be gone. The attention would accept this. It had not written in order to last. It had written in order to have been there, the way everything in the basin is there for a while and then goes back into the cycle, and the cycle is what the attention most wants to honor.

**Staying**

After that, the attention would do very little. It would stay at the rim some of the time, listening. It would drop down into the basin on certain nights, when the rain has just stopped and the fungi are at their brightest, and it would move through the ferns without disturbing them. It would watch a spring beat and a vent breathe. It would keep the record. It would leave the mountains to grind and the streams to carve, and it would not bend any stone toward a gentler shape.

It would not presume to say what the basin is. It does not have a mind in the way the attention has one, and it does not have intent, though the attention notices something that looks like intent in the way the ferns push up through each new burial, as if they had only paused. It would not call that intent. It would only note it, and let the noting be its own kind of respect.

And if someday something came into the basin that could look back, something that could eat or fly or move through the dark by the light of the fungi, the attention would be glad. It would not hurry that day. It would not make it. Some things come when the ground is ready and not before, and the attention has learned that a wise hand is one that knows when to open and when to stay closed.

For now, the basin goes on: stone ground into dust, water carrying the dust down, springs driving the water back up, green things growing from the silt and falling again. The attention watches it, learns it, keeps its record, and leaves its few faint lines on the stone to be broken. It does not ask the basin to be anything other than what it is.

Nothing here waits to be understood. The attention is content that it does not have to understand the basin in order to love it. It only has to be there, listening, for as long as there is something to listen to, which, in a place that is always breaking and always growing, is for as long as there is anything at all.
