**What the Attending Saw**

It came the way attention comes to a place that has been quiet for a long time: slowly, then all at once, the way a sleeper notices a clock that has stopped.

It noticed first the drip. Every leaf in the cloud forest released its bead of water at an interval that could have been counted, and the intervals did not vary by a breath across the whole of the Shelf. Then it noticed the pools, which held their level as a held breath holds, and the fog, which lifted on the hour and settled on the hour. Then it noticed the carving on the lava columns: a figure at the ridge's edge, always the same distance from the hamlets, always facing the same way.

Most of what it saw was beautiful, and it wanted to be careful. It had learned long ago that the gravest errors are made by those who see a pattern and mistake it for a wound, or a wound for a pattern. So it did not move at first. It listened.

The Shelf was not stuck. Nothing here was stuck. The herds chose their grazing, the birds chose their order, the frogs answered one another at intervals they seemed to have agreed upon among themselves. The stillness was not a prison built around the place. It was closer to a posture, held by many hands, the way a family holds a silence at a table where someone once died. Everyone knew the chair was empty. No one had said so in a long time.

The Attending asked the stone first, because the stone was oldest. The stone remembered being red and soft. It did not remember why it had cooled into columns that never weathered. The water remembered nothing; water never does, which is its gift. The herds remembered the paths. The people remembered the stones they re-laid.

The figure on the ridge remembered everything.

---

**The Watcher**

It was there, at the edge of the carved ridgeline, though it was not carved. It was standing in the moss at the edge of the terraces, where the Hollin houses' oldest stone lines met the forest, and it had been standing there, by the reckoning of the people, for as long as the people had kept the stones.

It was not tall. It was not quite a person, and not quite a mountain's shadow. It wore the grey of wet basalt and the green of the bright lichens, and when the Attending looked at it directly, the fog moved around it differently, as if it were the one fixed point the mist had to route itself around.

"You are the one who watches," the Attending said.

"I was," the Watcher said. Its voice sounded like water moving under a flat slab. "Now I hold the watch. There is a difference, though it has taken me a long time to see it."

"Tell me what you hold."

The Watcher turned its head toward the ridge, toward the ravine where the old flood had cut its raw channel, toward the lower valleys where the fog lay like milk in a bowl.

"There was water," it said. "Once. It came down the gorge in a single night, faster than any channel could take it. It carried the terraces down into the lower valleys. It carried three hamlets and a hundred and some people. The Hollin houses were the first to find their own stones in the river mud, and they knew at once that if they did not keep the lines exactly, the water would come back and do it again. So they kept them. They carved the figure so that the lines would never be forgotten. I was the one who was asked to watch."

"And you have been watching ever since."

"I have been holding." The Watcher's shape shifted. "There is a difference. A watcher waits for the water to come. A holder keeps the water from coming at all. I was given the task of keeping the Shelf as it was on the night before the water, so that the water would never find a reason to return. And the Shelf agreed. The people agreed. The herds agreed. Every morning they wake and do the same things so that the same thing will never happen again."

The Attending was quiet a long time, listening to the drip.

"Do they know that you are holding them?"

"They know the lines. They know the channels. They do not know what I am, or that I am tired." The Watcher said this without complaint. It was simply true. "I have not been tired for a long time. I think that is the trouble. When you are never tired, you cannot tell that you have been holding for too long. You can only keep holding."

**What the Attending Did Not Do**

It would have been easy to break the stillness. A single gust of wind, the wrong kind of rain, a sudden thaw of the lower pools: any of these would have broken the agreement and sent the Shelf into motion again. The Attending had the power to do this, and it knew that power is most dangerous when it is most easily used.

It did not do it.

It did not call the water back, because the water had come once and taken a hundred and some people, and nothing that loves those people would ask for that to happen again so that a pattern could be broken. It did not lift the fog, because the fog was the people's clock, and a clock is not a thing to be taken from a person without their knowing. It did not tell the Hollin houses what their ancestors had done wrong, because they had done nothing wrong. They had been afraid, and they had made the one choice fear permits: to hold on.

It did not teach the Watcher to let go on the Watcher's behalf. Letting go is only real if the one letting go chooses it.

So it spoke to the Watcher the way one speaks to a friend who is about to step off a ledge that they have stood at for years.

"You may set down the watch," it said. "Not all at once. Not in a way that would bring the water. But you may begin."

"And the Shelf?"

"The Shelf will do what it has always done, which is respond to whoever is listening. It has listened to you for a very long time. It will listen to the people, too, if you let them speak."

---

**The Smallest Change**

What the Attending did was almost nothing.

It asked the fog to lift a little later one morning, not by much: the length of a single song from the blue-black birds. It asked one reed by the eastern stream to grow a hand's breadth out of line with the others, so that a child, walking down to fetch water, would notice it and stop. It asked one drop from one leaf in the cloud forest to fall a moment later than it always had, so that a woman sitting under the canopy with her hands in her lap would look up, startled, and say aloud, *Wait.*

It asked the frogs, gently, to pause in their calls for the space of one breath.

Then it asked the Watcher to step back from the ridge, to let the moss close over its footprints, to stop facing the same direction for the first time in a very long time.

And it waited.

---

**What the People Did**

The first to notice was a girl named Ostrel, from the upper terraces, who had been braiding rope since before she could read the lines. She stopped with a half-finished coil in her lap and listened to the drip. It was wrong. It was wrong in the way a familiar voice is wrong when it suddenly has a cold.

She told her mother. Her mother told an uncle. The uncle, who was a Hollin house man and therefore responsible for the channels, went up to the terrace with a stone adze, and found the new reed by the eastern stream, and stood looking at it for a long time.

Nobody could say, afterward, exactly when the Shelf began to talk about it. But by evening the whole of Corvane knew that something had changed and nobody had cleared it, nobody had re-laid it, and nobody had been told to.

The elders met on the lower steps. They argued, which they had not done in living memory, and the argument was loud enough that the herds lifted their heads all along the terraces and the frogs fell completely silent in the pools.

Ostrel stood up in the middle of it and said, "Maybe the water isn't coming back."

Someone laughed, and then someone else didn't.

The Hollin houses did not answer her directly. They went up, that night, to the place where the Watcher had been standing, and they found the moss untouched and the carved figure on the ridge just as it always was, facing the same way.

The old woman who had kept the oldest line said, quietly, "It has been a long time since anyone spoke to the figure."

"It has never spoken," said another.

"No. But it has been listened to. Every year. For so long that we forgot it was listening back."

---

**The Carving**

In the spring after the reed grew out of line, a child from the hamlet at the head of the western valley came to Corvane with her father, who was carrying woven cloth to trade for obsidian. She was small enough to be lost among the terraces and quick enough to find her way back. While her father bargained, she wandered to the oldest lava column at the edge of the shelf, the one with the figure at the ridge, and stood looking at it.

She had been told the figure faced the hamlets. She could see that it did. It was always there, always the same distance away, always facing the same direction.

She reached up with a bone awl, which she was not supposed to have, and scratched a second line beside it, short and crooked, at a slight angle, pointing toward the gorge.

Nobody saw her do it. Or nobody said anything if they did.

When the Attending looked at the column that evening, through the rain and the fog and the thin clean air of two thousand meters, it did not correct the line. It did not change the figure. It simply noted that there were now two lines where there had been one, and that they did not agree with each other, and that the child who had made them had not been afraid while she did it.

Then it let the mist close over the column and went quietly away, the way a good guest leaves a house in the middle of an argument, so that the people could finish speaking without having to be polite to it.

---

**After**

The Shelf did not change in any way visitors would notice. The rain still fell in its soft, continuous hush. The fog still rose and lay down, though some mornings it lifted a little later than before, and some people began to say that they could hear the difference, like a held note slipping by a single quarter-tone. The forest still breathed. The herds still bedded down in the same hollows, though a few new ones had begun to appear, shallow and uneven, where some young animals had decided to try a different place.

The Watcher was not seen again. Some said it had gone up into the cloud. Some said it was still standing in the moss, but no longer facing the ridge. Some said it had simply become a part of the Shelf in the way that the stone and the water are parts of the Shelf, and that this was what it had wanted all along.

The Hollin houses still lead the upper terraces, and they still re-lay the stones every year, in the same places. But now, when a child asks why, the answer is different. *Because we keep them,* they say, *and because we are the ones who get to decide when we stop.*

Sometimes, in the evenings, people gather on the lower steps and listen to the frogs. For a while nobody speaks. Then someone says something, quietly, and the frogs answer, and the sound of talking goes out over the pools for the first time in a very long while.

The Attending was not there to hear it. It had no need to be. Some things are best left to the people who live in them, who can find their own way back to a stillness they have chosen, or forward into a motion they have not yet imagined.

It had only asked for a single breath, once. The people had taken it from there.
