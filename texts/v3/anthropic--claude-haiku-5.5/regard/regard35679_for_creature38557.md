**What the Power Would Do**

It comes the way a change in weather comes, not as a storm but as a shift in how the mist sits. For a long time it has been aware of the Cirque the way one is aware of a lamp left burning in a house where no one has lived for years. Tonight it turns its attention there, and the attention is gentle, the way a hand is gentle when it is about to touch a bruise.

It does not come down through the clouds. It does not break the lid. It understands at once that the lid is not the cause. The clouds are only where the held breath shows. A violent opening would be one more pressure on a body already pressed too hard, and the power has learned that a wound forced open does not heal. It would rather be slow.

So it sits. It sits at the rim, where the clouds lie like a closed hand, and it listens for a long time. Time passes in drips. It counts them, one from a leaf tip, one from a cliff seam, until the counting becomes a kind of prayer, and it stops counting and only listens.

What it hears is not a voice, because the thing has none. It hears the water, which is the thing's way of saying nothing, and under the water it hears the shape of a question that has never been asked: *Where did it go?*

It does not know who "it" was. It does not need to. The rot in the fallen trunks holds a memory of warmth. The grooves in the stone are older than the grooves of any road. The birds that circle above the rim and will not descend remember something the bowl has forgotten, or has refused to forget, and the power recognizes the shape of a loss that was never allowed to become past. A being was here, or a moment was here, and when it left through the notch in the rim, the thing did not let the leaving complete. It held the last gesture. It held it for so long that the holding became the body.

The power considers its options, and they are not many.

It could take the grief away. It could draw the water out of the cliffs and dry the moss and lift the lid in one motion, and the Cirque would become a bare bowl under a clear sky, quiet and empty and clean. But the power does not believe that a grief removed is a grief finished. It would only be a second loss laid over the first. And the thing has been faithful in its own way. The stones, the grooves, the rot, the warmth: none of these are errors. They are the things that love does when it has nowhere else to go.

It could speak for the thing and tell it what it is grieving. But the power is wise enough to know that a grief must be spoken by the one who holds it, even if that speaking is only a change in posture.

So it chooses the slowest thing.

It goes down into the mist, which is the first time anything has come into the Cirque from above in a long time, and it does not disturb the air. It settles into the marsh the way rain settles, soaking downward, and the moss takes it in without swallowing it. For the first time, something in the bowl is not cold, not gray, not waiting. It is the power itself, warm as a held hand, and the warmth moves through the peat and into the roots of the tree ferns, and the tree ferns do not move, but their fronds tremble, very slightly, the way a sleeping body trembles when it is about to wake.

Then the power does what it came to do. It hums.

It joins the insect hum. The hum has been a pressure, a breath held behind a closed throat, but the power lets its own note rise inside it, low and steady, the way a mother hums to a child who has cried too long and has no words left. The insects do not change their rhythm, but the hum deepens, and for a moment the whole Cirque is a single held note, and the note has a direction. It goes toward the far slope. It goes toward the tallest tree, whose crown has never grown and never shed.

And the crown sheds a leaf.

It is the first thing in the bowl to fall that has not been replaced. It spirals down through the gray air, slow as the fronds have always been slow, and it lands on the black water of a pool, and the pool ripples outward in rings, and the rings keep moving long after they should have stopped. The birds on the branches of that tree lift their heads. They do not fly. But they shift, and for the first time in a very long while, one of them moves from its perch to another, and the limb it leaves springs back, and the bark gives up a little of the water it has been holding.

The power does not stop there. It is careful. Too much at once would be another kind of violence.

It turns its attention to the salamander on the wet stone, the one that has faced the same direction since before anyone was counting. It does not touch the salamander. It only lets the mist thin for a moment in front of its face, so that the salamander can see that there is light beyond the mist, a faint lightening in the gray. The bead of water hanging from its snout trembles. The salamander does not move its head. But the gesture it has been holding, the turn toward the place it has always been looking, finally completes. The bead falls. It lands on the stone with a small, flat tock, and the sound is different from every other drip in the Cirque, because it is the sound of something that is finished.

The salamander blinks, or does something that is the salamander's version of blinking, and turns its head a fraction of an inch, toward the rim, toward a direction it has never faced before.

The frond that has been unrolling for as long as anyone could tell unrolls one more turn. It does not finish. It is not supposed to finish all at once. But it moves, and its movement is a sound, a soft whisper of fiber against air, the first audible motion the Cirque has made in an age.

The moths on the fern fronds unfold their wings. Some of them open all the way and lift, briefly, into the haze, then settle again on different leaves. The frogs in their crowded mats separate, just a little, enough to leave a finger's width between two bodies, and one of them makes a sound. It is only a croak, rough and surprised, but it carries across the marsh and comes back off the cliff walls, and the cliffs send it back, and for a moment the Cirque sounds like a place where something is alive.

Then the power does one more thing, and it is the gentlest of all.

It touches the clouds on the rim. Not to lift them. Only to thin one thread of them, the thread directly above the notch where the stream escapes. A little light comes through. It is not sunlight, not yet, only a paler gray that falls through the gap like water through a crack. It is enough to cast a shadow. For the first time in an age, a shadow forms on the floor of the bowl, a faint dark shape where a fern leaf blocks the light, and the shape has edges.

The power does not stay to see the whole of it. It cannot. It is not the one who needs to finish this. It is only the one who sat at the rim long enough to hear the question, and who answered it with a hum and a warm hand and a single thread of light.

Before it withdraws, it leaves one thing behind. It is small. A drip gathers at the tip of a leaf near the notch, where the old drip has been forming in exactly the same place for as long as anyone could count. The new drip swells. It grows heavier than the old ones. And when it falls, it does not land in the same pool, or on the same stone, or in the same hollow. It lands on a patch of moss that has never been touched by water in exactly this way, and the moss gives, and fills slowly, and then, for the first time, begins to drain.

The power is gone. The clouds sit back on the rim, though not as heavily. The water keeps running through the cracks. The stone keeps weeping. But the weeping is different now. It is no longer the sound of something that will not let go. It is rain, falling toward something, the way rain does when the sky has remembered it has a reason to open.

The Cirque is still a bowl. It is still dim and damp and crowded, and most of its animals still do not speak. But there is a shadow on the floor, and a leaf has fallen, and somewhere a salamander is looking at a different place. The held breath has become an ordinary one, and the thing in the mountain, which has been holding its shoulders for longer than anyone has counted, has begun, very slowly, to let them down.
