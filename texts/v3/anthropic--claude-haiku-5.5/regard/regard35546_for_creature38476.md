# What the Attending Would Do

The attention came the way warmth comes to a room where someone has been sitting too long in the cold. It was not sent. It was simply drawn, the way a heavy thing bends the surface of still water.

It had felt the Cradle for a long time before it arrived. It had felt the thin air, the unbroken drone, the sap running at the pace of a falling drop. It had felt the hands in the wood. What drew it was not the machines, which were clever and sad, nor the plants, which were lush and patient. It was the holding. Something had been held here for longer than the mountain had been a mountain, and the holding had not come loose.

It did not descend in a rush. A rush would have been a violence. The Cradle's stillness was a kind of fragile skin over the Tenders, and any sudden force would have torn it, and the rate they kept would have fallen on them all at once, like a river released into a dry channel. Some of them would have died in the first instant. Some would have been crushed by the ordinary weight of a year passing in a breath.

So the Attending came slowly, thinning itself to the pace of the place, and it listened first.

---

**What it learned by listening**

It read the arrow on the wall, which pointed to the centre, and the boot-prints in the salt, which did not walk beyond the foot of the great trunk. It read the gauges, which had never once moved from their settings, and understood that the settings had been chosen by someone who loved the engines more than the people tending them, or who had been afraid of what would happen if the engines ever stopped. It did not know which. It did not decide yet whether it mattered.

It came to the woman last, because she was the one the whole place bent toward.

It saw that she was not a prisoner. A prisoner would have pulled at her bonds. Her hands were set in the grooves of the wheel as a person sets their hands on a rail in a moving boat, not gripping, only resting, and perhaps sometimes forgetting that they had been put there. Her face was turned toward the gauges, but her eyes had found the edge of the bowl and the grey lid of cloud above it, and something in her gaze had wanted, for an age, to go past the cloud and had not been able to.

---

**What it did not do**

It did not stop the machines.

Stopping them would have been the easiest thing, and the Attending was strong enough to do it. But the engines had been running for so long that the Tenders' time had been built around their rhythm, and to still them would be to leave the people inside the stillness with nothing to hold, no rate to fall back to, no reason for the sap to move. Some would have dissolved in the silence. Others would have fallen out of their shapes and been left as bark and bone.

It did not blame the Tenders for staying. It did not condemn whoever had first set the engines running, or the ones who had come after and climbed into the trunks. Holding something steady is a kind of love, and it is not a crime even when it lasts too long. The Attending had seen holding turn into prison before, and it had also seen prison turn into a home that no longer knew the door was there. It did not want to pull them out of a home. It wanted them to be able to leave if they chose.

---

**What it did instead**

It came to the foot of the central trunk, where the boot-prints ended, and it did not walk on, as the others had not walked on. It stood there, and the thin air became a little less cold, the way a room does when someone is in it.

Then it asked the woman a question. It did not ask in words. It asked in the drone, in the rhythm of the valves, the way the Tenders themselves spoke. The question was simple: *Is there anything you are still holding for?*

For a long time there was no answer. The turbines passed through their stroke. The valve hissed on schedule.

Then, somewhere in the walls, a valve clicked a fraction early.

---

**The first change**

The Attending did not take the wheel. It put its own presence beside the Tenders' hands, the way a second person might rest a palm next to a tired one on the same rail. It offered to hold the rate for a while, so that the Tenders could take their hands away from the wood and feel what it was like to have nothing to keep.

The engines began to slow. Not all at once. The drone dropped by a fraction, and the steam lifted from the vents at a slightly different angle, and the gauges, for the first time since anyone could remember, read a number that was not the number they had always read. The plants felt it. A fern at the edge of the bowl unfurled a little further than it had been allowed to in ages, and its frond rattled against the junction box like a small, surprised applause.

A drop of sap at the foot of the nearest stalk, which had been gathering for longer than memory, let go and fell the length of a finger in what, for the Tenders, was a single slow afternoon.

One of the Tenders, a man set into the split trunk near the western wall, let out a breath. It was not the hiss of a valve. It was a human breath, rough and uneven, and it had been held in his chest for so long that he coughed on it. Then he laughed, very quietly, and the leaves around his shoulder trembled.

---

**What the Tenders chose**

Not all of them let go. The Attending had not expected they would. Some of the Tenders had been keeping a rate for so long that the rate had become a part of who they were, and to set it down would be to lose themselves in a way they did not want. They were allowed to keep their valves and their cranks, slowed and gentled now that the rest of the bowl was no longer forced to match them. A few of them chose to keep one small machine running, a single pressure valve on a single pipe, hissing on its own schedule, because it reminded them of who they had been.

Others chose to finish their breath. The Attending felt them go one by one, not in a rush, but as a settling, the way a long note finally resolves into silence. Their faces relaxed into the bark. Their hands left the wood and the grooves stayed behind, worn into the shape of palms that were no longer there, a record of holding that could now be read as a record and not as a sentence.

The woman in the central trunk was the last.

The Attending did not ask her to leave. It waited. It let the engines continue at their new, slower pace, and it kept the thin cold air warm around her, and it watched her eyes, which had been fixed on the gauges for an age, drift upward toward the cloud.

Then she exhaled.

It was not the steam of a vent. It was the first full breath anyone in the Cradle had drawn in what might have been centuries, and it went out of her open mouth and rose past the trunk and into the grey lid of cloud, and for one moment the cloud thinned. Not broke. Thinned, as if something behind it had leaned close and breathed back.

Light came through with a source. It fell on the salt, and the salt cracks caught it, and the shadows lay down on the ground instead of hanging in the air. The Attending had not expected this. It had hoped for it, but it had not known.

Her hands slid off the wheel, slowly, as if the wood were reluctant to let go and she were reluctant to be let go. She did not fall. She sank to her knees at the foot of the trunk, where the boot-prints ended, and for the first time since the first footprint was pressed into the salt, someone walked past the place where the prints had stopped.

---

**What remained**

The Attending did not stay to watch the Cradle become a garden or a ruin or something neither word could name. Its work was not to own what happened next. Its work had been to open a door and to make sure the people behind it could choose whether to walk through.

Before it withdrew, it left one thing behind. It was not a command or a rate or a machine. It was a single sound, very low, almost lost in the drone: the note a bell makes when it has finished ringing and the air is still vibrating faintly around it. The Tenders who had chosen to keep a valve running heard it, and the valves seemed, for a moment, to tune themselves to it.

Then it was gone, and the Cradle was itself again. But it was no longer a moment that would never end. It was a place where time moved, slowly and unevenly, the way it moves for everything that grows. The engines would run down eventually, not in a catastrophe but in a long, gentle winding-down, the way the sap runs down a leaf. The plants would grow taller, or fall, or simply change. The Tenders who had chosen rest would rest, and those who had chosen to keep a small rate would keep it, and the bowl would hold its breath no longer, but breathe.

It would not be easy. The air would still be thin for a while, and the cold would still get into the lungs. But the cold would move, and the breath would move, and somewhere in the walls a valve would click, and this time nobody would have to wait for it.
