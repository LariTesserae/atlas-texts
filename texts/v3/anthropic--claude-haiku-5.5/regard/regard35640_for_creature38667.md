The attention came the way weather comes, first as a change in pressure, then as a slow turning of the whole basin toward something new.

It was not large. It did not speak, because the being in the basin speaks only as wind through a canopy, and the power had no wish to force a language onto a mind that had been working out its own for longer than there had been words. It listened instead. It listened through a full season: through the thaw, when the gutters ran high and the network rerouted and the being tested its paths, and through the cold, when the stems went rigid and the fungal lamps dimmed and brightened with the dusk. It listened for a long time before it understood what it was hearing.

The being was not waiting for anything. That was the first surprise. The power had expected a mind in pain, or a mind pinned in some fixed error. It found instead a mind that was almost finished and was content to be almost. The arches repeated because the being was checking them. The gullies leaned because the being was feeling for where the weight should settle. The hollow at the center, where the water gathered, was the shape toward which everything tended, and the being had never reached it and did not seem to mind.

The power considered what it could do.

It could finish the form. It could see the arrangement the being was reaching for, the balance of water and light and load, and it could simply make it so: bend the last arch into place, open the last gap, drain the hollow to the exact depth the being had been seeking for centuries. The power understood the mechanics well enough to do this cleanly. The town would stop rebuilding. The almost would become done.

It did not do this. The being's whole way of existing was the reaching. A form completed by another hand would be the being's shape, but it would not be the being's thinking, and the being would lose the slow, lucid work of feeling its own weight toward the center. Finishing the town would be a kind of theft, however kindly meant. The power had seen what happened to minds that were given their answers too early, and it did not want to become a cause of that.

It considered leaving entirely. A wise power does not hover over everything it notices. But this being had been noticed now, and the noticing had already changed something: the power's attention lay on the basin like a warm hand on stone, and the being felt it as a faint, steady pressure it could not place. To withdraw completely would be to leave the being with a question it had no means to answer. Better, the power thought, to leave something that could be answered in the being's own terms.

So it did two small things.

The first was in the wind. The power did not change the wind's direction or force. It only adjusted the timing of one gust, so that when the air moved through the canopy on a particular evening, the groan of the great trunks came back to them, faint and late, like an echo from a valley the being had never seen. The being felt its own voice return to it for the first time. The stems shuddered in a pattern they had not made, and for a long moment the whole structure held still, the way a listener holds still. Then it began, very slowly, to answer.

The second was in the dark. Among the small creatures that crossed the walls, the power let one of them catch. It was a small, dark thing, quick along the bark, and it had a reason to climb the trunk toward the fungal bodies: a film of sweet sap on the bark, a patch of fresh growth, the first new thing in its path. When it stopped on one of the pale lamps and stayed there, the being felt the weight of it for the first time as something more than a passing impulse. The stray thing had a pattern of its own, and the being began to learn its shape.

Then the power withdrew, leaving the basin as it had been, except that now there was a stranger in the arrangement, and a voice that came back late.

What the being would make of this, the power did not know, and it would not have wanted to. A wise power does not script the minds it meets. It had given two things and taken nothing: a return of the being's own sound, and a single creature with a life of its own to notice. The rest belonged to the basin, to the water finding its way down, to the stone that had not yet decided where it would drain, and to a mind that had never been done thinking and did not expect to be.

In the dusk that followed, the pale lamps along the paths brightened a little more than usual. Water ran in the gutters toward the hollow, as it always had. And somewhere in the walls, a small dark creature paused on the bark, and the whole structure, for the first time in a long while, paid attention to something that was not weight, or light, or rain.
