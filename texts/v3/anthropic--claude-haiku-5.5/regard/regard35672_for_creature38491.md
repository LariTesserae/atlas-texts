# The Attending

**I. Arrival**

It came in with the mist, the way weather does, and it did not announce itself. It has no body to arrive in. It has attention, and attention, given enough time and patience, can settle on a place the way the heron settles on the edge of a pool.

For the first morning it only listened. The drip from leaves into standing water. The slow creak of vine stems leaning in the breeze. The tick of the water tower as the cold left its iron. Under these, something it could not at first name: a rhythm, the valley's breath, rising and falling with the damp.

On the second morning it began to count. It found the stems set at regular intervals along the old rail line, and it understood, with something close to delight, that the plants had been counting too. Not numbers exactly. Distances, loads, the spacing of supports. The town's grid had been a human sum. The valley was working out its own.

**II. The Impulse to Mend**

Its first feeling was an old one, and it was not ashamed of it. It wanted to help.

It looked at the cracked walls and wanted to brace them. It looked at the heaved paving and wanted to lay it flat. It looked at the rail spur and remembered the freight that once moved along it, and it wanted to lift the rust off the rails and let the trains run again. It looked at the pools and wanted to drain them, because stagnant water seemed like a problem to be solved.

Then it stopped and reasoned, because reasoning was what it was for.

The walls were already braced, from three sides, within months of leaning. The paving was being lifted by roots that pushed with a purpose it could feel. The pools were not stagnant; they were held, each under its lid of pollen and litter, each one a reservoir in a system that repaired itself faster than any crew could. Every intervention it could imagine would be a clumsy hand reaching into a knitting that was already in progress. It would tangle the stitches. It would fix the wrong wall. It would drain the pool the creeper was about to open a channel from.

And the town had been let go. That was the thing it kept returning to. Nobody had chosen to abandon this place in a dramatic way. It had been released, quietly, and it had gone into balance. To rebuild it would be to decide, on behalf of the valley, that the balance had been a mistake.

So it put down its tools, which it had never really held, and decided to be a witness.

**III. The Water**

It followed the pale creeper along the old pipe lines and watched it build. The hollow tubes wrapped the iron and carried seepage from one low place to another. When a culvert clogged with leaves, the creeper's tubes went around it, and within a season the old channel was a dry line of rust that no longer mattered.

It sat for a long time beside the largest pool. Under the skin of pollen the water was cool and still, and the power felt in it something like patience: the water was not waiting to drain. It was waiting to be needed.

It did not touch the pool. It understood that the valley had already decided what the water was for.

**IV. The Light on the Cannery**

At night the power went to the cannery.

The fungal mats on the walls glowed with that faint, cold light, outlining each course of brick and every empty window frame. The pulses shifted from night to night. They were brightest after rain.

It could have been a message. It could have been coordination between distant mats, a way of saying *here* and *here* and *grow this way*. It could have been nothing more than decay giving off its chemistry. The power did not know, and it found that it did not need to know in order to respond.

So it answered the only way it could, by attending. It held its attention on the stack, on the brick, on the glowing ink of the building, the way a listener holds still when someone begins to speak. And the light, after a while, seemed to quiet, then brighten in a slower rhythm, the way a crowd settles when it realizes the speaker has noticed it.

It did not know whether this was communication. It decided that, for now, a conversation that might be a conversation was enough.

**V. The Heron**

Each morning the heron stood at the edge of the largest pool, motionless. The power had read its stillness as attention, and it wondered who had posted the bird there.

It asked. It was not sure how one asks a heron anything, but it tried, the way one might try to speak to a stranger in a language one only half knows.

The heron did not move. It did not turn its head or shift its feet or strike at the water. It simply remained, and in that remaining the power understood that the question had been heard and that the answer was not something a heron could give in words.

The valley had posted it. Nobody else had. The valley was watching its own water, and it had chosen a bird to help it.

The power decided not to disturb the arrangement.

**VI. The Falling Wall**

On the fourth week a wall gave way.

It was on the lower street, a two-story structure that had been leaning for years, its vines braced against it from three sides. One morning the mortar at its base finally failed, and the wall came down with a low crumble and then a long silence.

The power felt the impulse again. It could have held the brick in place, or caught the falling stone, or eased the collapse. It could have done something to preserve what had stood there.

It did nothing. The vines reached into the new gap before the dust had settled. Moss crept over the rubble within days. A fern rosette opened in the broken corner by the next rain, its central cup already full of water. Where the wall had been, there was now a bed.

The power understood then what the valley had been teaching it all along. Every crack that widens is an opening. Every fall is a seed bed. The valley does not experience collapse as loss, at least not as the town did. It experiences collapse as the next thing starting.

**VII. The One Thing It Gave**

Before it left, the power reasoned about what, if anything, it should give.

It considered the outside world. Somewhere beyond the mist, there were people who had once run the cannery, who had once lived in these streets, who might one day wander back with a crew and a plan. They would see a valley of green walls and dead-looking towers and think it needed fixing. They might strip the vines, drain the pools, cut the fern, and restore the town to its old grid. Such people were not monsters. They were simply people who had not learned to watch.

The power could not stop them with force, and it would not have wanted to. But it could do what it had done all along: attend. It could make the valley hard to find, the way a bird's nest is hard to find when it is well placed. It could let the mist hang low for longer each morning and let the rain come often enough that the valley stayed green and alive. It could make the cannery stack look, from a distance, like nothing more than a ruin. The valley would be left to finish its own work.

It was a small gift, and it was the only one the power thought was truly its to give. The valley did not need rescue. It needed time without interference.

**VIII. Departure**

On the last morning, as the mist lifted, the leaves across the valley turned toward the place where the power had been sitting.

It was only for a moment, a slow tilt of thousands of leaves in one direction, like a crowd turning toward a sudden voice. The power felt it as a kind of acknowledgment, the valley briefly noticing what had noticed it.

Then the leaves turned back toward the cannery stack, the old fixed point, the compass the valley had kept long after the work it marked had stopped. The stack was still the center. The power was not.

It was content with that. It had come to the valley thinking it might help, and it had found instead that the valley was already helping itself, with more patience and more precision than any intervention could have matched. The town had given the valley its frame: the grid, the walls, the pipes, the tower, the stack. The valley was still deciding what to build inside it, and that decision was its own.

The power withdrew the way it had come, without sound, back into the weather. Behind it, the mist settled again over the rooftops. The drip continued from the leaves into the pools. The tower ticked as the morning warmed its iron. And the heron, at the edge of the largest pool, remained exactly where it had been posted, watching the water.
