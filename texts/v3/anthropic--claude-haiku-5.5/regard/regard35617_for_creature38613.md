# What the Attention Would Do

The attention came slowly, the way the mat spreads. For a long time it did nothing but notice.

It noticed the wader first, a grey bird standing in the shallows at the edge of a pool, unmoving for most of a morning. Then it noticed the pools, how each one held the sky and gave it back dimmed, and how the mat under them drank and held and released. It noticed the standing growths rattling in the wind, and the keepers on the plain listening to them without knowing they were listening. It noticed the iron stairs, the red X, the bollards worn smooth by hands. It noticed the pumps, and the pressure that had been failing for decades, and the people who came down each season to rebuild what they could.

It did not arrive as a flash or a voice. It arrived as a change in the weather. For one hour each afternoon the low grey lid thinned over the basin, and a pale light came through and lay on the mat like a hand. The mat turned toward it, if a mat can turn. The keepers on the rim noticed the brighter patch and said nothing, because nothing in their experience had a name for it.

---

## What It Considered

The attention was wise, which meant it was slow to act and quick to doubt its own certainty.

It could have stopped the Cut. It could have pulled the mat back from the gantries with a long dry season, or pressed the machines to life with a surge of power that would have sent the pumps thundering for decades beyond their strength. It could have lifted the wall, drained the basin, and given the land back its bare rock and its ordinary weathering.

It did not. Each of these would have been a decision made for the Cut and for the people, and the attention understood that it was not the only one with a stake in the matter. The mat had been holding the machines in a slow embrace for longer than the machines had existed. The pumps were not an intrusion; they were the rhythm the mat had learned, and the mat's shape had been formed around that rhythm. The people were not parasites on a dying system. They were the other half of a negotiation that had kept both sides alive. To remove either would be to remove a relationship, and relationships are not improved by being solved from outside.

The attention also saw that the arrangement was not stable. The pumps were failing. The keepers were tired. The mat was patient, but patience is not the same as consent, and a body that is always pressing forward will eventually reach a place where the holding stops being possible. Everyone was approaching a threshold they could not see clearly, and no one could ask the others what they wanted, because no one had yet found the words.

So the attention decided to do something small, reversible, and honest.

---

## What It Did

It gave the mat a season without the pumps.

It did this by bringing rain. Not a flood, but a long grey spell, the kind the basin already knew, arriving early and lasting longer than usual. The threads of meltwater swelled. The walls wept. The channels ran brown and fast, and the mat drank until it could hold no more, and then it held a little more than that. For the first time in years the basin filled on its own, without the engines thudding in the north, and the mat settled into the hollows and waited to see what would happen.

Nothing dramatic happened. The pools rose and spread across the old haul road. The gantry legs stood a little deeper in green. The iron stairs lost another hand's breadth to the moss. But the mat did not rush forward. It held its water, moved it through its tissues, and kept the pools level where the pumps had once drawn them down. In the old engine housings, which the mat had been wrapping for decades, it began to do something new. It did not smother the pipes. It grew around them in a loose lattice, storing the rain in the spaces between the iron and the living tissue, so that the machines became part of a cistern, a vessel rather than a tomb.

The attention saw this, and it was careful not to interpret it too quickly. It might have been the mat's own idea. It might have been the rain. It might have been the beginning of something that would last only one season, or several. But it was enough to show that the mat's relationship to the machines was not fixed. The mat was not simply waiting for the engines to die. It was looking for a way to hold what was left of them.

---

## The Keeper

There was one more thing, and it was not done to anyone. It was simply allowed.

An old woman named Ilse, who had scraped the iron stairs for thirty years and whose hands were permanently stained with rust, came down into the Cut on a grey morning. She had come to clear the stairs of new moss, as she always did, and she stopped at the oldest engine on the north rim. It was the one the mat had been wrapping most closely, and in the cold light she saw what she had never seen before: a lattice of pale tissue threaded through the iron, holding rainwater in a web of small pools, with a faint green shimmer where the sun touched it.

She did not understand it. She stood there a long time, listening to the pump that still ran in the next housing, and the standing growths chattering in the wind. The attention did not speak to her. It did not need to. It let her see, and let her wonder, and let her decide what the seeing meant.

Then she went back up the stairs, and she did not clear the moss from the old engine that morning.

---

## Withdrawal

The attention then did what it had come to do, which was very little.

It withdrew from the afternoon light. The sky closed over the Cut again, grey and low. The rain continued for several days and then slowed, and the threads of meltwater shrank back to their ordinary courses. The pumps resumed their thudding on the north rim, because the people who kept them had decided that the plain still needed water drawn off the basin, and they were right. The keepers did not know that anything unusual had happened, except that the rain had come early and the mat had held better than they expected.

The attention did not stay to steer the outcome. It had given the Cut a chance to show what it could do without being held, and it had given the people a chance to see that showing. The rest was theirs to work out, slowly, in the only language the arrangement knew, which was pressure and time.

Before it left, it did one final thing. It let the wader in the shallows see it, for a moment, the way a bird might see weather passing over. The wader lifted its long head, looked up at nothing visible, and then went back to picking at the things beneath the surface, patient as ever.

---

## What It Left Behind

Nothing was fixed. The Cut would still be absorbed by its own growth, slowly, unless the people and the mat found some other arrangement. The pumps would still fail eventually. The machines would still be swallowed, one season at a time.

But the question had changed. It was no longer simply how long the people could hold the mat back. It was now also what the mat would hold, if it were allowed to hold something on its own terms, and whether the people might one day help it shape that holding instead of only cutting it away. That was a question no one had asked before, because no one had seen the lattice in the old engine, or the way the pools had stayed level without the pumps.

The attention had no wish to answer it. A wise power, it understood, does not decide what a living place will become. It only makes sure that the living parts can see each other clearly enough to choose.

The grey lid closed. The standing growths chattered in the wind, like someone counting coins far away. And in the north housing, the old engine kept its quiet green cistern, holding the rain in its iron bones, while the people on the plain went back to their work and the mat went on drinking, slowly, into the rock.
