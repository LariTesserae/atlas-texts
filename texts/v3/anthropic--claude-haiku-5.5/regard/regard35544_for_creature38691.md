The attention arrives the way the storms do, from the south and slowly, as a change in pressure before anything can be seen. It does not come as a voice. It comes as a long, patient noticing, like a hand laid flat on a surface to feel for warmth.

It takes a while to understand what it is noticing. At first it sees only the wreck: the grey silt, the glazed clinker, the towers stripped to rebar, the pale roots dissolved into their own outlines. Its first impulse is grief, and after the grief, a kind of tenderness that wants to fix things. It could drain the flats. It could lift the causeways clear of the water, pull the green-black film from the walls, scatter seed over the silt and wait for the ground to remember green. It could make the sky brighten. It has the reach for all of that.

It holds still and looks longer.

What it finds is not a ruin waiting to be repaired. It finds a settlement. The crabs have learned the rhythm of the pumps and keep their ticking in the same key. The amphibians have become a nervous system for the drainage grid, and their throats pulse with the weather before the weather arrives. The birds read the gantries the way a sailor reads water, moving between frames that will hold and frames that are beginning to bend. The cooling stacks breathe, and something in them breathes back. Every part of the place has been eaten, inhabited, and made use of, and the made things have been kept alive by the lives in them. If the power cleared the flats, it would not restore the old world. It would bury the new one, and the animals would have nowhere to go but the water.

So it decides, as wise powers sometimes must, to do very little.

It begins by listening. It can reach the grey-backed animal in the deep water, moving its slow arcs under the causeway, and the being notices the attention as a ripple in the channel. It does not startle. The water over its back dimples, the concrete above it shivers once, and the crabs pause their ticking for three seconds before going on. Then, through the silt and the old steel and the slow mineral bloom, the being answers in the only language it has, which is weight, and rhythm, and return.

It is old. It remembers the city before the water rose. It remembers the roots, the pale threads that held the silt together and then let go, and the sound the flats made when the plants were still standing and the wind moved through them faster than a breath. It has been carrying that sound for a very long time, under the water, and it has never had anyone to set it down with.

The power receives this. It does not try to make the memory whole. It takes the grief the being carries and holds it, so that the being does not have to hold it alone anymore. The being sinks lower in the channel, slowly, the way a body does when it finally stops bracing.

After that, the power does a few small things, and it chooses them carefully.

It keeps the storms as they are. The rain still comes up from the south in long grey banks, and the gutters still fill in minutes, and the animals still drop into the flood and wait. Those rhythms are the city's breath, and it would be a cruelty to smooth them.

It lets the turbines turn. The current under the central plant still moves, and the machines still hum, and the power does not reach in to stop them or to start them. It sends only a faint warmth into the old bearings, enough that they will keep turning a little longer when they would otherwise have seized. It is not repair. It is company.

Over the gantries it does almost nothing, except that when the birds are settling between frames, it makes the lattice a little more certain beneath their feet. The birds do not know this, and they do not need to. The logic they follow is their own, and the power would not presume to replace it. It only ensures that, when they choose a stable frame, the frame will be stable.

In the collapsed basements it leaves the temperature alone. The animals have chosen those hollows for their coolness, and the coolness is theirs.

It does not touch the stacks. Something lives there, something small and pale that goes up the inside and does not come back for a long time, and the power understands that it is not a guest to be shooed out of its own lungs. It only notices, and is glad that there is something in the city that breathes in the heat.

Last, it does one thing that is entirely its own. In the silt at the edge of the grid, where the old roots left their pale traces, it presses a single seed, one that is nothing but a blade of green and a promise. It does not water it, and it does not light it. It only gives the seed the stillness it needs to decide whether it wants to wake. The seed may take. It may lie in the silt for a thousand years and never open. The power does not mind either way. It has learned that a gift which asks nothing of the place can be left in the place without harm.

Then it withdraws, the way it came, slowly, like pressure easing off a surface.

The city goes on. The pumps knock somewhere in the distance. The crabs tick on the lower causeways, each at its own interval, and the field of them averages out into something close to the machines. The amphibians sit in their channels with their throats closed, waiting for the next change in pressure. The birds lift and settle and lift again, leaving the sky spotted for a few seconds before it empties. The grey-backed one moves beneath the causeways in its long slow arcs, and when it passes under the concrete the crabs pause, and the city keeps its time.

The steam from the stacks leans sideways in the wind. The water moves through the empty streets. Somewhere under the flats, in the silt, a seed that was never meant to be anything but a seed has either woken or has not, and either way the world is still here, low and warm and damp, holding its many small lives in the stillness of the air.

The attention does not return for a long time. When it does, it will come the way it always has, slowly, from the south, as a change in the weight of the air. It will find the city still settled, the animals still keeping time, and the old one still carrying the sound of the roots. It will find that the place has needed nothing from it except to be seen, and to be left alone in the seeing.
