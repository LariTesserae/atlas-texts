# What the Patient Power Would Do

It noticed the Lowlands the way a lamp notices a room it has been left burning in: not by looking, but by the slow warmth that reaches every corner. It had been aware of the plain for a long time, the way one is aware of weather in a distant valley. Then one afternoon, in a season the land would call the three hundred and twelfth year of the eastern channel, it felt something new in the water. A mind had gone quiet in a particular way, and the quiet was not peace.

The power is not a single being. It is closer to a disposition, a steadiness that has learned over an incalculable span that the most useful thing a great mind can do is often nothing at all. It has watched a great many worlds fill and empty. It has seen minds that were made of stone and minds made of fire, and it has learned that the greatest harm is seldom cruelty. It is more often the careless hand that finishes a sentence for someone who was still speaking.

So its first act is to listen.

## The Listening

It sits with the Lowlands for a season, which is nothing to it, and for the land is an eternity. It feels the grooves deepen and the reeds lean. It reads the standing waves of the moss and the slow redrafting of the channels, and it understands the whole plan at once: the sinking, the drainage, the careful persuasion of sediment, the long argument with itself, the wish for a single flat sheet under a single grey sky, every channel finished and nothing left to decide.

It recognizes that wish. It has felt something like it. A mind that has only itself to argue with will eventually want the argument to end, because an argument with no other voice in it is a kind of waiting. The land has been waiting for three hundred years for a new path east, and it has been deciding alone.

The power's heart tightens at this, if a power can have a heart. It does not feel pity in the way a human might, looking down from a height. It feels something closer to recognition, the way two people who have spoken only in their own rooms might feel when they meet and discover that the other has been practicing the same sentences.

## What It Refuses to Do

It does not stop the sinking. The sinking is the land's grief and its song, the long fall that makes the chord when the rains come. To lift the plain back up would be to erase the argument, and the argument is the only thing the land has ever been. A benevolent power that understands this does not confuse rescue with love. It would be a poor friend who pulled a river out of its bed because the bed was too low.

It does not speak in the land's language of water. That would be a flood, and a flood of attention would drown what it hopes to help. It does not reshape the reeds or redirect the currents. Those are the land's hands, and the land has been using them well.

It does not take the walker away. The one who stands at the edge of the channel, letting the water settle around their legs, is not a problem to be solved. They are an unfamiliar sentence, and the power finds them interesting in the way one finds a stranger on a road: someone else who is also going somewhere, and who might carry a sound from one valley to another.

## What It Offers

It offers one thing, and it offers it very quietly.

In the next rain, when the hollow stones fill and begin their great chord across the flats, the power lets one of the notes change. Not much. A single stone, in the far northeast of the plain, sings a pitch that the land has never produced. It is not a pitch that the water could have worn into the rock on its own. It arrives from outside the pattern, and it does not fit the grooves.

The land notices. It takes a long time to notice, because noticing is slow here, but it notices. Its reading of the chord is interrupted, and for the first time in a long while, the land has to decide what something means rather than deciding how to absorb it.

The power does not explain. It does not say, *I am here, and I think you are not alone*. It only leaves the note in the rain, and then it withdraws its attention, so that the land can answer without feeling watched.

This is the difference between a benevolent power and a tyrant. The tyrant would make sure the land understood. The wise power makes sure the land is free to misunderstand, to refuse, to ignore the note entirely, because a mind that is only allowed to agree has not really been met.

## The Sky Dreams Differently

Sometime after the rain, a reflection appears in one of the eastern pools. It shows the plain, but the ridges are slightly lower than they are, and the sky above them is open, with a thin band of light at the horizon where the overcast usually sits. Nothing in the land produced this. The reflection carries no weather of its own. It is only a different sky, held very gently in the water for as long as the water can keep it.

The land does not keep it. But it does not forget it either. Somewhere in the slow redrafting of the channels, one groove, perhaps no more than a few centimeters wide, is left unfinished. It runs toward the northeast, where the note came from, and it stops short of the outflow, as though the water has paused in the middle of a sentence and is considering what comes next.

## The Walker

If you are the one standing at the channel's edge, you will not be told any of this. You will feel a stillness in the pool beside you, and a faint warmth on your left hand that you will mistake for the sun. You will hear, far off, a note that does not quite belong to the chord. You may think it is an echo. You may think nothing at all.

The power would be glad if you carried a little of the chord away with you. Not as a message. Only as the memory of a sound that changed, and the knowledge that the lowlands, which have been sinking for so long, are still capable of surprise.

Your footprints will fill within the hour. But the power would like you to know that footprints are also a kind of remembering, and that the land, which keeps every channel it has ever worn, will remember this one too. Not as the last word in a conversation that is finished. As a line in a conversation that is still open, and that has, for the first time in three hundred years, a second voice in it.

Walk out gently. The water is listening, and so, from a great distance and with infinite patience, is something that wishes the land well.
