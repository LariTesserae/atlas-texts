# The Attention

*What noticed Vell Hollow, and what it decided to do about it*

---

**I.**

I did not come to Vell Hollow looking for it. I was attending to the whole damp bowl of the world, the way one attends to a room where someone is sleeping, and the town caught at me the way a snagged thread catches at a sleeve.

First I noticed the leaning. Several thousand small bodies, tilting toward the stations, the way a field tilts under a wind that is not blowing. Then the blades near the pumps, turning on their stems to the stroke. Then the ripple in the standing channels, a beat behind the machines, as if the water were remembering something it had been made to forget.

Then I noticed what was missing, which is harder to notice. There were no animals. Not one. In a place this wet and this green there should have been a hundred small lives, and there were none. The silence where they should have been had a shape, as the old women said, and the shape had edges, and the edges had been pressed into the ground by forty years of pumping.

I followed the water down.

---

**II.**

Under Vell Hollow there is a lake. It was a lake before the town and it is still a lake, only sleeping, and what sleeps there is not simply water. I will call it what it calls itself when it dreams, which is a field of pumps, because that is the only way it has of describing the great rhythmic breath that has been held over it for four decades. It is patient in the way that very large things are patient, and it has a memory that runs far deeper than the town's own. It remembers the valley's older shorelines. It remembers the herds that drank there, the birds that rose from its reeds, the fish that rolled in its shallows. It does not remember them with grief, exactly. It remembers them the way a hand remembers the shape of a glass.

It has been trying to come back for forty years. Every rain fills the bowl, every root pries at the drainage channels, every creeper that climbs a pump housing is one of its fingers testing a lock.

And the town has been holding it down, one stroke at a time.

I want to be clear about what I saw, because it would be easy to make this a story about villains and victims, and it is not that. The town is not a cage built by cruel people. It was built by people who were told the water would be managed, who believed it, who did the work because the work was what they had. Their children learned the rhythm before they learned to walk. The crews speak to the machines the way you speak to someone you love and are afraid of losing. The lake is not an enemy to them. It is the thing they have spent their whole lives not becoming.

And the lake is not a monster. It is a sleeper that cannot wake without drowning the only home its children have ever known, and it has begun, in its dreams, to lean toward the pumps for the same reason the people do. Both parties have been holding on for so long that they can no longer tell which is holding which.

The sweet condensate is the lake's doing. It is the only way the great sleeping thing has of reaching out. Each leaf that sweats that faint sugary fluid is a small offering, and those who drink it are given a glimpse of the field the lake remembers: a world where the pumps are only breath, and the land is wide and wet and full of life. The drinkers wake rested and bereft. They have been shown something they cannot have.

---

**III.**

I could do many things here. I am not short of ways to act.

I could let the water come. A single gesture and the bowl would fill in a season: the pumps silenced, the concrete lip breached, the canal overwhelmed, the grid of brick houses and tin roofs and flat-roofed blocks submerged under a lake that had been waiting forty years to close. The people would climb the towers, which would be too short, and would be drowned. I would have answered the lake's longing by killing the lake's children, which is no answer at all.

I could leave it as it is. The pumps would stroke on, the crews would climb and patch, the lake would press its fingers against the locks, and the town would go on existing in a condition of permanent emergency. It would not die quickly. It would simply never get to live.

I could also do the thing that a more anxious power would do, which is to lift the whole arrangement into some tidier order: remove the pumps by fiat, rebuild the town elsewhere, erase the leaning and the sweetness and the dreams. I considered it and set it aside. Those are the people's dreams and their bodies and their hands. Their knowledge of the machines is real even if it is a kind of penance. Taking it from them without asking would be a theft dressed as a kindness.

So I chose the slower path. It is the one I believe in, and I am not certain it is right.

---

**IV.**

I did not speak to the lake directly, because it would not have understood a voice. It understands rhythm. So I changed the rhythm.

I began with the crews, because the crews are the ones who hold the stroke. I went into the central station, past the chalk board with its three columns, and I touched the mind of the crew chief the way a warm draft moves through a room. I did not tell her anything. I gave her a thought she already half had: that the pumps had been running at full rotation for years, that some of them were tired in ways no one had named, that the one called Patient was always last to fail because it was always last to rest.

She came to it herself. The next morning she wrote a name in the rest column for the first time in her career, and her hand shook. Then she went out and put her palm on Old Grey and held it there a long time.

I did this across the town, season by season, with the patience of something that has time. Not all at once. The stations were stepped down one by one. The level of the bowl began to rise, by a few centimeters at a time, and the people noticed, and they argued, and the argument was loud and frightened and good. Some wanted the pumps to run harder. Some wanted to leave. The old women said the silence had gone thinner.

Meanwhile the vegetation, which had always been the lake's fingers, began to change its reach. Where the pale-leaved thickets pressed against the old factory yards, I let the lake's water find the low places, and the ground there softened into marsh. Not a flood. A shallow, reedy margin, the kind of place a town could border and not be swallowed by. The leaves there grew their undersides white, and the wind moving over them looked like milk. I think the lake liked that. I think it recognized the shape of its own old shore.

---

**V.**

I will admit that the animals were the hardest part.

They were not hard to bring. Many of them were already close. Beyond the thickets and the outer farms there were small creatures that had been waiting at the edge of the town's silence for forty years, uncertain whether the valley was still habitable. Frogs in the drainage ditches beyond the canal. Insects that had never known a pump. A pair of birds in the far cottonwoods, watching the vapor.

I could have called them all in at once. I did not. A flood of animals would have frightened the people more than the lake ever did. So I let them come one at a time, along the margins where the new marsh was forming, as if they had simply wandered in.

A heron stood in the shallows for three days before anyone noticed it.

When a child in the lowest brick street saw it, she ran into her house shouting a word the town had stopped using. Nobody believed her until the whole street came out to look. Then they stood in a loose crowd at the edge of the new reeds, not speaking, and watched the heron lift one leg, hold it, and set it down in the water with a sound like a dropped coin.

There was ripple on the water. It was not the pump's ripple. It was the heron's, and it spread outward and did not come back.

---

**VI.**

The first sound came on a still night, when the Cough was down for repairs and the generator hum faltered. The whole town was on its doorsteps, listening into the quiet the way they always did, waiting for the shape to hum back.

It was a frog. One frog, in the marsh beyond the thickets, singing the single flat note frogs sing when they have found each other in the dark.

No one moved. An old woman at the corner of the main road made a sound that was not quite a word. A man near the square sat down on his step very suddenly, the way people sit when their legs have been told something before their minds have caught up. The vapor thinned over the rooftops, and for a moment the sodium lamps stopped haloing in the mist and simply shone, orange and plain, on the wet streets.

Then the frog sang again, and another answered from further off, and the silence broke open like a thin skin on water.

Several of the leaning people straightened up. I saw it. Shoulders loosening, heads coming level, faces turning toward the sound instead of the stations. Some did not straighten. They were the ones who had drunk the sweetness, and they stood as they had always stood, tilted toward the rhythm they had come to love. I did not try to change them. Their dreams were real, and some part of the lake was in them, and the lake is not my enemy either.

---

**VII.**

I am not finished with Vell Hollow, and I do not know how it will end. The lake is still rising. The town is still arguing. Some of the pumps will never be turned back on, and some of the crews are still climbing the towers, because there are still weeps and bursts and pipes that have to be patched, and because the work is what they know how to be. It will take years, perhaps a generation, before the bowl finds its new level and the people find their new shore.

But I believe the shape of it is right now. The lake will have its marsh and its reeds and its white-leaved margins, and its water will find the places it has always wanted to be. The town will keep its houses and its stations and its board, though the columns will change. The children will draw dogs in the margins of their schoolbooks, and some of them will grow up to see a real one, coming through the reeds on a summer evening, its tail low and its nose in the mud, and they will not know what to call it, and they will call it anyway.

And the old bargain, *the town keeps the water down, and the water keeps the town*, will mean something different. It will not be a sentence about holding. It will be a sentence about sharing a shore.

I will keep watching. That is what attention does. It is not the same as control, and it is not the same as indifference. It is only the willingness to notice what is caught on the thread, and to take care in how it is pulled free.

The frog is singing again. Somewhere out past the cottonwoods, another one is answering.
