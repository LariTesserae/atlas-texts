# The Attending

## Arrival in Cloud

It came slowly, the way the land rose. Nothing in the high country would have noticed a sudden arrival, and the power that was drawn to this shelf understood that a sudden arrival would be a kind of noise. So it approached over many seasons, the way a thought approaches a question it does not want to frighten away. Its first sign was a change in the drizzle. The fog that sat on the basin grew a little more attentive, and the midges over the tarns rose in patterns that seemed to answer something.

When it finally settled into the shelf, it did not appear as a figure or a light. It appeared as a patience. The people noticed it first as a stillness in the calls, a pause before the answering hail came back from the next terrace, as if someone had listened and then decided to wait.

## What It Considered

It looked at the rising crust and understood what the people already knew: the ground was not broken. It was becoming. A young range that lifts a fraction each year is doing the slow, necessary work of making mountains, and the sharp ridges and raw faces are not wounds but the first honest shapes of stone that has not yet been worn into comfort. The power felt something close to reverence for the angular scree, the pale grit that had only just been freed.

It looked at the cold and the damp together and found them neither cruel nor kind, only exact. The frost broke the soil into heaves and the cloud fed the moss, and the land had found a balance between them that did not require anyone's help.

It looked at the thresholds, the tilted sills and skewed doorframes, and it felt the old impulse that visits anyone who has great power and sees something that will fall. It could lift the ground. It could slow the rising or hold the stone level for a thousand years. It could smooth every crack and quiet every slip.

It stayed with that impulse for a long time, and then it looked at the tally-stones.

## What It Declined

Each niche beside each door held a record: a line scratched for each season, a row of marks that showed the sill lowering and the leveler returning, a patient correction written down and kept. The power saw that the people were not fighting the lift. They were in conversation with it. Every shim was a sentence, every reading a reply, and the whole district was a long, slow dialogue between a rising earth and the hands that answered it.

To lift the ground, or to hold it still, would have been to end that conversation. It would have been like finishing a sentence for someone who was still thinking, or like carrying a child up a stair she had been climbing alone and proud of climbing. The power understood that benevolence is not always the same as intervention, and that the most helpful thing a wise power can do in a place like this is to refuse to take over what the place is already doing well.

So it declined to stop the lift. It declined to still the cloud, or thin the midges, or quiet the seabirds, or straighten a single leaning wall. It let the cushion plants flower in their brief pale stars and let the whistlers sit upright on their boulders, judging. It let the tarns drain and refill, and it let the tarn-keepers keep their maps and be wrong about them in the ways that kept them honest.

## The Small Things

It did not do nothing. It understood that a wise attention can still be practical, and that there are gaps in any keeping where help is not an intrusion but a courtesy.

When the fog came down thickest and a child stood in the open lane between two walls, unseen and calling, the wind shifted for a moment, just enough to lift the cloud from her face. She stepped into the light and saw the path, and the power withdrew before she could wonder about it.

When a slip began on a lower terrace, a quarry-cutter's song broke off in mid-loop, and the crew felt a sudden urge to move two paces left. They moved, and the scree came down where they had been standing, and nobody could say afterward why they had shifted, only that the urge had felt like their own.

These were small acts, and the power kept them small. It knew that a people who are capable and attentive should not be made dependent on a force they cannot see. It knew, too, that the most dangerous gift is the one that teaches a community to stop listening to its own instincts.

## The One Thing It Left

The power did leave one thing, and it chose it carefully.

At the highest terrace, where the oldest foundation met the newest course, there was an unmarked stone. It was a flat slab of grey sandstone, set in a niche that had not yet been used, beside the threshold of a house the crews had recently re-leveled. The power did not carve it. It did something stranger and simpler: it set the date of its arrival into the grain of the stone, a fracture that ran along the bedding plane and marked a moment in the same language the people used for every other reading. Only someone who knew how to read a tally-stone would see that the mark was not a crack.

It was an outside reading. The people had kept their history in layers of their own corrections for generations, and now there was a line that did not belong to them, a point from which they could measure something other than their own rising. It would matter to them, the power thought, not because it showed them anything they did not know, but because it gave them a second witness. Their records were careful and true, but they were kept by people who would someday be buried beneath new foundations. The stone would outlast the cells, and the line would stand.

## Afterward

The power did not stay. It rarely stayed anywhere long enough to be expected, and it knew that the land preferred its own rhythms. It withdrew slowly, the way it had come, letting the cloud settle back over the shelf and the drizzle return to its measured beat.

Some weeks later, a leveler climbing the highest terrace found the new fracture in the sandstone, and she knelt beside it with her bubble-tube and her slate wedge for a long time. She did not know who had marked it, or whether it had been marked at all. She felt the bedding plane, traced the line with her thumb, and then went to get her tally-tools.

She did not change the mark. She did not try to explain it. She simply scratched a new reading beside it, the sill's tilt for the season, and when she was done she called the count across the terraces in the rising two-note hail that means *come here*. Several voices answered from the fog, and then, from a boulder on the scree below, a whistler sat upright in the thinning light and watched her, as it watched everyone, with an attention that might have been judgment or might have been something gentler.

The power, wherever it had gone, could not have wished for a better reply. It had come to the shelf thinking it might be needed to hold the ground still. It left understanding that the ground had never needed holding. It needed what it already had: people who kept paying attention, who answered each other across the fog, and who knew that a land that will never settle is still a land worth keeping level, one fraction at a time.
