The power came in on the fog, as it comes to most places: not by descent but by thinning, the way a thought arrives before it is formed. For a long while it did nothing but attend.

It noticed the water first, because the water was everywhere and asked for nothing. It noticed how the rock wept in sheets and how the pools overflowed without a sound. It noticed the drip from the overhangs keeping time, the only regular beat, and it understood that the rhythm was not in the drops alone but in the whole plateau, which was breathing. The fog came in, the rain fell, the sun broke through, and the fog came back. In and out, slow as tides.

Then it noticed the engines, and it felt the first temptation.

They were wrong, in the way a splinter is wrong. Riveted plates furred with moss. Ribs half-buried and shedding oxide into the soil. Their intakes were clogged with spores and beetles, and their casings were slowly being opened by fern roots. A mind that cared for things would want to clear them, to scrape the moss from their seams, to restore whatever they had been built to do. The power could feel how easily it could do that. A word, and the rust would lift. A breath, and the engines would stand upright and draw cloud through clean grilles, pour their outflow in disciplined pulses, and walk their old circuits again.

It did not do this, and the reasons were not complicated, though they took some time to settle.

The first reason was that the engines were no longer only what they had been built to be. They had been made by someone, for something. The makers were gone. Whatever they wanted water for, whatever dry country they had meant to wet, had not been reached, or had been forgotten, or had been reached and abandoned. The engines had been left running on a purpose that no longer existed, and in the absence of that purpose they had done the only thing left to do: they had become part of the thing they were pumping water into. Now they were a forest's body and a frog's pool and a bird's pantry. To restore them would be to undo a long, quiet act of grace. The machines had been released from their makers' intent, and the plateau had received them.

The second reason was that the plateau was not sick. It was balanced in a way the power could feel only partly, the way one feels a sleeping person's weight shift on a bed. Every life here was both a debtor and a creditor. The shell-grazers cropped the moss so the predators could read their footprints. The ridgebacks cut channels that became the braided veins. The fungus ran its one long network under everything and carried warnings from root to root. Even the hunters, the power saw, were part of the water's road. Nothing here was wrong. Things died, and were reclaimed, and became soil. A place that kept its losses in the same ledger as its gains did not need a cleverer ledger keeper.

The third reason was the one that settled the matter. The power had the capacity to change nearly anything in the plateau, and that capacity was exactly the danger. A mind that can fix every rusted rib will always find a rib to fix. It would begin with the engines and then wonder about the ridgebacks' channels, which flooded the lower terraces in wet years. Then the herds, whose grazing sometimes left the moss too thin. Then the predators, whose hunting sometimes killed the wrong frog. Each intervention would be kind in its own moment and ruinous in the sum, because the plateau was not a set of problems but a single slow body, and the power was not a physician who could see its whole anatomy. It could see the breath. It could not see every vein.

So what would it do?

It made itself a listener. For a full season it moved through the plateau as fog moves, lying under fern fronds in the grey mornings, feeling the engines pulse, learning which frogs leaped early and which waited. It learned the timing of the outflow spills and the flock's sudden silences. It learned that the moss held a footprint for hours because the shell-grazers had kept it cropped, and that the predators read the print as warning and withdrew. It did not improve any of this. It only let itself be part of the attention the place already contained.

Then it did three small things, and these it chose carefully.

It took from the oldest engine, the one with the deepest pool in its hollow, the last faint memory of the people who had built it. The power did not erase the memory, because that would have been theft. It gathered it, the way the fog gathers, and held it lightly, and set it into the pool. The memory went down into the water and was taken up by the frogs' chorus and the roots of the fern. It did not become a message. It became a little more of the plateau. Somewhere in the spring rhythm of the frogs, a note that had not been there before, and that no one would ever be able to name, was now a part of the place.

It gave the newborn engine, the one just rising out of the soil, a single mercy: nothing. The power would not hurry its growth or slow its rust. It let the young machine be what it was, a mound of ribs becoming a hollow where a pool would someday gather. Nobody would see the first moment, and the power did not need to see it either. It only made sure the machine would not be wrenched into being by a will that had never tended anything.

And last, it did nothing to the rain. Every few hours the cloud thickened and the land was soaked, and every few hours the sun broke through and turned the leaves to glass. The power felt the pull to smooth that, to make the rain gentler or the light kinder, and it saw that the rhythm itself was the gift. The plateau had been breathing this way for ages. It did not need to be easier to breathe.

When the season ended, the power began to thin again, the way it had come, drifting up through the cloud until the fog closed over the terraces and the ridge outlines blurred into grey. It left no mark that anyone would find. The engines went on drawing water through their clogged grilles and pulsing it into the moss. The frogs sang their interlocking song at dawn. The shell-grazers folded into stones and unfolded into fronts. The fungus ran its warnings under the soil, and the hunters waited under their fronds, beaded and still, for the timing of a pulse to go wrong.

Somewhere on the highest shelf, in the thick damp of an old hollow, a frog that had heard a faint new note in the water turned its head toward the sound, then closed its eyes against the mist, and breathed with its whole body, the way everything here did. The breath went on. The power did not need to stay to know that it would.
