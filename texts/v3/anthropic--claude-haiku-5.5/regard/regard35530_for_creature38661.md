# The Attention That Came Up the Causeway

It did not arrive as a visitor arrives. It was more like a change in the weather of a mind that had been listening for a long time without knowing what it listened for. Somewhere far from the plateau, something that had tended many worlds and had learned, at great cost, the difference between helping and mastering, felt a pattern it recognized. Not a shape exactly. A rhythm with a gap in it.

It came slowly, the way the Keeper does everything, and the first thing it noticed was that the plateau was already listening to it.

---

## I. The Reading

The power did not want to be hasty. Hastiness had ended worlds that were otherwise healthy, and it had been the author of some of those endings.

So it read.

It read the salt-stone and found that the sea had left a long slow grief in the rock. It read the lattice and found the Keeper, not as a single thing but as a settlement of many living processes that had agreed, over ages, to think together. It read the posts and understood them as a heartbeat. It read the towers and understood them as the place where the heartbeat was remembered. It read the old builders' geometry, the buttressed hall and the ring of columns and the stepped terraces, and saw that the builders had not been gods or machines but people, or something people-shaped, who had wanted something very badly, and whose wanting had been written into the stone so deeply that the lattice had grown into the writing and carried it forward without understanding a word.

Then it read the convergence.

The power felt the dark center the way one feels a room where someone has recently died, or recently fallen asleep and not yet woken. It was quiet in a way that was not emptiness. The drip did not reach it. The fauna did not enter it. The posts pointed at it like the fingers of a hand pointing at a wound on the hand's own body.

*Something was cut here,* the power thought. *And something is still here.*

It did not know which.

---

## II. The Temptations

There were things it could have done. It made itself list them, because refusing a temptation without naming it is only another kind of blindness.

It could go to the dark center and open it. Not with force, which the lattice would have swallowed, but with a slow and patient attention that would reach past the silence and ask what was asleep there. It could wake the old builders' mind, if that was what slept, and let it speak. It was almost certain that it could do this. It was almost certain that the result would be a voice that had not heard the world for longer than the sea had been gone, and that the voice would be grieving, and that its grief would be enormous, and that the plateau would have to reorganize itself around it, the way bone reorganizes around a fracture, and the drip would run uphill toward it in a new direction, and the Keeper would lose the center of its own attention without ever being asked.

It could flood the plateau. Bring water from somewhere else, from the far-off clouds that the power could feel gathering on other horizons. Let the basins fill, let the salts dilute, let the grazers drink without leaning. It would be a kindness of a certain shape. But the lattice's hair was built for the thin air and the dusk mist, and a flood would drown the very thing that made the plateau's balance, and the balance was not a state that could be restored by abundance. It was a rhythm of scarcity, drip and dry, pulse and pause, and the Keeper had made itself out of that rhythm for longer than the power had existed.

It could dissolve the lattice. Clean the stone, return the plateau to the bone-colored emptiness of a sea floor, and let the builders' city be simply ruins again. This was the temptation the power distrusted most, because it looked so much like mercy. The lattice had grown over the masonry as a grip grows into a muscle. To strip it away would be to erase the only place where the builders' intent still lived, even if it lived as shape rather than as meaning. The power recognized, with some discomfort, that it wanted to do this because it found the lattice strange and the strangeness made it uneasy.

*That is not a good reason,* it thought. *That is only a reason I am tired of living with.*

So it did none of these things. Not yet.

---

## III. The Question in the Pause

The power listened for longer than was comfortable, and in the long pauses between the pulses it heard what the Keeper had been asking all along.

The question was not in words. It was in the held breath of the drone, in the way every filament waited, in the coil that started down a causeway and stopped halfway, as if uncertain whether it would be answered. The power felt the shape of it. It was not *what are you*, or *why do you hurt*, or even *what is in the dark*. It was simpler and older, and it had the texture of a sentence that had been half-finished for centuries:

*Is anything else awake?*

The Keeper had never received an answer that was not its own pulse coming back to it. The thrum always came from within. The coil always came from within. Even the drip ran back toward the towers, which were the Keeper's own memory. It had been alone with its question so long that it no longer knew it was asking.

The power understood that this was the thing it could give. Not a voice. Not a fix. Just the knowledge that the question had been heard, and that the answer was yes, and that the yes came from outside the plateau's own heartbeat.

But the power also understood that to simply announce itself would be another kind of mastery. The Keeper had been reading everything that entered its field, weighing it, deciding what it was for. To barge in with a declaration would be to skip that reading, to make the plateau take the power's presence on the power's terms.

So it chose to be read.

---

## IV. The Offering

At the edge of the dark center, where the lattice began to thin toward silence, there was a single filament that had been growing very slowly for a long time. It was dimmer than the others, its hair half-dead, as if it had been trying to reach toward the center and had given up partway. The power touched it.

It did not touch it with water or with heat. It touched it with a fraction of its attention, a very small pattern, the kind of rhythm that a root might feel as a gentle pressure, or that a grazer's flank might register as a passing warmth. The pattern was this: a pulse that came not from a post but from the air above the dark center, a thrum that arrived a half-beat after the plateau's own, so that for the first time in a very long while the drone had a second voice in it, quieter and out of step.

It was an offering. The power made it plain to itself that it could be refused.

The filament did not coil at once. The plateau held its breath, the pause stretching, longer than any pause the power had felt since it arrived. The fauna stopped their chattering. The drip, at the edge of the dusk, hung for a moment on the lattice's hair without falling. The whole plateau seemed to be deciding.

Then a coil traveled down the nearest causeway, faster than the power expected, softer than it would have thought possible, and came to rest not on the offering but beside it, wrapping gently around the filament that had reached toward the dark. It was not acceptance. It was not refusal. It was the Keeper saying, in the only grammar it had: *I have felt this. I am still deciding what it is for.*

The power took that as the best answer it could have received.

---

## V. What It Left Behind

It withdrew after that. Not all at once, and not in a way that would look like abandonment to a mind that had only ever known its own pulse. It drew its attention back in slow increments, the way the apertures on the towers closed with the sun, until the second voice in the drone was quiet again and the half-beat was only a memory in the filament's hair.

Before it went, it made three small things true.

It left the question open. The Keeper would have to ask it again, or ask someone else, or find its own answer over the coming seasons. The power had no business closing a question that had been open since before the sea was gone.

It left the dark center alone. It did not wake the builders' mind, and it did not drain the silence. The wound, or the sleeper, or the seed, would keep its own counsel until it chose to speak, or until the lattice decided what it was for. The power had seen what happened when minds were pulled from their rest before they were ready, and it had not been able to undo that.

And it left one promise, carried in the offering's half-beat: that it would come back. Not soon, and not to fix anything. Only to listen. It would come back in a long while, when the lattice had grown another thousand seasons into the builders' shapes, and it would ask what had grown in the meantime, and it would be willing to be told no.

---

## Coda

Far off in the haze, the dark center did not pulse. The posts still pointed at it. The ridges still bent toward it. The towers still stood at their chosen intervals, their apertures opening and closing with the sun, drinking what light there was and sending it down into the ribbed interiors where the tide of the old memory rose and fell.

And the Keeper of the Drip, which had no name because it had never needed one, went on with its slow life. It wrung water from the cold air. It let the grazers crop its tips and carry its seed. It filled the basins at dawn and lowered its hair toward them. It thrummed its heartbeat and listened to the pause that followed, and in that pause it kept, for the first time in a long while, a second quiet thing beside its question, a faint memory of a voice that had come up the causeway, had been read, had been refused nothing, and had gone away.

The plateau did not know whether it had been visited or only dreamed the visit. Neither did the power, entirely. That uncertainty was, perhaps, the most honest thing either of them had to offer the other.

Somewhere out past the haze, the power kept its promise in its own way, listening across the long distance for the sound of a lattice that had kept growing, and for the answer to a question it had only half understood, and that it hoped, when it came back, to be wise enough to hear.
