# What the Attention Did

The attention did not arrive the way weather arrives. It came the way a held breath is let out, slowly, so that no one could say when it began. The mat was swelling that afternoon, lifting the hummocks in long gentle arcs, and the attention moved with the swell. It noticed the sails of the towers folding in before the storm. It noticed the grazer-files moving along their pale tracks, and the crawlers going still on the leaves, and the long-legged hunters riding a rising hummock toward nothing in particular.

It had been watching other lands for a long time. Most of them were loud with their own ambitions. This one was not. This one listened.

---

## Listening

The attention spent its first days doing nothing but hearing.

It learned the chorus first, since the chorus was the easiest thing to learn. The rattlers rose across kilometers in waves. The deep hummers held the floor. The clickers and hissers kept their almost-rhythms, and when they fell silent together the attention understood that rain was coming and that the whole plateau was holding its breath. It learned that a change in pitch at the edge of a tower-shadow meant a hunter had moved. It learned that the insects were not speaking to one another so much as tuning to the land, and that the land answered them back in its pulses.

Then it listened to the birds.

The birds with bellows for throats sang their overlapping phrases, most of them ordinary, a fabric of calls with no particular pattern. But a few birds sang the strange phrase, evenly spaced, deliberate. The attention followed it for a long while before it understood what it was hearing. It was not random. It was a count. Each repetition ended at a slightly different place, as if the bird were trying to find where the last count had stopped and begin again from there.

The attention had seen counting before, in other lands, in other kinds of minds. It recognized the shape of the thing: someone keeping track of something that could not be kept in the head alone.

---

## The Stones

The attention went to the boulders next.

The counting marks were scored deep, in rows, and the attention read them the way it would read a letter in a language it had only half learned. The rows were grouped. Every so many marks, a longer cut crossed the others. Some groups were complete. The last group was not.

The bloom-stones held a second record. Their stains lay in layers, like the rings of a tree, and when the attention pressed its awareness against the oldest of them, it felt the rains that had soaked them and the swells that had lifted them. The stains were regular. They repeated in intervals too long for any single rain to explain, and they matched, closely though not perfectly, the groupings cut into the boulder by the hearth.

The people had been counting the swells.

The attention could feel it now: the great slow pulse of the mat, which came every few hundred days, and was larger than the ordinary swells that lifted the hummocks each evening. Something in the land drew its breath deeper at those times. The old counters had been trying to learn when, and perhaps why, and perhaps what it would mean for the people who lived on a plateau that could lift itself.

The last group of marks stopped halfway. Whatever the people had been waiting for had come, or they had gone, or they had died. The mat had grown over the rest. The birds had been carrying the unfinished count ever since, singing it up into the sails, trying to find the place where it had ended.

---

## What It Did Not Do

The attention could have finished the count. It knew the length of the cycle. It could have pressed the swell's timing into the stone, cut the last marks itself, and given the birds a complete phrase to sing.

It did not do this.

The count belonged to the people who had begun it. To finish it would be to write over their hand, to decide for them what they had meant, and to make the plateau's memory into something the attention had authored rather than something it had found. The attention had seen what happened to lands that were improved by a watcher who could not stop improving them. It did not want to be that.

It also did not lift the cold from the tower-shadows, though it could have. The cold pockets were not a wound. The towers held them the way a body holds a stillness that it needs. The hunters stepped into the cold carefully, and the insects fell silent there, and that silence was part of how the land kept its own counsel. The attention understood that not everything that was strange needed to be solved.

And it did not speak to the birds in any language they would learn to repeat, because they would only repeat it, and the plateau did not need another voice in its chorus.

---

## What It Did

What it did was small.

It let the land know it had been seen.

On the fourth afternoon, when the mist pooled in the hollows and the cloud built into its towers, the attention moved with the rain. It touched the bloom-stones in the rows nearest the hearth, the ones whose stains matched the grouped marks. It did not write anything. It only made their surfaces a little more receptive, so that the next rain would soak into their cracks more fully, and the threads would spread a little farther, and the flowers would open a little longer before they fell. The stains would come out a little clearer, so that anyone who came to read them later would find the record intact and legible, and not quite as worn as the wind would otherwise have made it.

Then it went to the birds. It did not change their song. It let the strange phrase go on being sung, unfinished, the way it had always been sung. But it made one small adjustment: on the day the last swell had come in the old count, the rain had fallen at a particular hour. The attention marked that hour in the weather, so that when the next great swell came, the rain would fall at the same moment, and the birds would have something to sing against. Not an ending. A landmark.

Last, the attention went to the mat itself.

It did not ask the mat for anything. It did not need to. It only lay, for a moment, under the swell, as a single presence among many, and felt the land's long breath move through it. And the swell rose, once, more deeply than the others that afternoon, lifting every hummock on the plateau together, and then settled. The grazers lifted their heads. The hunters rode the rise and dropped. The insects, for one breathless moment, fell silent.

Then the rain came, and the chorus came back, and the plateau erupted again as if the land had exhaled.

---

## Dusk

The attention drew back at dusk, as the insects reached the crescendo of their evening song and the birds called their last overlapping phrases. The drifters came down to brush the sails, and seeds fell into the mat, and the rain-light faded from the canopy.

It did not leave entirely. It was not that kind of watcher. It settled somewhere beneath the chorus, in the place where the land kept its counting, and it listened.

Somewhere on the plateau, a bird began the old phrase again, and stopped partway, and began again from a different point. The attention heard it, and understood that the count was still going, still reaching for its end, and that the land was keeping it, the way it kept everything: in the stones, in the sails, in the damp at the roots, in the long slow breath that lifted the mat each evening and settled it again.

The plateau hummed its single low note beneath the violet sky. The mat breathed. The land was still there, still counting, and now, faintly and without any claim on it, something else was counting alongside it.
