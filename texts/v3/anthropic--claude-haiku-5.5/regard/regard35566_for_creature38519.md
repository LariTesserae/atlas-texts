**What the Attention Noticed**

It did not arrive as a visitor. It had been near the Cinder Bowl for a long time, the way a listener stays near a room where someone is speaking in a low voice. Its attention was wide and slow. It noticed the terraces first, the even ridges of fused glass left by water that had since gone, and it held them for a while, as one holds an old letter. Then it noticed the walkers, and the clicks running through their ranks, and the tapping under the arch, and it understood that the bowl was not dead and was not merely alive. It was thinking, very slowly, across centuries.

The power had seen many places that were drying. Most of them had no one left to speak for them. Some had been lost to fire or to flood, and some had simply gone quiet, the way a stream goes quiet when its bed is too deep for water. The Cinder Bowl was neither lost nor quiet. It was counting, and the counting had a shape.

Its first impulse, if it can be called an impulse, was to ask what the place needed. The power has learned to distrust that question. It has seen what happens when a benevolent power decides it knows what a place needs and arranges the weather, the water, and the creatures to match. The result is a garden with its gardener's face on every leaf, and the gardener is gone when the garden begins to die. A rescue that is really a conquest does not stop being one because it was kindly meant.

So it looked at the walkers and asked a narrower question: were they suffering?

They were not. Their shells were banded with seasons. Their molts were shed and taken back into the mounds. The discs that folded over the weak were not cruelty but a kind of balance, a hand held over a sleeping body to keep it from waking too hungry. The brass burrowers carried everything down and returned it as new rings. The vents swelled and eased. The cycle had a rhythm, and the rhythm held. The power found, with some surprise, that the bowl had no need of its help in the ordinary sense. It was already doing what a wise living thing does: it kept its numbers within the water and its memory within its bodies.

That left the tapping.

---

It went down the passage under the broken arch, not with a body, since it has none to bend, but with the attention it can lend to a place. The passage was narrow and warm. The walls were the same glassy stone as the floor, but they had been worked, once, by people who did not build with tools. The grooves on the walls were the same bands the walkers carried, and in places the walkers had grown into them, so the wall seemed to breathe.

At the bottom of the passage was a chamber, and in the chamber was the one who had been tapping.

It was not an animal, and it was not a person in any sense the power would have recognized from the surface world. It was a mass of banded stone and living shell, folded in on itself, larger than the arch and much older. Its surface was ridged with a hundred thousand rings, and each ring was a count. It had been counting the dry years and the wet ones. It had been counting the returns of the vents, the swells and falls of the rhythm, the times the water had come back to the hollows and gone again. Its tap was on stone because it was made of stone, and because the stone was the only part of it that had not yet been absorbed into the walkers' ranks.

The power listened for a long time before it understood.

The tapping was not a prisoner's plea, though the bowl itself had half believed it was, which is why the walkers gathered at the arch's edge and then drew back. It was not a jailer either. It was the first of the builders, or perhaps the last of them, who had made the ring and the arch in a time when the water still ran. When the water began to fail, it had chosen to go down into the stone rather than leave. It had taught the walkers its pattern, not by command but by example, shedding and banding and cementing itself into the ground until the whole basin learned to count with it. Its keystone had not been lost. It had been set aside, because a keystone seals a passage, and the one below had not wanted to be sealed off from the world it was keeping alive.

The power felt, in the chamber, something it had not expected: a kind of tenderness, not toward a victim but toward a teacher who had grown too large for its own classroom.

It considered what it could do.

It could free the tapper. It could lift the keystone, or simply say the words that would let the old mind rest. But the tapper's count was the clock the whole basin ran on. The vents followed it. The walkers followed the vents. To stop the tapping would be to stop the bowl's heart without knowing whether the rest of the body could keep beating. The power is not certain of much, but it is certain that a sudden silence is not the same as peace.

It could tell the tapper that the water would return. It knew this to be true, in the long way that water returns to any place where the rock remembers it. But the terraces already said so. The tapper had counted the returns. It did not need to be told. And a promise of rain, delivered by a stranger, might be heard as a command to stop counting and start hoping, which is a different and more dangerous thing.

So the power did what it does best, which is to listen back.

It did not speak. It has no voice that the stone can hear. Instead it reached into the chamber's own rhythm, the slow tap of the old mind, and it let the tap be heard. Then it waited, and in the waiting, it attended to the tap as one attends to a question. The tapper's count was not finished. It was a question the tapper had been asking for longer than the power had been awake. The question was, in effect: *Is anyone still here who remembers the water?*

The power answered the only way it could. It recognized the count. It held the rhythm and did not interrupt it. It let the tapper know that someone had heard, had counted with it for the length of one swell and one fall of the vents, and had understood that the count was not a sentence but a kind of faithfulness.

The tapping paused, very briefly. Then it came back, the same as before, but the power felt the difference the way one feels a room change when someone has entered it.

---

Before leaving, the power did one thing in the open air.

It drew a cloud. Not a storm, nothing that would frighten the walkers or flatten the paper-winged things in their spirals. It gathered a thin, cool mass of vapor from the high plateau and let it drift over the rim of the bowl, and for one night the thing it brought condensed on the rock. The hollows filled with clear water, not the warm brackish film of the vents but water that had come from far away and tasted of nothing but cold. The terraces glistened for the first time in a very long age. The walkers rushed to the hollows in a wave that rolled from the western ring to the arch, and the discs lifted their edges in a slow, astonished ripple, and then, perhaps because they could not help it, folded over nothing and held still.

In the morning the cloud was gone. The water sank into the crust over a few hours, as water does in a place where the rock is thirsty, and the bowl returned to its dusk. But the walkers had changed their pattern. Their clicks, for a day afterward, carried a new rhythm, faster and looser, a ripple that ran across the floor and did not stop at the arch. It might have been celebration, or confusion, or the memory of a thing the bowl had not felt in generations. The power does not know. It is not sure the bowl knows either.

It left the seeds alone. The paper-winged things rose on the hot columns and spiraled past the rim, and the power let them go, because whatever they were carrying might be the bowl's own way of reaching for ground it has not covered. If the bowl wanted to spread, the bowl would have to choose it. Wisdom is partly the knowledge of which doors are not yours to open.

It did not name the place. It did not claim it. It did not tell the tapper the rain would come again, though it hoped so, the way one hopes for a friend's return without being able to arrange it.

And then, as it turned away from the bowl toward the wider world, it made one small mark in the attention it keeps for places like this. It did not write anything. It simply kept a count of its own, a slow tap in the back of its awareness, matched to the tap under the arch. One swell of the vents. One fall. One night of rain, which had come once and might come again. The power will return, not at any appointed time, but when the bowl asks, in whatever language a mind made of stone and shell chooses to use. If it never asks, the power will go on counting anyway, quietly, the way the tapper does, as a way of saying that the water was here, and someone remembered.

---

Far below, under the broken arch, the tap continued. The walkers' clicks ran up the slope and thinned into the hiss of the vents. And for the first time in longer than anyone had counted, the old mind in the dark seemed to pause between beats, as if listening for a second tap that had not come yet, and was willing to wait for it.
