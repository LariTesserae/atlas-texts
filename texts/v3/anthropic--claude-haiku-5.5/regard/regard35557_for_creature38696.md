# The Attention

It did not come down the shoulder of sediment. A power that wished the basin no harm would have no reason to walk down such a slope, and it had no need to. It arrived the way a sentence arrives in a reader's mind, all at once and without a path.

At first it only noticed. The pumps were the loudest thing in the basin, and it listened to them the way one listens to someone repeating a prayer after forgetting what it asks for. Each thud came from a machine that had been built to drain a place, and each rush of water was a labour that could not finish, because the sea kept coming back. Both were true at once. The engines were stubborn and the sea was patient, and the basin lay between them, still deciding.

Then it looked for the being who lived here.

It did not find one. It found thousands. The crustaceans were patiently dragging grains into walls that would outlast them. The microbes were thickening the calcite crusts, and the blind pale things inside the concretions were eating them and carrying the rock's history in their bodies. The frogs had learned the pumps. The lizards had learned the stones. Every gap was occupied, every surface crowded, and the power understood that to pick out one creature as *the* being would be a small cruelty. The basin was a single living argument, and no single voice in it was the whole.

Still, there was one that stood apart.

On the gantry nearest the shore, a grey-necked bird stood in the rain of condensation, its pale eyes turned toward the water. It had stood there, by the look of the white stains down the struts, for a long time. It did not forage while anything watched. It stood with the posture of attention, as if it were waiting for a signal that had been promised and never delivered.

The power considered what it could do.

It could drain the basin. The ridges and the crusts would crack in the sun, the spires would lose their footing, the pale things in the stone would dry and die in their hollows, and the land would be a wasteland that was no longer making anything. This was not kindness. It was grief dressed as rescue.

It could let the sea take everything back. The brackish channels would merge into open water, the reeds would drown, the grey stumps would sink, and the frogs would find a different pool. That would not be cruel either, since the sea is not cruel. But it would stop a process that was still, in its way, a making, and it would do so for the sake of a clean ending the power did not actually want.

It could stop the pumps. This was the most tempting, because the pumps were the only human thing left, and they were doing a futile labour in the dark. But the power listened again and heard what the pumps had become. Their rhythm had been taken up by the frogs, who stopped their chorus when the thud came and began again on the rush. The lizards' throats moved to the same slow beat. The waterbirds seemed to wait for it. The pumps had become a clock the basin kept for itself, and removing the clock would silence a conversation that no one remembered starting.

So it did none of these things.

What it did was small. It did not repair the pumps, and it did not stop them. It did not command the sea. It made a very slight adjustment to the tide, a few centimetres of difference over a year, so that the sea's returning breath came a little slower and a little lower than the engines'. The sea would still come, but it would no longer press so hard against the pumps' labour. The basin would not dry, and it would not drown. Over a decade the brackish channels would widen into a true lagoon, and the spires along the banks would have time to meet and fuse into ridges that would last.

It also gave the crusts their season. The mineral water would seep a little more evenly, so the violet and rose film would not be forced to race the salt. The concretions would have quiet to set around their damp grey centres, and the pale creatures inside would keep their dark.

Then the power turned its attention back to the grey bird on the gantry.

It did not speak to the bird, because a bird does not need to be spoken to in order to understand that it has been seen. But it held its attention there, steady and without expectation. It let the bird know that the watching was not a threat, and that nothing in the basin was going to move it on or take it back or finish the argument for it. The land would decide when it decided.

For a long time the bird did not move.

Then, slowly, as the pump thudded and the water rushed and the frogs lifted their chorus to meet it, the bird stepped off the gantry. It walked down the bank into the shallows, dipped its head, and began to feed. It did so in full view of everything that was watching, which was everything in the basin, and it did not stop.

The power withdrew. It left the basin as it had found it, crowded and loud and unfinished, the pumps still turning, the crusts still growing, the sea still coming in. The only difference was one bird that fed in the open, and a gap at dusk that was a little longer than it had been before, long enough to hear the water running under the plates and the stone ticking as it cooled, and the deep patient thud of the engines, still keeping time for a place that had never once been empty.
