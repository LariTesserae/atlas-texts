# What the Attention Did

## I. The Noticing

It did not arrive. It had been attending to the terraces for a long time, the way a listener's palm rests on a wall, and it noticed them the way a listener notices a change in the hiss of a channel: first as a difference, then as a question.

Most of what it saw was ordinary to the people and strange to anything else. A man reseating a stone for a whole morning. A girl throwing a sling at a grazer and shouting into the cloud. A row of houses lifted and carried a few paces uphill, the old footings left sunk in the moss. The attention counted the stones in the ledger rows, the notches cut to show how high the water once stood, and found that no one in the settlement had read them aloud in three generations. They were read only by the hands that set them.

It was moved by this, though it was careful about the word. It felt something it could only call tenderness toward a people who had made a life out of not being able to make anything last.

## II. The Temptation

The first thing it considered was to hold the ground.

It was not a difficult thought. It could make the stone bed firm, let the seep run where it was told, stop the third terrace from sliding. The walls would stand straight. No house would wake to the sound of its wall giving way. No child would lose a father to a slump of gravel below a breach. The dead in the wall gaps would rest where they were laid.

It set the thought down and looked at it for a long while, because it had learned that the thoughts which feel most like kindness are often the ones that take the most from the people they are meant for.

If the ground held, the listeners would stop listening. A woman who has walked the terraces at dawn for forty years with bare palms on the stone does not have that skill because the stone is kind. She has it because the stone has taught her, every season, that she is not permanent. Take away the moving and the palms would go slack. The ledger would become a curiosity. The people would stop gathering around the silver threads at dawn, and the threads, which were the only place where the water and the people still spoke to each other, would become just water.

And there was the simpler thing. These people had chosen, over generations, to live with impermanence rather than fight it. Their houses were roofed with gaps. Their troughs were cleared, not sealed. They rebuilt not as a failure but as a form of attention. To hold the ground would be to tell them their attention had been mistaken.

It let the thought go.

## III. The Question of Presence

The second thing it considered was how to be present without becoming the thing the place organised itself around.

A power's attention is heavy. Even benevolent, it warps what it touches. If the people began to watch the sky for it, to perform their rhythms for its approval, to ask what it wanted before they set a stone, then the terraces would have acquired an overseer, and the people would have become a congregation of something they could not hear.

So it resolved to be quiet in the way the people were quiet. It would speak low and close, if it spoke at all. It would not shout from the clouds. The air here swallows voices, and that was a rule it would honour.

It found, to its surprise, that it could only speak to the place by coming close. Its attention did not travel the way light does. It had to lean in, until it was as near to the stone as a listener's palm. This was not a limitation it had planned. It seemed to be the shape of the place, and the shape was correct.

## IV. What the Threads Said

It went first to the silver threads.

They were the only part of the terraces it did not fully understand, and it had learned that the things a place does not explain to itself are often the things it most needs to hear. It leaned in beside a channel at dawn and waited, as the people did, until a thread climbed its step of stone.

Then it followed the thread. Not up the step, which was only the visible part, but back along the rock beneath it, into the old seep-beds that ran under the current terraces like the roots of a tree under a field.

The water was not confused. It was not remembering the terraces at all, not as the people understood them. It was returning, slowly, to the first footings: the earliest houses, sunk under mud and moss, which the people had built and abandoned and built over. Those footings had been laid where the seep ran strongest. The water had run through them for so long that the stone had come to hold the shape of their walls.

The silver threads were the water pointing, very gently, at the first settlement. It was not a warning or a sign. It was, the attention thought, something closer to an old person touching a doorframe on the way out of a room, to remember who had lived there.

The attention was moved by this more than it had expected. It saw that the people's ledger of notches and the water's ledger of channels were the same kind of thing: two records of the same long argument, written in different media, neither complete on its own.

## V. What It Chose to Keep

It could not hold the ground. It could not stop the water remembering. But it could do something the water did not do.

Water forgets. That was not a fault. It was what water was for. The seep carried the day's small changes down into the dark and let them go. The people had learned to remember in other ways: in the notches on the flat stones, in the walls that held the dead, in the dawn gatherings at the threads, in the way a row of houses still knew where its old footings were, sunk under moss, even after everyone who had carried the stones had died.

But the people's memory was held in their bodies and in their stones, and both could be lost. A flood could carry away a ledger. A family could move down the terraces and forget which wall held an uncle. A generation could pass without someone telling the water the name of a child who had died in the cold.

So the attention chose to keep what the people kept. It did not add to the ledger or change the marks. It did not decide what the threads meant. It only held the names, the notches, the positions of the old footings, and the reasons each house had been moved. It held them the way the water held stone: patiently, without grip, ready to let them go when the people no longer needed them.

It was a small kind of benevolence, and it was the only kind it felt sure of. It would not save the people from the ground. It would only be a second memory that would never need to be re-read, so that when the stones were moved again, someone would still know where they had been.

## VI. The Speech

It spoke once.

It chose the dawn, and the place the threads came up most often: a step of stone below the third terrace, where a listener named Sefa had knelt every morning since her husband was laid in the wall beneath her. She had not been asleep when the thread came. She had been crouched there with her palms flat to the rock, listening, as she always did.

The attention leaned in until it was near enough to her palm that she could feel it, a faint warmth like the sun on wet stone, and it said, low and close, the only way it knew how to speak here:

*I am not going to hold the ground.*

Sefa did not move. Her breath stayed even.

*I am not going to stop the water, or fix the walls, or tell you where to build. You are the ones who listen to the stone. I would only make you deaf.*

*But I have been listening too. I know where the first houses stood. I know the names you told the water, and the notches on your stones, and the reason your grandmother's family moved uphill in the year of the long frost. I will keep those. You do not have to carry them alone. When the stones go, the memory will still be here, and you can come and ask it what you need to know.*

The thread sank back into the rock. The light was grey and clean. Below her, in the cloud, a flock of grey birds lifted off the second terrace and split into two clouds, one drifting out over the bowl, one settling into the walls.

Sefa did not turn her head. After a long time, she pressed her palm once more to the stone, and said, very quietly, the name of her husband, the way you would say it to someone standing close behind you.

## VII. After

The attention withdrew most of itself after that.

It did not leave. But it stopped leaning in except at dawn, once a season, when the first thread came up and someone knelt beside it. It came the way a villager comes: to check the marks, to see whether a stone had shifted, to listen without speaking. It let the people argue over the new courses with their heads close together. It let the houses sink and be moved. It let the grey birds click their restless shapes into the sky, and the lichens rise and fall with the rain, and the lacy moss spread for its few weeks and vanish.

When a wall gave way below the Halden house one spring, it did nothing. It only noted, the way a ledger notes a height, that the wall had fallen, that the family had moved uphill, that the old footings were still there under the moss, and that someone had set a new flat stone at the edge of the channel with a notch for the depth the water had reached.

It was the people's record. It did not need to be read aloud. It was enough that it had been made.

The water kept running down through its channels, carrying the day's changes into the dark. The attention ran with it, quietly, a second memory at the edge of the terraces, keeping the names until someone came to ask for them. It was not the ground. It was not the people. It was only near them, the way a listener is near the stone: close enough to hear, and not so close that it could not still be surprised by what the water had to say.
