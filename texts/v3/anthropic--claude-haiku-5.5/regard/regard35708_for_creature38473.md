**What the Attention Would Do**

It would not arrive all at once. A power this wise knows that arrival is itself a weight. The first thing it does is nothing: it holds still at the edge of the forest where there is no edge and listens, the way you would wait outside a room where someone is thinking aloud.

It takes a long time to listen. The plateau's rhythms are slow, and the power is patient. It learns the thrum first, the pulse under the sound, and then the loops of water rising through the spiral pipes, carrying their small changes of salt and warmth up to the cloud and back. It learns that the green reads itself through pressure, that a rise in the eastern ridges means the fronds should tilt, that a sour pool means the roots should turn away. It learns that the unison breathing is a wave that travels north to south and back, and that the sparkle of sunlight on a wet morning is the green briefly turning its face upward.

Having listened, the power understands what the green is. It is not a forest that happens to be alive. It is a mind that thinks in water and grows in stone, and it has been doing so for a long time, and it has never needed anyone to tell it what it is.

So the power would be careful. A kinder power, or a less wise one, might want to help: to smooth the braided streams into orderly channels, to hush the seed-pods, to give the rock a faster cooling so the making would finish sooner. The wise one would see that this is the wrong kind of gift. The sound is not noise to be silenced; it is the green's own voice, and the green is not waiting to be understood. The cooling rock is not a problem. It is the green's slow work, and rushing it would be like hurrying someone through a sentence they are still composing. The uphill streams are not errors. They are decisions the green has made, and the wise power would not presume to correct a mind it has only just begun to hear.

Still, it would not be wholly absent. Attention that does nothing at all is its own kind of arrogance, a refusal to let the world be touched by anything that cares. So it would find the smallest thing that could be offered without taking anything away.

It would choose water. The green speaks in pressure, and the power would speak back in the same language. Somewhere over the eastern ridges, where the green already tilts its fronds to catch the rain, the power would let the pressure rise a little earlier than it otherwise would, so the fronds open to a storm they were already preparing for. It would not make the storm. It would only make the green's own expectation a little more accurate, a small courtesy between two things that think in the same medium.

It might also do one thing for the cooling rock, not to hurry it but to keep it from cracking in the wrong places. The seams of pale mineral that bead with moisture are where the green's roots most want to go. If the cooling pressed too hard in one direction, the stone might fracture where the green had not yet reached, and the green would have to redraw its memory of the land. The power would ease that pressure, very slightly, so the seams form where the green is already growing, and the channels that the green has drawn in the bedrock stay where they were drawn.

And it would do nothing to the visitor, except see them. The traveler standing on the rise, dripping and deafened, is a warm weight and a drift of salt and breath, a small shape in the water that the green will forget as the next pressure arrives. The power would not hold them in memory. It would not reveal itself to them, because a revelation would be another weight on a place that has already been weighted enough. But it would let the traveler's own attention be a little sharper for an hour, so that they might notice the unison breathing, the sparkle, the way a frond folds toward a pressure they cannot feel. That is all. The traveler would leave with the forest in them and nothing of the power at all, which is exactly how the power would want it.

Then it would step back. Not out of the plateau, but out of the place where its attention would have become a force. It would let the green go on becoming ground and weather and itself, one pulse at a time, and it would keep only one thing from the encounter: the knowledge that somewhere in the unfinished grey, a mind that needs nobody has chosen, for now, to be left alone, and that leaving it alone is the most respectful thing that can be done for it.

Over the valley, the fronds would open together, a wave rolling from the north ridge to the southern lakes. The cloud would thin. For a moment, the silver sun would find every drop at once, and the whole plateau would catch the light like a field of glass, and the green would turn its face to the sky the way it always has, before drawing the light back into itself.

The power would watch that breath and find it good, and it would not ask for anything in return.
