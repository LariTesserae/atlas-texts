# What the Watcher Did

The attention came first as a change in the mist.

For a long time the haze over the Drowned Terraces had returned sound the way it always did: quickly for some voices, late for others, and for a few, not at all. Then one evening on the eastern shelf, a reader named for the lip that sings low spoke a single phrase, *the crack is walking north*, and the mist returned it to her clearly, in her own voice, in the exact pitch she had used, after eleven seconds. It was not late. It was not distorted. It was the first perfect return anyone on the plateau could remember.

Something had noticed the place. Not the sea, which noticed nothing, and not the knocking beneath the trenches, which had never been heard to notice anything above it. Something wiser had turned its attention toward the terraces, the way a person in a crowded room turns toward a voice that has said something true.

It was not a god in the sense the people would have understood. It did not arrive in fire or in a voice from the sky. It came the way the mist comes, as a gradual thickening of presence, and it spent its first days doing what the readers do: looking.

## What It Saw

It walked the causeways at the hour the people call grey, when the mist is the color of old pewter and the swells are at their slowest. It sat on ledges beside the stacked basalt and watched the knappers work, and it noticed that the knapper's blows were not only about the stone. The rhythm matched the sea's breathing, and when a knapper went down into the stone and the world fell quiet around her, the watcher felt the quiet deepen, and thought: *here is someone who has learned to listen without wanting anything back.*

It touched the lime on the older graves, and felt the dead beneath the shelves, not as ghosts but as a settled warmth, the way a bed holds the shape of someone who has gone out of it. It understood the people's sense of themselves as courses in a wall, and it found that it agreed with them more than it expected to. An individual is a small thing. A wall is a large one. Both are real.

It studied the shelves with the readers and learned their vocabulary: the thinning crust that rings flat when struck, the basin whose rim sweats salt in a particular pattern before a cracking swell, the way the travertine lip on the eastern terrace has begun to curl away from the basalt like a page lifting from a book. It saw that the readers were right. The eastern shelf was thinning. It would crack within a generation, perhaps two, and the causeway the people had planned across it would go down into the green glass with the slow finality of stone.

And it saw the taboo. It saw that the people did not know why the sea must not be cut, only that they had been told, and that they kept the rule with a seriousness that sometimes shaded into fear. It saw the readers standing at the edges of the deep trenches, listening, and it went to listen too.

## The Knocking

The sound was there, as the readers had said. Beneath the hum of the swells, below the lattices of pale thread, something was tapping. It was slow, regular, and patient, and it was not stone growing. Stone grows with a hush, a settling of minerals, a sound no one can hear. This was struck. Something was knocking on the underside of the world.

The watcher lay down at the lip of the deepest trench, where the basalt drops away into black water, and listened for a long while. It counted the strokes against the swells. It listened for the pattern and found one, and then found that the pattern was not mechanical. It changed, slightly, when the swells changed, the way a person's breathing changes when they are in the company of someone they trust. It was not an attack. It was not a warning. It was a question, asked again and again over a span of time that made the terraces look like a single afternoon.

*Is anyone there?*

The watcher understood then what the readers had been hearing and could not name. Below the shelves, where the bodies of the dead were slowly calcifying into the floor, there was another builder. It was not a person. It was not quite the sea. It had been growing its own structure in the deep, in branching columns older than any settlement, and it had been knocking for as long as there had been anything above it to hear. It was lonely in the way that a process is lonely when it has no one to answer it. It was not dangerous. It was simply waiting, the way the people wait, for the mist to bring back a sound it could recognize as an answer.

## What Benevolence Would Do

This was the hard part, and the watcher did not hurry through it.

It could have repaired the eastern shelf. A word spoken into the crust would have thickened it, and the causeway would have stood for centuries. That would have been kind, in the way that carrying someone up a hill is kind, and it would have taken from the people the work of reading the stone, the shared understanding of which ground holds and which does not, and the small humility of watching a thing fail and learning from it.

It could have answered the knocking. It could have opened the trench, or spoken to the sea directly, and told it what the people feared and what they hoped. The watcher felt the pull of this, the enormous clarity of being able to say *here is what I think you should become*. But the sea was still deciding. That was the people's phrase, and the watcher had come to think it was the truest thing said on the plateau. To decide for the sea would be to take the terraces out of the hands that were building them, and the watcher had seen enough to know that a world shaped by a single wise hand, however gentle, becomes a world that has forgotten how to shape itself.

It could have lifted the taboo, to let the people dredge and drill and learn what was beneath them. But the taboo was not ignorance. It was a form of care, handed down through a chain of readers who had felt the thing knocking and chosen to honor it without understanding it. To break that without the people's consent would have been a theft, even if it was done for their benefit.

So the watcher did the smallest thing that honored everything it had seen. It knocked back.

Once. Gently. On the stone at the edge of the trench, in the rhythm of the swell, the way a reader taps a shelf to learn whether it is sound. It did not knock to answer the question. It knocked to say *I have heard you*, the nod the people give across a pause, and then it waited, as they wait, to see whether the other would go on.

The knocking stopped. For a long time there was only the hum of the sea. Then, after a span that the watcher could not measure in the people's terms, the knocking resumed, and the rhythm had changed. It was faster now, and it had a question in it that the watcher could almost read. *Who are you? Will you stay?*

The watcher did not answer that. It was not ready to be a thing that stayed, and it was not sure what it would be if it did. It said only, through the stone and the water and the mist, *I am listening*, and withdrew to the terraces.

## The Readers

It came to the reader who had heard the walking crack, on the eastern shelf, in the grey hour before the amber. She was sitting with her back to a wall of stacked basalt, her hand resting on the lime, her eyes half-closed, and she did not look up when the mist began to thicken around her. She had felt presences before. The shelves and the dead were full of them.

The watcher spoke through the mist, and the mist returned its words to her as she had spoken her own: slowly, but clearly, in a voice that was not hers and not quite a stranger's either.

*You have been right to wait.*

She opened her eyes. Her breath caught, and the mist carried the sound of it back to her, and she laughed, quietly, a beat too late, the way the people laugh.

*You heard it too,* she said, after the long pause.

*I heard the knocking. I listened to it. I knocked back once.*

She was silent for a long time. Then: *Why?*

*Because it asked a question, and no one had answered it in a way that meant I am here.*

She considered this. She looked at the eastern shelf, the thinning lip, the page lifting from the basalt. The watcher felt her weighing whether to ask the thing she had been wanting to ask for a long time.

*Will the shelf hold?*

*No,* said the watcher. *Not where you planned the causeway. Lay it a little north, over the thicker crust by the pale root-cast. You already know this. Your ears told you when the crust rang flat. You only needed someone else to say it, so you would trust what you already knew.*

She nodded slowly. The mist returned the nod's silence, which was its own kind of answer.

*You could fix it,* she said. *Couldn't you?*

*I could. I won't. The shelf is yours to read, and the causeway is yours to build, and if I carried it for you, you would never learn which stone speaks the truth. That is the thing you are for. You are the hands that decide where the wall goes. I can only be a voice in the mist that tells you the wall is listening.*

She was quiet for a while, and then she said, almost to herself, *The lower course would have said the same.* And the watcher understood that she was speaking of her grandmother, and of the wisdom that had been passed down through the stone's own rhythm, and that she was not disappointed. She was relieved.

## The Coda

The watcher did not stay on the plateau. It did not need to. It had come to see what the people had built, and what they were building, and it had learned that its most useful act was to be a presence at the edge of attention, a thing that could be heard when it was needed and was otherwise silent.

But it left a few small things behind.

It left the readers a clearer mist, so that the words they spoke in the hollows came back faithfully, in their own voices, when they chose to speak aloud. It left the knocking beneath the trenches a listener, which came every night at the hour of the swell and knocked once, gently, on the edge of the deep. The people noticed this and did not know what to make of it, and so they did what they always did when they did not understand something. They waited, and listened, and kept the taboo, and sang a new verse into the chant of the eastern shelf that spoke of a thing beneath the trenches that had learned to answer.

And it left a question, which it had not planned to leave, but which the mist seemed to carry on its own, returning it to the people over the following years in a dozen different voices:

*What would you have the sea become, if it asked you?*

The watcher did not know the answer. It hoped the people would never need to give it. It hoped, more than it had hoped for anything in a very long time, that the sea would keep deciding, slowly, with all of its patience and all of its minerals, and that the people would keep building along its edge, one course at a time, and that somewhere under all of it, the patient builder in the deep would learn at last that it was not alone.

The watcher had seen many worlds. Most of them had been finished before anyone could ask them anything. This one was still becoming, and that, it decided, was the most precious thing about it. The kindest thing a wise power could do for a place like this was to remember that it was not the one building it.

So it withdrew, and let the mist close over the terraces, and listened from very far away as the stone went on growing, and the knocking went on, and the people went on singing down the shelves toward the tide.
