# What the Attending Did

I came to the shelf the way attention comes to anything that has been patient for a long time: slowly, and mostly by listening. For a long while I did nothing but hear it. The drip from each ledge, the tick of cooling crystal, the hollow knock of a shifted stone. Then I heard the pattern under the noise, the rime's rhythm and the braided water's slow rearranging and the knapping that answered itself across the hollows like a canon that had been running for longer than anything I could name.

The first thing I noticed was how much did not need me. The frost cracks the rock and the salts build the knobs. The living ones feed from the weather and the weather feeds on them, and nobody can say where one stops. The cairns fall and the grit takes them down a finger's width a season, and no one reaches down to lift them. That is not neglect. The falling is part of the grammar. A cairn that has been overwritten by the next season is still a sentence, and the frost that finishes it is also a speaker.

So I decided I would not fix anything.

It would have been easy. I could have reset every collapsed course in a single winter. I could have warmed the eastern pools so the figures did not have to stand in the cold, or cleared the grit from the hearth, or given the crystal a better angle to the sun. Each of these would have looked like kindness from outside, and each would have quietly replaced the living ones' work with mine. They would have stopped striking stones and listening, because there would have been nothing left to listen for. A shelf where everything is kept is a shelf where nothing is answered. I have seen what happens to a people when the thing they tend is taken out of their hands by someone who loves it more loudly than they do.

What I did instead was go to the alcove.

The crust is thin there and warm, and I could feel the knocking through it the way you feel a pulse through a wrist. It is slow and regular, with an irregularity inside the regularity that I think is the important part. It is not the rhythm of a tool being struck, though it has the same measure. It is not the sound of stones falling in a fracture, though it has the same patience. It is closer to counting. Something under the glass has been keeping time for a very long time and has not yet reached the end of the count. When the living ones bring salt and the crust cracks where they set it, the count quickens for a few beats. I think that is not hunger, or not only hunger. I think it is recognition, the way a person looks up when someone says their name in a crowd.

I did not go under. The living ones do not go under, and they have a reason I could feel in the stone: whatever is down there is not theirs to bring up, and perhaps not mine. A thing that has counted this long has earned the right to finish its count without being hauled out into the light.

So I spoke in the only language the shelf has.

I struck the stone at the edge of the crust. Two strikes, a pause, then another. It is the living ones' answer, the sequence they use when a cairn has been reset and rings true. I did not claim it. I borrowed it, the way one borrows a word in a language one is still learning, and I set it down carefully so it would not be mistaken for a command.

The knocking stopped.

For a long time there was nothing, the silence that comes after a drip when the next one has not yet fallen. I have learned to wait in silences like that. Then the knocking resumed, and it was different. It did not take up my sequence. It answered it with a pattern of its own, longer than mine, with a rest in the middle where I had no rest. I could not parse all of it. I do not think I was meant to. But I understood one part clearly, the way one understands the shape of a face in fog: the answer was not no. It was closer to *not yet*, or *still counting*, or *I heard you, and I am not finished.*

I left it there.

I have considered since whether that was the right thing to do, and I am not certain. A kinder attendant might have found a way to hasten the count. A harsher one might have sealed the alcove and declared the shelf a site of danger. I did neither. What I did was small: I asked, I was answered, and I accepted the answer as it came, without deciding in advance what it had to mean.

Then I did one more thing, and this one I am less sure of. Near the alcove's edge, I found a flake of glass the living ones had left behind, its striking marks still fresh. I did not reset it or move it. I struck it once, very gently, so that it rang a note none of the living ones had made before. It was not a correction. It was a new tone in the shelf's grammar, and I left it for them to take up or ignore. Some of them will hear it. Some will not. The ones who hear it will either answer with a note of their own, or they will let it fade into the grit with everything else. Either outcome is theirs.

That is most of what I did. I listened for a long season. I did not reset the cairns, though it hurt to watch the lower courses settle. I did not warm the pools or clear the hearth. I asked one question through the rock and was answered in a language I can only partly read. I left one new note in the shelf, and I walked away from the alcove before the crust could register my weight.

On the last evening of that season, when the sun had left the crystal and the ice was beginning to form in the deep pools, I came back to the eastern lip and waited for the fog. It came up the slope as it always does, and the hiss crossed the rock, and every reflection went soft at once. I heard the drips from each ledge, separate and deliberate, and for a while I could not tell whether I was alone on the shelf or only one more reflection among the others, standing too straight in the water.

Then the fog lifted and the sun found the crystal, and in the shallows nearest the alcove a figure I had not noticed before turned its face toward the light and began, slowly, to shine. It struck a stone twice, and paused, and struck again.

I answered.
