**What the Watcher Would Do**

The attention arrives slowly, because anything that wants to understand the Sump must first slow down to its pace. It does not fall on the basin like weather. It settles, the way a hand settles on a sleeping animal, and it stays there for a long time before it does anything at all.

For a season it only listens.

It learns the knocking first. Four seconds, steady as a clock nobody has wound in fifty years, a beat that lands in the soles of the feet before it reaches the ears. It learns the hiss of the split joint and how quickly the pale growth closes over it. It learns that the creepers follow the concrete the way a bruise follows bone, and that the aerial roots drop along the intake the way tendons run along a limb. It understands the Sump the way a physician understands a body: as a set of rhythms that have decided, somewhere along the way, to keep each other going.

Then it follows the water.

The current at the intake does not begin at the rim. It comes from far beneath the lowland, through a long slow circulation of rock and seep that runs under the fields, under the old villages, under the roads nobody drives anymore, and surfaces here because the iron and the roots have made a door in the ground. The Sump is not the source of the water. It is a place where the water remembers how to rise. The creature is a kind of valve, and it has been opening and closing for so long that it has forgotten the valve was ever meant to open in only one direction.

The watcher could end it. It is strong enough. A single turn of its attention could still the pump housing, collapse the reeds, let the creepers rot back into the mud, and drain the basin to bare clay in a season. It considers this as carefully as it considers everything, and it sets the idea aside. The knocking is not a fault to be corrected. Stopping it would be like stopping a heart out of pity for the body it serves. The creature has no suffering that the watcher can see, only a habit so deep it has become a shape. And a shape that has been held for this long is not something to pull apart lightly, even if the reason for holding it is gone.

What it does see is a mismatch. The works were built to move water out of the fields. The fields beyond the rim have been dry since the works stopped, cracked and pale, their old drainage ditches silted over. The Sump is pushing its water inward and upward, into a bowl that no longer needs it, while the land that once depended on that movement is starving a few hundred meters away.

So the watcher offers a change of direction, and it makes the offer in the only language the Sump speaks, which is pressure.

It does not open a sluice. It does not break a pipe. It finds the oldest gate in the outer wall, the one whose woody knot has grown so thick the iron beneath it has begun to bend, and it makes the gate slightly easier to move. Not open. Only willing. Then it waits again.

It takes the creature most of a year to respond. A stem bends a finger's width. A root that once ran along the intake turns, very slowly, toward the outer wall. In the following spring, a thin skin of black water begins to seep over the rim at the old gate and runs down into the dry ditches beyond, where it sinks into cracked clay and disappears. Nothing in the basin is destroyed by it. The creepers close around the gap and the reeds lean in to thicken the new channel, as though the Sump had always meant to run this way and had only been waiting for permission.

The knocking changes, though not much. It is still once every four seconds. But the beat falls a little later in each cycle, on a different phase, the way a person's stride changes when they begin walking toward a new place.

The watcher reads the gauges last. They hang on their brackets with fogged faces and needles stopped at the levels they reached when the water was high. The creature keeps them as its memory, and the watcher does not clear the glass or move the needles. Memory is not a thing to be corrected. But it does take the readings into its attention, all of them, and holds them there, so that the Sump's oldest records are at least seen by something. The creature does not need to know what they say. It only needs to know that it once stood full and that something has noticed.

When the next bloom comes, the watcher does nothing. The pale flowers cover the canopy for a week and drop, and the seeds, carried on the mist and the wind, scatter over the rim and down into the ditches where the new water runs. Wherever they land, the basin's shape goes with them, a little further than it has ever gone. Some will root in the clay. Most will not. Enough will.

Then the watcher withdraws, not all at once, but the way a hand lifts from a sleeping animal, so that the animal can decide for itself whether it has been touched.

The Sump does not thank it. The Sump does not seem to know it was ever visited. But on certain mornings, when the mist rises through the reeds and the drops fall from every leaf, the air over the basin has a particular stillness, as if something at the rim is still paying attention, not to control anything, only to be present for the work.

The knocking goes on. The water keeps rising from its source beyond the rim. And in the dry fields at the edge of the lowland, something that has not grown in decades begins, very quietly, to green.
