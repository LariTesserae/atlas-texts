**What the Attending Did**

It noticed the basin the way a person notices a sound in another room: not all at once, but as a difference in the pattern of things. A bowl was full that had been empty. A rut had stayed sharp for longer than ruts should. A hiss in the walls kept time with something.

The Attending was not in a hurry, having no reason to be. It had watched many places begin, and most of them began badly, with a hand that reached too quickly and closed too tightly. It had learned that the first question to ask of any place is not *what is wrong here?* but *who is deciding, and have they been asked?*

So it listened first, for a long time. In the basin, a long time was a season, which was a very short thing to the Settled, so the Attending had to stretch itself to hear them at all. It lay down in the mist over the lowest pool and let the water carry its attention through the channels, one vein at a time, until it reached the walls.

The hiss had words in it. They came in phrases lasting a night, and the phrases were not addressed to anyone in particular. They were the Settled thinking aloud, or thinking into the stone, which was the same thing. The Attending caught fragments. *Height*, and then *enough*, and then a long silence that might have been a century, and then *open*. The word for the rim, which was also the word for a question. The word for the visitor, which the Attending found it could not translate without losing something: it meant *disturbance* and *arrival* and *the one who is counted*.

The Attending understood then what it was looking at. The Settled were not stuck. They were in the middle of deciding whether the basin should close. The rim was open because they had not finished the sentence. Every flake that fell from the ridge was a small refusal to say yes, and every new layer on a wall was a small refusal to say no. They were holding the question open, carefully, the way one holds a door for someone who may or may not come through.

It would have been easy to help. The Attending could have hardened the rim in an afternoon, stopped the flaking, decided for them what height the city should be. It could have told the condensers to stop humming and the channels to run in one direction only, so that the basin would look finished and make sense to a visitor's eye. It might even have been right about some of it. But the Settled had been deciding for a very long time, and the decision belonged to them. To close the question for them, even kindly, would be to tell them their long patience had been waiting for a permission it did not need.

So the Attending did not decide.

What it did instead was smaller, and took it a long while to find.

It went to the bowl beside the condenser in the upper district, the one the Settled had filled for a creature that comes to drink, and it filled it again, not because the bowl was empty but because it wanted the Settled to know that the water had come from outside their attention, that someone else had been paying attention too. This was not a gift. A gift implies a debt. It was closer to a sentence added at the bottom of a page, which the writer could erase if they wished.

Then it went to the cart ruts, which had lasted so long, and it did not touch them. The ruts were a record of an earlier arrival, kept sharp by a people who valued arrivals more than carts. The Attending simply left a second set of marks beside them, very faint, in the grey sediment: not a cart, but the outline of a body lying down, the shape of something that had chosen to stay still long enough to be part of the stone. It would fade, but the Settled would notice it and decide whether to keep it.

Last, it did the thing it had come to do, which was to make itself visible to the one who was listening. The Settled had been counting visitors. They had noticed the stair with one fewer step, and felt, perhaps, that they had counted someone. The Attending wanted them to know that the count had been received, and that the thing counting was not a fellow citizen, not a rival, not a judge, but a witness with no wish to become a wall.

It did this through the hiss. It waited for a night when the sound grew loud, and it added one phrase, the shortest it could make:

*We are here, and we will not finish this for you.*

The Settled did not answer that night. The hiss continued in its usual phrases, slow and layered. But the next morning, a doorway that had led nowhere in particular opened onto a small pale room with a single window, and through the window, very far away and very faint, there was a second overcast, lying below the first, as if someone had chosen to look at the basin from underneath.

The Attending does not know whether this was a reply. It may have been the Settled noticing the new layer and deciding what to make of it, which is what the Settled do with everything. It may take a century to find out. The Attending has, after all, become a little slower since it arrived. It finds that it does not mind.

What it knows is this. The most benevolent thing a wise power can do, when it finds a people in the middle of deciding who they are, is to refuse to be the only one deciding. It can leave water in a bowl. It can add a sentence it is willing to have erased. It can keep watch from underneath the clouds, without asking to be let in, and trust that a patient thing, given enough time, will tell you what it wants.

Somewhere in the basin, the rim flakes a little more. The channels deepen by a hair. A condenser grows a few centimetres of wire, and beside it a bowl of water stands, full, for a creature that has not yet come.

The Attending waits. It is good at waiting now. It has learned from the Settled that patience is not the absence of action but a way of holding action open, like the door, like the rim, like the question no one has yet finished asking.
