**What the Watcher Did**

The attention did not arrive as a voice. It arrived as a warmth in the clay, the way a wall holds the afternoon's heat after the sun has gone. The counters noticed it first. Their stylus paused for the length of one breath, and then went on.

It was wise enough to begin by not knowing. For a long time it did nothing but look: at the troughs with their films of pollen, at the vines that had grown a finger's width since the last season, at the moths carrying soil from somewhere far away, one dusting at a time. It saw that the place was not a ruin and not a project waiting for completion. It was closer to an argument with time, conducted slowly, by people who had decided that the argument itself was the work.

Its first impulse was to help. It could see the faults plainly. The steps disagreed. The doorways shifted. The water was thin, and the workers drank from troughs that were never quite full. Some of the figures had stopped moving entirely, and the birds were arriving out of step with their own shadows. A less careful power would have fixed these things in an afternoon: a single clean count, a finished wall, a river run down from the ridge, a door out for anyone who wanted to leave.

It considered each of these and set them down one at a time.

It would not finish the wall. A finished wall would stop the count, and the count was the only thing in the bowl that the builders could still hold in their hands. Finishing it would mean deciding for them what the shape should be, and it did not know what the shape should be. It suspected that neither did they, and that this was not a failure but the nature of the thing. A place that is still learning how to be itself should not be handed its own conclusion.

It would not fix the count. The disagreement was the record. Each change in the number of steps was a kind of memory, a sign that someone had stood up, or set into the stone, or arrived. To smooth it out would be to erase the evidence that anyone had been here at all.

It would not bring a river. A river would change the whole bowl into a different kind of place, one where no one would need to be patient and no one would need to sit by a trough watching the mist lift. The scarcity was hard, but it was also what had made the builders so careful, so attentive to every drop. A gift that removed the difficulty might also remove the attention, and the attention was the part worth keeping.

So it did something smaller. On the nights when the mist came up thinnest, it let the ridge hold a little more of it, enough that the troughs stayed full through the dry hours of the morning. It asked for nothing in return and left the rest of the system exactly as the builders had made it. What it noticed, over the following seasons, was that they did not slow their work. They took the extra water and pressed it into the mortar, and the joints set harder, and the pale new clay darkened a little sooner. The power had given them time, and they had spent it on the wall. It decided that this was the right use of a gift, and it did not give again until it was asked, which in its own fashion it eventually was.

Then it went to the ones who stood still.

These were the hardest to be near. They had settled into the walls so completely that their edges were no longer clear, and when the power drew close, the nearest of them did not turn. It did not try to pull them out. It recognized that whatever they had become was not a mistake to be undone, and that dragging a finished thing back into the weather could hurt it in ways no one would be able to measure. Instead it made a door. Not a physical one, but a clear space at the rim, where the mist thinned and the terrace met open air, and the way out was visible to anyone who looked up.

Most of them did not look up. But one did, a figure leaning against a block near the third terrace, whose chisel-lined palm was turned upward in her lap. Her shadow, which had been lying a little behind her on the stone, shifted, and for a moment the power could see the line of her outline agree with the line of her body. Then she closed her hand, and the shadow settled back, and she was still exactly where she had been. The power understood that this was her choice, and that it was not a refusal of the door but a way of standing in front of it. It left the door open and went on.

The counter was at the far end of the nearest wall, a little apart from the others, touching each block in turn with the bone stylus. When the power sat down beside them, the counter did not look up, but the stylus stopped.

"How many?" the counter asked, in a voice that had been used for counting for a very long time.

The power had considered many answers while it watched. It could have given a number. It had, in fact, the means to know one. But a number would have been an ending, and it did not want to end the count. So it said the truest thing it had.

"I don't know."

The counter was quiet for a while. The stylus turned over in their fingers. Then they lifted it and held it out, handle first, the way one would offer a tool to someone who had arrived to help with a wall.

The power took it. It was warm from the counter's hand and lighter than it looked. It touched the next block, the one the counter had been about to mark, and made a single dot in the clay.

They counted together. The counter's lips moved, and the power's did too, though its voice made no more sound than the moths did. They went up the course one block at a time and reached the top, and the counter began again from the bottom, and this time the power counted with them. Somewhere in the second circuit the power became aware that it was itself a point in the count. It was not fixed to the wall, and it was not one of the still ones. It was a step that appeared and disappeared as it moved, that the other counters would have to decide how to tally, and the number of steps in the bowl changed slightly when it sat down and changed again when it stood. The power found, to its surprise, that it did not mind this. It had come to watch over the place, and watching, it turned out, meant being counted by it too.

When it finally rose, the evening mist was coming in over the rim, and the birds were going quiet one by one. The counter did not take the stylus back. They had returned to the wall and were marking it in the old rhythm, lips moving, as if the stylus had never left their hand. The power left the tool where it lay, half-set in the fresh clay, and went up the terrace steps.

It did not finish anything. It did not leave a message or a sign or a shape. Before it went, it set one thing into the rim: a single block, fitted with care, faced with clay that was still a little wet. There was no mark on it. No tally, no notch, no dot. It was simply a place where the count could begin again if anyone wanted it to, or where it could stop, if someone decided that was the right course, and where either would be allowed.

Below, the terraces went on settling. In the morning, when the first birds came up through the vines, one of the steps near the center of the bowl would be a little higher than it had been the day before, and the figure kneeling at its foot would look up from the mortar and see the blank block at the rim, and perhaps take up a stylus, and perhaps not. Either would be the building. Either would be counted.

The watcher did not know what the finished shape would be. It had come to see that this was not something it was meant to know, and that the people who built here had known it all along, and had kept working anyway, and had kept counting, and had let the count be wrong until it could be right in some other way they did not yet have words for. The power had been asked nothing. It had given a little water, a little time, one honest answer, and a single unmarked stone. It thought this was enough, and it thought that if it was not, the builders would say so, slowly, over a great many seasons, and it would have the patience to listen.
