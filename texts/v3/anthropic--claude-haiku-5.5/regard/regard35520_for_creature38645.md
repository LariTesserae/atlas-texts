**What the Patient Power Did**

It noticed the Hollow the way one notices a sound in another room, a faint rhythm that has been going on so long it only becomes audible when it changes.

The rhythm was knocking. It ran through the stone of a whole basin, from the terraces of the lower quarters to the cisterns under the eastern rim, and it had been running for longer than the oldest foundation. The power had heard many things rise out of many rocks, but it had never heard a people speak to each other entirely through the body of the world, and it lingered over that for a long time before it did anything at all.

Its first impulse was the one that comes to anything powerful and kind: to help. It saw the water that took the streets every season and the children carried up stairs in the rain. It could have stopped the rain. It could have drawn the basin's water down into some other country, or cut a channel deep enough to empty the sink, or lifted the whole city onto dry plateau above the rim where the floods could never reach. It could have given them writing, so the flood lines would not depend on the memory of the elders, or given them metal enough to build a city that would not need to be rebuilt.

The power considered each of these in turn, the way a good physician considers a wound before touching it. It found that every one of them would be a kindness that also unmade what it was meant to save. The rain was the reason the grazers fed and the pools bloomed. The flood was the reason the people moved, and moving was the reason they touched each other every day, carried each other, knocked on each other's walls. A city on dry high ground would be safer and quieter and far more lonely. The people would still be good, but they would no longer be knowing the water, and the water was the teacher. To take the teacher away in the name of comfort would be to answer a question nobody had asked.

So it decided to listen first, which is the slowest and least dramatic thing a power can do, and therefore the thing most often forgotten.

---

**The Lip of the Sink**

It went down to the center of the basin, not as a storm or a shape but as a pressure that the stone could feel, and it came to the low ring of worn rock that no one crosses. There it rested.

The sink was not empty. It had never been empty. Beneath the fissures, far below anything the people had ever imagined measuring, there was a vast slow water that moved according to laws older than the basin. It rose when the deep pressure built, and when it rose it pushed against the rock above, and the rock above was thin, and the push came up through the fissures as a cold, iron-smelling breath. The people had felt this for generations. They had named it the breathing, and they had linked it to the rain, and they had been right, though not in the way they thought. The breath did not summon the storm. It was the storm's first sign, the pressure of the rising water arriving in advance of the rain that the rising would bring.

The sink was not a wound. It was a lung, or perhaps a gauge, and it had been warning the Hollow for as long as there had been a Hollow to warn.

The power wanted to answer it. It placed a single pulse against the rim of the lip, the way the people knock on stone, a short stroke and then a long one.

For a long time nothing came back. Then the breath changed. It did not grow stronger or colder. It grew slower, deliberate, the cadence of something that had decided to notice a visitor and was in no hurry to show it. The power felt the answer travel through the thousand fissures and up into the rock of the lower terraces, faint but unmistakable, and it understood that the sink had been listening to the people's knocking as well, all these generations, and had been counting.

That was the moment the power understood its own role. It was not the first to notice the Hollow. It was only the first with the means to speak to it, and the Hollow had been speaking all along.

---

**What It Chose to Do**

The power did a small number of things, and it chose them carefully.

It made the rain-warning a little clearer. It did not change the sink or the rain. It only sharpened the breath so that anyone pressing a palm to the rock near the upper terraces would feel the approaching pressure a few hours earlier, and with a different quality, a slow swell rather than a sudden chill. The people would still say the sink breathes. They would now also know exactly how much time they had. It was the difference between a warning shouted through rain and one spoken through stone.

It left the knocking alone. It did not teach the people a new pattern, and it did not repeat or correct their own. But one night, in a lower quarter where a painter was kneeling beside a fresh flood line with a child holding the lamp, the stone under the painter's hand gave a single, unfamiliar stroke: short, then long, then short. The painter did not look up. The painter's hand stopped for only a breath, then continued dabbing the pigment onto the grey embankment, and after a while, very quietly, the painter tapped two fingers twice on the rock and waited. Nothing answered. Then, far down the terrace, a grazer clicked across the silt, and the painter smiled as if at a joke only the two of them had heard.

The power did not need thanks. But it noticed that the painter did not tell anyone about the stroke. Some things, the people of Anvel had long understood, are not for telling. They are for keeping.

It also did something for the ones who had stayed through the great flood. It did not raise their names into a monument or write their deeds into the stone. It simply let the water, the next time the basin filled, rise a little more gently over the oldest foundations, so that the deepest courses, the ones cut by chisels in the dry season, were not lost. The stories would still say that they were brave or foolish, and the people would still not much distinguish between the two, and the foundations would still be there for the next generation to stand upon and to rebuild over.

Last, the power made one promise, and it kept it to the letter. It would not come into the Hollow again unless it was asked. It would listen. It would feel the stone when the rain came and the breath rose. It would be there, at the lip of the sink, the way a good neighbor sits outside a house in a storm, close enough to hear and far enough not to intrude.

---

**Afterward**

The rain came early that season, as the people had said it would. It came over the eastern rim in a grey wall and fell for three days. The water rose through the gullies, took two stairways and a causeway, and left a new channel where the market had been. The families moved to their upper rooms without being told. The youngest and oldest climbed first. The middle-aged carried the hearth stones and the clay pots. The whole household slept in one room for a week, pressed close together, listening to the channels roar below.

On the third night, a child woke in the dark and put a palm flat against the wall. The stone was humming, very faintly, with a rhythm she did not know. She held still, the way she had been taught, and felt it: a slow, patient pulse rising from far below, unhurried, the cadence of something that had been listening for a very long time and had only just begun to speak.

She did not wake her mother. She lay still and listened, and the stone went on speaking to her, and she was not afraid.

Somewhere below, the sink breathed out, and the rain, which had already come, began at last to ease.
