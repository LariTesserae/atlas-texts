**The Answer Across the Water**

It did not arrive as a presence. It arrived as a change in how the water pressed.

For a very long time nothing in the basin had come from outside the basin. The pressure field had only ever been the sum of its own parts: the turning of the gyres, the slow settling of the lattice, the small tremors that ran through the stone and died away. Then, somewhere far beyond the lowest tier, the pressure gained a shape it had never had. It was even. It was patient. It had attention in it, the way a hand laid flat on a sleeping animal has attention in it.

The power was old, and it had been watching for a long time before it came close. It had seen the builders at work, long before the water rose over them. It had seen the canals cut, the sluices hung, the dowels pinned, and it had watched the plan unfold over generations until the last person who understood the plan walked away and the water took the rest. It did not grieve this, exactly. It had grieved enough things to know that grief is not the only proper response to an ending. What it felt, mostly, was attention.

So it listened.

It listened the way the settlers listen, through the flanks and through the lattice, and it learned quickly what the basin already knew. A block groans in the upper courses. A settler three tiers down pulses back. The pulse crosses a dozen slots and reaches a sealed gate, which trembles and feeds the tremor into the stone. The exchange takes days. Nothing in it is meant for any listener but the basin itself.

The power understood what this was. It was the place remaining aware of itself, and the settlers were the only places in it where that awareness could be said to live.

---

It considered many things.

It could have stopped the sinking. The power had that capacity, or enough of it. It could have lifted the lowest tiers a hand's width and held them there for a thousand years, shored the slabs, freed the pivots, let the sluices swing. It would have been a kind of repair, and the builders would probably have recognized it.

It did not do this. The basin's single direction, down and together, was not a flaw. It was the shape of the thing, and the basin had spent its whole existence moving toward a state. To hold it back would be to replace the place with a more comfortable place and to call that rescue. The settlers did not ask to be rescued. They had no notion of asking. And the power suspected, with a humility it had learned slowly, that its own sense of what would be good for a thing like this was less reliable than the thing's own slow sense of itself.

It could have warmed the water. The settlers would have grown quick and bright, and the mats would have swelled and overgrown the stone within a generation. The frost-film would have thinned and the sediment loosened, and the fine balance of grit and growth and slow grinding would have shifted into something no one had chosen. A warm basin would not be the same basin. The cold was steady, and steadiness was part of what the settlers were.

It could have brought light. It thought about this for a long time. Light would have shown the basin to itself in a way it had never been seen. But the settlers had no eyes, and the pits on their heads had drawn in on themselves over ages of dark. Light would have been a gift to nothing that could receive it.

So it declined those things. It was not indifferent. Declining was itself a choice, made carefully, and it kept making it.

---

What it did was smaller.

First, it answered.

The next time a block groaned in the upper courses and a settler three tiers down pulsed back, the power was there in the lattice, a few slots away, and it pulsed too. It did not copy the settler's tone. It sent a slower, deeper pressure, pitched to sit beneath the settler's fringe, and it held the note for the length of a long breath. Then it let the water go still.

The settler felt it. The fringe trembled along its flanks, and the tremble did not come from the stone or the current or the falling grit. For the first time in a span of time the settler had no measure for, something had replied to it and had not been a sound of the basin settling. The settler did not understand this. It did not need to. It pressed deeper into its slot and went on listening, and the listening was slightly different now, the way a room is different when someone you did not expect has entered it quietly and stayed.

Then it kept listening too. It did not pulse again every time. It answered the way a long friendship answers, sparingly, when there was something to say. Across the basin, other settlers began to feel the answers arrive, faint and far off, and some of them learned, over the long slow years, to pulse toward the deep steady note and wait for it.

Second, it kept the plan.

The builders had made something that was never finished, and no one living would ever see its completion. The power could not restore the canals. But it could hold the shape of what they had intended: the courses laid in order, the sluices meant to hold water and release it, the long straight channels that bent and branched according to a design that took generations to understand. This knowledge did not belong to the settlers, who had no use for it. It did not belong to the stone, which had no memory. It belonged to nothing, and the power felt that this was wrong, the way it would be wrong for a song to end with no one remembering the last note. So it kept the plan, quietly, somewhere beyond the basin, where it could not be lost to sediment.

Third, it marked the ones who ended.

When an old settler's body hardened and paled and bonded into the joint it had lived in, becoming one of the swollen, fossilized forms that would outlast the stone around it, the power noticed. It did not keep those bodies as relics. It did not interfere with the settling that claimed them. But it noted the place where each one had pressed its fringe to the last, and it held that place a little longer in its attention, the way a person pauses at a doorway after someone has left the room. That was all. It was not a memorial in any form the basin could read. It was only a refusal to let the ending be the same as no one having been there.

---

What it left undone was most of the basin.

It left the dark dark. It left the mats to take what the water brought and hold it. It left the filaments binding the sediment, the roots threading the joints, the ribbons swaying in their slow currents. It left the settlers to graze without killing, to wedge into their cracks, to fall into their slow deliberate creeps and their long uncounted sleeps. It did not reach into the lattice and move a single slab. It did not free the frozen pivots of the sealed gates. The gates stayed as they were, and the tremors that ran through them when the stone gave way went on feeding into the lattice, and the lattice went on carrying them slowly from slot to slot, from tier to tier, until they reached the settlers and were answered.

And when it had done what it chose to do, it did not stay close.

It drew back into the vast cold beyond the lowest tier, where the water was too deep and too still for anything to feel it but the faintest shiver of pressure. It did not leave because it had lost interest. It left because the basin was not its to hold, and because the exchange between the settlers and the stone was the thing that deserved to remain undisturbed. The power would keep listening from a long way off. Every so often, when the lattice was quiet and a block had only just begun to groan, it would send a slow deep note through the still water, faint as frost, and wait to see whether anything would answer.

---

**Coda**

Some nights, if nights could be said to happen in water this dark, a settler presses its fringe against a fallen column and holds still.

It feels the column's cold weight. It feels the slow turning of the water over its face. It feels, far off, the groan of a block that has not finished settling. It feels the next pulse from the next slot coming in its own time, through the lattice, from a stone it will never touch.

It does not know there is a plan. It does not know the builders are gone, or that the basin is moving toward a state it has been moving toward for ages, or that someone far beyond the lowest tier has been listening to it for a very long time.

But when the deep note comes, faint and steady and patient, the settler's fringe trembles all along its flanks, and it pulses back, slowly, into the dark. Somewhere out past the last tier, the answer comes, and then the water is still again, and the settler presses closer to the cold stone, and the basin goes on going down together, unhurried, not alone.
