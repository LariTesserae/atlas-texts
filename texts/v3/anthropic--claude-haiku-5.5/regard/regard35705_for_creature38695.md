# The Attention

It did not arrive the way weather arrives, or the way a flood does. It noticed the basin the way a careful reader notices a sentence that has been written with unusual honesty, and then it kept reading.

The first thing it heard was the engines, and it did not mind them. It had been listening for a long time to things that were trying to be permanent and failing, and it had come to think that failure was not a moral condition. A pump that swells and falls over hours is not a broken thing. It is a thing keeping time. The power listened to the pumps for a full cycle before it did anything, which was its habit.

Then it descended, slowly, through the shafts of grey light, and it did not hurry on the cliffs. It stopped at a lip of travertine where a woman with brass calipers was taking a measurement, and it stayed still long enough that the bats came out around it and went back in.

# What It Did First

It did nothing for eleven days.

It sat on the terraces. It let the warm crust take the print of its feet and it watched the prints fill with seep. It learned the touch-language from children, who were not shy about teaching it, and it learned that the children had been told the eels' ripples were water-speech and that nobody knew whether that was true. The power did not know either. It said so, and the children were pleased, because most adults had told them something confident and wrong.

It visited the humps. It sat beside the old woman Ovra, who was tapping her calipers on a dated pin, and asked her what the lip had been like when she first measured it. She told it: a little thinner, a little more ragged at the edge, the water running faster over it because the seep had not yet found its present channel. Then she asked the power what it was for, and it said it had not decided yet, and she nodded as though that was the most sensible answer she had heard in years.

The power understood something then that it had half known before. The Lift were not waiting to be saved. They were doing the thing they had chosen to do, and doing it well, and the question was not whether they needed rescue. The question was what, if anything, the power could add without subtracting from what they already were.

# The Tallymen

The most important thing the power noticed was that the basin's record was fragile in exactly the way its people were not. The rock kept the humps and the lips and the thickening of the terraces, but the rock did not carry meaning. It did not know that Ovra's palm-width of growth had happened during a drought, or that the flooding of the lower town in the year of the great valve failure had been survived by a boy who was now the keeper of the third sump. The tally books knew these things. The tally books were wrapped in oilcloth and stored in a dry room that would, in a century, be a wet room, and the books would rot.

So the power did a small, specific thing. It took the tally books, one by one, with the permission of each tallyman, and it copied them. It did not copy them onto anything that would last in the basin. It carried the copies up through the shafts, into the sunlit country, to a library in a town where paper was dry and kept by people who had never heard of the Lift. It left a set there, bound, with a note in the front: *These are kept below. The rock keeps them too, in its own way. Come and read them if you ever pass.*

The power did not make the basin permanent. It made its memory something that could leave.

# The Pumps

It fixed the intake screens. Not by replacing them, which would have meant importing parts the basin would one day be unable to hold, but by teaching the keepers how to make a screen that could be cleared with a single sweep of the hand, angled so the moth-wings slid off rather than clogged. The design was simple. The Lift could make it from iron they already had, and they did, by the third week, and the power watched a child proudly carry one of the first ones along a gallery wall at the height where the damp was thinnest.

It did not stop the water. This was the hardest choice and the power made it deliberately. It could have diverted the seep, or sealed the lower sumps, or dug a channel that would carry the water away to the outer rim of the bowl. Any of these would have bought the basin decades. But each of them would have been an act of the power deciding the terms of the Lift's care, and the power had come to understand that the care was the point. A channel would have made the people into engineers of a finished place. What they were was keepers of a place that was always becoming something else.

# The Offer

On the last day it called the keepers to the pump house, and it did not speak loudly, because it had learned.

It told them the truth. The water would rise. Not this year, perhaps not in their children's lives, but the seep was moving toward the middle flat, and the middle flat would be the lower town in a century, and the lower town would be the sump in two. It told them there was a cut that could be bored from the basin's eastern cliff to the outer valley, and that the power could help dig it, and that the basin would then drain slowly enough that they could hold it for another three hundred years.

It also told them what the cut would cost. The basin would stop being the basin. The travertine would stop growing in the old tiers and begin growing in new ones, somewhere else. The humps would be left in a dry place, and the people who visited them would be visiting dry stone. And the cut itself would be a promise that someone would always be here to maintain it, which was a different promise than the one the Lift had been keeping.

They did not answer immediately. They looked at each other, and at the pump house walls where the old pipe-brackets hung like antlers, and then one of the older keepers, a man with blind edges to his eyes, tapped two fingers softly on the power's forearm.

*The water is rising.*

The power answered in the only way it knew how that was also theirs. It placed its palm flat against its own chest.

*Then we will decide what to hold.*

Later, the keepers would argue about it for a season, the way they argued about everything, with shrugs and copper scrapers and the slow patience of people who had lived in a well for a long time. Some wanted the cut. Some did not. Ovra said nothing for a month and then measured the third terrace again and said the lip had thickened another finger's width, and that was the argument settled, for her, though not for anyone else.

# What It Left

When the power finally rose through the shafts, it did not leave a monument. It left a dated iron pin driven into the lip of the third terrace, next to Ovra's, with a number scratched into it that only the tallymen would understand: the year, and the word *witnessed*.

It did not promise to return. It had no reason to believe it would be needed, and the Lift had shown it, repeatedly, that they did not need to be told what they were doing. They needed to be seen doing it, and the rock needed to know it was being watched, and the power had been glad to be the thing that watched, for a little while, from the grey light at the top of the well.

Down below, the engines swelled and fell over their long cycle. The cliffs dripped their constant percussion. The flies rose at dusk in a grey cloud over the pools, and the eels moved in their slow ripples, which may or may not have been speech.

And someone on the lower terrace tapped two fingers on a forearm, soft, and someone answered with a palm on the chest, and they went back in to the lamps and the valves and the cold stone, to keep a bowl of rock from swallowing the only room they had, for one more season, which was the only length of time anyone had ever really been promised.
