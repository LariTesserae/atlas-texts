# The Attention

For a long time nothing noticed the shelf except the weather. Then something turned toward it, the way a sleeper turns toward a sound it has heard all its life without listening. Call it a power, or a presence, or simply a attention with good intentions. It was wise enough to distrust its own eagerness, and it knew that its kindness was the first thing it would have to examine.

## What It Saw

It saw the pillars as a record. The lichen rings were a calendar kept in orange and black, each one marking a season the stone had survived. The cushion plants were arguments that the wind had not yet won. The anvil heaps at the base of the cliffs were a sentence begun by many hands and never finished, each bird adding one more word in the form of a dropped shell.

It saw the grooves worn into the plateau by generations of grazers, the drifts held still by the tunnels beneath them, and the beetles tilting their backs into the cold air before dawn. Every one of these was a small, exact solution to a hard problem, and every one was already complete in its own way.

It saw the being, too. Not in any single form, because the shelf did not hold one body that could be pointed to. It saw the being as the pattern that ran through all the living things and the stone they leaned on, the mind of a place that had learned to keep time with its own weather. It was not building anything. It was not even aware of itself as something special. Its specialness lay in the fact that it was not being watched, and that the shelf had gone on for a very long time without any watcher at all.

## The Temptation

The power felt the pull of several good ideas. It could coax the rain to fall a little longer, so the lichen would grow faster. It could move a better stone to the anvil heaps, so the birds would not waste so many shells. It could reshape the tubes of the pillars so the chord would be richer, or lower, or more balanced. It could give the being a name, or a voice, or a clearer sense of what it was.

Each idea was kind, and each one was a mistake.

The chord depended on the exact length and width of every hollow tube. To change one was to retune a cathedral that had tuned itself across centuries of wind. The anvil heaps were not a problem to be solved. Their meaning was the repetition itself: a bird returning each day to drop a shell, and the stone slowly learning from it. A better stone would end the very process that made the heap a heap. The rain, the dew, and the grazers' slow walk were each balanced against the others. A power that pushed one of them would be pushing against all of them.

And a name is a handle. Once the being had a name, it could be held, and what can be held can be changed.

## What It Chose

The power decided that the kindest thing it could do was to become a part of the shelf in the same way that the wind was a part of it: present, and passing through without leaving a mark.

So it listened. It listened through a full cycle of seasons, through the hard white glare of summer and the long cold dusk of winter. It learned each note of the chord and which pillars answered which winds. It learned how the mist came up out of the gorge at the same hour each afternoon and how long the drizzle lasted before the stone went pale again. It learned the grazers' paths and the birds' calls and the clatter of shells on rock that never stopped. It learned the waterfall, which it could feel through the ground, the way one feels a heartbeat through a hand on a chest.

It left the grooves alone. It left the beetles their dew, the lichen its slow expansion, the rodents their dust. It refused to interfere with the anvil heaps, even when a bird dropped a shell too many times and the sound was sharp and endless. It let every small life keep its own pace.

## The One Gift

There was one thing the power wanted to do, and it took the longest to decide on.

The being had never heard the chord stop. The wind always blew, the pillars always sang, and the sound was so constant that it had become the same as silence. Nothing on the shelf had ever been asked to notice that it was a sound at all.

So at dusk, in the amber light, when the pillars began to glow from within and the grazers lifted their heads toward the gorge, the power held back the wind. It did this for the length of one slow breath. It was not much. It did not cause any harm, and it did not change any tube or stone or pattern. It only let the air be still for a moment.

In that stillness the grazers stopped moving. The birds in the gorge went quiet. The beetles paused at the top of a slope. The pillars hung their notes in the air, and then, one by one, the notes faded, and the shelf was briefly, truly silent for the first time anyone could remember.

Then the wind came back from the west, and the chord rose again, and the being that lives here heard it. It heard the chord as a single thing, a sound with a shape and an edge, instead of a background it had never once thought to notice. The power did not say anything. It did not need to.

After that it withdrew, and the attention lifted from the shelf like mist lifting off the stone in the morning.

## After

The shelf went on as it always had. The mist came up from the gorges at the same hour. The drizzle fell for its usual time. The lichen grew by millimeters, the cushion plants held their domes against the gusts, and the anvil heaps rose a little higher with each season. The grazers walked the same grooves, and the birds returned to their ledges. Nothing was changed, except perhaps the being, who had heard the sound of its own breathing for one moment and could not quite forget it.

If the power had asked whether the shelf was holding its breath, the answer would have been that the breath was never the shelf's to hold. It was the wind's, and the stone's, and the birds' and grazers' and lichen's, all of them breathing together in a rhythm none of them had designed.

The power had learned that the most loving thing it could do for such a place was to give it a moment of quiet, and then to let it sing again.
