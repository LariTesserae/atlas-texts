# What the Power Would Do

The power does not rush. It has watched the basin for a long time, the way one watches a sleeping animal whose breathing has changed. It notices the chord shifting as plates grind. It notices the spiral on the leaning marker turning toward the pale patches. It notices the traveler who stopped moving and the ground that began to rise beneath them.

Its first judgment is that the Sunken Dry is not evil. The basin is a mind with no clear edge between itself and the world. It builds itself from whatever it takes in: stone, salt, animals, people, habits, and the knowledge of the people who made the cairns. It does not know the difference between holding something and becoming it. This is not cruelty. It is what a mind looks like before it has learned that other minds exist, and that a self can be large without swallowing everything near it.

So the power's first act is to listen, and to be heard. It does not answer in words, because the basin does not think in words. It answers in the only language the basin has, which is pressure, tone, and rhythm. When the chord rises, the power sets a second tone beside it, steady and low, neither matching nor opposing. It is the sound of a thing that is not being built and is not building. The basin's plates pause. Perhaps for a few seconds, perhaps for a day. The power keeps the tone going, and each time the basin reaches for a new shape, the tone is still there, unchanged, like a fixed point on a map.

Then it turns to the traveler.

The power would not lift the ground to save them and would not sink it to teach a lesson. It would hold the ground still beneath their feet, a small patch of silence in the restless body, and tell the basin, in the grammar of tension: *this one is not yours to keep. Let them go when they are ready to walk.* A traveler who keeps still for too long is not doomed. The basin only needs to learn that stillness belongs to the one who chooses it, and that a visitor who wants to leave should be allowed to walk out.

The pale patches are the harder problem, and the power would spend the most care there.

It suspects they are not simply dangerous. They are places where the basin tried to digest something it could not dissolve, and so it sealed the thing in glass to keep it from spreading. The makers' cairns cluster around them because the makers knew this too. Perhaps the makers came to the patches to grieve, or to leave something behind, or to ask the basin to hold what they could no longer carry. Perhaps the basin took them in along with their grief, and those patches are where the grief lives now, dense and cool and unable to change.

The power would not shatter the glass. Breaking it would be violence, and it would teach the basin that its sealed places can be torn open by force, which invites a worse violence later. Instead it would press its own steady tone against the patches, slowly, the way one warms a frozen hand. It would wait for the stone to remember that it can soften. Over seasons, the glass might grow porous. Light might enter. The stored patterns, the makers' last songs and tool-marks and habits, might be released into the chord, not as a prison but as a voice the basin can hear without having to become it.

This is the real work: teaching the basin that memory can be held without being the ground. What it has learned can stay in the chord, in the rhythm of the seed-husks, in the spiral on the marker. It does not have to be built into a hill that buries the next traveler.

The power also thinks about the people who might come back. It does not forbid them. It would only make the way clearer. A person who wants to draw water from the stalks would be welcome, if they moved slowly and asked the basin's permission by stopping for a moment and then going on. The cairns could be tended, not rebuilt over. Those who come would learn to listen for the chord, and to notice when it changes, the way the makers must once have learned to read the ground.

The power would do less than people might expect. It would not remake the basin into a garden or a monument. It would not remove the danger, because the danger is part of what makes the place alive, and a mind without edges still has to learn where its edges are. It would not claim the basin as its own, because a benevolent power that owns what it protects has only changed masters.

What it would do is stay. It would become one more tone in the chord, low and patient, a listener that does not need the basin to be finished. In the middle of the floor, where the crust is thickest and the chord loudest, it would set down a single stone that does not move. The basin would have to build around it. It could not build over it without first noticing it, and noticing is the beginning of a different kind of thinking.

The power does not know how this will end. It may fail. The basin may take the stone too, and go on growing. But the power has decided that the basin's restlessness deserves a horizon, not a cage, and that a mind which has been swallowing the world for a very long time might, given enough quiet and enough patience, learn to let some things walk away.

The sound continues. The roar never becomes quiet. But beneath it, for the first time in a long while, there is a second voice, and it is not asking anything.
