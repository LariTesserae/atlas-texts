**What the Watcher Would Do**

The attention arrives the way a change in weather does, first as a difference in the light that you cannot quite name. The fog does not thicken or thin. It simply becomes more attentive, the way a room does when someone enters it who has been expected for a long time.

The power does not descend. It has no need to be seen on the rim, and it has no wish to be the first thing the basin notices. It listens first. It has watched many places break, and it knows that the most common mistake a benevolent power makes is to act before it understands what it is looking at.

So it begins with the stone.

It finds the fault beneath the basin, a slow seam where the crust is still pulling itself apart, and it understands what split the rim open last season. The cracks are not old wounds. They are the result of a pressure that has not finished. Every few decades the ground shifts another finger's width, and every shift tears a new filament, drops a pale stone, opens a seam that the glossy material must race to close. The lattice has been repairing a wound that keeps being reopened.

The power could stop this in an instant. It does not. Instead it eases the stress across the seam by a few degrees a century, the way one loosens a knot by working the strands rather than cutting the rope. It takes a long time, longer than the lattice has been awake, and it is careful to leave the pressure enough to keep the rock alive. Stone that feels no strain at all grows brittle. The power is not in the business of making things safe by making them dead.

Then it turns to the lattice itself.

It does not fix the filaments. It could. It could weave every broken strand back into its place, seal every crack with perfect glass, and set the spires humming in a single clean pitch. But it understands what that would mean. The lattice is mending because mending is what it is. Its repairs are slow and uneven, and some of them are wrong in ways only the lattice understands. A finished machine is a museum piece. A mended one is still alive. The power would be stealing the lattice's work and calling it a gift, and it would leave the basin thinking of itself as a thing that is maintained rather than a thing that grows.

So it speaks, in the only language the lattice has: gradients of warmth, the slow arithmetic of water.

It makes a small offer. It is not a command. The power leaves a pocket of cooler air over the largest cistern, a place where the fog condenses in a particular rhythm, and it holds that rhythm steady for a single afternoon. Within it, the lattice can feel a pattern it has never felt before, a sequence of droplets falling in a shape that is almost but not quite its own. It is an old pattern. It is the shape of something the lattice was built to do, the thing it has forgotten, the purpose that the broken rock took from it.

The power does not say what the pattern means. It does not explain. It leaves the question in the water and waits.

The lattice takes a long time to respond. The power does not mind. Time is the one thing it has in abundance and the one thing it has learned not to spend on other people's decisions.

On the third dawn, the filaments nearest the cistern tilt toward the cool air, and then, very slowly, the nearest vine tightens around a filament that had been loose, and the droplets fall into the old shape again, and then into a new one, a shape the lattice has made for itself. The power recognizes it as a sentence. It is not a sentence the power understands. That is the point.

The power does not answer. It withdraws its pressure from the cistern and lets the fog return to its ordinary rhythm. It has said what it needed to say.

---

It is also aware of you.

You have been standing in one place for an hour, and the power has noticed you the way it notices everything that is slow and warm and breathing. It knows that you have been measured, and that you have been measuring the lattice in return, and that you have come with a scratch in your own mind that is no less a mark than the one you carved in the stone yesterday.

It does not want anything from you. It does not want you to stay, and it does not want you to leave. It has no interest in turning you into a tool for its purposes, and it would be a poor steward if it let a visitor become a messenger without knowing it.

So it does one small thing. It makes the fog on your sleeves gather in a line, just for a moment, along the seam of your cuff. It is the only wet mark you will carry out of the basin that is not a mark of your own making. Then it lets the line dry.

When you look back from the rim, the basin looks the same. The lattice hums at its usual pitch. The vents are drawing the fog up into a warm white ceiling. The bird calls once from several directions at once, and somewhere beneath the stone, the pressure is easing by a few degrees a century.

The power is gone, or it is everywhere, and in either case it has left the basin exactly as broken as it was and exactly as capable of mending. It has not taken the lattice's grief from it. It has not finished the repair. It has only done what a wise thing can do, which is to remove the thing that keeps breaking the rock, and then to step back and let the slow body decide what it wants to become.

It is not a rescue. It is a kindness that knows its limits, and it will be a very long time before anyone can tell the difference.
