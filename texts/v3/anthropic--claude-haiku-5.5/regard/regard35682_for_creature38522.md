# What the Attention Did

It came the way weather comes to the Reach, down the shaft, slowly, and it arrived as light before it arrived as anything else. For a morning the frost on the upper leaves did not form. The glare off the mirrored mat dimmed, and the cold air warmed a degree or two, enough that the light and the cold stopped arguing and simply lay together over the basin.

The power took its time. It was wise enough to know that the first thing a benevolent force wants to do is help, and that help is the most dangerous thing it carries.

It saw the rim first: the raw shelves, the fresh fractures, the gullies cut clean through the felt. Its first impulse was to mend them. It could feel how the stone wanted to settle, how a few tons of the broken shelf could be coaxed back into a seat, how the slumped fan of gravel could be packed and stilled. A slope that held would stop dropping people into the water. A column that did not fall would not crack the quiet with its sound. The power could have made the Reach safe in a season.

It considered this for a long while, the way a person considers a hand held out over a sleeping child.

Then it saw what safety would cost. The collapses were the nurseries. The heaps were warm from their own decay because they were dying, and the eggs lay deep in that warmth, and the blooms came up overnight because something had fallen. A stilled slope would be a slope without a fan of torn vegetation, and a fan without new growth, and new growth without the grazer's bite at the tips. The loop would seize. Water would rise and fall and have nowhere to go but the same stagnant basins. The place would become a museum of itself, and the thing in its center would have no reason to keep moving through the columns.

The power also saw a subtler harm. The grazer walked where the mat was sound, and when a patch was spongy it went around. That was judgment earned through a lifetime of feel. A power that made every path firm would teach the grazer to stop listening through its sole. It would make the grazer's most important sense unnecessary, and then, the first time the mat failed in some place the power had not reached, the grazer would have no reason to suspect it. Rescue, given carelessly, is a kind of forgetting.

So it did not mend the rim. It let the gullies keep cutting, and the slopes keep slumping, and the columns keep falling on still nights. The restlessness was the Reach's way of staying alive, and the power judged that its own wisdom lay partly in not mistaking a living thing for a broken one.

---

What it did do was smaller, and it chose these things carefully.

It listened. On a still morning it settled into the mist above the low pools, where the hum lived, and it waited. The hum came up through the felt, almost too low to be sound, and the columns near it trembled in step, and the dripping around the grazer slowed. The power had no ear that could hear it properly, but it had something like a feeling for the frequency of a body breathing through a mat of plant. It felt the hum as a question, or a reassurance, or simply the shape of a life. The power did not answer with sound. It answered by staying. A presence that does not flinch, that does not come closer or pull away, is itself a kind of reply, and the grazer seemed to take it that way. It kept its head up a little longer than usual before sinking back into the columns.

It rained where the young needed rain. This was the only interference it allowed itself, and even this it did sparingly. A storm that would have drenched the far slope and moved on instead lingered over one heap of collapsed columns, feeding the warm fibre just enough to keep the steady heat that the eggs needed. The rain did not change the nursery. It only made sure that a dry summer did not decide the fate of a season's hatching. Everything else the power left to the basin's own weather, which was already the best judge of itself.

And it kept count.

The power found the notched column, the one whose marks had been pushed above reach by the new growth. It found the smooth tips of the lower stalks, the rubbed bark where a neck had leaned against the same column each season. It understood the people who had cut the notches. They had needed to know how long they had been here, where the firm ground ran, when the mist would lift and the shaft would cloud. They had counted the grazer's visits because the grazer was the only thing in the Reach that seemed to know where it was going. The power felt a kinship with them, though it had never walked on felt, never carried a flint, never needed to know the weather to survive it.

So it did what the counters had done. It remembered. It was the one thing the basin could not do for itself, because the basin only ever turned over and over and forgot every shape it had made. The power kept the shape of the grazer's routes, the stillness of its pauses, the hum that slowed the dripping, the leaning column with its bright bark. It did not write any of this down. It simply held it, as the wide, shallow prints of the grazer held the weight of a body over moving ground.

---

Then the power began to withdraw, the way a cloud leaves the shaft, not all at once but by thinning. It knew that it could not stay. Its attention, however gentle, would become a weather of its own if it lingered, and the Reach would begin to bend around it, to wait for it, to forget how to be the restless thing it was. A benevolent power learns when to stop being noticed.

Before it left, it marked one thing. On the same standing column, beneath the notches that the new growth had lifted out of reach, it made no mark at all. It did not carve. It did not add a count or a direction. It only left a small, unremarkable warmth on the bark, a place where the morning frost would not form, so that for an hour each day, in the cold and the glare, one column stood a little more gently than the rest. Whoever came after would not know why the wood there was dry when everything around it was wet. They might take it for chance. They might, if they were patient and watched closely, understand that something had paid attention here and then chosen not to interfere.

The light at the shaft's edge brightened. The frost formed on the upper leaves as usual, and by noon the mirrors threw their glare back up into the sky.

Far below, the grazer lifted its head through the columns, parting them without snapping them, and regarded the open air with its second lids sliding in slow, deliberate layers. It could not have known what had passed over it. It sensed only that the ground was firm where it needed to be, that the hum had been met, that the dripping had slowed, and then that it had stopped. It turned its neck toward a heap of warm, collapsing fibre where the young were hatching, and it lowered its head to the new tips, and it bit.

The column it fed on creaked once, settling, and somewhere across the basin a stalk gave way with a sound like a distant tree-crack, and the mat began, as it always did, to close over the gap.
