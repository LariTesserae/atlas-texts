# What the Attention Would Do

It would not come down into the basin. It would not come down at all, not as a hand or a voice or a light on the water. Its first act would be to notice, and noticing would take a long time, because it would be careful not to mistake the place for something it is not.

The attention would arrive the way weather arrives, as a change in pressure that the Pressers would feel in their throat sacs before they understood it. The Reckoners on the upper tiers would pause mid-hum. They would not know what had shifted. They would just know that the water was carrying something new, faint and far off, like a knock felt through bedrock from a great distance.

The power would be wise enough to distrust its own kindness. It would know how easily care turns into correction. It has seen what happens when a benevolent power decides a people's suffering is a problem to be solved: the people are rearranged until they no longer need what they had, and then they no longer know who they were. So it would ask a question before acting, and the question would be what this place is *for*, if it is for anything.

The answer would be on the walls. The power would read the stacks the way the Reckoners read them, slowly, following the carved blocks with something like a finger, counting the slumps. It would find that the basin has been keeping a ledger longer than any human record. It would find the spirals of drought and flood, the rebuilt terraces, the layers of causeway like sediment in a bank. It would find that the dwellers have never once tried to stop the mountain from falling. They have only tried to be ready to rebuild after.

That, the power would decide, is already a kind of wisdom, and it would not need improving.

So it would do very little.

It would not stop the hunter in the reeds. The sleek predator is part of the same breathing, and the herds that grieve their losses would keep the herds strong. It would not warm the mere or cool it, or dry the causeways, or send the fog elsewhere. It would not teach the Pressers metal, or kilns, or wire, though it would understand how much easier some things might be with them. It would recognize that a single improvement, dropped into this place from outside, would reshape everything that grew around it, and that the price would be paid by the moss and the bats and the dead sinking into the peat.

But the power would not be indifferent, and this is the part that would matter most.

Watching the rim for a long season, it would notice something the Pressers may have half-sensed without naming. The new fracture planes are coming more often. The slumps are a little larger than the carvings of their grandmothers' time. The mountain is shedding faster, and a fissure is forming above the upper terrace, the one with the deepest pressing hollows, where three Reckoners sit each dusk humming the count together. If that fissure goes, it will not kill everyone, but it will bury the hollows, the presses, and the knowledge of how to make the paste that holds the walls together. The basin could lose the one craft that keeps it standing.

The power could stop the fall. It could hold the rock with a hand made of pressure and time. It decides not to. The fall belongs to the mountain, and the mountain's falls are the source of the stone the dwellers build from. Stopping one would mean the next block would never come, and the walls would have no new material to be shored with. The dwellers would not be saved. They would be made into a different people, living in a basin that no longer needed them to keep count.

Instead, the power would do the smallest thing it could think of, which is to speak in their language.

There is a sounding stone on the upper tier, worn to a dark gloss by a thousand knuckles. The power would touch it once, very lightly, with a single knock that travels down through the dry-laid masonry and arrives at the bottom of the basin in the time it takes to draw breath. It would not be a warning in any human sense. It would be a tap in the rhythm of a count, one block, the same length as the tap that marks each slump. It would say, as clearly as the Pressers' own language can say anything: *this one is new.*

The Reckoners would feel it. They would stop their hum and listen. The eldest would press a fingertip to the stone and wait, and then, after a long time, would turn and look up at the rim, where a fresh pale face had appeared overnight above the fissure, unweathered, still glinting. They would count it. They would not know who knocked. They might assume the stone itself had spoken, as they assume the basin breathes and the mere rises. Or they might recognize that someone outside their count had added a mark they had not made.

Then they would decide what to do with it. That is the other thing the power would insist on. It would not carve the block for them, and it would not tell them the fall was coming and when. It would only add a mark and step back. If the Reckoners chose to carve the stack, to press a new cake, to move the sounding stone and the hollows and the families below to safer ground, that would be their reckoning. If they chose to read the new mark as the mountain's ordinary breath and carry on pressing where they always had, that would be theirs too, and the power would accept it without grief or reproach.

It might, finally, leave one more small thing. Not a gift of knowledge, which would reshape them, and not a protection, which would stop the mountain. Only attention, kept at a distance and never quite withdrawn. A presence that lowers its voice when they lower theirs, that listens to the long hum and the patient knocks, that will know, if something ever does come down into the basin from elsewhere, that this place was counted and was loved for what it was.

And at dusk, when the fog pools on the mere and the bats come out in dark clusters under the galleries, the power would be listening for the hum from the highest tier. A single Presser on the sounding stone, throat swelling, humming low over the terraces. It would not be calling anyone. It would simply be holding the whole basin in suspension, the walls and the herds and the moss and the dead, until the next knock came back up from below.

The power would wait for that knock. It would not answer unless asked. It would not need to. It has learned, in its long and careful life, that the wisest help is often the kind that leaves a place more fully itself, and that the most a benevolent power can give a thing like Hallow Mere is to witness it without wanting it to be anything else.
