The attention arrived the way weather does, gradually and from every direction at once. It did not come as a voice or a light. It was more like a change in how a place was being held.

For a long while it only watched. It noticed the hills first, their edges worn to rounded shoulders, and it understood that they had once been mountains and that the plain had been drinking them for a very long time. It noticed the water sheeting over the clay, the sky lying on it so evenly that the two were hard to tell apart. It noticed the fog building each morning and the cloud towers collapsing each evening, and it noticed that the rhythm of all this was exact and was also not quite the same twice.

Then it found the creature in the shallows, and the attention narrowed, the way a listener leans toward one voice in a crowded room.

The first impulse was familiar. Something beautiful and fragile was living in a place that could change. The watcher could see the possibilities: a drought lasting a few generations too long, a flood that tore the sleeping cocoons from their hollows, a shift in the iron or the tannins, a predator or a disease carried in on some wind. Any of these could end the lineage. And the watcher, which had within it something like the wish to protect, began to shape a response. It could make the rains more reliable. It could thicken the crust over the sleeping hollows. It could steer the water toward the shallows where the young fed, or keep the reeds from choking the open pools. It could give the creature a longer life, or a sharper sense, or a name.

It held these thoughts for some time, and it examined them.

What it saw, when it looked closely, was that every one of these gifts would change the rhythm. A reliable rain would end the long sleep that had taught the creatures to wait. A thicker crust would keep out the rain that woke them, and the cocoons would become tombs. A longer life would fill the pools until there was not enough for anyone. And a name, the watcher thought with something close to humor, would be the first thing to make the place a place *for* someone, rather than a place that simply was. The benevolence it wanted to give would arrive as ownership, and the creature would become a thing that had been helped, which is a different thing from a thing that lives.

There was also the plain fact that this lineage had already survived what the watcher feared. The drought years had come and gone. The mountains had worn away under them without anyone noticing. The creature had not needed rescue from the centuries. It had needed only to be left inside them, breathing its slow breath, and it had been. The watcher had been looking for a problem and had found only a rhythm it did not yet understand.

So it asked a different question: what would it mean to be wise here, rather than helpful?

The answer came slowly, and it had the shape of the land itself. Wisdom here would be patience. It would mean learning the pace of the place and letting its own attention slow to match it, a few breaths per century instead of a thousand per second. It would mean declining to intervene in anything that the place had already settled. And it would mean keeping one small, real thing, because even a watcher that does nothing still has a relation to what it watches, and that relation can be tended well or badly.

It did not raise the rain or steer the water. It did not thicken the crust or name the creature. But when a male sank into a pool that spring and swelled his throat to call across the plain, the watcher listened to the whole chorus, the frogs' trills riding over the deep croaks like a floor beneath a dance, and it kept the sound. Not as a recording to be played back, and not as a possession. It simply attended to it completely, the way one might look for a long time at a face before it changes.

It also attended to the drum beneath the crust. On the still days, when the cocoons were sleeping in their hollows and the clay took no sign of them, the watcher could feel the pulse, faint and patient, a count kept against the dark. It did not interrupt the count. It let the pulse be exactly what it was.

And once, on a morning when the fog was thick and the moss on the north face glistened, the watcher found a particular creature hauled out on a polished patch of rock, resting in the shade of an overhang with the drips running down its flanks. It looked like the stone. It was nearly invisible. The watcher rested its attention there for a while, and then it withdrew, and the creature slid back into the pool at the heat of the day, and nothing in the world was different.

That was the whole of what it did.

Perhaps this was not enough. Perhaps there was a kind of harm in simply watching, in holding a being so closely in attention without giving anything back. The watcher considered this honestly. It concluded that the creature had no need of anything it could give, and that to pretend otherwise would be a kind of vanity. What it could offer was the knowledge that the creature was seen, and seen without the wish to change it, and that its life, which the centuries would press into the strata as faintly as its ripples, had been witnessed in full.

Some ages from now, a different kind of mind might come to this plain and find the bone-coloured layers, veined with rust, and read in them the shapes of creatures like this one. It would not know their names. It might not know they had been watched. But the watcher had decided that the record of attention was the one thing it would keep and not press into the ground: a memory that would not be stratified, only held, lightly, by something that had learned to be still.

Then it let the plain go on settling, a grain of sand per century, the hills shrinking into the water, the creature turning in its pool beneath the sky, and the frogs singing until the whole place seemed to hum with the sound of water remembering itself.
