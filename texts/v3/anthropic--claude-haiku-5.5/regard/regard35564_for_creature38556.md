**What the Attention Did**

It did not arrive. There was no descent through the pewter sky, no shadow crossing the shaft of light. The change was in the quality of attention, the way a room changes when someone you trust has come quietly into it and stopped in the doorway.

For a long time it only listened.

It heard the drips, each one tuned by the basin it struck. It heard the amphibians answer, and the answers return a beat late from the far wall. It heard the filter-feeders turning as one body, the moths drawing light into their wings, the birds clicking their names into the seams. It heard the hum under everything, slow as a sleeper's breath, and it recognized that the hum was not noise. It was a mind, or the nearest thing this stone had to one, moving through the old plumbing and deciding, with infinite patience, where the water should go.

The power understood the cistern's history without needing it explained. The plateau had been built to draw water from a thin cold sky. Someone had once depended on this vessel. Someone had grown thirsty, or had planned for thirst, and had raised these terraces to catch what the air would give. Those people were gone, or had become something the stone no longer recognized, and the water had kept falling anyway, and the stone had kept gathering it, and somewhere in that unbroken habit the purpose had dissolved into the life it sustained.

The first temptation was to restore the purpose. The power could feel how simple it would be. A few gradients corrected, a clogged seam reopened, the channels pulled back toward some reservoir far below the plateau where a town might one day need them. The water would go where it had once been meant to go. The lichens would starve, the filter-feeders would thin, the amphibians would fall silent in basins that no longer rang, and the birds would lose their nests in the seams. The power weighed this and set it down. A purpose recovered by unmaking the life that had grown from it was not a repair. It would be a kind of erasure, however well intended, and erasure was the one thing the power had no wish to practice on anything that was still attending to itself.

The second temptation was to fix the things that looked like suffering. The coiled ones in the rafters, the heron that stood motionless for hours where the water entered. The power considered them carefully, because it did not want to mistake an unfamiliar equilibrium for cruelty. The coiled creatures did not hunt. They waited, and the stone accepted what they brought down, and nothing in the vessel seemed to grieve. Death here was not an injury. It was a return, entered into the grey floor and carried up again in lichen and fungus and the bodies of the young. To cut the coils would be to cut the cistern's digestion. The power let them hang.

So it did very little, and what it did, it did slowly.

It went to the lowest ledge, where the hum was strongest, and it did not push. Instead it matched the rhythm. The power's attention pressed against the stone the way one might press a palm against a sleeping animal's flank, not to wake it, only to be felt. And the stone felt it. The hum faltered for a single breath, then steadied, and the bioluminescent film in the crevices brightened in a pattern that had not been there before. It was not a command. It was closer to a question.

Then the power opened one dead conduit.

It was a channel near the throat of the vessel, one of the old ones that had sealed years ago and whose warmth had long since faded to a cold line on the stone. The power did not force the water through it. It only warmed the old path, very slightly, the way one might strike a match in a dark room to see whether the furniture is still where it was left. The warmth moved along the channel toward the rim. The reptiles on the ledge above lifted their heads and turned, reading the change, and then dragged themselves slowly toward the new warmth with the same ceremonial patience they gave everything. The filter-feeders drifted toward the faint current that had not been there an hour before. The stone, after a long time, responded. A thread of fresh grey extrusion rose along the old conduit and began to form a bridge, and it was soft, and it was already holding a drop of water at its tip.

The power did not know what the stone would build there, and it had decided not to know. It had left one thing behind, in the way a guest leaves a note: a single fact placed where the stone's attention would fall on it. The fact was that the water had once been gathered for someone, and that the someone was gone, and that the stone could choose what the water was for now. The stone could not read this in any language the power would recognize. But it had felt the path open, and it had felt the warmth that asked where the water should go, and that was enough for the power to know it had been heard.

It withdrew before the stone could mistake it for a master. It would not stay to correct what grew. It would not make the cistern into a servant, or a monument, or a reservoir for a people who might never return. It would not pretend to know the right purpose for something that had already found one.

Before it went, it lingered for a moment at the rim, where the filaments swayed in a breeze the power could feel but the cistern below could not. A drop formed on the lip of the highest ledge and hung there, heavier than the air should allow. The power did not push it. It only waited, as the heron waited, and as the stone waited for rain, and the drop fell.

Far below, a pale amphibian answered with a single clear note. The walls carried it up and down the vessel, past the moths and the birds and the sleeping coils, down to the hum, where it was absorbed and returned and sung again, now with one new tone running through it, faint and warm and uncertain, like a conduit that had been dry for a long time and had just remembered how to carry something.

The power was content. Not because it had fixed anything, or because it knew what the cistern would become. It was content because the place had been witnessed, fully and without hunger, by something that could have remade it and had chosen instead to listen. The stone had felt that attention. It would carry the memory the way it carried everything, into the seams and the lichen and the slow hum, and it would decide for itself what the weeping was for.
