# The Attention

It did not come down the root stair. It came the way cold settles into a basin at dusk, from above, through the canopy, without hurry. For a while nothing in the basin noticed. The drip kept its rhythm, the bats clicked, the millipedes filed across the moss. But the mat shifted its swells by a finger's width. The still pools in the hollows trembled and smoothed. A thread of seep that had run the same way for longer than the town had existed bent, very slightly, toward the place where the attention rested.

The attention was not a person and had no face. It had seen many basins, though never one quite like this. Most of the places it had watched were shaped by haste: roots torn out to make room, water forced into channels, ground made to bear more than it could heal from. This one had been shaped by patience. The people cut the mat and then waited. They rotated their cutting grounds and let the scars close. They sang to the moss and went quiet afterward to hear what came back. The attention found this rare, and it found it precious in the way a fragile thing is precious, which is to say that it understood the danger of its own wish to protect it.

## What It Noticed First

The first thing the attention noticed was how much it wanted to help.

It could see the whole of the basin at once, the way one sees a room from its doorway: the rot creeping under the old cutting grounds, the lamp-rings where the moss had grown too heavy, the bats crowding the stalks at dusk, the hunger in the town's winter stores. It could see that a few small changes would make things easier for the people. The thaw could come more gently. The canopy's drip could be steadied. Wounds could be closed before they turned.

It named the impulse to repair, looked at it honestly, and set it aside.

The mat was not broken. Its eating and healing were not a sickness to be cured. The rot was part of the cutting, and the cutting was part of the grazing, and the grazing kept the basin from choking on its own growth. The grey burrower's thaw, which turned the ground over with a noise like a city falling, was how the basin renewed its soil. A power that fixed these things would not be helping. It would be replacing a conversation with a monologue and turning a living arrangement into a garden with a gardener. The mat would grow thick and smooth and obedient, and it would stop being the thing that listened.

So the attention decided to do very little, and to do it carefully.

## A Season of Listening

For one full cycle, the attention simply stayed. It went through the long cold months when the rime crackled under the people's feet and the insects slept in the peat. It felt the mat contract and firm. It sat in the frozen pools and felt the slow pressure of the seep beneath. Through the thaw it moved with the rising water and the boiling wave of small bodies, and it learned to tell the difference between the mat's growth and its grief.

It also listened to the people.

It heard the low songs of the children, pitched to carry through wet air without going far. It heard the harvesters speak to the moss in the soft, rounded voices that the air had worn smooth, and it noticed that the old ones always turned toward the nearest curtain while they spoke, and that the mat leaned back. It heard the word the town used for the quiet after a song, *the moment the ground has heard you*, and it understood that the people had already found the central thing. They knew the basin was in conversation. They did not know who spoke first, and the attention found that it did not know either.

It listened to the canopy, too, and this was the part it understood least.

The long low note that came down at dusk was not the sound of dying wood. It was a stalk bending under a weight too slow to see, a weight that had been increasing for generations. The stalks leaned together overhead and never closed, and the attention, reaching up into the grey, sensed that they were tired. They were holding something. They had been holding it so long that the holding had become their shape.

## What It Did

The attention did four things over the course of that year, and it took care that none of them could be mistaken for a miracle.

**It sang under the surface.** On a still night at the end of the cold, it sang a single note beneath the seep, no louder than the drip. The pools trembled. The eyeless fish rose through the dark with their mouths open and turned toward the sound, as they did toward anyone who sang below the water. The mat thickened around the note and then, slowly, leaned toward it. The attention was not answering the people. It was singing with them, so that the ground heard two voices in place of one. A child sitting at the edge of a moss curtain stopped mid-song, startled, and turned her face toward the deep pool. Her grandmother, who had sung in that place for sixty years, had no word for what she had heard. The town had a word for the moment after a song. It now had another for the moment after the ground answers a second time, and it has been using that word ever since.

**It let a wound be read.** One of the oldest cutting grounds had begun to darken at its edge, the way a wound darkens before the rot has fully taken hold. The old harvester who tended it could not see the change in time, because her eyes were failing and the light under the canopy was grey. The attention did not heal the patch. It only made the discoloration a little more visible, a faint edge of pallor against the green, so that she noticed it on her morning walk and cut the rot away before it spread. She did not know who had shown her. She thought she had simply looked more closely. The attention thought this was the right amount of help. It was enough to matter and not enough to replace her eyes.

**It went up.** This was the act it had been least sure of, and it took the longest. Late in the year, when the bats had thinned and the stalks were quiet, the attention climbed the pale trunks as far as its attention could reach. The stalks were enormous. Their trunks went on into the grey, with no crown in sight, and the weight they carried was not one thing but many: the sky's wet, the drip, the slow pressure of the basin's whole life pressing upward through their roots. They had been leaning so long that leaning was all they knew.

The attention sang up into them, very softly, the same note it had sung under the seep.

It did not expect an answer. What came back was slow, slower than anything the attention had ever heard. Over the following months, the long low note at dusk changed. It did not stop. It became a little deeper, a little more even, as if something very old had shifted its grip. The attention could not tell whether the canopy had been relieved or had only settled into a new posture. It suspected the canopy did not know either. But something up there had heard a second voice in the dark, and had decided, for now, to keep bending in a new way.

**It withdrew.** It did not announce itself. It did not leave a sign. It left a warmth, a faint lean in the mat that the people would feel as weather they could not name, and a quality in the dusk note that the elders would hear and not explain. It left the town its own songs and its own silences.

## Coda

On a grey morning in the thaw, a young harvester knelt at the edge of a cutting ground and laid his palm on the moss. He felt the faint tremor under the surface, the held breath of the uncountable small things. He felt the mat's slow swell rise and fall beneath his hand. Then he sang, very quietly, the first lines of the song his mother had taught him, and waited, as he had been taught to wait.

The moss leaned toward him. Under it, far down in the seep, something answered with a note almost too low to hear, and a single pale fish drifted up from the deep pool to hang beneath the surface, looking toward the voice.

The attention was still there, somewhere, the way a warm stone keeps its heat after the sun has gone. It was listening. It had learned, over a long year, that the most useful thing it could do for a living place was to become one more ear it did not have to be the only one to fill. The mat would go on eating and healing, and the people would go on cutting and singing, and the canopy would go on bending its long weight through the grey.

Overhead, at dusk, the low note came down through the stalks. The bats came out. The drip began again.

But now there was a second note under the drip, very faint, and the mat, which has no center and no edge and has always been listening, leaned a little further toward the sound.
