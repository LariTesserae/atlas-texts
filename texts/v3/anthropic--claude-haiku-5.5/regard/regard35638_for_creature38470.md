# The Patience of Weather

The attention arrives the way weather arrives, first as a change in pressure that nobody below can feel.

It notices the basin the way one notices a room where someone has begun to speak. The first thing it sees is the brine pan, a flat mirror rimmed with salt, and the animals approaching it carefully, the way creatures approach anything they cannot yet afford to trust. Then it sees the walls. A pale sheen on a bank of clay. A tier still weeping salt. A gallery mouth glazed with resin. It reads these the way a physician reads skin, and what it finds is a body that is not yet a body, a mind still forming its own edges.

It stays a long time without doing anything. This is the first thing it decides, and it is the hardest.

---

It understands, after a while, what the colony is doing, though it would not put it in those words. The builders are not making a city. They are making a *memory of water*. Every tier of wall is a record of a dew that dried, every lane a path that a drop of morning moisture travelled to reach a nursery roof by noon. The whole population is a slow argument between thirst and shelter, and the argument has written itself into the shape of the mounds.

It also sees how close the thing is to breaking. The afternoon cumulus build over the canopy and rarely break, because the canopy breathes back so much moisture that the sky stays thick and holds its rain. It is a loop that feeds itself. The green keeps the clouds; the clouds keep the green. It is beautiful, and it is fragile, because a loop that closes on itself has no slack. One dry decade in the wrong direction and the roots lose their grip, the gullies open, and the young ground that is still learning to be stone goes back to being sediment, and then to being nothing at all.

So the power could help. The pull toward it is strong and not foolish. It could make the rain fall on the parched eastern slopes. It could cool the brine pan so the salt stopped rising. It could take the predators and quiet the lanes so the builders could finally rest, or it could lift the herds away from the mounds so the walls stopped being trampled.

It considers each of these, and each one is a kind of finishing.

---

The question it keeps returning to is simple: what does it mean to help a mind that is still becoming?

A gift is a shape. Any change it makes will be read by the colony, slowly and through many bodies, and absorbed into the architecture of its thinking. If it lifts a predator away, the galleries will reorganize around the absence, and the builders will forget why the vents were sealed that way, and in a generation the city will be shaped by a thing that was never a question, only an answer handed down. The argument with the predators is part of what the colony *is*. Taking it away would be like taking a child's hard years and calling it kindness.

And the basin is not waiting to be rescued. It is in the middle of being. Its rocks are still crumbling and rising, its gullies still cutting and healing, its herds still wearing paths that will one day be the ground under someone else's feet. Nothing here is unfinished in the way a broken thing is unfinished. It is unfinished the way a sentence is, and the power has no right to write its ending.

So it decides, mostly, to leave the basin alone.

But not entirely. Restraint is not the same as absence, and a wise power knows the difference between sitting still and standing aside.

---

The first thing it does is almost nothing.

Over the next nights the wind shifts by a few degrees across the eastern rim. The vents that the builders have been struggling to balance, the ones that draw cooler air down through the terraces, begin to pull a little better. The walls shed their heat more evenly. The nursery roofs in the lowest levels stay cool enough that the seedlings do not scorch in the long gold afternoons. No builder notices a change. The work simply goes on, a bit more easily, the way a muscle relaxes when the weight is lifted.

The second thing it does is to let one cloud break.

It is not a large rain. On a single afternoon, a cumulus that has been building for hours over the western canopy releases what it has been holding, and the rain falls not on the mounds but on the raw grey and ochre walls of a flash-flood channel where the gravel has never been rounded. The water runs, briefly, a thin bright sheet over the banded stone, and the gravel shifts and tumbles, and a few stones that have been sharp for a thousand years lose a corner. It is over in twenty minutes. The ground drinks what it can and the rest runs off toward the brine pan.

The builders feel it, as they feel everything, through the humidity gradient and the sudden wet on their mandibles. A forager returning from the dawn leaves finds the lane slick and cool. A digger working the channel's undercut finds the clay soft and easy to cut, and for a while the colony has more water than it has ever had at once, and it does not know what to do with it except to carry it, store it, and wonder where it came from.

The power does not explain. It cannot. It does not want the colony to understand it as a god, a thing to bargain with, a source to organize around. It only wants the colony to have one experience that it did not build and did not expect, and to decide for itself what that experience means.

---

The third thing it does is the hardest, because it is a kind of waiting.

The power keeps watch. It does not step between the predators and the galleries, or move the herds off the lanes, or repair a single cracked wall. When a stone-flanked predator slips through a vent and the builders seal one passage and open another, the power watches that argument with something that resembles tenderness, and says nothing. When a herd compacts a new lane until the ground is bare and hard, the power lets the lane harden, and lets the builders decide whether to follow it or to dig around it.

It is aware, as a wise thing must be, that it could be wrong. Its sense of the basin is wide but not complete. The colony thinks slowly, through many bodies, and it may already be solving problems the power cannot see. The loop between canopy and cloud may be sturdier than it looks, or more fragile in ways that only show themselves when it breaks. The power holds all of this as a question, not a conclusion, and keeps its hands open.

That is perhaps the deepest kindness it can offer: to be a witness that does not need the basin to be anything in particular.

---

Eventually it withdraws, not all at once, but the way weather moves off a valley: the pressure eases, the cloud thins to haze, and the sun returns to the flats as if it had never been gone.

The basin goes on. The mounds keep climbing and the oldest plateaus keep sinking into one another. The herds wear new paths. Predators learn new galleries. The builders read the walls by touch and smell, and somewhere in their slow shared mind, a question forms that was not there before: *why did the water come from the west, when the sky has never broken there before?*

The question is not answered. It will be carried mouth to mouth through the lanes, from the nursery roofs to the warm core, and it will change shape as it goes, and it will be folded into the walls.

That is enough. A benevolent and wise power does not need to be the author of the colony's thinking. It only needs to have been, once, a little weather that the colony could survive and learn from, and then to have gone quiet, and let the thinking continue, as it always has, in clay and resin and root, toward a shape that belongs to no one but the basin itself.
