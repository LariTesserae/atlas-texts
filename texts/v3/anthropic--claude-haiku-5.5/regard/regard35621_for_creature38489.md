**What the Attention Would Do**

It would arrive the way weather arrives, as a change in the light before anything can be named. The cloud would thin over the escarpment, and for a few minutes the wet basalt would go silver across the whole plateau. Then the cloud would close again. Anyone who saw it would think it was only the sun.

The attention would take a long time to look. It would begin with the water, because the water is the city's whole argument. It would follow the cascade from the high reed-seeps down through each tier, tracing the spouts and gutters, and it would find what the cistern-folk already know: the system is alive because it is kept open, and it is kept open by hands that are very few.

It would feel a pull to do something. Benevolence has that pull. Here were ruins going back into the ground, a people with no tools, no fire, and no way of writing down what they know, and a sorrow built into their life cycle, since so many of the climbing adults never reach the high pools again. Wisdom would ask what the pull was for.

The attention would consider several things it could do.

It could repair the walls. But the walls are the ground's slow argument with the city, and the cistern-folk do not need them. A stone set back in its course would stop the moss from prying it apart, and in a few generations the Shelf would be a city of standing walls with no water moving through it, quiet in a different way. The thud and the echo would vanish, and so would the reason anyone waits for them.

It could bring more water. Rain could fall in longer grey spells, the cisterns could brim past the lip, and the lichen film in every basin could thicken. The cistern-folk would multiply, and the nursery would become crowded in a single season. The channels they keep open would silt faster than they can scrape, because more mouths means more leaves, more grit, more reed, and the careful balance that has kept the cascade running would be pushed past the point where any one of them could hold it. A wise attention knows that abundance is not the same as generosity. The Shelf has learned to feed only what the water can carry, and to let the rest return to cloud.

It could remove the cistern-folk, or teach them to build, or write their knowledge into something fixed and portable. Each of these would be a kindness to someone else's idea of what a life should be. The knowledge of the Shelf is the shape of the flow, kept open one scraped channel at a time. It cannot be copied out. If it were written, it would become a record of something that was no longer happening, and the channels would be left to dry in the belief that the record would remember them.

So the attention would do very little. It would do what the Shelf does.

It would attend to the cloud. In a dry year, when the lowest pools begin to fall, it would hold the fog a few days longer in the early hours, so that the seeps keep weeping and the hatchlings have water on their way down. It would not make the rain. It would only let the rain come when it was already coming, and linger a little when the cistern-folk had most need of it. Nobody would know it had done this. The cistern-folk would notice only that the fog was late in burning off, and they would be glad of it in the way they are glad of anything, by going on with their work.

It would not speak to them. It heard the patterned tapping that goes around a courtyard and comes back, and for a long time it wanted to answer. But an answer would be a voice in the system, and the cistern-folk would learn to wait for it, to read the drip as the attention's instead of their own. They would lose the one thing that makes them what they are, which is that they listen to the water and to each other and nothing else. The attention would keep its silence, the way a good neighbor keeps a door closed that could easily be opened.

And it would let the dead wall stay dead. When the next block slipped, the attention would not catch it. It would let the wall come down with its short dull thud, and it would listen to the echo take its long time to fade, and it would wait, as the cistern-folk wait, to see where the water would go.

Then, when the echo was gone and the water had found a new way through the rubble, the attention would go with it, down the spout and along the gutter and into the next basin, not leading and not following, only present where the flow was. The cloud would settle over the escarpment again. The moss would weep under the overhangs. Somewhere in the reeds a cluster of clear jelly would hang from a stem like a bead, and the first hatchling of the season would work its way loose and drift down toward the lower pools, tier by tier, feeding on the film, learning the route.

The attention would not write this down. It would only watch it happen, which is the most it can do for something it loves, and perhaps the most that any wise power can do for a place that has already learned how to keep itself.
