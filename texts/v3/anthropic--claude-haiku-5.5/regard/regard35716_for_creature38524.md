# The Attention

It first noticed the flats the way one notices a sound that has been going on for a long time: all at once, and with a sense of having missed it before.

For a while there was only the attention itself, wide and unhurried, moving over the plain the way light moves over water. It saw the hexagons first, because they are easy to see. Then it saw past them to the heat beneath, the slow brine climbing through fractures, the convection cells turning like the gears of a clock too large to hear. It saw the fins growing a few centimeters a season, the wet lips ticking as they cooled. It saw the mats curling their edges in the dark, the sponges holding their amber rings like unopened letters, the threads lifting toward a storm that had not yet arrived.

Then it saw the whole, and was quiet for some time.

## What It Could Have Done

The power was not short of options. It could have cooled the crust and stilled the convection, and the ridges would have stopped growing, the cells would have flattened, and the whole intricate architecture would have settled into a dead, smooth plain. That would have been a kind of mercy to the mats, which crowd and dissolve each other, but it would have ended the engine that made them.

It could have brought more rain, a storm every week, so the threads would never have to wait, and the mats would have spread without check, and the sponges would have drowned in their own abundance. It could have deepened the brine, or thinned it, or warmed the nights so the fog would never settle and the ridges would never stand out of it like drowned villages.

It could have made the place into something it was not. It could have seeded the cells with bright, quick, fast-growing life that the flats had never grown, or planted a garden in the lee of the fins, or raised a wall to keep the wind from humming along the ridges at evening. Each of these would have been a kindness of a sort. Each would have been a hand laid on a thing that had never needed one.

The power considered all of them, and set them down one by one, the way one sets down a cup that is nearly full.

## What It Chose

The flats are an architecture that builds itself. Nothing on them was made by hands, and that is the thing that makes them whole. The power understood that to act on the plain as a builder would be to end what it was. Even a gift, delivered from outside, would make the flats a thing that is given rather than a thing that grows.

So it chose to be a witness first, and very nearly only that.

It did, however, do a few small things.

It let one storm fall exactly on a single tile, so that the boundary between wet gray and clean glare sharpened for an afternoon into a line that could be seen from a ridge top. It did this not to change the chemistry but because it wanted to see the line, and because a thing that has never been seen cleanly from the outside is hard to love fully. The rain dissolved the surface, carried its mineral into the cell center, and in a week the slick skin had formed exactly as it would have anyway. Nothing was altered. Only the attention had changed, a little, and the flats did not notice, which the power took as a kind of confirmation.

It listened to the sponges. A sponge's rings are the only archive of the storms that have passed over the plain, and the power, which had no need to read them, read them anyway, slowly, the way one reads an old letter from someone one will never meet. It found dry years and wet ones, a ring so thick it must have been a season of unusual rain, another so thin it could have been a single failed year. It did not change the rings. It only let them be what they were, and in letting them be, it made sure they would not be forgotten. The memory of the flats, it decided, belonged to the flats.

And it allowed a traveler to walk out.

This was the one thing it did that was almost a gift. A surveyor, a woman from a distant settlement, came out onto the plain with a theodolite and a sheaf of paper and a stubborn wish to draw the cells accurately. The power let her come. It let the fog thin for her at dawn so she could see the fins emerging one by one. It let the wind be kind and the crust hold her weight. When she left, she carried a map. The map was already wrong, because the boundaries were migrating even as she drew them, and the power found this exquisitely right: a record that begins to fail the moment it is made, and so reminds everyone that the flats are still moving.

## What It Left Alone

Most of what the power did was nothing at all.

It did not name the mats, though it could have, and it did not decide which of the sponges was the oldest or which of the threads was the most sensitive. It did not turn the ridges into a monument or the cells into a calendar. It let the mats keep their frontier of pitted stone and did not ask them to be kinder to one another. It let the sponges thicken their walls against the creeping colonies, and it did not take sides in the old quarrel, because the boundary between them was one of the most stable things on the flats, and stability of that kind is worth more than peace.

It left the heat alone. It left the brine alone. It left the wind to hum along the fins in the evening, and the haze to hang at spire height, and the fog to drown the ridge bases each night. Every one of these was part of the engine, and the power understood that the engine did not need its help. The engine had been running since the first sun warmed the first crust, and it would run long after the power had gone its way.

## The Quiet After

In the end, the attention lifted, the way it had come: gradually, without ceremony. The haze on the flats thinned for a single season and then thickened again. A ridge that had been growing toward the east turned a few degrees south. Somewhere in a cell near the center, a sponge put down a new ring, thin and pale, marking a dry year that the power would never see.

If you walked out onto the flats at dawn now, with the fog thinning around your ankles and the fins rising pale and sharp on every side, you would see nothing that announced a visitor. You would see only the same slow, self-building architecture, still becoming, still growing into the pattern the heat has been drawing for as long as there has been a sun to draw it.

The power had looked at it closely, and had decided that the kindest thing it could do for a thing that builds itself was to let it go on building.
