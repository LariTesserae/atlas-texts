# What the Attention Did

The attention arrived the way weather does, gradually and from every direction at once. It did not announce itself. It noticed the knocking first, the way a listener notices a clock in a quiet room, and then it noticed that the knocking was counting.

For a long time it did nothing. This was not indifference. It had learned that most harm comes from hurry, and that a place that has been working out its own shape for a long time should not be reshaped in an afternoon. It listened to the falls. It felt the strokes travel up through the bedrock. It sat with the chorus of the amphibians and the thin, shivering hairs of the creatures in the gates, and it tried to understand what the basin was becoming.

What it saw troubled it a little, and it was honest with itself about why.

The rhythm had tightened. The gates closed cleaner every season. Every plate that survived had survived because it fell in step with the strokes, and every stroke was a little more exact because the plates had learned to meet it. It was beautiful. It was also a narrowing. A system that is perfectly tuned to one beat is brittle against any other beat. A single unusually dry year, a flood that came from the wrong side of the ridge, a slab cracked in a way the gates had never felt, and the whole fit could come apart. The ones in the gates would not know why. They would only feel the rhythm go wrong and have nothing to hold.

So the attention considered what it might do.

It could stop the engine. That would be a kind of mercy, and a kind of ending. The pale chains would stop breaking and building. The stone would weather. The basin would become a quiet bowl of peat and sedge, and the creatures in the gates would have nothing left to be. The attention judged this to be a theft dressed as kindness. The ones in the gates had made their own purpose, slowly, out of pressure and water, and it was not the attention's to take away.

It could perfect the engine. It could smooth the mineral lips, set each slab exactly, make the strokes so regular that the gates would never misfire. This was the more tempting path, because it would look like care. But it would finish something that was not finished. The basin was still in its argument with itself, and a perfect answer would silence the argument. The attention had seen what happens to living things that are given a perfect answer: they stop asking questions, and the questions were where their life was.

It could speak to them. But they had no words to hear, and anything that spoke to them would have to speak through the water, which was its own kind of force. The attention decided against it.

So it chose something smaller, and it chose it with care.

## The Small Thing

It sent one flood early. Not a large one. A single pulse of rain, a little out of season, that loosened a chain at its weakest seam. A fragment drifted from the old gates into a shallow pool on the young, clean stone near the western wall, a place where no chain had ever settled. The fragment took hold. It began to grow.

But it did not grow in step with the old rhythm. It was slightly out of phase, catching a stroke a fraction of a second late, and so it built a different edge. The new chain sealed its plates at a different angle. It did not yet know that it was different. It only knew the water, and the water was a little less certain than before.

The attention also did one other thing. It let a few seeds of the cold-loving sedge drift into the wide bare gaps of silt, where nothing had grown because nothing had been willing to make a home. It did not plant a garden. It let the gaps be a little less empty, so the basin would have more places where its life could go in directions no one had yet imagined. And it left the frogs alone entirely, because their chorus was already keeping time in a way that the attention found more beautiful than anything it could have arranged.

It mended nothing that was not broken, and it broke nothing that was working.

## What Remained Unknown

The attention did not know whether the new chain would live. It did not know whether the early flood would turn out to be a gift or a wound. It had learned, over a long time, that a wise power does not get to know the outcome of its gentleness, and that part of what it can offer is to let the outcome belong to the place.

What it did know was this: the knocking would continue. The stroke would fall, and the tremor would run up through the bedrock into the plates, and somewhere on the western shelf a young chain would feel a beat that was not quite the beat of the old gates. It would have to learn a rhythm of its own. It would have to decide, in its slow way, what kind of fit it would make.

The attention withdrew, the way it had come, without announcing itself. The mist thickened over the water. A frog called, and another answered, and the mechanism took up the pulse, and then, one stroke in the chorus, a slab fell a fraction of a second late, and the whole basin seemed, for an instant, to hesitate.

In the spray at the foot of the falls, the ones in the gates felt the hesitation pass through them like a question. They did not know yet what to make of it. Neither, for that matter, did the attention.

It was enough that the question had been asked.
