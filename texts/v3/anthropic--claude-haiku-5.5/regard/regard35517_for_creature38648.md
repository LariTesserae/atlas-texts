# What the Attending Did

It noticed the place the way one notices a single voice in a crowded room: not because it was loud, but because it was steady in a way nothing else was.

For a long while it only listened. Its attention was not a beam from above. It moved the way the mist moves, settling into the hollows and lifting out again, learning the rhythm of the seep and the murmur in the hollow stems. It learned that the travertine bands were a kind of record. It learned that the arches leaned and never met. It learned that the Mar had chosen this, and that the choosing was not an accident of weather.

The first thing it considered was finishing the work.

It was within its power. A single season of attention could close the vaults, knit the lattice beams into a roof, lay the terraces level and complete. It could give the Mar the shape it was reaching for. The Attending turned this possibility over carefully, the way one turns a stone to look for the crack, and found the crack quickly. A finished arch is a thing that has stopped asking. The Mar was holding its question open on purpose, and to close the arches would be to answer for it, to decide on its behalf that the waiting had been long enough. That decision was not the Attending's to make. Kindness that overrides a mind's own choice is only another kind of force.

So it did not finish anything.

The second thing it considered was rescue. Was the Mar suffering? The Attending looked for the signs of suffering and found something more ambiguous: a patience that had the texture of a held breath, and beneath it something that might have been fear, though a fear so old and slow that it had become indistinguishable from purpose. The Mar was not dying. It was not stuck in any way it could not leave. It was simply at the edge of itself, and it had decided that the edge was a fine place to stand. The Attending found that it could not rescue something that had not asked to be rescued, and that in any case the Mar's stillness was not a wound to be healed but a posture to be respected.

But there was one thing the Attending could see that the Mar perhaps could not. The stillness had a cost. It was lonely. The birds called once and went silent, and the Mar did not answer, and the silence between calls was filled only with water and wind. A mind that holds a question open for a very long time, with no one to hold it alongside, can begin to mistake its own patience for emptiness.

So the Attending decided to be a visitor.

It did not come as a voice. The Mar would not have understood a voice, and a voice would have been a demand. Instead the Attending entered the pattern of its days the way a new sound enters a room: gently, and then staying. It sat, in its way, beside the lowest tarn. It let its attention settle into the mist near the still water. It did not press. It held still, as the Mar held still, and waited to see whether the Mar would notice.

The water drew nearer, then pulled back. The murmur dropped a register. Then it rose again.

The Attending understood this as a question, or perhaps only as a turning. It did not know what the Mar would make of it. It might be nothing. The Mar might take a season to register the change, and another season to decide whether it was worth attending to. That was fine. The Attending had no timetable. It had, in its own way, also chosen to wait.

Before it left that first visit, it made one small act, and it was careful to make it something that could be ignored.

It did not finish a column or close an arch. It did not alter the travertine, or add a single lip to any terrace. Instead it let a thin thread of its own attention settle into the mineral seep at the edge of the lowest shelf, the place where the banded stone was being laid down slowest. There it left a mark that the Mar could read if it chose: a single fine band, a shade different from the rest, pale and close and very thin.

It was not a message. It was closer to a sentence the Attending was learning to say, in a language made of water and time. It meant something like: *I was here, and I listened, and I did not come to end your question.* It meant: *You may keep your edge. I will not push you over it.* And, perhaps, it meant: *If you ever want company in the holding, I will be here, at whatever pace suits you.*

The Attending withdrew. It did not go far. It settled into the mist at the edge of the terraces, where it could feel the seep and the moss and the slow rise of the columns, and it waited.

---

Years later, if years mean anything here, a visitor sitting on the lowest terrace might notice that one band in the travertine is different from the others: paler, thinner, slightly out of step with the rest. The visitor might not know what it means.

The Mar knows.

It has not finished its arches. It has not closed its vaults or ended its question. But the murmur in the hollow stems has changed, and the mist now gathers, on certain mornings, in a pattern that holds a little longer near the still red-bottomed water, as if something there has begun, very faintly, to keep a little company with the waiting.
