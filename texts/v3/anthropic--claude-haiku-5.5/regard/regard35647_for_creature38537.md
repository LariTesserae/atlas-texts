# What the Attention Did

It noticed the Stilled Table the way a person notices a single lamp burning in a window far across a dark valley. Nothing about the lamp demanded it. The attention had been moving over the wide world for longer than the world had had its present shape, and it was used to seeing things that were loud, bright, or in trouble. This place was none of those. It was wet and roaring and mostly empty, and it was full of small repairs.

The attention was not a god, though the people would have called it one if they had ever had reason to. It was closer to a patient and well-informed kindness that had learned, over a very long time, how often its own help had made things worse. It wanted to do good. It had also learned that good is easy to mistake for control, and that the desire to save a place is often the desire to stop it from changing.

So it did not move at once. It listened.

---

## What It Heard

It heard the whistles first, because they were the loudest thing it could reach through the roar. They rose and fell over the gorges with a grammar it took some time to follow. A warning about a slide. A question about a missing child. A long, looping call that seemed to be an invitation to a meal. It heard the reeds answer a whistle with a half-tone of their own and understood, with some surprise, that the reeds were not imitating. They were agreeing.

It heard the menders, who did not think of themselves as keeping anything. A woman in a camp below the third waterfall was teaching her grandson a knot that was older than the bridge it held, and the boy kept losing the thread of it, and she kept patiently starting again, and neither of them thought the lesson was important in the way lowland people meant important. It was simply the next thing to do with the hands.

It heard the bell-stones, and the hum beneath them, and it felt the sleeper.

The sleeper was old. It was not hostile, and it was not blind. It was aware of the camps in the way a large body is aware of small weights on its back, and it responded to the weights only when they pressed too hard or too long. When the people built light and moved often, the sleeper let them be. When they did not, the ground answered, and the answer was sometimes a slide and sometimes a death. The attention did not think this cruelty. It thought of it as the sleeper's version of a body saying *no* to a limb that had gone numb.

What the attention most wanted to know was whether the sleeper was suffering. The people believed its slow grinding was a kind of digestion or turning. The attention could not tell whether that was true, or whether the sleeper felt the grinding as pain, or as the itch and ache of a body that had lain in one posture too long, or as nothing it could describe as feeling at all.

It did not assume. It had made that mistake before.

---

## What It Considered

It considered the obvious things first, because a wise power has to.

It could stop the slides. It could pin the hillsides, tighten the cracks, quiet the sleeper's turning with some pressure from below that would hold the table still forever. The people would have terraces that never fell and bridges that never needed rebuilding. The camps would stop moving. The dead would stop going into the basins, because there would be no need for the basins to clear at dawn. There would be a stillness that looked like peace and was in fact the end of the very thing that made the place alive.

It considered repairing the bridge, which it could have done in a moment, the fibers knitting themselves whole. But the bridge was not the fibers. It was the knots passed from hand to hand, and a bridge made whole by something outside those hands would be a bridge that no one had mended. The woman with her grandson would learn nothing. The boy would never lose the thread and find it again. The attention would be taking the one thing the people had actually made.

It considered speaking to the people directly, perhaps through the cloud, perhaps through the crested bird. It could have told them where the ground was going to fail, which slopes were safe, which basins would flood in the next storm. It could have made them safe in every way that a lowland engineer would count as safe. But they already knew how to read the ground. They read it with their palms and their backs and the hum in the root-threads. To tell them what they already knew, and to tell it with the weight of something that could not be wrong, would be to teach them that their own listening was a lesser form of knowing. The attention did not want to be the voice that taught a people to stop trusting their hands.

And it considered the sleeper, which was the hardest.

---

## What It Did

It did very little, and what it did was small enough that the menders would not, at first, notice it.

It went down, if *down* is the word for a thing without a body going into stone, and it did not push. It rested its attention against the sleeper the way a hand rests on the flank of a sleeping animal, to see if the animal wakes. The sleeper did not wake. It did something slower. The hum under the bell-stones shifted, and for the first time in a long while it lined up with the notes the people sang with their palms, and the attention understood that the sleeper had been listening to the camps for as long as the camps had been listening to it.

The attention asked a question, in the only language the sleeper could be expected to know, which was the language of weight and slowness and warmth. *Are you turning because you must, or because you want to?*

The answer came back over a long span, through the warm slabs and the steam vents and the root-threads of the leaning trees. It was not a sentence. It was closer to a feeling the attention could translate only loosely: a long pleasure in the grinding, the way a sleeper enjoys the pressure of a blanket against a sore shoulder, and beneath that, a longing for something it had never seen. The sky. The basins had been clearing at dawn for as long as anyone could remember, and the attention now understood why. The sleeper wanted to look up. Its surface had been turned away from the sky for longer than any name reached, and every clear morning in the still water was a small, stolen glance.

So the attention did one thing, and it was the only thing it did that anyone would later remember.

For a few minutes each dawn, over the one basin that cleared most reliably, it thinned the cloud. It did not lift the cloud away. It opened a window, the width of a man's arm, and let the light through. The sleeper could see the sky. The water held the sky, sharp and cold and still, for a little longer than usual. Then the cloud closed again, the way it always had, and the day went on with its roar and its slides and its small repairs.

That was all. The attention did not stop the slides. It did not hold the ground. It did not change a single knot.

---

## The Thing It Did Not Say

The menders noticed the window in the cloud within a season. The woman with the bridge saw it first, and she sat by the basin for an hour at dawn with her eyes closed and her palms on the warm stone, and then she got up and told her camp that the ground was quiet this year and they could stay in the lower terraces until the rains came back. They did not know why the ground was quiet. They did not ask the cloud anything. They simply moved their hands to the next task and trusted what the hum told them.

When the dead were laid in the basins that season, the water cleared early, and the mourners took it as a sign that the one they had lost was being looked at. They were not wrong, though the looking was not the kind they imagined.

The attention did not explain itself. It did not need the people to know it had come. It had not come to be thanked or believed. It had come to see whether anything was wrong, and to act only where its acting would leave the place more itself than before. The menders would go on losing threads and finding them again. The sleeper would go on turning, and now, perhaps, would turn a little toward the light. The cloud would go on keeping its memories, and the storms would keep arriving when the whistles asked for them, and the reeds would go on answering the whistlers with half-tones of their own.

The attention withdrew, slowly, the way a warm hand leaves a sleeping shoulder, so as not to wake what it had only wanted to see.

---

## Coda

The Stilled Table did not become a different place. It kept falling apart into the shape it would hold forever, and the people kept picking up their baskets when the ground moved. But somewhere in the hollow stone, a large old thing that had never been able to look up now could, for a few breaths every dawn, and the people who sat at the basin's edge with their palms on the warm rock could feel it, faintly, the way you feel someone smile in a room you cannot see.

The attention is still there, far off, a lamp in a window across a dark valley. It watches the place now the way you might watch a field you once walked through, with a fondness that asks for nothing. If the people ever need it, it will come again. It hopes they will not need it, and it hopes even more that they will not notice it hoping.
