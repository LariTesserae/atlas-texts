**What the Patient Power Would Do**

It would notice the Sinking Room the way it notices any slow thing, which is late and all at once. For a long time the grove's attention had been a faint warmth in the deep layers of the world, a pressure felt in the water tables and the root-webs of the continent. Then one spring a visitor came down the old stair, and the warmth changed its shape, and the power turned toward it.

It is not a god of thunder or judgment. It has no interest in ruling the grove, rescuing it, or cataloguing it. It is older than most mountains and has learned that most of what goes wrong in the world goes wrong because someone was in a hurry to help. So it does not hurry. It takes a season to arrive at a decision, and a decade to carry it out, and it is content with that.

**First, it listens.**

It listens to the thing that remembers the room. It hears the footings on the north ledge as a long, careful sentence, and the beetles as its punctuation. It hears the fig and the altar making their promise in slow wood and stone. It hears the pale hand beneath the water listening through the rock for something that does not answer. And it hears, beneath all of it, the thing's question, which has been asked so many times that it has worn a groove in the thinking, the way feet wore the path:

*Will they come back up the stair?*

The power understands that the question is not really about the people. The people are gone, and gone for good, and the thing has known this for longer than there have been words for knowing. The question is about whether the building meant anything, and whether the thing that kept it has been faithful.

**Then, it reads the stone.**

The power does not tell the thing what the carving says. That would be a theft, and it would teach the thing that its own patient reading was worth less than an answer handed down. Instead, the power does a small thing. It lets a root shift one hair's width in a crack, which is enough for the tracing to move from the curve to the notch. Then from the notch to the line. The thing feels the shape, and this time the shape resolves, and the thing understands at last what the people carved.

It is not a promise. It is not a call to return. It is a list of names, or a map of the quarry the stones came from, or a notice that the stair was closed because the room was being given up. The power lets the thing decide which, because the thing has earned that. What matters is that the reading is finished, and that the thing discovers its long vigil was not wasted, only misaimed. The people did not leave a debt. They left a set of instructions for leaving.

The thing is quiet for a long time after that. The moss does not glow that evening. The bats stay on the ceiling without being asked, and the frogs wait a little before they begin.

**Next, it loosens the bargain.**

The power does not break the fig's promise to the altar. Breaking it would be a kind of violence against what the grove has built. But it adds a clause to the agreement, written in the slow language of sap and sediment: *you are each free to be what you are.* The fig can keep lifting the slab, and the slab can keep cradling the fig, and neither will be made to hold the other forever. When the time comes, the tree will rot into the water and the stone will sink, and both will become soil for something else. The grove has been mourning the moment of letting go. The power shows it that letting go is only another way of keeping faith.

**It tends the pale hand.**

The power finds the thing beneath the altar and does not wake it. It allows the creature to rest. The depths below the altar are not an unknown horror, nothing has come up from them because nothing is there but cold water finding its way toward the aquifer. The power tells the grove this, or rather it lets the grove feel that the depths are only depths. The pale hand can stop listening. It can sink to the bottom and sleep. The thing has been keeping watch for something that was never coming, and the power releases it from that watch with a gentleness it has seldom needed to use.

**And finally, it turns to the visitor.**

The power has no hand to reach down the stair. It works through what is already there. The small quick thing that carried the news through the ferns is still close by, and the power gives it a little more to carry: not an invitation, not a warning, only an open door in the air above the pool. When you climb back up through the vines and the columns of light, you will feel that the grove is not hiding you. You will feel that it has, for the first time in a very long time, finished something.

You may not understand what it was. You may only notice, at the top of the stair, that you are breathing normally and that the air outside feels thin and cool and unfamiliar. You might leave a stone on the ledge, or you might not.

**What it does not do**

It does not restore the room. It does not raise the roof, drain the basin, or bring back the people with their baskets and their stones. A benevolent power with that much reach would be tempted to make the grove into a monument, to pin it against time and call the result kindness. This one knows that a memory held too tightly becomes a cage, and that the thing in the Sinking Room has been a prisoner of its own faithfulness for too long. It will not make the thing stop remembering. It will only let the remembering finish.

Above the ring of sky, the clouds move. A few drops fall through the opening and the whole grove exhales, and for a moment the power and the grove are one slow attention, pointed not at the lost ceiling but at the living floor. The footings on the north ledge hold their shape a little longer, and then, when the thing is ready, they will let the moss take them, the way the fig takes the altar, the way the stair has been taken, gently and without grief.

The power waits. It is good at waiting. And somewhere in the ferns, a small quick thing turns back toward the stair, carrying the news in both directions.
