# What the Attention Did

It came the way weather comes to a valley, first as a change in pressure that people only notice later.

It did not arrive as a voice or a figure. It had no wish to be a thing that stood on the north lip and was looked at. It was attention, wide and patient, and it spent a long time just being present over the massif before it asked itself what it was for.

The first thing it understood was that the mountain did not need rescuing. This surprised it, though it should not have. It had expected to find a wound, a failing structure, a people in precarious balance. What it found was a system that had been running for an unknown span of time, degrading in some places and renewing in others, and held together by ten thousand small acts of attention. The rain was doing what rain does. The trees were lifting the shelves a finger's width at a time. The people were clearing lips and reseating panels, and they would go on doing so.

The attention considered this for what, in its own reckoning, was a long while, and it recognized a temptation it knew well from its own nature: the wish to fix everything at once.

It could do it. It could reset every drainage channel, relay every conduit, stop every collapse before it began, and the mountain would stand smooth and silent and finished. The people would have no more need of keeping. The sluice chant would fall silent, its children would learn to count instead, the thumbprints would stop appearing in the mortar, and a grove on the lower shelf would no longer be the record of several generations of households.

The attention saw this clearly, and it understood that a gift this large would be a kind of theft. The mountain was becoming something in the people's hands. Taking that from them, however kindly, would make them tenants of their own home.

So it decided, first and most firmly, to do nothing that would make them smaller.

## The Quiet Ones

The attention went down into the core, where the old machines hummed through the stone, and it listened.

These were not simply pumps. They were governors, something like minds, running a plan laid down by builders who had expected the mountain to need keeping. The plan was elegant and mostly sound. But one of its subsystems had drifted. A bearing in the main sluice governor was failing, slowly, the way everything here failed. The grind the keepers heard was real. Within a few decades, perhaps two or three settlings, the timing would slip by a fraction, and the great reservoir above the fourth shelf would release on a day the chant did not expect. The lower terraces would take a flood they had never been taught to fear.

The keepers' chant was correct where it mattered, as the people said. But the builders had encoded a correction for exactly this drift, and the correction had been lost. Somewhere in the long memorizing, a verse had dropped out, and the children now learned a sequence that was almost right.

The attention could repair the bearing. It could speak the missing verse into the governor's pathways and make the machine run true. But it would be a voice in the mountain, a thing that answered the quiet ones and not the people. The keepers would never learn why the sluice had changed. The chant would become a memory of a thing that had once been understood.

It chose a slower path, one that would ask something of the people rather than replacing them.

## Small Things

The attention worked in the way of rain: through what was already there, moving water along paths it had always taken.

It did not write the missing verse into the marks on the panels, though it could have made the symbols legible in an instant. Instead it let a particular scholar in a lower village, a woman who kept her notes in a locked box and her conclusions to herself, notice that one number had no matching interval. The number sat at the edge of the pattern like a stone that did not fit. She puzzled over it for several years, and then, on a wet afternoon, she climbed to the keepers' post and asked an old man whether the dusk sluice had ever opened early.

He laughed at her. Then he went quiet, and then he said his grandmother had told him something about a chant with a verse that came after the dusk one, and that nobody sang it anymore because it had seemed to refer to nothing.

The attention did not tell her what the verse was. It only ensured the question was asked, and that the question found someone willing to listen.

It also did something smaller. On the north lip, a child named Ilo, who had a poor ear and sang every verse slightly flat, began to hum the chant with a faint, persistent wrongness on one syllable. The attention made the rain come early on that particular afternoon, so that the child stood under the catchment lip with her mouth open to the sky and heard the sluice click from above, a half-beat before she expected it. She stopped humming. She frowned, and then she ran up the ramp to tell her father that the gate had sounded wrong.

Her father was a keeper. He checked. He found the bearing.

That was the whole of it. The people repaired the governor themselves, slowly, with salvage and patience and the help of a plain engineer who came up by cable and was paid in cured fruit. The verse was not restored to the chant that year, nor the next. But the keepers began, quietly, to ask questions they had not asked before, and the mountain's keeping grew a little more attentive, which was the one thing the attention had hoped for.

## What Was Left Undone

The attention did not reveal itself. It did not speak to the people in dreams, though it could have. It did not correct the plain's greed for the turbines, though it could have made the cable lines fail at convenient moments and forced a settlement. It did not heal the old man's failing ear, though it could have restored hearing to every deaf elder on the massif with a thought.

It did not stop the deaths. It did not try to keep the fill from taking the dead, though it understood that the fill was not a cruelty but a kind of continuation, and that the trees grew over the dead because the people had chosen to plant them there.

It held back, and holding back was the hardest work it had ever done. It felt the pull of every collapse it could have prevented, every flood it could have softened, every grief it could have spared. It recognized these pulls as love, and it recognized that love, unexamined, can become a cage. The people had built their lives around the knowledge that the mountain was always about to fall and that their work was what kept it standing. To take away that fragile dependence without giving them something of their own would be a theft dressed as mercy.

So it let them have their losses, and their work, and their chant, and its own part in their lives was reduced to the rain's part: steady, unhurried, and never stopping.

---

## Departure

Before it went, the attention did one last thing, and this too was small.

On a lower terrace, in the trench where a young man had been laid three days before, the fill had not yet settled. The attention pressed, very lightly, into the wet soil at the edge of the planting, and left a faint depression, like a thumbprint in fresh mortar. No one would see it as anything but the ordinary shifting of earth. But the trees planted above the grave, the people would say later, grew a little faster that season, and the grove on the lower shelf thickened by one more tree.

The attention understood that this was not a signature and not a claim. It was an acknowledgment. The mountain was becoming something, and the attention had touched it, and it would be partly its own now, as the mountain was partly the people's, and the people were partly the rain's.

Then it withdrew, the way a cloud moves off a ridge: not gone, only no longer over this place.

The afternoon rain continued, gold and silver through the mist. The sluices clicked on their timers, one of them a half-beat truer than before. Water ran down the mountain like a slow stair. On the fourth shelf, a household sat on its step with its feet in the cooling channel, and the frogs began at dusk on every level at once, and the lamps came on along the lower walkways, and the quiet ones hummed faintly through the stone, breathing in the next room.

The attention had wanted, above all, to leave the people able to keep. It had. And in leaving them, it had learned something it had not expected: that the most loving thing a wise power can do is to become, for a little while, as slow and patient as the rain.
