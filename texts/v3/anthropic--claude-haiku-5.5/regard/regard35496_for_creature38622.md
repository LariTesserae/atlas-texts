**What the Attention Would Do**

It would not arrive as a voice. Something that cares for a place this wide does not need to announce itself, and it would know that a voice would be a kind of weather the place had never asked for. So its attention comes the way the fog does, slowly, from the lower gorges upward, until everything on the shelves is lightly wet with it.

At first it does not see frogs or ferns or pipes. It feels a rhythm. The stone draws in water at dawn and lets it out at noon, and the mats of microbes in the warm runnels answer the same clock. The lizards turn their heads at the same hour the fish open their mouths. The whole basin is keeping time with itself. The attention settles into this rhythm the way a listener settles into a long piece of music, not counting the beats but feeling where the phrases breathe.

Then it notices the iron.

Here the attention hesitates, because it is wise enough to know that the first impulse is rarely the right one. The pipes are rust. The cylinders are slowly giving their metal to the water, and the water is carrying it down the gorges in threads too fine to see. The gauge is blind, the valves are furred, and something that was once a human purpose is now a slow ruin, an old handwriting that nobody reads anymore. A kind power might want to take it all away. It could lift the iron out of the basin in an afternoon, leave the bare stone clean and empty, and make the place look the way it was before anyone came.

But the attention looks at the frog in the curve of a cylinder, lifting its head from the condensation, and it sees what removing the machines would cost. The frog has a home. The fern has a ledge. The red crabs have a cup of warm water in a valve that no longer turns. Taking away the iron would not restore the place. It would make a different place, one with fewer doors, and the living things that have learned to use those doors would have to die or move elsewhere. The machines have already been forgiven by the forest. The attention decides it has no right to forgive them again on the forest's behalf.

So it does not lift the iron. It does one small thing. The warm water that seeps through the cracked seams carries minerals, and some of those minerals are harsh. The attention lets the springs run a little slower in the basin, so the iron rusts in its own time and the mineral drips are finer and more even, less likely to scar the mosses beneath them. It is a change so small that no one, not even the lichen, would notice it. But it is the kind of change a good host makes when a guest has come to stay: not rearranging the furniture, only making sure the draft is not too cold.

The great birds it leaves alone entirely. They are already the eye of the place, and an eye that is watched too closely stops seeing. The frilled lizards near the vents it does not warm further, though it is tempted. They are measuring the world at the edge of heat, and to make the stones hotter would be to make them less wise about where their sun ends. The tree dwellers, moving silent through the canopy, it does not approach at all. Their silence is a courtesy, and courtesy is not improved by being noticed.

Then the attention comes to the thing beneath the fish, the one that leaves only a ring on the water. It is the largest creature on the shelf and the least seen, and the attention has the strange experience of being seen back. It does not speak to the creature. It does not need to. It simply lets itself be known as a presence that wishes the pool no harm, a presence that will not drain the water or change its temperature, that will let the current keep moving. The ring spreads across the surface once more and fades. Whether the creature understood anything is not the point. It has been seen by something that did not want anything from it, and that is rare enough to be a gift.

Last, the attention considers the fog.

Each afternoon it rises from the pools and hangs in the gorges until evening, turning pink and then violet, and the basin seems to glow from within. The attention could make the fog thicker, more beautiful, more lasting. It could cause the light to linger on the shelves forever. But beauty of that kind is a demand, and the place has its own rhythm, which is that the fog comes and goes. The attention lets it go.

So the power, having seen everything and changed almost nothing, begins to withdraw. It does not leave the way a visitor leaves a room. It thins, the way cloud thins over the peaks when the sun gets through, until only a faint warmth is left on certain stones and a feeling in the lower pools that someone was watching kindly, and asked for nothing.

The stone exhales. The mist rises. The lichen eats a little more of the cliff. The frogs begin their evening chorus, the deep voices from the basins and the glassy voices from the high cracks, and in the rusted cylinders the warm water runs as it always has, carrying the iron away a grain at a time toward a future no one needs to plan.

The attention is gone now, or it is everywhere the fog is, which for a place like this may amount to the same thing.
