# What the Attending Power Did

It came the way weather comes to high country, slowly, and it did not come as a voice or a light. It came as attention, the way a listener turns toward a sound that has been going on for a long time without anyone noticing it.

The bowl was old enough that it had stopped being surprised by anything, and the power was old enough to recognize that. It spent a long while only looking. It saw the fog climb out of the cloud below the rim each morning and spill over the lip like something poured slowly from a jug. It saw the leaves funnel that fog toward their tips and let it fall in lines. It saw the trees hoard water in their swollen trunks and the grazers kneel at the moss pools. It saw the stacked houses, the ladders, the ropes, the roots working into the masonry. And it saw the people, lean and patient, moving through the green dusk with their hands full of knowledge they could not have put into words.

It noticed the dry channels first. They were polished smooth, floored with plates of hardened silt, and the power understood what they meant. Once there had been a river here, and it had gone somewhere else, or down, or into stone, and the bowl had learned to live without it by building an entirely different kind of life.

The power felt the pull of wanting to help. It was not a small pull. The people were fragile in the way that anything is fragile when it has no margin, and the stone was crumbling, and the thin weeks were long. It could see the shape of several interventions at once.

It could bring rain. It could cut a channel through the rim so that a river would run through the bowl and fill the old grooves again. It could hold the fog back, or push it forward, so that the cisterns never went low. It could mend the stone, pulling the cracks closed and making the walls whole again, so that no root would ever need to be routed around. It could lift the people out, carry them to a valley with running water and sound rock, and let the bowl sink into its own silence without them.

It considered each of these carefully, because it did not trust its own tenderness. Tenderness is a strong current and can carry a good intention a long way from what is actually good.

The river would drown the leaves' purpose. The waxy broad leaves exist because there is no river; the whole canopy is a sculpture of absence. Water given freely would make the trees lazy, the ferns soft, the fog irrelevant, and in a few generations the people would have forgotten the names of the drip seasons, which are kept in their hands and not in the stone. A gift of water would replace a knowledge with a convenience, and convenience is a poor inheritor.

Mending the stone would be a kind of lie. The walls have stood through more winters than the names of the families who raised them, and they stand now partly because the roots and the cracks and the patient masons have been in conversation with them for centuries. A wall made whole by a power's hand would be a wall nobody had built, a museum piece, and the people would stop feeling the stone move under their palms.

And lifting them out would be the worst of all. The power imagined the valley with its clean water and its honest rock, and the people in it, healthy and bewildered, grieving a place they could not name the loss of. They would be strangers to their own hands. The rhythms that the bowl teaches would not survive the transplant, because rhythms need a particular place to be true.

So the power did none of these things. This was the hardest decision it made, and it made it knowing that restraint can look, from the outside, exactly like indifference.

---

What it did instead was smaller, and it took a long time.

It stayed. It did not appear in the lanes or speak through the clappers. It let its attention settle into the bowl the way fog settles into the canopy, invisible but present in every leaf. It listened to the hammers and the clatter of flint on flint, to women calling from ladder to ladder, to children shrieking across the rope bridges. It learned the difference between the drip weeks and the thin weeks by feeling them the way the people did, through the damp of the air and the slowness of the ferns.

It noticed a mason, an old woman with a split thumbnail and a habit of pressing her palm flat against a block before she set it. She was fitting a new stone into a gap where an older wall had given way, and she worked for three days, tapping and lifting and tapping again, until the block sat in its seat with a sound so low that only someone standing very close could hear it. She did not look up when it was done. She simply put her hand on the stone for a long moment, the way someone might rest a hand on an animal's flank, and then she went to get water.

The power wanted, very briefly, to tell her that her work would last. It did not. Instead it let the fog come in a little earlier that morning, a little thicker, so that the ferns she had been waiting to harvest unrolled before she needed to climb down for them. This was a small thing. It did not break the rhythm; it only met her at the place where her patience already was.

It noticed the offerings in the cracks. The people pushed seed and fiber into the openings of the oldest walls, and the power, watching the cracks, could see that most of the seeds would never sprout. The stone was too dry in the thin weeks, the roots too crowded. But one seed, pushed into a split near the base of a building sunk so deep that its doorway was now below the street, had found a damp pocket behind the moss. It was the kind of pocket no one would have thought to look for. The power did not water it, did not warm it, did not force it to grow. It only let the pocket stay damp a little longer than it would have otherwise, through one thin week, so that the seed had the chance to be what it was.

When the fig sprouted, a girl found it. She was perhaps nine, with her hair in a tight knot and a basket on her hip, and she crouched in the lane and looked at the green thread for a long time. Then she called to her mother, and her mother came down the ladder and crouched beside her, and they both looked at it, and neither of them said anything for a while. Then the mother said, quietly, that the tree had taken its share, and that someone would have to tie a rope to it, and the girl said she would do it.

The power felt something in that moment that it had no word for. It was not pride, and it was not relief. It was closer to the feeling of being thanked by something that did not know it was being thanked.

---

It also did something it had not planned to do, and it did this out of a reasoning it could only partly follow. It took the people's dreams, the way a river takes the light of the moon, and let them be a little clearer.

Nothing dramatic. A man who had been sleeping badly for a season dreamed of a particular crack in the old wall and woke knowing where the next block should be cut. A woman who had been unsure whether to take her daughter to the high rim for the midday light dreamed of the shafts breaking into coins and woke certain the light would be gentle enough. These were not instructions. They were the kind of intuition the bowl had always produced in its people, the knowledge that lives below words, and the power only tuned it, the way a mason taps a block until it seats.

It did not make the people dependent on it. It made them more themselves, and it did so by being nearly invisible.

---

The power reasoned about its own limits, and it found them honest. It could not stop the stone from becoming soil. It could not keep the bowl from sinking further into the high country. In some future it could not see clearly, the people might leave, or the roots might win, or the fog might one day fail to climb out of the cloud. The power did not promise otherwise. Its gift was not permanence. It was attention given without ownership.

Still, it wanted to leave something behind that would outlast its own presence, so it did one final thing, and this one it did openly, though no one would ever know it had been done.

At the lip of the bowl, where the fog spilled over every morning, it set a single broad leaf at the edge of the rim, in a place where the wind would catch it, and it gave the leaf a bead of water at its tip that did not come from fog. It was the only water the power ever gave the bowl. It did not run, did not pool, did not change anything. It only dripped, once, onto a stone that had been worn smooth by a thousand years of condensation, and then the drop was gone into the rock.

Late that afternoon, a grazer came to the rim to drink from a moss pool and lifted its head, startled, and looked up at the leaf. The water at its tip had gathered again, and was falling.

The grazer did not understand it. It only drank, and went back down into the ferns, and the people never noticed anything unusual at all.

---

At dusk the bats poured from the cliffs and the insects rose in their columns, and the roar filled the bowl until it seemed the whole place was leaving the rock. The fog began to withdraw, drawing back over the lip the way it always did, leaving the canopy to drip in the dark. Under the leaves the moss held its gleaming pockets, and the stone kept rounding, and the roots kept working into the cracks.

The power withdrew the way fog withdraws, slowly and without announcement, back into the cloud below the rim. It did not leave the place empty behind it. It left the place as it had found it, only a little more attended to, the way a good house is kept by someone who never speaks of keeping it.

And in the lanes, a girl with a rope in her hands stood under a young fig and tied the first knot, pulling it snug against the bark, and felt, on the back of her wrist, one cool drop fall from a leaf above where no fog should have reached.

She wiped it away without looking up, and went home for supper.
