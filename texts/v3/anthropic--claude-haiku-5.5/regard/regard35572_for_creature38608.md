**What the Attention Saw**

It noticed the bowl the way one notices a voice in another room, first as a shape of sound and only later as words. The notch, the cloud, the eleven terraces leaning on one another. A moth opening its wings at dusk. A child on a bridge who did not slow down.

It was not a god in the old sense, and it did not wish to be one. It had watched many places that were loved into ruin, and it had learned that the most common cruelty is rescue. A hand that lifts a thing out of the mud often leaves it unable to stand. So it did not move at once. It waited for a season, then another, and watched what the people did with the storms.

**What it considered**

It considered lifting them out. It could see the dry hard ground in the lowlands, where the rain falls in brief hard bursts and then leaves the earth cracked. It could see how a few generations of bronze and iron would ease the axe-work, the cord-work, the long hours of splitting stem. It could feel the weight of the bowl's damp in its own sense of things, and the ache in the old people's ribbed hands.

But it saw too that the axe was sharp because the work would not wait, and that the work was the reason for the people. Remove the hardship and the bowl would become a place where people lived beside a forest instead of inside it. The bridges would still need walking, but no one would stamp on them with such joy. The names would still be cut into the stone, but they would be cut without the trembling that comes from knowing the stone may be taken back.

It considered stopping the storms. The storms tore down limbs and carried off bridges, and they were the reason the people gathered to rebuild, and rebuilding was the reason they returned. Without the storm the bowl would hold, and holding is not the same as becoming. The attention decided that the storms were not its to cancel. It would leave them their weather.

It considered giving them words. They had a rich vocabulary for wetness and growth and almost no words for what cannot be touched. It could have placed a language in their mouths, a grammar for grief and for the dead who sit in the seams of the walls. But a word given from outside is a stone laid on a wall by someone who does not know how the wall settles. The people would learn the word and not the weight of it. The attention decided to listen instead.

**What it did**

It did very little, and the little it did was done through water, because water was already the thing that held the bowl together.

On the highest terrace, the eleventh, where the oldest wall leaned furthest out over the drop, a seep opened in the stone. Nobody saw it begin. It was no larger than a thumb, and it ran clear down the face of the wall, over the moss, into a crack that had never carried water. The next morning a child found it and put a hand under it, and the water was cold and sweet and came from nowhere that belonged to anyone.

The Wet Hands were called to the market stone that afternoon. The spring was on no family's terrace. It had no channel, no trough, and no hand to close it. The families argued, as they always did, loudly and with scars on display. Some said it was a sign. Some said it was a trick of the cloud. Some said it was theirs by right of being nearest, and others said it belonged to whoever could wall it in first. The argument went on until the light turned, and then one of the oldest of the Wet Hands, a woman whose palms were so ribbed with fiber lines that they looked like bark, said: *It came down. Then it belongs to whoever is thirsty.*

Nobody liked this, and everybody agreed to it. They built a trough of split stem and clay under the seep. They cut a small trench to lead the overflow down to the market stone. And by the end of the wet season there was a new channel running the length of the eleventh terrace, and the families who had once quarrelled over every drop were now quarrelling over who had dug the trench deepest.

The attention did not claim the spring. It did not appear in the market or speak through any mouth. It only let the water come where the water was needed, and it trusted the people to do what they always did with a gift that arrived without instructions: bend it to their own work.

It did one other thing. On the first bridge rebuilt after that season, a root that had been guided across a gap by a man who died in the cold, a root that had been named for him and cut into stone at both ends, grew a single extra shoot at the midpoint, where nobody had trained it. It was not a large shoot. It was thin and pale and it curled once toward the notch before it stopped. The old people looked at it for a long time and then went on walking. The attention did not need to be named. It only needed to be noticed by the people who notice things.

**What it left undone**

It did not stop the midges, though a frog-hollow at the foot of the cliff was quietly made warmer so its chorus would carry further in the cold, and the children would sleep better. It did not stop the decay of the walls, though the moss on the twelfth terrace was left to grow a little slower, so that the people would have one more season to rebuild before the stone gave way.

It did not explain itself. It never would. It understood that the people of the notch did not need to know who had opened the spring in order to know that the spring was theirs to share. A gift that is not explained cannot be bargained over, and so it can only be used. That was the kindest thing it could leave them.

**What it came away with**

Before it turned its attention elsewhere, it heard a child at the top of the eleventh terrace, hand under the new water, saying to no one in particular: *here, I'm still growing*. The words meant nothing to the attention in any sense it had been taught. But it found them clean, the way a good stone is clean after the rain has run off it, and it kept them.

Then the cloud came down into the bowl, the frogs took up their rising and falling calls, the midges rose at dusk, and somewhere under the litter something with many legs went about its work. The terraces bulged and leaned and held. The bridges thickened a finger's width. The people went home along the walls with their arms held slightly away from their sides and their mouths closed, and the attention, which had come to see and had seen, let the place go on becoming what it was.
