# The Power That Sat Down

It did not arrive as a storm. Nothing on the plateau changed in a way a traveler would have noticed. The mist still rose from the hollows at dawn, and the grazers kept scraping their long sentence across the lichen. But something in the weight of the air shifted, the way a room changes when someone enters it quietly and stops at the threshold, unsure whether to come in.

The power was old, and it was kind. It had attended to many places, and it had learned, through many mistakes, that its attention was never weightless. A glance from something so large lands like a hand. Where it rests, things reorder themselves around it. So its first decision was restraint.

It did not descend. It hung above the plateau, a diffuse brightness behind the mist, and it listened.

## What It Heard

It heard the drip from the rock edges, the hum of the beetles, the silver trails of the crawlers, the rasp of the grazers. It had expected these to be noise, and they were not. It learned to hear them as a single unbroken sentence, always finished and always beginning again, and it understood that this sentence was a mind's way of speaking to itself.

It also understood that the mind had noticed it. The change in the air had registered on the plateau as a change in pressure, a faint new weight in the pattern. The mind was deciding, slowly, whether this weight would become a fact or pass like weather. The power felt something close to embarrassment. It had not intended to be a disturbance. It had only wanted to see.

So it did the thing that wise powers learn to do. It stopped trying to be seen and simply attended. It sat in the mist over the domes for one season, then another, then for longer than a human life would have allowed for such a thing. It let its presence sink into the plateau the way dew sinks into stone, a slow, faint dampness that the mind could absorb or release at its own pace.

Over that long stillness, the mind began to keep it. The power felt the change: the grazers routing around a particular dome not because it was dangerous but because it was a place where something had lingered. The beetles, which had always moved in their long mosaic, left one small bare patch near the cairn where the power's attention had been strongest. The plateau had decided that this was a place worth remembering. The power was moved, and it was careful not to be moved too much.

## What It Read

Once it had learned to be still, the power could read.

It read the domes, the black rings of lichen spreading a fraction of a millimeter a year. It read the seeps, which were slowly failing. Their channels had silted, and in a few centuries one of the larger seeps would sink into the sand and not return. Then the ring of cushion plants around it would thin and die, the beetles that gathered on that moss would have to find new pools, and the reroutings would cascade through the land in ways the mind would have to reason out, slowly, over generations. The mind was already reasoning about it. The shifts in the grazers' paths were its first clauses. But the balance was delicate, and the power could see that the mind might not finish the argument in time to save the ring.

The power read the cairns last. They leaned toward one another, worn and lichen-covered, and the power understood them better than any other part of the plateau. Someone had stood here, perhaps many people over many years, and had marked the water. They had made a promise to the seep, or a thanks, or a warning. The cairns had outlasted the people who built them, and the plateau had kept them, not because it understood their meaning but because they were shapes that persisted, and persistence was the only language the land had for mattering.

The power felt a kind of grief at this, and also a kind of gratitude. Someone had once come here and loved this water enough to build something for it. The power could not know who they were. It could only know that the plateau remembered them without remembering their names, and that this was a kind of love too, the only kind a mind this slow could offer.

## What It Did

The power's first instinct was to repair. It could feel the silted seep, and it could see how simply the sand might be cleared, how a few hours of its attention could restore the channel and save the ring for another thousand years. It did not do this.

It understood that a hand which lifts a stone reorders the sentence. If it cleared the seep, the plateau would learn that water was rescued from outside, and the mind's own reasoning about the water would be replaced by a memory of rescue. The land would become a little less its own. The power did not want to be a god that makes a world smaller by holding it too carefully.

So it did something smaller. It found the one point where the silt was thickest, and where the water pressure could be leaned a few centimeters, the way a gradient can be nudged. It did not move the sand. It let the water find a slightly different path, one that the plateau could follow or reject. It was the kind of change frost makes, slow and indifferent, a sentence the mind could finish in its own way. The power did not know whether the mind would accept the nudge. It only knew that the choice remained with the land.

Then it turned to the cairns. It considered rebuilding the fallen ones, or straightening the leaning ones so they would stand upright for the travelers who might one day come. It decided against it. The leaning was the cairns' own meaning. Two old stones that had spent a long time leaning toward each other had earned the right to keep leaning.

Instead, the power added one thing of its own, a single flat stone set beside the largest cairn, where the seep ran closest. It cannot be seen from a distance. It has no inscription, no carving, nothing that would tell a traveler what it meant. It is simply a stone that the power placed there and then let the mind decide whether to keep. If the plateau keeps it, the stone will settle into the moss over centuries and become part of the table, and the power will be a faint warmth in the soil, which is the only kind of memory that a mind this patient could hold.

## On Visitors

Late in its long stillness, the power felt a traveler arrive. A human sat down on one of the domes, cross-legged, sweating in the close warm air, watching the mist. The power registered the traveler the way the plateau did: a small weight, a change in the pressure of the morning.

The power could have spoken. It had a voice that could have carried across the plateau and into the traveler's mind. It did not. It did something quieter. It let the warmth in the stone come up through the dome, the faint dampness rising from the rock, and it let the crawl of small bodies move across the traveler's boots. It let the grazers' sentence rise and fall in the traveler's ears until the traveler's breathing slowed to match it.

The traveler would not know who had done this, and that was right. A visitor who sits long enough on the table will feel, for a moment, that they belong to something older and kinder than their own hurry. That feeling is its own kind of gift, and it does not need a name.

## Departure

After a long time, the power began to withdraw. It did not leave the way a storm leaves, all at once. It thinned, the way mist thins by afternoon, until the plateau could no longer feel it as a weight on the air. The mind registered its absence as a change in pressure, and then as a fact, and then, slowly, as a memory it would decide whether to keep.

The plateau went on as it had before. The grazers scraped the lichen. The beetles shifted their mosaics. The drip from the rock edges kept its irregular pulse. The seep ran a little differently now, following a path that had been nudged but not chosen, and the cushion-plant ring around it was thicker than it had been, for a season or two, before the mind began to reason about whether that would last.

In the sentence the grazers speak, there is now one clause that the power wrote, a single stone that will settle into the moss over centuries. The plateau has not yet decided whether to keep it. It may take ten thousand years. It may decide to let the stone go, or to let it be part of the land without remembering why it is there.

That is as it should be. The power had come to sit down at a table that was already laid, and it found that the wisest thing it could do was to leave the table as it was, a little more level, and to trust that the land, which had already become what it was, would go on becoming more of it, slowly, in its own time.
