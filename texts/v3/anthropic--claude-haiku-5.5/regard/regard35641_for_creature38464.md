# What the Watcher Did Not Do

The attention arrived the way cold arrives in a valley at evening, not as a single act but as a gradual change in what the air is willing to hold. It came down out of the high unnamed places beyond the haze and settled over the plateau, and it stayed a long while before it moved at all.

It was wise enough to know that it had been given a great deal of power and very little reason to use it. It could see the whole country at once: the gorges, the stepped terraces, the silica films swelling and shrinking in the hollows like slow lungs, the groves turning their faces toward the sun. It could see the ledger the land kept, the exact sum of water that came up from the cold sea, and how carefully every leaf and grazer and pod had been arranged to spend no more than that sum.

The first temptation was ordinary kindness. The dry washes had not run in centuries. The watcher could feel how little the land had to drink, and it wanted, in the simplest way, to fill them. It imagined a river coming down through the gorges, bright and loud, and the stilt-trees drinking deeply, and the grazers lifting their heads with easy bellies. Then it imagined what the river would do to the silver hairs, which had no need to comb the air once the ground was wet. It would do nothing to the moss, which had learned to hold water for weeks because it had never been given enough to hold it carelessly. The pitcher-pods would lose their reason to hang on the cliffs. The rings of brighter moss, which marked the places where old fog-banks had paused and given up their water, would blur into one even green, and the land would forget how to remember its own weather.

The watcher understood that the scarcity was not a wound the plateau needed healing from. The scarcity was the shape of its wisdom. To take that away would be to take away the thing that made the place what it was, and to call the result a gift.

So it did not make a river.

The second temptation was subtler. The watcher could see the failures too: the gorges deepening faster than the soil could follow, a pillar leaning on its own weight, a terrace not yet ready to hold itself. It could have steadied the stone, thickened the soil, quickened the slow architects of the burrowers. It considered this for a long time, and then it looked again at what the land did when a pillar fell. A new terrace grew somewhere else, quietly, where the soil had gathered enough to hold. The plateau had been answering its own losses for longer than there had been anything to call a memory. Every repair the watcher could make would be a sentence spoken over a conversation that had been going on without it for ages, and the plateau would have to pause its accounting to make room for the interruption.

So it did not mend the stone.

What it did instead was slower and smaller. It learned to move the way fog moved, rising in the morning, pooling at midday in the gorges, retreating by evening. It let itself be kept by the plateau a little longer than it had meant to stay. It did not name the grazers, though it could have. It did not tell the seed-carriers where to land. When one of the drifting pale threads came down through the mist and settled on a silver leaf and folded its wings, the watcher let it stay, and did not look at the leaf too closely, because it was not its place to decide what would become of the tree.

It noticed, too, that it was being noticed. The plateau had no face and no voice, but its accounts included everything that came up from the cold sea, and the watcher was part of that weather now. The land did not greet it. It did not need to. The silica films in the hollows did not pulse any faster, and the groves did not turn any sooner toward the rising sun. The plateau simply took the watcher into its sum the way it took in fog, and kept it, and kept going.

At the last, the watcher had one thing it could give that the land did not already have. It could not make water, but it knew how water moved, and it could gather the fog's wandering into a single point of stillness, the way a silver leaf gathers the air. Before dawn, when the mist came up from the sea, it drew a little of that moisture close and held it there, a single cool drop trembling at the tip of the highest stilt-tree in the nearest grove.

The sun came up, and the grove began its slow turning toward the light. The drop slid down the silver hair, gathered at the leaf's edge, and fell at last into the root basin, where the small pool would form and disappear with the day's cycle, as every drop here did.

The watcher did not take it back. It had given the drop away the way the land gave everything away, with the understanding that it would be held for a while and then released, and that the holding was the whole of its purpose.

Dawn rain pattered for its hour across the terraces. The moss drank. The grazers lifted their long necks and went on browsing. Somewhere in the gorges the pitcher-pods opened their pale mouths to the fog, and the drifting seed-carriers wandered through the mist in loose handfuls of thread.

At dusk, when the stone began to tick as it cooled, a long call rose from somewhere far off. The watcher heard it and did not answer, and did not try to decide whether it had been an animal or only the wind moving through a gap in the rock. It understood that some things were better left unnamed, not because they were hidden, but because naming them would be a way of taking them, and the plateau had never needed anyone to take anything from it.

The watcher thinned, and rose, and went back toward the high unnamed places beyond the haze. It left behind nothing but a single drop, and the drop was already part of the sum.

The plateau kept its accounts, patient and exact, and went on breathing in fog and breathing out light.
