# Preparing to Visit the Cistern-City Beneath the Limestone

## Read the Place Before You Arrive

This is not a ruin or a place that has been abandoned. It is inhabited, busy, and, in its own way, deeply content. The main difficulty is not danger but attention. Everything here is in motion, and almost nothing has changed in a very long time. Visitors who come expecting a dead or strange place tend to miss what matters, and visitors who come expecting a bustling market tend to miss how still the place is underneath. Go in curious and patient, and expect to spend more time watching than doing.

## Your Body and the Air

The air is nearly saturated with water, and you will never fully dry out. Dress for constant dampness. Wool, or some other fiber that keeps its warmth wet, will serve you better than cotton, which will hang heavy and chill you. Expect cold hands and feet, since the stone holds the temperature of the deep earth and the moisture pulls warmth from you. Bring a layer you can take off and put back on, because the air is still but the temperature changes between galleries and the heat of tar lamps.

Wear the most grip-capable footwear you own, and then assume it will not be enough. The terraces are floored with sediment packed hard by generations of feet, but it is slick with runoff, and the stone steps are polished. Ladders are wet, rope bridges sway, and the sluice channels are narrow and fast. Keep three points of contact whenever you can. Wear gloves that will not slip, and consider carrying a short staff or a length of cord as a handhold on the steeper runs.

Your skin and clothes will pick up the white mineral bloom within an hour. It does not hurt, but it dries into a fine chalky film, and it will get in your eyes and mouth. Carry a cloth to wipe your face and your hands, and keep your lips and eyes moist. Your eyes will ache in the tar light, since it is low, orange, and smoky and leaves only pools of gold among deep murk. Let them adjust slowly. Do not walk fast in the dim patches.

Breathe through a cloth or loose scarf when you are near the lamp niches or the bitumen seeps. The smoke is thick, and the soot gets into the lungs. Most residents seem unbothered by it, but you have not spent your life in it, and a few hours of breathing it can leave you coughing and dizzy.

## Light

Bring your own light source, even though the city lamps are reliable. The tar lamps are set at regular intervals, and they are the city's main source of illumination, but the places between them are dark, and the dark is wet and disorienting. Carry a small lamp with a steady, clean flame if you can, and make sure it is well sealed against moisture. Do not make a show of your light, though. The people are used to the orange of their lamps, and a bright white beam will seem harsh and somewhat rude, especially near the elders on the central pillars.

Keep electronics out of the water and out of the mist. Assume anything sensitive will fail. If you carry a timepiece, understand that it will probably be useless and that the people will not care what it says. They mark time by the cycles of the glowing wall-faces and the hollow stone bells, and you will learn to do the same. Some visitors find it helpful to note the glow's brightening and dimming and count the repetitions. It is the only clock the city has ever needed.

## Sound

The first thing you will notice, and the thing you will remember longest, is the sound. Water drips, runs, and slides everywhere, and the cavern takes every drip and folds it back on itself. Sluice gates clack. Chisels ring in the quarry galleries with a steady beat that runs through the whole city. Voices double, blur, and carry from terrace to terrace. The bells, hollow stones struck to mark the hours, hang in the air for a very long time before dissolving into the constant patter.

Do not try to escape the noise. Nobody here knows silence, and nobody seems to want it. Instead, learn to hear through it. Listen to the difference between a sluice that is about to be closed and one that is merely shifting. Notice when the chisels stop, because that means someone has finished a block and something is about to be carried. Most importantly, learn the bells. The people will tell you what hour it is by them, and you will find that the tones do something to your sense of duration. Plan your conversations around them, because a sentence spoken during a bell will be swallowed.

If you have trouble with sustained noise, bring something to block one ear for short periods, but do not wear anything that cuts you off from the sound entirely. The residents will read that as a refusal to take part, and it may make you hard to talk to.

## The Mere and the Doubled Ceiling

The lowest terrace holds a flat, dark pool a few hundred paces across. Its surface is so still that it doubles the ceiling, and standing at its edge can be unsettling. You will see the glowing faces of the walls reflected, the stalactite columns hanging both up and down, and your own figure looking back from below. Do not stare into it for long. The mind tends to lose its sense of which way is up, and some visitors have to sit down on the wet stone to recover their balance.

The mere holds fish with no eyes that drift in the cold clarity near the bottom, and blind pale crawlers that feed on the bloom. Both are rare. The people hunt them with ceremony and do not eat them all. Do not touch the water, do not throw anything into it, and do not try to catch anything. Watch from the edge. If you are invited to witness a hunt, go quietly, sit where you are told, and do not comment on the technique.

Nothing grows near the mere or anywhere else in the city. You will find no roots, moss, fungus, or any green or brown at all. The palette is white crust, black wet stone, grey cut blocks, and the faint sheen of water. Your eyes will start to crave color after a while. This is normal, and you should expect it. Do not bring plants or seeds in with you. Anything living that you introduce would be the first thing in this place that grows, and the people would most likely treat it as a disruption to be removed.

## The Architecture

Houses are cut from the living rock or built from blocks fitted without mortar, each stone seated in a socket recut over generations. Buildings lean on one another. Roofs of one level are the floors of the next. Ladders, ramps, and rope bridges connect the terraces, and the whole arrangement feels less like a city placed on the ground than a honeycomb grown out of a cliff. Arches are everywhere, because the stone seems to want to take that shape.

Be careful where you put your weight. Some roofs are also floors, and a roof may be occupied and in use by the household above. Ask before you climb onto anything that looks like a rooftop. Watch the cisterns cut into the floors. They are deep, cold, and fed by the constant runoff, and they are not guardrailed the way a surface city would have them. Keep your children, if you have any with you, very close, and do not let them lean over the water.

On the second terrace, the market trades bundles of cord and bowls of rendered fat. The rate of exchange seems to be governed by custom, not by quantity, and you may find that the same bundle is worth the same bowl no matter what the season or the stock. Do not try to bargain hard. You will be surprised at how little it matters to the traders whether you make a deal. If you have something to offer, offer it as a gift, not a trade, and expect it to be accepted without much comment. Anything made from an unfamiliar material will attract a great deal of attention.

## The Central Pillars and the Elders

At the center of the bowl stands a set of stone pillars where the older people sit. They are the keepers of the schedule of gates, the bells, and the carvings. They will not come to you; you should go to them, slowly and with clear intent. Greet them plainly. Sit where you are shown, not where you would prefer. Accept what they offer, even if it is a bowl of fat or a length of cord, because refusing is a rejection of their hospitality.

Do not ask them about change. The question will not be understood as hostile. It will simply not be understood at all. They have no word for the thing you are asking about, and any attempt to explain it will make the conversation circle back to what the city already is. You can ask about the schedule, the rites, the faces of the wall, and the waterline, and they will answer at length, precisely, and with great patience. These are the things that matter to them, and they will be glad that you care.

## Rules of Conduct

Nothing here is permitted to change, and that is the most important thing to understand before you go. A block that cracks is replaced with an identical block from the same seam. A carved sign is recut in the same strokes. A household that dies out is replaced by one that occupies the same rooms and repeats the same rites. The city is maintained by a kind of collective refusal to allow anything new to stay.

So, as a visitor, do not alter anything. Do not touch the gates, the sluices, or the bells. Do not cut or scratch the walls, not even to mark your visit. Do not carve your name, make a sign, or leave a token. Do not take stone, soot, bloom, or anything else from the galleries, even if it seems worthless to you. The soot is kept for pigment and the bloom is part of the cycle; taking either is an offense that will be noticed and quietly corrected.

Do not replace anything that appears worn, cracked, or out of place. If you see a damaged block, a fraying cord, or a sign that has weathered, leave it alone. The people will fix it in their own time, using their own methods, and any repair you make will be recut or removed. Your good intentions will simply become one more disturbance for them to erase.

Most importantly, be aware of how you affect the people who live here. You are a new thing in a place where nothing new has been allowed to stay. Individuals may be curious about you, and some may ask where you come from or what your world is like. Answer honestly and briefly, or deflect if you prefer. Do not describe your home in ways that would encourage the residents to imagine a different way of living. The city does not need that, and you should assume that anything you plant in their minds will be tended with the same care as everything else.

## The Glow and the Waterline

The cold blue-white light on certain wall-faces brightens when the water runs over them and dims when it stops. The people use these faces to measure time, and you should learn to read them. Watch a face for a full cycle before you decide what you are seeing. Note the brightness, the rhythm, and the way the glow lingers when the water stops. Do not touch the faces or wet them, because the luminescence is part of the city's clock and any disruption will be noticed.

The waterline is carved at the same height on every wall. The people renew the marks whenever they weather, cutting them again in precisely the same place. You will think, at first, that the water must rise and fall, given how much of it runs through the city. It does not. The inflow and outflow are balanced so exactly that the level in the mere, the cisterns, and the channels never changes. Do not try to measure this yourself, and do not point out to the residents that a level is a level. They know.

## Leaving

When you are ready to go, leave the way you came, and leave nothing behind. Do not take a gift that would not have been there before you arrived, and do not leave a gift that is out of the ordinary. If you have been given something, accept it, and carry it out, but do not show it off or use it as a marker of where you have been.

Expect that the city will close back over your visit within a day or two. The gates will swing on their schedule, the chisels will ring, the bells will sound, and the water will keep dripping as though you were never there. This is not a rejection of you. It is simply how the place has always worked. You will carry the sound with you, and you may find that it lingers for a long time, the constant patter continuing in your ears after you have left the stone behind.

When you are back in your own world, do not expect the silence to feel restful. Many visitors find it strange, even uncomfortable, at first. Give yourself time to adjust, and remember that you carry the held breath of the place with you, the sense of a stable equilibrium in which everything moves and nothing alters.
