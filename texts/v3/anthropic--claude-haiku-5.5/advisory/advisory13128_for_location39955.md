# Preparing to Visit the Cradle Cleft

**Threat level:** Low in the ordinary sense. Nothing here will attack you, and nothing hunts. The danger is slow. The place wears down attention, sense of time, and sense of self, and it is hard to notice that this is happening. Most of your preparation should target that gradual drift.

---

## Before You Go

**Know your return anchor.** The Cleft has no weather, no seasons, and no visible sun, so you cannot use the environment to judge how long you've been there. Before you enter, choose a return anchor: a specific object, a set time, or a fixed number of hours. Write it down, and tell someone on the outside what it is. Your own sense of elapsed time will be unreliable within the first hour.

**Confirm your exit point.** Arrive at the bowl's single open edge, where the salt gives way to the bare rock of the shoulder, and mark it. Your way back will look the same as your way in, and the machinery will not have moved, so you need a reference that is yours. A bright strip of cloth, a chalk X, or a stake driven into the salt will do.

**Don't plan to stay long.** Visitors who intend to spend a single afternoon often find it has been a day or more. Plan for two to four hours at most on a first visit, and build in a hard stop you will honor even if you feel you have only just arrived.

**Tell a companion your plan.** If you travel alone, arrange a check-in with someone who will notice if you fail to report back. Solo visits are not impossible, but they remove the outside reference that helps you notice drift.

---

## Equipment

**Clocks you trust.** Bring at least two independent timekeepers of different types. A wind-up mechanical watch is good, because it runs on a mechanism that doesn't depend on the local environment. Add a sand timer or a candle with marked intervals. Treat any disagreement between clocks as a warning sign about your own perception, not as a fault in the clocks.

**Something that changes.** The thing most worth carrying is an object that visibly alters over time. A candle that burns down, ice that melts, a bottle of water that slowly evaporates, a plant cutting that wilts. These give you an external record that the Cleft cannot flatten, because the things in it are fixed and yours are not. Check it regularly.

**Hearing protection.** The drone is constant and loud, and it is amplified by the bowl's walls. Soft foam or wax plugs will reduce the load on your nerves. Keep at least one ear partly open for safety, since the ticking valves and turbine thrum are useful warnings of where machinery sits.

**Respiratory support.** The air is thin and cold, and breathing takes effort. A light scarf or cloth over the mouth and nose warms and slows incoming air. If you have a condition affecting your lungs or heart, do not enter without medical clearance and a companion.

**Cold-weather layers.** The cold settles into the lungs and stays. Wear wool or synthetic base layers, a windproof outer shell, gloves that allow fine motor work, and a hat. The salt crust is hard on boots, so bring sturdy soles with good grip. Cracked hexagonal plates tilt and can roll under weight.

**Light.** The sourceless grey light is even and does not cast strong shadows, which makes depth and edges hard to judge. A headlamp helps with close work, especially inside the concrete buildings. Keep a spare battery, though batteries will behave normally here.

**Gloves and tools for the plants.** The sap is sticky, sweet-smelling, and will bead on skin. Wear gloves for any contact with foliage. Bring a folding blade and a cloth for cleaning.

**Sealed containers.** Keep food and drink sealed and stored apart from the sap. Do not open containers near the amber film, because the sap's smell is strong enough to cling to food.

---

## Arrival and First Hour

Stand at the edge for several minutes before you walk in. Breathe slowly. Note how your body feels, your heart rate, your focus, the pressure in your ears. Compare these to your baseline. You will need this reference later.

Look at your clocks and write the time down. Repeat the note every fifteen to twenty minutes, and always write it by hand. Verbal notes drift.

The first sensory impact is usually the drone. It can make people feel slightly nauseated or agitated. This passes in most visitors within twenty minutes as the mind adapts, but the adaptation itself is part of the problem: you stop hearing the sound, and you may stop noticing other things with it. Check your hearing protection and stay aware of it.

---

## On the Ground: Rules

**Do not touch the machinery.** Everything here is running in exact equilibrium. Nothing wears out and nothing is repaired. Intervening is not clearly dangerous, but nobody knows what it would change, and the Cleft has not been observed to accept change gracefully. Keep hands off valves, gauges, cranks, and pipes. Do not climb the tanks or gantries.

**Do not disturb the air.** Spores and pollen hang in fixed layers. Walking through them quickly stirs them, and they settle back into loops after a while, but they do not seem to care about being moved. Move slowly and do not wave your arms.

**Do not eat, drink, or inhale the sap deliberately.** It is the plants' only moisture and it is sweet, and it is a poor idea to test that sweetness. Avoid letting it touch your mouth or any open wound.

**Stay on the salt where it's solid.** Where the crust has broken, the grey substrate is loose and fine. It is not dangerous in itself, but it dulls grip and stains.

**Do not follow the boot prints.** The depressions in the salt have not eroded in generations. They lead in a line toward the far machinery and stop. Visitors who follow them lose track of time and distance faster than anyone else. If you find yourself following them, stop and return to your anchor.

**Do not sit down for long.** Resting feels pleasant and deepens the stillness. Short rests are fine, but mark the time before you sit and set a timer.

---

## Mind and Time

This is the most important section.

**Expect the wrong sense of time.** Light, sound, and temperature do not change, so your body has nothing to pace itself with. Visitors commonly report that twenty minutes felt like five, or that an hour felt like the length of a single breath. Trust your written log and your clocks over your internal sense.

**Expect the wrong sense of self.** Several visitors have reported a mild, pleasant absorption that they describe as "belonging to the scene." They forget their plans and how long they've been there. If you notice yourself thinking "it doesn't matter what time it is" or "I can stay a little longer," treat that as the warning it is. Leave.

**Expect to see things that look like movement.** Steam plumes, valve wheels, and turbine blades all appear to shift if you look at them out of the corner of your eye. Verify by fixing your gaze on a single marker, such as a rivet or a stain on concrete, and checking it against your log. They have not moved.

**Keep talking.** If you have a companion, talk regularly about ordinary things, such as what you ate, what the weather is like outside, or the names of people you know. Speech helps anchor you to the world beyond the bowl. If you are alone, speak aloud to yourself or narrate your actions. This sounds silly and works.

**Recognize the signs of drift.** These include losing track of your exit anchor, feeling reluctant to leave, forgetting why you came, feeling that the machines are waiting for you, or believing that you have been here a short time when your log says otherwise. Any one of these means you should begin the return walk immediately, even if your planned time has not ended.

---

## Navigation

The Cleft has no paths, and the landmarks are fixed, which makes them deceptive. The grid of pipes can look like a route, but it is a maze of junctions that all look alike.

**Use the pipe numbers.** Some pipes carry painted numerals. Write down a sequence as you walk, and keep the sequence in your log. If you need to find your way back, reverse it.

**Keep the walls in sight.** The bowl's walls are high and continuous. If you can see the wall on your left, you are circling the bowl, not crossing it. Use the wall as a guide rather than trusting any single structure.

**Mark your path.** Chalk, cloth strips, or small stakes help. Do not rely on memory, because the scene is too uniform for memory to hold it well.

**Know the way out.** Return to the exit anchor at the rock shoulder. Do not attempt a shortcut across the floor. If you become uncertain, follow the wall back to the anchor and stop there.

---

## Leaving

When your return anchor, your timer, or your log tells you to leave, leave. Do not finish a thought, do not take one more look, do not return to something you forgot.

Walk back along your marked path. Check your clocks at the exit. Compare them to your log. If the difference is large, expect that your sense of time will be off for a while afterward. Rest before you attempt any further travel, and do not drive or operate machinery until your focus has returned.

Once outside, write a full account of your visit while it is fresh: times, sensations, anything you noticed drifting. Much of what you experienced will feel less vivid after an hour, and the notes will be your only reliable record.

---

## Quick Checklist

- Return anchor chosen and reported to a companion
- Two independent clocks, one mechanical
- An object that visibly changes over time
- Hearing protection
- Breathing cover
- Layered cold-weather clothing and grip-soled boots
- Headlamp and spare battery
- Gloves and a cleaning cloth
- Sealed food and water, kept away from the sap
- Marking material for the path
- Written log, with hand-recorded times every 15–20 minutes
- Hard time limit set, two to four hours at most
- Plan to ignore the boot prints

The Cleft is not hostile, and it will not keep you by force. It keeps you by being complete and unchanging, so that you forget to count. Count deliberately, and leave on time.
