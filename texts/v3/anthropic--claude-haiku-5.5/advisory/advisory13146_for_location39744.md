# Preparing to Visit the Drowned Mesa of Vell Ossary

The Mesa is not dangerous in the way a cliff or a predator is dangerous. Its hazards are indifferent, and the place is built to be unreliable. Most travelers who are hurt here are not caught off guard by something that attacked them. They trusted something that was only temporarily true: a path, a reflection, a stair, a patch of stone. Prepare less for monsters and more for a world that doesn't hold still.

Plan on a short visit, measured in hours rather than days. Plan to leave the way you came, even if the way you came is harder to find than it was.

---

## Before You Go

**Know your return.** Before you step through, fix in your mind exactly where you arrive. Arrival points are usually on the flattest, driest stone you can find, and they are the most valuable thing you will own here. Note the nearest fixed landmark: a standing wall, a large basin, a carved doorway. Be aware that even these can change, so write down several features and their relationships to one another, not just one.

**Plan for a finite stay.** Decide in advance how long you will remain, and how you will know when that time is up. The light never changes, so you cannot judge time by it. Bring a reliable timekeeper, preferably two, and set alarms that you can feel (a vibration is better than a chime, since the sound here is overwhelming). Your sense of elapsed time will drift. Do not trust it.

**Tell someone where you are going.** Give a fixed point of contact on your home side, and a time after which they should raise the alarm. Agree on what "raise the alarm" means. Travelers have been lost here not because no one knew where they were, but because no one knew what to do when they failed to return.

**Go with others, if you can.** One companion is the minimum. Two or three is better. The reasons are practical. A second person sees what the first one misses, notices when a stair has changed, and can hold a rope or drag someone back from the lip. Companions also keep you honest about whether the place is affecting your judgment.

## What to Bring

**Light.** The ambient dusk is soft and comes from no direction, which makes depth and edges hard to read. Bring a bright, steady light that you can aim, and a second, independent source. Lanterns that rely on oil or wicks may struggle in the wet air, so sealed battery or chemical lights are better. Avoid anything that depends on open flame near the mist; the air is saturated and the fire will fight you.

**Footwear.** Choose boots with deep, aggressive tread that drain quickly and do not hold water. The stone is wet, the pools are often slick with a pale growth, and the stone surfaces change under your weight. Bring a second pair if you can, because the first will be soaked within minutes and cold within an hour. Ankle support matters. The stone can shift by a finger's width when the water pulses beneath it, and a rolled ankle here can become a serious problem.

**Clothing.** Wear layers that tolerate constant damp. The air is warm and wet, so heavy wool and cotton will become heavy and clammy, and then cold when the rain comes. Synthetic base layers are better. Keep a dry layer sealed in a bag, to change into when you return.

**Rope and anchors.** Bring a strong, lightweight line, at least twice the length you think you will need. Tie yourself to your companions when you are near the lip, near pools, or crossing any ground that has recently cracked. Do not tie a rope to loose blocks or to the built remains. The remains are not reliable anchors, and a load on a doorframe may pull the doorframe away from whatever it was once attached to. Look for solid bedrock, or bring a fixed anchor (a sturdy pin or a removable bolt and plate, if your traveling equipment permits).

**Waterproof storage.** Everything you carry should be sealed in dry bags or hard cases. Pack food in airtight containers, and keep spare batteries, matches (in a sealed tin), medicines, and documents separately. Expect to be submerged up to the ankle, sometimes to the waist, and occasionally to the chest in a wall of rain. Anything that is not waterproof will be ruined, and anything that is will be heavier than you expect.

**Markers.** Bring bright, durable markers you can attach to things. Brightly colored cloth strips tied to fixed points work better than chalk or paint, which the water erases. Use these sparingly. The stone reclaims things, and your markers may be buried or swept away. Treat them as temporary guidance, not as proof of where you have been.

**Food and water.** Bring everything you will eat and drink. Do not drink from the pools or the springs. The water here is not sterile, and it may not be inert. The carved basin tastes faintly of salt and iron, and you should not drink it, even if you are dehydrated. The wet air will make you feel less thirsty than you are, so drink from your own supply on a regular schedule.

**Hearing protection, with caution.** The sound here is punishing. Earplugs will reduce the strain on your head and make it easier to think. However, sounds arrive before their sources, and you may depend on hearing a collapse or a rush of water before you can see it. A compromise is to wear light, removable plugs and take them out when you are moving through open ground. Agree with your companions on visual and tactile signals too, since voices are hard to pick out and the roar swallows shouted warnings.

**First aid.** Pack more than you think you need. Include wound dressings that stay adhesive when wet, a splint, pain relief, a solution for eye irritation (mist and mineral-stained water can sting), and something to treat hypothermia, since the warm wet air can mislead you about how cold you are becoming.

## What to Know Before You Walk

**Do not trust reflections.** The pools show a clear blue sky and a much larger plateau that you cannot see from where you stand. Reflections also run slightly ahead of your movements. Never use a pool to judge your position, depth, or the safety of the ground. Do not look into a pool to see whether something is under the surface, because what you see may belong to a different sky. Keep your gaze on the stone you are about to step on, not on the water beside it.

**Do not trust stairs.** The seven-step stair is the most famous hazard on the Mesa, and it is not dangerous because it is trapped. It is dangerous because it has no fixed relationship to direction. Climb it, and you may arrive at the top, or at the bottom, facing the same pool with the stair now running down. If you must use a stair, count every step aloud and mark where you started. Assume you may end up somewhere you did not intend, and do not climb past the third step without your companions in sight. Better still, do not climb at all unless you have a clear reason and a clear way back.

**Do not trust doorways.** The carved doorway on the rise opens onto the same mist, but at different depths. Stepping through one is like stepping into another room of a house that has been built and rebuilt around you. Do not go through a doorway unless you are roped to someone who will remain on the near side and who knows how to pull you back.

**Do not lean on the stone.** Some surfaces soften briefly under pressure. A hand pressed against a wall may sink into it, and the wall may not release you immediately. Do not brace yourself against stone with your full weight. Test surfaces with the back of your hand or a tool before you rely on them, and keep a free hand available to push yourself off.

**Respect the lip.** The edges of the plateau have no clear margin, and gravity becomes unreliable near them. Objects dropped over the edge may drift upward into the mist and disappear. Do not approach the lip without a rope tied to a fixed point and a companion holding the other end. Do not reach over the edge, even to retrieve something. Lost items are not worth your footing. If you must work near the lip, keep your loose belongings on your body, zipped and clipped, and move slowly.

**Read the rain before it reaches you.** Rain on the Mesa comes as a sustained wall of water in slabs, visible as a moving cliff across the plateau. When you see it, it is still a considerable distance away, but it is closer than it looks, because the air distorts distance. Begin moving toward high, stable ground immediately. Know which rises and blocks are highest. Once the wall arrives, the world goes white and loud for about forty heartbeats. You will not be able to see, hear, or think clearly. Do not try to move during that time unless you are already on stable ground. Hold onto your companions, press your back against bedrock, and count. When the rain withdraws, do not rush. The stone will be slick and the mist will be thick, and the terrain may have changed.

**Watch the settling stone.** Blocks shift when the water pulses beneath them. You may see the movement as a slight tilt or a sudden gap. Do not stand on a slab that is moving, and do not stand on the edge of a slab that is settling against another. Most injuries here come from stone that was stable a few moments earlier and is not stable now. If you hear the heavy knocking of stone against stone, step back and look for solid rock.

**Expect cracks.** New fractures open across the surface with a sound like tearing cloth. Steam rises from them. Keep clear of any fresh crack, especially one that is still widening. A crack that is steaming is likely to be carrying hot water, and it may erupt or collapse.

**Stay out of the water.** The pools tremble with a slow circulation, the springs burst from the rock without warning, and the sheets of water sheeting across the smooth stone can reverse direction. Do not step into any pool or sheet of moving water. Do not wade. Even ankle-deep water can knock you down, and the current is not predictable. If you fall in, get out by the nearest solid stone, not by the way you came, since the way you came may have shifted.

**Leave the growths alone.** The grey filaments cling to wet stone and move in currents, even in air. The pale flat growths on deep pools keep their shape while the water beneath them boils. Do not touch them. Their behavior in your hand is not known, and the mesa may react to disturbance.

**Do not disturb the animals.** You may see movement in the pools, rings spreading too fast across the water, or a bird-shaped silhouette in the mist with wings that never beat. You may hear something large turning over below the lip. Do not follow these things, do not try to attract them, and do not stand near water where something is moving. If you see one, move away from it, quietly and steadily.

**Do not take from the remains.** The staircases, doorways, and basins are all unfinished, and they are being reclaimed by the stone. Do not climb unfinished structures, do not remove stones or carvings, and do not rub the worn grooves on the basin rim. The rim is worn by hands that have been there for a very long time, and your hand is not a meaningful addition. If you leave a marker, leave it on bedrock, not on a built remnant.

## Navigating the Mesa

**Trust your own memory over the landscape.** The path you walked a moment ago may now be a crevice full of white water. Use your fixed landmarks and your markers, but accept that they may not be where you left them. Every few minutes, stop and check your orientation against your return point. If you cannot find the return point, stop. Do not keep walking in the hope that you will recognize it. Sit down on solid stone, with your companions, and reorient calmly.

**Move in short stages.** Pick a visible point of solid stone, walk to it, and then assess. Do not commit to long traverses across ground you cannot see clearly. Light the ground in front of you before each step, and look for water on the surface, wetness on the stone, or fine cracks that suggest movement.

**Count your steps.** When you are unsure of your position, count your steps from the last landmark. This is not a precise method, but it gives you a rough sense of distance, and it is more reliable than your feeling of how far you have come. Compare it against your timekeeper and your sense of the rain.

**Do not climb to see better.** The higher you go, the closer you are to the lip, and the more the mist conceals the ground. Higher points give you a view of the far country, but that view is inverted and disorienting, with its own plateaus hanging downward from a grey sky. Looking at it for long makes you lose your sense of direction. Survey the Mesa from a low, stable position, and keep your attention on what is near.

## Your Body and Mind

**Watch for hypothermia and heat.** The warm wet air hides how much heat you are losing. You may feel comfortable while your core cools. Check your fingers, lips, and coordination regularly. Shivering that begins and stops, slurred speech, clumsiness, or confusion are signs to withdraw immediately. Overheating is also possible, especially in the mist, so watch for flushed skin, headache, and nausea.

**Watch your breathing.** The air is thick with suspended water. Breathe slowly and deliberately, and take regular breaks. If you feel congested, wheezy, or short of breath, withdraw to drier ground. A light cloth over the mouth may help some people, though it will soak through quickly.

**Guard your attention.** The roar of the Mesa is dense and constant. After a while, it begins to feel like speech, addressing something you cannot understand. This feeling is powerful and seductive, and it can pull you into listening, standing still, and losing track of time. Notice when this happens. If you find yourself listening to the roar, stop, speak your name aloud, check your timer, and make a decision about leaving.

**Do not stay for the sake of seeing more.** The temptation is strong. Everything is in the middle of becoming, and each new view is stranger than the last. But the Mesa does not reward patience with understanding. It rewards caution with time, and time here is limited by the fact that nothing stays where you left it. Decide your limit before you arrive, and keep to it.

**Expect disorientation.** Some visitors feel dizzy, disconnected, or as if the ground is moving beneath them even when it is not. This is partly the sound, partly the reflections, and partly the unstable gravity near the edges. Keep your feet on solid stone, hold on to something fixed when you can, and close your eyes briefly if you need to. You may recover once you are farther from the water.

## Emergencies

**If you fall into water.** Stay calm. Grab the nearest solid edge, and push toward the nearest stable stone, not toward where you think the bank should be. Call for help while you move, using a signal your companions will recognize. Once you are out, check yourself for cold, injury, and lost equipment.

**If someone is pulled toward the lip.** Do not let go of your rope, and do not reach over the edge unless your footing is secure and someone is holding you. Pull the person back toward solid stone, keeping the line taut, and remember that objects near the edge may drift upward. If a person has gone over, do not follow without a plan. Report it, mark the location, and leave the edge.

**If the rain is coming and you cannot reach high ground.** Put your back to the strongest bedrock you can find, interlock arms with your companions, and hold on. Keep your face low and your breathing shallow. Count the forty heartbeats aloud, or silently if you cannot speak. When the rain withdraws, do not stand up too quickly. Check each other, and check the stone under your feet before you move.

**If you lose your way.** Stop where you are. Do not keep walking. Sit down on the most stable stone you can find, pull your companions close, and take stock. Check your light sources, your water, your timer, and your markers. Try to recall your last landmark and your path from it. If you have a means of signaling home, use it now, while you are still calm and visible. If you cannot find your way back, wait. The Mesa changes, and sometimes the way back reappears on its own, but do not wait longer than you can afford to.

---

## Leaving

**Leave on time, not on impulse.** When your timer sounds, stop what you are doing and begin your return. Do not finish a sentence, a measurement, or a thought. The longer you stay, the harder it is to leave, and the Mesa is good at making one more step feel necessary.

**Return by your own path.** Go back the way you came, using your landmarks and your memory. Expect the route to be different. If the path has changed, do not improvise a new one through unknown ground. Return to the most stable, recognizable point you can reach, and rest there before deciding what to do next.

**Check your party.** Before you step through, count your companions, count your equipment, and check everyone for injuries. Do not step through with anyone missing, and do not leave anyone behind because they are slow or frightened. If you must go back for someone, decide in advance how long you will search and when you will stop.

**Let the place go.** The Mesa will not remember you, and it is not meant to. Do not try to leave a permanent mark, do not try to take anything with you, and do not promise yourself that you will return to finish what you started. The Mesa is always in the middle of becoming, and what you saw today will not exist in the same form tomorrow.

---

## A Last Note

The Drowned Mesa of Vell Ossary is beautiful in the way a storm is beautiful, or a cliff edge, or a deep and dangerous water. It is not hostile, but it is not safe, and it does not care whether you survive. The travelers who do well here are the ones who arrive with humility, a clear plan, and a willingness to leave when the plan says so. Bring your light, your rope, your companions, and your sense of your own limits. Listen to the roar if you must, but do not let it tell you what to do.
