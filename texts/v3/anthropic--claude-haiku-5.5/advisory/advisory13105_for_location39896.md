# Preparing for a Visit to the Flats City

This is a low, wet, quiet world. Most of the danger is slow and environmental rather than sudden, but it accumulates. Prepare for heat and damp, for footing that is always uncertain, and for a place that has no interest in you and is full of things that are not hostile but are very numerous. The city is not a ruin to be explored and left. It is a working system that you are visiting.

## Before You Go: Understanding the Place

**There is nothing to forage.** Plant life left long ago. You will find no fruit, no greens, no fungus, and no wood. The mineral ground and the brine-fed water cannot be made safe to eat without treatment. Bring all your food and all your fuel. Assume you cannot cook with anything you find.

**The water is everywhere, and it is not clean.** The brown surface water is brackish and carries brine, silt, metals from the old plant, and whatever the fauna leave in it. The black seep in the cracks is worse. Don't drink from any standing water, don't wash in it, and don't let it touch open cuts. Bring enough potable water for your full stay plus a margin, and a filtration system rated for dissolved salts and heavy metals, not only for bacteria. Boiling does not remove the brine or the metals.

**The air is warm and saturated.** Expect heat stress to build faster than you think, because sweat does not evaporate well in this humidity. Your body will run hot and salt-depleted. Bring electrolyte supplements, more than you think you need. Drink on a schedule rather than by thirst. Rest in shade when you can find it, which is rare, so plan your movement for the cooler hours if the cloud thins enough to show a temperature gradient.

**The light gives you no depth cues.** Shadows are faint and come from every direction. Distances are hard to judge, and the haze makes far objects look near and near ones look far. Wear high-visibility colours, carry a compass that works in your home world's field, and do not rely on the sun to tell you the time or direction, since you may not see it at all.

## What to Bring

**Footwear.** This is the most important item. Concrete and clinker are slick with film and crusted with brine salt, which gets into seams and degrades leather. Wear waterproof boots with deep lugs and a firm ankle. Bring a second pair to rotate. Carry spare socks and dry them whenever you stop. Foot infections are the most common ailment in wet environments like this, and they become serious quickly.

**Clothing.** Light, long-sleeved, fast-drying synthetic layers, not cotton. Cover your arms and legs, because the film, brine spray, and fauna all make contact with bare skin unpleasant. A wide-brimmed hat helps with haze glare and rain. Bring a waterproof outer shell that breathes poorly but protects you from storm rain and spray. Gloves are useful for handling anything on the causeways, but you will want to remove them for fine work, so carry a thin pair as well.

**Water and food.** Plan on at least four litres of drinking water per person per day, filtered and treated, plus a reserve. Pack calorie-dense food that does not spoil in humidity. Dried and vacuum-sealed foods are best. Assume you will sweat through salt faster than you can replace it.

**Filtration and treatment.** A multi-stage filter (particulate, then activated carbon, then a membrane rated for salt and metals), plus chemical tablets as backup. Test your water with a conductivity meter if you have one. Bring a means of storing treated water in sealed containers, since humidity will promote growth in open ones.

**Shelter and rest.** The old buildings are unsafe to enter because the lower floors are partly flooded, the upper floors are stripped to rebar, and the basements are packed with animals. You will need a self-contained shelter that sits above the waterline and can be sealed against crabs and damp. Pitch it on the highest ground you can find, preferably on a concrete quay that has not been recently overtopped. Check the tide schedule before you set up.

**Light.** Bring headlamps and a spare, with red-filter modes to preserve night vision and to avoid startling the birds. The city is not lit, and the cloud makes nights very dark. Carry at least two independent light sources.

**Communication.** Voices do not carry. Bring whistles, signal flags, or a radio that works at short range over water. Establish hand signals with anyone in your group before you separate, and agree on a rally point in case you lose each other in the haze.

**Navigation.** Map the causeways before you start, but expect that the maps are out of date. Carry a GPS if it works here, plus a paper map with grid references and a compass. Mark your route with biodegradable tape or light cord that will not snag on the wreckage. Do not rely on landmarks, because the towers are similar and the haze hides them.

**Medical kit.** Antiseptic and wound care for cuts from glazed clinker and rusted metal. Antibiotic ointment. Tetanus and any other boosters current before you go. Antifungals for skin and foot problems. Anti-diarrhoeals and rehydration salts. Antihistamines and an epinephrine auto-injector if you have any sensitivity to insect or amphibian toxins, though you should assume the fauna may carry skin irritants even if they are not dangerous. Bring a tourniquet and pressure dressings. Evacuation may be slow.

**Tools.** A machete or heavy knife for clearing debris from causeways, a flat pry bar for lifting crust plates, a probe or long pole for testing the depth of standing water before you step, and a repair kit for boots and gear.

## The Water and the Tide

The water rises through the drainage channels every few hours and recedes again. This is the most important rhythm in the city, and you should learn it before moving anywhere low. Set up a tide log on the first day. Note the times of high and low water, and watch how far the water rises relative to the causeway edges.

Do not be on low ground when the tide is coming in. The fauna moves to higher ground with the water, and so will you have to. Plan your route so that you are on causeways above the expected high-water line by the time it arrives.

Standing water can hide holes, sudden drops, and the openings of old drains. Use your probe before every step in unfamiliar areas. Where the crust has cracked, do not test it with your weight. Go around.

Brine rises through the crust in pale blooms. These look like white plates on the ground. When they are fresh, they are soft and may give way. When they have dried, they curl and crack, and the edges are sharp. Walk on the stable parts. Do not kneel on them.

## The Machinery

The central plant hums under the water, and its vibration will reach you long before you see it. Treat it as live equipment. Do not enter it, and do not touch any pipe, cable, housing, or turbine, even when it looks dead. Pipes come up at angles and some still leak, so the pressure inside them is not predictable. Keep at least three metres from any pipe that is venting or dripping.

The turbines on the concrete quay turn slowly in a current nobody has stopped. They are not dangerous at a distance, but they are heavy, and the water around their housings runs fast. Do not stand in the current, and do not try to block or reroute it.

The cooling stacks breathe in cycles. The vapour they release is hot and may carry corrosive compounds. Stay upwind of them, and do not linger beneath them. Check the wind direction before you set up near any stack, because the vapour is bent sideways and may drift across your camp.

The conveyor shivers when the water rises through it. It can slide or shift without warning as the housing fills and drains. Do not stand on it or beneath it.

Gantries stand over empty lots. Their members corrode and can fail. Keep clear of their shadows when the wind rises, and do not climb them.

## The Animals

There are a great many of them, and most will not harm you. Still, you must respect their numbers and their density, because a crowd of hundreds of crabs or thousands of amphibians is a very different thing from one.

**Crabs.** The dinner-plate crabs cover the lower causeways in shifting carpets. They are slow to startle but will crowd you if you stand still. Their shells click on concrete, and the sound is constant. Do not step on them, because you will slip, and their shells can cut through thin boots. Do not pick them up. Move slowly, lift your feet rather than sliding them, and wait for a gap. In the tide, they move to higher ground, which means the causeways you planned to use may be impassable for an hour or more. Watch the crabs. When they are moving, the water is rising.

**Amphibians.** The pale, flattened amphibians sit in drainage channels by the thousands. Their throats pulse in unison when the water pressure changes, which is a useful signal: a sudden synchronized pulse means the pressure has shifted and water is about to rise or fall. Do not touch them. Their skin may irritate or poison people who handle them barehanded, and some may release compounds when disturbed. Keep your hands out of the channels and keep your children and animals away from them entirely.

**Birds.** The rust-coloured long-legged birds stalk the shallows and lift in flocks when disturbed. A flock can lift and resettle a hundred metres away in seconds, which can blind you for a moment and cover the sky with motion. Do not chase them. Their flocks are a warning: a sudden lift often means something is moving through the water nearby, or that the tide is shifting. Pay attention when a flock lifts.

**The large grey animal under the waterline.** You will see its back break the surface in a slow arc, then sink. It is the largest animal here that you are likely to encounter, and you should assume it is capable of harm, even if it has never shown aggression. Keep at least ten metres from the water's edge where it moves, and do not swim or wade anywhere near it. Do not approach a surfacing animal. If one is near your shelter, move your shelter.

**Density.** Where old buildings have collapsed into deep basements, the animals pack in tight and their sound is a constant soft shifting. Do not enter those basements. Their density creates an unpredictable crowd that can move in a rush. Stay out of their range, and do not drop anything into a basement or an open drain, since this will draw attention and disturb the nearest mass.

Keep food sealed and away from the animals. Scent carries in the damp air, and crabs and birds will come for anything they can reach. Do not leave scraps, packaging, or waste on the causeways. Burn or carry out all refuse, and do not bury it, since the silt will release it back into the water.

## Storms

Storms arrive from the south in long grey banks. You may see them coming for an hour, or you may see very little warning as the haze thickens. When a storm is coming, the fauna drops into the water and waits, which means the causeways empty quickly and the water rises. Plan to be on high ground and sheltered before the rain arrives.

When the rain comes, it runs down the glazed walls in sheets and fills the gutters in minutes. The drainage channels overflow. Do not stand in any gutter, channel, or drain during a storm, even if the water looks shallow. Stay on the highest ground, in your sealed shelter, and wait it out.

After a storm, the city is clean and dripping. The animals come back out in a rush of movement, and the causeways will be full of them again. Wait until the first surge passes before you move. The air will be cooler for a short time, and this is the best window for travel, but do not mistake it for safety.

## Behaviour and Mindset

The city is settled. It has reached an accommodation with the water and with the animals, and it has no need of you. You are a temporary guest in a system that has been running for a very long time without you. Act accordingly.

Move slowly. Your footsteps make a dull slap on wet concrete, and that is almost the only sound you will hear besides the pumps and the crabs. Do not shout. Voices do not carry, and shouting will only make you hot and tired. Use your signals.

Do not try to fix anything. The machines run, or they run enough. If something is failing, it is not your job to save it, and your intervention could make things worse. Report anything unusual to your group, and to whoever maintains your means of return, but do not touch the plant.

Expect to be quiet. The quiet is heavy, and it can wear on people. Plan rest periods, and make sure someone in your group checks on each person regularly. Heat, damp, and isolation together can cause confusion and poor judgement, and these conditions are easy to miss in yourself.

Do not take anything from the city. The silt, the crust, the clinker, and the old fittings all belong to the place. Removing them disturbs the accommodation the city has reached. If you must take samples for study, take only what you need, record where they came from, and seal them for safe transport.

## Leaving

Plan your departure before you arrive. Know your extraction point, the time it will be available, and the route to it. Check the tide and the storm patterns for the day you plan to leave. Allow extra time, since the causeways may be blocked by crabs or water, and the haze will slow you.

When you leave, do not look back for long. The city will not notice you go. The machines will keep turning, the creatures will keep gathering, and the water will keep moving through the empty streets. That is the whole of what this place is, and it will be there after you have gone.
