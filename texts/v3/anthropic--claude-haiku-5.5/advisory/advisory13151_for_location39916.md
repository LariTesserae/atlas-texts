# Preparing to Visit the Weeping Lowlands

Most people who travel to the Lowlands are unprepared for how completely the place ignores what they expect. You will not be fighting it, and it will not be hostile. You will simply be in a world that moves at a different tempo and measures distance and time by rules you haven't learned. The best preparation is to slow down before you arrive, lower your expectations of what your senses will tell you, and accept that you will be wet for the entire visit.

## Before You Go

**Attune your body to slowness.** Your nervous system will want to rush. The land rewards patience and punishes hurry, because hurry means stepping onto a moss mat before it has finished settling or wading toward a pool that looks shallow. Spend a few days before departure practicing stillness. Sit outside in the rain if you can find it. Notice how long your attention holds before it wanders. You will need a long attention span here.

**Accept the moisture.** Nothing will stay dry. Your skin will stay cool and damp, your clothes will never fully dry, and your breath will hang in front of you. Bring two sets of clothing in fabrics that hold warmth when wet, such as wool or certain synthetic blends, and avoid cotton, which will cling and chill you. Pack waterproof bags for everything you care about, and double-bag anything you cannot replace.

**Bring your own light, but don't trust it alone.** The light rises from below as much as it falls from above, and shadows fall in directions the sun doesn't justify. A lamp will show you the ground but may confuse your sense of where things are. Carry a light that you can aim at the water, not just the path, and carry a second light you can keep fixed on your companions. Teal fungal glow will be your ambient light. Learn its brightness so you can tell when it's dimming, because dimming usually means a change in the water's mood.

## Footwear and Movement

Wear boots that are fully waterproof and have grip on wet stone, but that you can kick off quickly if you go into deep water. Bring a long pole, at least two meters, to test depth. Many pools have no bottom, and a probe will tell you more than your eyes.

Learn to move on the hummocks, not the moss carpets. The moss springs back, and when you step off it, the spring can throw you. Step on the rounded tops of the basalt ridges where the stone is sure. When you must cross moss, step lightly, distribute your weight, and let it settle before you commit.

Use tethers. Link yourself to your companions with lines long enough to give freedom but short enough to pull someone back. A lost person in this land may not be lost in the usual sense, but they will be very hard to find.

## Navigation

Distance is the first thing that will fail you. A ridge you can see clearly may take an hour to reach, while a pool three steps away may be the edge of a drop. Do not navigate by sight alone.

Count steps and note the time between landmarks, and check your count against the feel of the ground. Carry a compass, but expect it to drift slowly, and check it against the reeds' lean, which tends to point toward the central channels. Keep a written map that you update as you go, with notes on sounds and smells in each area, since these will be more reliable than what you see.

Mark your route with cord, not paint or cairns. The channels rearrange over seasons, and anything you build may be absorbed or moved. Cord can be pulled up and reused, and it leaves the land as you found it.

## Sound

Sound is your best guide and your greatest hazard. You will often hear things before you see them, and sometimes you will feel them land on your skin before you hear their source. Learn the difference between the drips, which ring and sustain, and the pool-falls, which sound like breath through a flute. A change in the pitch of the drips can warn you that a rock is about to stop weeping.

Keep track of the hum in the substrate. It rises and falls with the reeds' breathing, and when it swells, the reeds are gathering toward a shift in the weather. Learn the rhythm before you venture far.

Wear earplugs if the rains are coming. When the hollow stones fill, the chord spreads for kilometers and can disorient you, making it hard to tell where a sound comes from or how far away it is.

## Time

Your clock will be wrong, or at least it will not describe what you experience. The light is always late in the day. The reeds' long rhythm of growth and withdrawal may be a better guide to the passage of time than the sun, which you may not see at all. Keep a record of the reeds' cycles. If you return to camp and the reeds have closed, you have been gone longer than you think.

Plan your visit in short segments, with fixed rest points. Do not plan on a day's hike. Plan on a few hours of walking, followed by a long rest, then a return. Fatigue here arrives unevenly and can be hard to notice until you are deep in a low place.

## Reflections and Weather

The water holds reflections of things that are not there, and those reflections move with their own weather. Do not stare into the pools for long. Reflections of companions can drift out of step with the people themselves, and a long gaze can make you lose track of which figure is you.

Read the standing waves on the moss mats. They map the subsurface currents, and their patterns predict rain with surprising accuracy. Learning to read them will give you advance warning of the rains that fill the stones and turn the plain into a chord. Find high ground before the chord starts.

## Fauna

Few animals will engage you. The long-legged skater leaves trails of light and is harmless. The flat-backed thing surfaces once a day and sinks. Do not follow it into deep water. The bird-like shape that circles at dusk never lands and should not be signaled. Do not call to it, wave at it, or try to attract its attention. Simply watch, and note its course, which may be a clock of sorts.

Do not hunt, collect, or disturb the lichens on the weeping rocks. The lichens grow where the water decides to pause, and disturbing them may disturb the water's pauses too.

## The Watching

Many visitors report feeling observed. The land does not appear hostile, but it is interested in how you move, the way a river is interested in a stone it means to wear down. You will not be able to prove it is watching, and you should not try. The feeling is part of the place.

Do not try to wear the land down, pull at its channels, or make it bend to you. Walk as you would through a home you've been invited into. Speak quietly. Leave no marks but cord, and take that with you.

Keep a journal, and write in it every hour. Describe what you see and hear, and note how your sense of direction and time changes. The journal will anchor you to your home reality when the soft rules blur, and it will help you recognize when you have drifted.

## Emergencies

If the rocks stop weeping for a day, every nearby pool will drop a few millimeters at once, with a sound like a sigh. This is not an emergency, but it is a signal. Move away from low ground and toward the ridges. Your pools will be shallower for a while, and some channels may be exposed.

If you lose your companions, do not call out loudly. Sound carries strangely here and can arrive before its source, making it hard to find a person by voice. Stay where you are, make a regular tapping on stone that your companions will recognize, and wait. Lost people here are usually found by sound, not sight.

If you feel the terrain has become unfamiliar in a way that frightens you, sit down on a basalt hump, put your hands on the stone, and feel the seepage. Breathe slowly until the rhythm of the reeds reaches you. Then retrace your steps by the cord.

## Checklist

- Waterproof clothing in two sets, and waterproof bags for essentials
- Boots with grip, plus a long probe pole
- Two lights: one for the water, one fixed on your companions
- Cord for route-marking, and a compass
- Earplugs for the rains
- A journal and a pencil that writes on wet paper
- Tethers linking every member of your party
- A plan for short segments, with fixed rest points
- Patience, practiced beforehand
- The understanding that you will be wet, slow, and watched, and that this is the nature of the place

Go gently. The Lowlands have been sinking for a very long time, and they will be sinking long after you leave.
