# Preparing for the Sponge Plateau

Most travelers who go to the Sponge Plateau go badly, and the reason is rarely the danger they expected. They pack for mountains, expecting cold, altitude, and rock. What they get is water, softness, and a landscape that does not hold still. The plateau does not kill people quickly. It wears them down, misleads them, and then they make one small wrong decision on wet ground. Prepare for the slow kind of hazard.

## Before you go: the mindset

Accept three facts before anything else.

**Your map is a snapshot.** The land reorganizes itself on cycles you cannot predict. A route you memorize will be wrong within a season, and possibly within a week. Plan to navigate by principles and landmarks that change slowly, not by paths.

**Distance is unreliable.** You will misjudge how far things are, consistently, in ways that do not average out. Ridges look close and are not. Sounds arrive out of order. Budget time, not distance: assume any destination is at least half again as far as it looks, and carry more food and water than a trip of that apparent length would justify.

**The place is not hostile in the usual sense.** It has no malice, and most of what lives here wants nothing from you. Your job is to not be a sudden, unexpected weight on something that is always in the middle of shifting. Respect the ground and you will mostly be fine.

## Clothing and gear

**Waterproofing matters more than insulation.** The air is cold and saturated, and the mat is wet to the bone. Wet cold kills faster than dry cold. Wear a waterproof outer layer that breathes poorly but keeps water out, and layer underneath with wool or synthetic fabrics that retain warmth when damp. Cotton is a mistake. It holds water against your skin and will chill you within an hour of the first seep.

**Footwear is the most important decision you will make.** Use tall, sealed boots with a sole that can grip soft, slick growth, not hard rock. Gaiters are essential; without them the mat fills your boots by the end of the first day. Carry a second pair of dry socks in a sealed bag and change them whenever your feet go cold. Trench foot arrives quietly on this plateau. Check your feet every few hours, and do not wait for pain.

**Pack for a long wet fortnight, not a dry week.** Expect everything you own to be damp. Store electronics, food, medicine, and fire-starting materials in multiple sealed layers, and keep one fully dry kit you do not touch except in emergencies. A small reserve of silica gel or a sealed container of dry tinder is worth more here than extra clothes.

**Eye and respiratory protection.** Spore storms arrive without much warning, turning the light sickly green and filling the air with fine drifting particles. Carry a half-face respirator with particulate filters and a pair of sealed goggles. Wear them when the light goes green. Do not wait to see whether the storm is a bad one. You will not be able to tell from inside it.

**Hearing protection, optional but advised.** The turbines' hum can carry through the stone for kilometres and becomes uncomfortable within a day. Light earplugs help you sleep. Keep your ears open enough to hear the warning silences described below, so do not use anything that blocks sound entirely.

## Navigation

Bring a compass but do not rely on it. The machines and the rising and falling of the ground can produce local deviations, and the faint northern light sometimes seems to pull the needle slightly. A compass is useful for general direction over short ranges. It is not a substitute for judgment.

Bring a good altimeter or barometer. The swells and hollows give you elevation information that helps with orientation, and the cloud ceiling changes with pressure in ways that can warn you of coming rain or spore fronts.

Carry a physical notebook and pencil, not only a device. Record your route, the time between landmarks, and any changes you observe. Your notes from the morning may be the most useful thing you have by afternoon.

Find your landmarks by sound and structure rather than sight. The turbines, the refinery stacks, and the dead power towers are useful because they are large and slow to change, though even they can seem to shift their facing when you are not looking. Use several at once and triangulate. Never trust one.

**Mark your route, but mark it carefully.** The mat grows fast and will swallow unmarked stakes, cairns, and ribbons within a season. Use metal stakes driven into bedrock where you can reach it, and set them deeper than you think necessary. Tie bright markers high on fern trunks or dead pilings, where the growth is slower. Keep a record of where you placed each marker and check it regularly.

**Do not assume that a path you walked will still exist.** If you return to a place and it looks wrong, stop. Do not push ahead into unfamiliar ground to prove that you are right. Go back to your last confirmed marker and reorient from there.

## Water

You will not need to carry much water, and that is the trap. Water is everywhere. The seeps and pools run warm enough not to freeze, and the streams surface clear and cold from deep in the stone. Your body can dehydrate in cold, wet conditions without you noticing, because the air never feels dry enough to make you thirsty.

Carry a filtration system rated for biological contaminants and a supply of chemical or heat-based purifiers. Do not drink directly from a pool or stream without treating it. The water is clear, but the mat releases dissolved compounds as it decays, and some of these will sicken you slowly.

Keep a reserve of drinking water sealed in your pack. The seeps can dry up or reroute within a day, and a pool you planned to refill from may be gone when you arrive.

Also note that some water runs uphill for a few metres before resuming its course. This is harmless in itself but disorienting. If you are following a stream by sight, check it against your map and your notes, not only your eyes.

## Food and fire

Do not forage from the mat without expert guidance. Some fronds are edible, some lichens are nutritious, and some of the pale fibrous strands are both edible and digestible, but the plateau's flora changes and what was safe last season may not be safe now. Pack all your food. Bring more than you think you need, since the cold and the walking will consume calories quickly.

Fire is possible but difficult. The wood is wet, the mat is wet, and the air is saturated. Bring a reliable fire-starter that works when wet, such as ferro rods with dry tinder stored in a sealed tin, or a chemical starter with its own oxygen. Build fires on bare stone or on a platform of dead wood raised well above the mat. A fire on the mat will sink into it, smoulder, and may start a slow burn that spreads through the hummocks.

Cook in the morning and evening when you can, and keep a cold meal available for midday.

## Shelter and sleep

**Do not pitch a tent directly on the mat.** The surface looks solid and is not. Hummocks can swell overnight into domes, split along hidden fault lines, and slide into hollows. Several travelers have woken to find their shelter sinking, tilted, or dropped several metres into a valley. Sleep only on platforms you have built above the mat, anchored to rock or to a sound dead trunk, or on bare exposed stone where it is safe to do so.

The dead power towers and the refinery structures are tempting shelters, but do not sleep beneath anything that might fall or that is still moving under its weight. Iron here responds to the living ground, and the valves and pistons may shift in ways that become dangerous under load.

**Keep your shelter sealed and dry.** Use a bivouac bag or a double-walled tent with a waterproof floor. Expect condensation to pool inside even a good shelter and plan to wipe it out in the morning.

**Set a watch.** The mat makes noise when it moves, and the warning sounds are not always obvious. A sudden splitting crack, a rush of water, or a deep wet sliding sound at night means the ground is shifting near you. If you hear one, get up and move to a safer position before you wake fully.

## Wildlife and the warning silence

Most fauna will leave you alone. The grazers are large and heavy, and they move through the hollows slowly, but they are not aggressive unless cornered or surprised. Give them room, do not block their trails, and do not walk through the middle of a herd. Their trails fill with water behind them, which can trap a careless traveler. Use the higher ground when you can.

The winged things that dip above the seeps are harmless to most people, but do not touch them or let them settle on your gear. When they land on the mat they sink, and they may bring the mat up with them if they are disturbed.

Predators are rarely seen. What you will notice is the silence. When a patch of the plateau that was full of sound goes quiet all at once, a predator is nearby or something has just died. Stop. Do not move toward the silence. Move slowly away and to higher ground, and keep your light and sound low until the noise returns.

The burrowers and the deep thumping are not a threat in themselves, but do not stamp, drive stakes, or dig heavily in one place for long. The vibration can wake whatever is below.

## The light, the machines, and the loose edges

Do not chase the northern light. It brightens and fades in a slow rhythm, and people who walk toward it tend to lose hours and sometimes days. Note its position, keep your back to it when you navigate, and do not let it draw you off your route. Local residents do not remark on it. You should not either.

Keep your distance from the old machines, especially the turbines and the valves on the refinery. Do not stand directly in line with a turbine's facing, and do not leave metal objects resting against pipes or pistons. Some of the iron responds to pressure changes in the mat, and a hiss or a sudden lift can injure someone standing too close. Move past them in the lee of the hollows when the wind is high.

The loose reality of the plateau is real and you should plan for it without panic. Distances will stretch. Sounds will arrive out of sequence. Shadows will lag and lean a second behind their objects. Your sense of which way is up in a running stream may fail. None of this is an immediate threat, but it erodes confidence and clear judgment over time. Keep a fixed routine: check your position every hour against two landmarks, check your companions' faces against your memory of them, and write down the time at each check.

If you find yourself certain that you are going the wrong way, or that a companion has changed in some subtle way, or that a landmark has moved, stop and write it down before you act. Your certainty is the first thing the place will bend.

## Companions and contact

Do not go alone. A companion is useful for practical reasons, and also because they can confirm what you are seeing when your own perception drifts. Choose someone you trust to tell you honestly when something looks wrong.

Agree on a check-in schedule with someone beyond the plateau, and a plan for what happens if you miss it. Give them your route, your markers, your expected return point, and the coordinates of your last known shelter. If you do not return, they should know whether to wait, search, or assume the worst.

Establish a return anchor: a fixed point you can always find, such as a stretch of bare rock or a particular dead piling, that you agree to make for if you lose the group or the route. Pick two, one close and one farther out, and make sure everyone knows both.

## Leaving

When you leave, do not simply walk away from the plateau. Walk out the way you came in, checking your markers, and note that the place will feel strangely clear and sharp for the first hour past its edge, as if the air itself has been brightened. Do not be fooled. Take a full day to rest somewhere dry before you attempt any further travel, and let your sense of distance and direction settle back to normal before you trust them again.

Keep your notes. Write up your route, your observations, and any changes you noticed, and file them where other travelers can find them. The plateau is always becoming something else, and the best guide to it is a record kept by people who went before and were paying attention.

---

**In short:** bring waterproofing and sealed dry storage, trust landmarks and notes over your eyes and your compass, budget extra time and food, never sleep on the mat, keep clear of the iron and the northern light, treat sudden silence as a warning, and go with someone who can tell you when you are wrong. The Sponge Plateau is patient with people who are careful, and merciless with people who assume it is still the place they saw yesterday.
