# Preparing to Visit the Sunken Works

## Before You Go

Learn the rule of the place and treat it as the first item on your checklist: **the water must be kept down.** Everything else in the basin serves that rule. The pumps are not scenery. If you are ever unsure whether something is safe to touch, ask whether it helps the pumps run. If it does, leave it alone. If it doesn't, leave it alone anyway until someone who works the sumps tells you otherwise.

Find the pump crew before you find anything else. They keep the basin alive, and they know its moods. Ask at the pump houses on the north rim, not at the overlooks. Expect them to be brusque, practical, and better informed than any guidebook. They will tell you which causeways are safe this week, which wells to avoid, and when the breathing is due.

Check the local rhythm. The sump levels rise and fall every few hours, and the crews listen for a change in pitch before each rise. Ask them for the schedule and follow it. Do not plan a long traverse of the lower terraces during a rise.

## Clothing and Gear

**Footwear** matters more than anything else you carry. The causeways are slick with condensation, the concrete is sharp-edged, and the water runs across surfaces that are no longer level. Bring boots with deep, aggressive lug soles and a high ankle. Do not wear anything you would hate to lose, because the sumps will take it.

**Layers** should be wool or synthetic, never cotton. The air is saturated and the cold is wet, so anything that absorbs moisture will chill you within twenty minutes. Wear a waterproof outer shell with a hood that stays up in the drip from the gantries. Carry a second set of dry base layers in a sealed bag.

**Gloves** should be thin enough to feel the edges and grip enough to hold wet steel. The arrises are sharp enough to cut through a hand that touches them carelessly. Bring a spare pair. The first will be soaked by mid-morning.

**Eye protection** is worth more than you expect. Condensation fogs glasses, and the freezing needles form along railings at the height of your face. Goggles that seal against the brow are better than sunglasses, which are useless under sodium light anyway.

**Hearing protection** is a judgment call. The pumping is loud enough to damage hearing over a long day, but the pumps also carry warnings. A failing impeller, a changing valve, or a sump beginning to rise can all be heard before they can be seen. Consider earplugs for the pump houses and a single open ear elsewhere. Learn the sound of a valve venting before you leave the rim, because you will hear it many times.

## Light and Vision

The basin is lit by a mix of sodium lamps and diffuse overcast. Sodium light distorts colour badly, and it creates a dense amber haze that hides depth. Bring a headlamp with an amber or red filter. A white light will blind you in the mist and wash out the water surfaces, which you need to read.

Expect your eyes to struggle in the lower terraces. The sump water reflects only in broken smears, and the dark causeways can look solid when they are not. Move slowly. When in doubt, test the surface with a trekking pole or a loose object before you put your weight on it.

Carry a waterproof map, but do not trust it. The causeways do not match the posted plans, and some have settled. Use the map for orientation along the rim and for the pump houses, which are reliable landmarks. Below the rim, navigate by the gantries and the sodium lamps, and note their numbers as you pass.

## Water

Water is the central hazard. Drink only what you carry, sealed and dated. The well water at the centre tastes of iron and something faintly sweet, and it stains the hands for days. Do not drink it, do not wash your face in it, and do not let it touch cuts. Wash your hands only in water from the rim taps.

Learn to read the sheets. Where water runs uphill across a settled terrace, it is moving toward a low point that has not yet been drained. If you see a sheet forming across your route, stop and wait. Do not try to cross a moving sheet, even a shallow one, because the bottom of a causeway can give way under the weight of standing water.

If a causeway begins to go under, do not run toward the lowest point. Move toward the nearest gantry ladder, climb, and call the pump crew on the radio. Most people are caught because they try to retreat along the path they came by, and the water is already there.

Keep your distance from the discharge pipes. They are hot, heavy, and fast, and they carry the outflow out over the lip in thick black arteries. Do not climb them, sit on them, or lean over them.

## Warm Concrete and Growth

Some sections of concrete are warm to the touch, and the heat is not explained. Test the surface with the back of your hand before you kneel or lean. Lichen grows fastest where the concrete is warm, following the reinforcement bars in geometric patterns. Admire the patterns from a distance. Do not touch them. The grey-green crust spreads visibly, and it stains skin and clothing in ways that take weeks to fade.

The dark filamentous mats in the drainage channels thrive in the warm outflow. Do not put your hands in the channels, and do not sit near them. Keep food and water well away.

## Life You Might See

The pale, blind fish in the sumps surface briefly beside the causeways and then sink again. Do not try to catch them, and do not try to follow them. You will not succeed, and the sump will not reward you.

Birds cross high overhead but do not land. If you see a bird circling low, that is a sign that something has changed in the air or the water. Note it and tell the crew.

The small grey scavenger lives in the ducts and emerges at the edge of the lamplight to pick at the rims of the discharge pipes. It is not dangerous in the usual sense, but it carries whatever it finds. Keep food sealed, keep packs closed, and do not feed it. It will remember you if you do.

## Breathing

Every few hours, the sump levels rise and fall by a few centimetres. The pump crews call it breathing. You will feel it before you understand it: a change in the sound of the water, a slight shift in the pitch of the pumping, a pause in the drip. When you hear it, move to higher ground and wait.

Do not try to measure the breathing with instruments. The readings do not agree with the gauges on the pump houses, and the crews have stopped trusting them. Learn the change in pitch instead, and time your movements to it.

## Your Way Back

Here is the part that matters most for travelers who move between worlds. The basin does not keep records of who has walked on it. Footprints fill within the hour. Markings on the concrete weather away. Do not rely on any trail you leave to find your way out.

Decide your exit before you go down. Pick the gantry ladder you will climb if the water turns. Pick the pump house whose crew you will call. Carry a physical anchor that the basin cannot take: something you would notice missing from your own world, kept on your person, sealed from the water. Check it at every rise.

If you are ever unsure whether you are still in the same place you started, stop. Do not walk on until you know. The Sunken Works is patient, and it is not in a hurry, but it is always moving, and so should you be when it is.

## Before You Leave

Before you climb back to the rim, tell the pump crew you are leaving. Tell them where you were and when you expect to be at the surface. Leave nothing behind. Take out everything you brought, including the water you spilled and the sealed packs you did not open.

When you reach the top, stand a moment and listen. You will hear the pumps. You will hear the drip. You will hear the water finding its way back into the basin, and you will understand what the crews meant by breathing. Then walk away from the rim without looking back to see whether the causeways are holding. They always are, until they are not, and that is not your job.
