# Preparing to Visit the Braided Lowland

## What you are walking into

Most places reward preparation with comfort. This one rewards it with survival and attention. The main thing to prepare yourself for is that nothing here will hold still long enough for you to rely on it. The river moves, the banks slump, the ground under your feet may be firm now and liquid in an hour. Plan for change, not for a fixed place.

Expect to be overwhelmed for the first hour, and expect that the overwhelm will fade into a working kind of alertness. Your nervous system will adapt faster than you think if you let it. Go in with that expectation.

## Before you go

**Set your return point in advance and treat it as sacred.** Choose the exit point, the location where you will be collected or where you will step back through, and fix it by the most stable features you can find: a particular basalt outcrop, the bend of a river, the high ground on the eastern margin. Do not plan to return to the place you entered, because the channels may have rerouted by then. Agree on the return time with your companions or your anchor, and build in a generous margin. Floods are not predictable enough to schedule around, so the margin is not optional.

**Understand the time problem.** The place does not keep records, and you should assume that your sense of elapsed time will drift, especially under the constant gloom. Wear a waterproof watch with a date function, and check it deliberately at set intervals. If you are crossing between realities, record your departure timestamp on paper that you keep sealed in a dry bag. Do not trust the light to tell you what time it is. It is dusk at noon and dusk at midnight.

**Get the vaccinations and prophylaxis your home reality would recommend for tropical wetland travel**, then assume they are incomplete. Ask your medical provider about leptospirosis, hepatitis A, typhoid, and any local equivalents. Bring a course of broad-spectrum antibiotics and anti-parasitic medication, along with antifungal cream. Standing water, silt, and constant moisture make skin infections and gut parasites near-certain if you are careless.

**Practice your signals.** You will not be able to talk to your companions at normal volume. Shouting works only at close range, and the cicadas, frogs, and river will swallow everything else. Agree on a set of hand signals before you go: stop, come here, danger, water rising, I am hurt, I am fine, turn back. Add a whistle, and make sure everyone can hear it over the din. Test the whistle in the place, not at home, since it may need to be louder than you expect.

## What to bring

**Footwear** is the most important decision you will make. Get boots with deep, aggressive tread and a sturdy, high ankle, since the silt will pull at lighter shoes and may take a boot off entirely. Bring a second pair of socks in a sealed bag, and a spare pair of boots if you have room. Gaiters help keep silt and leeches out.

**Clothing** should be lightweight, long-sleeved, and quick-drying. Synthetic fabrics outperform cotton, which will stay wet and heavy. Choose muted colours. Bright clothing draws attention from the larger animals and from the insect swarms. Wear a wide-brimmed hat or a mesh head net, since the bats and insects will come at your face.

**Insect protection** matters more than almost anything else. Bring a strong repellent that you have tested on your skin for reactions, along with permethrin-treated clothing if you can obtain it. Bring a mosquito net or head net. Expect the swarms to be drawn to lights, so use a headlamp with a red filter when you can, and avoid carrying bright white light at dusk.

**Water** must be treated. The rain looks clean and is not. Carry a filter rated for bacteria, protozoa, and viruses, plus chemical tablets as a backup. Drink more than you think you need, since humidity and heat will dehydrate you without making you feel thirsty. Carry at least four litres per person per day in dry weather and plan for resupply.

**Light** is less of a problem than you might expect, because the fungi glow enough to navigate by in the understory. But you will need a headlamp, spare batteries, and a small, waterproof lantern for camp. The fungal glow is brighter during thunder, and sometimes that is the only reliable light you will have.

**Shelter and sleep** should be elevated and waterproof. Do not sleep on the ground, which will be wet, and do not sleep near the river or the braided channels. Hammocks with rain flies and insect netting, slung high on stable trunks, are better than tents. Check the trunk for uprooting risk before you sleep under it. Sleep in shifts if you are in a group, with one person awake to listen for rising water and falling trees.

**Navigation** needs redundancy. Magnetic compasses still work, but the geography will not match any map you carry. Bring a GPS device that stores coordinates from a pre-trip survey, but expect the terrain features it references to have changed. Carry a notebook, a pencil, and a waterproof sketchbook for recording bearings and landmarks as you go. Use the river's general direction and the basalt outcrops as your most reliable references, since the rock does not move, even if the sand does.

**Tools** should include a machete for clearing vines, a rope and carabiners for crossing channels and securing packs, a small shovel for digging out of mud, a first aid kit with extra attention to wound care, and a repair kit for boots and gear. Bring a wooden or fiberglass pole, about waist height, to probe mud before you step and to lever yourself up when you sink.

**Emergency equipment** includes a satellite beacon or emergency locator, a whistle, a signal mirror, and a means of making fire. Matches and lighters will be wet, so bring a waterproof kit with a ferrocerium rod and tinder, and keep it in a dry pouch on your body, not in your pack.

## Movement and safety

**Stay on high ground when the rain comes.** The downpours are sudden and total. They turn the braided channels into a single brown rush within minutes, and they can strand you on a bar that was dry an hour ago. When the sky darkens and the frogs go quiet, move uphill immediately. The pause between downpours is the time to climb, not to linger.

**Watch the banks.** Bank slumps happen without warning and can take you into the current. Keep at least two body-lengths back from any cutbank, and never stand on an overhanging edge, even to look at the river. When you hear the sound of a slump, that is your cue to move away from the water, not toward it.

**Test the ground.** Silt that looks firm can liquefy under weight. Probe with your pole before each step, and move with your weight spread, not planted. If you start to sink, do not struggle. Lie back, spread your arms, and work your legs free slowly, since panic will drive you deeper. Have someone on firmer ground ready with a rope.

**Respect the trees.** Uprooted trunks lie across the channels with their root-plates raised like shields, and they can roll or shift without warning. Do not stand under leaning trunks or at the base of a root-plate. Do not sit on a fallen log near the water, since it may be the next thing to go.

**Cross rivers carefully.** Find the widest, shallowest, slowest section, and cross facing upstream with a rope anchored on the bank. Expect the current to be stronger than it looks, since braided channels are shallow but fast. Cross early in the day, before rain. Never cross during or just after a downpour, and never cross a channel you have not watched for at least a few minutes.

**Expect to meet large animals.** You will hear them before you see them. The understory will crash and then go silent. Do not run. Move slowly, speak in a low voice where possible, and give them room. If something large crosses your path, stop, back away, and wait for it to pass. Do not approach fresh tracks or disturbed vegetation without caution.

**Be wary of the voices.** Many of the birds mimic other sounds, including, perhaps, human voices. If you hear someone calling your name from the understory, do not answer or move toward it until you have confirmed by sight that your companions are where they should be. Set a rule with your group that no one leaves the group without a signal.

## Health

Expect leeches on the wet ground and in the shallow channels. Check your legs and feet at every stop, and remove them by scraping, not pulling. Use salt or a lit match rather than force if they have attached.

Expect fungal infections, especially in the feet, groin, and armpits. Keep the skin dry where you can, change socks often, and apply antifungal powder daily. A small cut in this environment can become infected within hours, so clean every wound with treated water and cover it.

Expect insect bites, some of which will swell badly. Monitor any bite for spreading redness, heat, or fever. Carry an antihistamine and any prescribed antibiotic for skin infection.

Watch for heat exhaustion. The air is saturated, so sweat does not evaporate and cooling does not happen the way it should. Rest in shade, drink water frequently, and take salt with your fluids. Stop early if you feel dizzy, nauseous, or confused.

Watch your feet. Macerated skin, the constant wetness that softens and breaks down the skin on your feet, is one of the most common injuries in places like this. Dry them at every rest stop, and do not walk all day in wet boots if you can avoid it.

## Ethics and the traces

There are marks of people here. The flaked stones on the sandbar, arranged in a rough circle, and the split stick wedged between roots with its end scraped smooth, were made by someone who was once working in this place. You do not know when they were made, and you should not assume they are less important because they are small.

Do not take them. Do not move them, rearrange them, or add to them. Photograph them if you must, and record their positions, but leave them where they are. The land will bury them or wash them away in its own time, and that is part of what the place is.

Do not leave anything behind either. Nothing you carry in should be left in the forest, not even food scraps, which will attract animals and change the local ecology. Carry out everything, including human waste, which should be buried deep and away from water, or packed out if the ground is too wet to dig.

Do not bring seeds, soil, or organisms out of the place, and do not introduce organisms into it. Clean your boots and gear thoroughly before you leave, and inspect your clothing for hitchhikers. The place is in the middle of its own changes, and your presence should not add to them.

Above all, understand that you are a visitor to something that is not waiting for you. The forest will continue whether you come back or not. The river will rearrange the sand that you walked on. The living things will go on pressing against one another, loudly. Your task is not to master the place. It is to pass through it carefully, to leave it as you found it, and to bring back an accurate account of what it was like on the day you were there.

## A final note

Go in quietly, even though it will not be quiet. Keep your signals clear, your return point firm, and your attention on the ground under your feet and the water above your head. The place is young, restless, and generous with its noise. Treat it with the same attention you would give a living animal that could turn on you without warning, because in this place, almost everything is one.
