# Preparing to Visit the Sunken Mat

This is a place where the ground has not decided what it is, and where nothing has changed for a very long time. Most of the risks are not dramatic. They come from the heat, the glare, the soft footing, and the way the stillness wears on you. Prepare for those, and the basin is not hard to visit. Treat it as a single place that will keep your attention, and expect to come back changed in small ways.

## Before You Go

Set a turn-back rule before you step down, and keep it. Decide how far you will walk, how long you will stay, and what will make you return even if you feel fine. The basin does not signal the passage of time, so you cannot rely on the light to tell you when you have been there too long. Your own watch or a written schedule will do that job.

Tell someone on the far side exactly where you are going, including the rim point you descended from. Be specific: "the south rim, past the ring of upright stones" is useful, "the basin" is not.

Plan to go with at least one other person. Solitary visitors tend to walk farther than they meant to, and the sameness of the mat makes it hard to judge distance. A companion is also your best check against drifting into the stillness.

## What to Wear

Wear loose, light, quick-drying layers. Your clothes will soak through within an hour, and wet cloth clings and chafes in the heat. Avoid cotton that holds water. Synthetic blends dry faster and keep their shape.

Wear boots with ankle support and a firm, sealed sole. The mat gives under you, sometimes to the ankle, and the stems can roll. Choose a boot you can pull out of wet, sinking material without losing it. Laces should be double-knotted, and gaiters are worth bringing to keep the mat's fine debris out of your socks.

Wear long sleeves and trousers despite the heat. The columns are fibrous and the cushions can be sharp where they split. The raw grey substrate, where the mat is thin, has crystalline edges that will cut.

Bring a wide-brimmed hat, but understand that it will not give you shade. The light has no source you can point away from. A hat still keeps the glare off your face and hair, and it lets you keep your eyes partly covered.

## What to Carry

**Eye protection.** This matters more than anything else on the list. The light is bright, nothing casts a shadow, and the silver undersides of the leaves throw it upward. Bring dark-tinted glasses that wrap around the sides, and bring a spare pair, since the glare can crack or fog lenses in the damp. Some visitors wear a cloth band across the lower eyelids as well. Your eyes will water constantly. Keep a clean cloth for them.

**Water.** You will be wet from the air, but you will still need to drink. Bring more than you think you need, and bring a filter and purification tablets anyway. The pools in the hollows are the colour of weak tea and come from the pale membrane. Do not drink from them without treating the water thoroughly. The seep threads between the roots look clean and still carry things you do not want. Boil or filter everything.

**A probe.** A long, sturdy pole or staff, at least as tall as your shoulder, lets you test the depth of the mat before you put your weight on it. Thick mat feels firm. Thin mat over raw stone feels the same until the moment it breaks. Test every step, especially near the columns and the low walls, where the mat often hides deeper voids.

**A line and a stake.** Tie one end of a strong cord to a stake driven into firm ground at the rim, and keep the other end with you, or tied to your waist, for as far as you walk. The path you make in the mat closes behind you, and nothing you leave behind persists. Cairns, ribbons, and chalk marks will sink or be swallowed. A physical tether back to the rim is your most reliable way home, though you should not tie it to any plant or built thing.

**Timekeeping.** Bring a mechanical watch or a hand-wound clock in addition to a digital one. Digital displays are often unreliable here, and no one has yet confirmed whether they keep time in the basin. Check them against each other regularly, and write down the time whenever you pass a landmark.

**A light.** Bring a headlamp and spare batteries, and plan to use it inside the hollows. The arch-rooms are dim, and the basin's bright exterior will blind your eyes for several minutes after you come out of them. Do not rely on your eyes adjusting.

**Notebook and pencil.** Pens may skip in the damp. Pencil works. Write down your route and times, and sketch anything you find before you lose it to the mat.

**Camera, optionally.** Images may come out washed or flattened, since the light has no shadows to give things shape. Photographs of the built things are useful for records. Do not expect your photographs to show the scale or depth of the basin.

**A small first-aid kit.** Include antiseptic, wound dressings, a blister kit, and something for insect-free stings (there are very few insects, but the jelly and the segmented creatures should not be touched bare-handed). Include an antihistamine. Some visitors develop irritation from the plant sap.

**A bag for returning things.** Use it only for what you bring out, not what you take in. More on that below.

## The Light and the Heat

Expect to be overheated and damp. Drink on a schedule rather than on thirst, since the air takes away your sense of how much you are sweating. Rest every hour in one of the dry hollows, out of the worst of the glare. Keep your hat on when you rest.

Watch for heat exhaustion. The early signs are a headache that does not go away, dizziness when you stand, and a sense that the stillness is pressing on you. If any of these appear, stop, sit down on firm ground near the rim, and drink. Do not push on to see something.

The heat settles in the hollow like warm bathwater, so it is worse at the bottom of the slope. Walk along the upper edges of the basin where you can when you have a choice.

## Footing

The mat is soft, layered, and uneven. Walk slowly, keeping your weight low and your knees slightly bent. When you feel the mat rebound under you, shift your weight to the side and test again before you commit. Do not run, jump, or stamp. The vibration travels, and the whole mat may give way in a patch.

Avoid the thin places, where the grey substrate shows through. The crystalline faces will cut through boots and gloves if you fall onto them. Thin areas often look like the rest of the mat, so keep your probe working ahead of you at all times.

The seep threads run between roots and can be deeper than they look. If you step into one, do not panic and pull hard. Lean back and work your foot free slowly, then use your probe to find firmer ground.

Keep at least one hand free for balance. If you need to kneel or crawl, clear a spot with the probe first.

## Navigation and the Path Behind You

The mat closes behind you within minutes. Your trail will not be there when you turn around, so do not navigate by retracing steps.

Navigate by the rim, which holds its position, and by the far ridges, which are slow to move. Note the direction of the sky's brightness and the line of the ridges before you start, though the light is even and will not give you a clear compass point. Your tether to the stake is the most dependable guide. Keep it taut as you walk, and coil it back on the way in.

Do not count on landmarks. The built things are scattered, half-swallowed, and easy to mistake for one another. The columns look alike, and the low walls disappear into the mat at the same rate. Treat them as points of interest, not as navigation.

If you lose the tether or the rim, stop. Do not keep walking. Sit down on the firmest ground you can find, drink water, eat something, and wait for your companion to find you or for you to recover your bearings. Do not try to find your own way out by guessing.

## Sound, Silence, and the Pressure of Stillness

The basin is quiet, and the quiet is deep enough to become uncomfortable. The drips are the only regular sound. You will begin to hear your pulse, then the blood in your ears, and then a silence that feels like pressure against your skin.

This is not dangerous in itself, but it wears on people. Expect to feel uneasy, short-tempered, or dreamy. Some visitors find their thoughts slow down, or start to repeat. Talk to your companion regularly, even if the conversation is trivial. Count drips. Count your steps. Name the plants aloud. These habits keep you tethered to the present moment, which in the basin is otherwise hard to find.

Do not sleep in the basin. The stillness is restful in the way deep water is, and the mat is too soft and damp to lie on for long. If you need to rest, sit upright in a dry hollow with your companion awake.

Pay attention to the slow pressure and release you may feel or hear beneath the mat. It is like a breath taken under a blanket. Do not try to locate its source, and do not stamp or dig to find it.

## The Living Things

There are almost no animals, and you should treat the few that appear as wholly unknown.

The pale segmented creatures cross the pools and leave rings that spread and fade. Do not touch them or lift them from the water. They may not be harmful, but their secretions are unknown.

The translucent jelly on the leaves does not respond to approach. Leave it alone. Do not touch it, even to move it off your path. Do not put your hand under a leaf where one may be clinging.

If you see the tall grey thing at the edge of the mat, do not approach it, and do not follow it if it moves. Keep your distance, note its position and the time, and go back toward the rim. You will not be able to say when it left, and this is not a thing to test.

Do not take plant material home. The mat grows, and cuttings may root in your luggage, your clothing, or your home. Seed pods release dust when they split, so keep your mouth and nose covered near the columns. Do not collect samples without the proper containment.

## The Built Things

The walls, the slab, the ring of stones, and the niche with the pebbles are the marks of others. They are not recent, and they are not abandoned. They are part of the settling.

Do not move, arrange, or take anything from them. The pebbles are arranged by size in a line that leads to nothing, and disturbing that line seems to be a bad idea. The channels in the flat slab may once have carried water in a pattern. Sketch them, photograph them, and leave them alone.

Do not add to the built things. Do not leave offerings, marks, or stones. Anything you place will be taken into the mat within a few days.

## The Hollows and the Doorways

The arch-rooms and passages are cool, dim, and lined with pale membrane. Some hold still pools. Others are dry and smooth.

Enter only with a light, and only through a gap you can see the back of. Test the floor with your probe before you step in. Keep one hand on the wall for orientation, but know that the membrane is slippery and may be wet.

Look for the scratched lines. They repeat in short parallel strokes along the curve of the chamber. You may feel the urge to trace or copy them. Resist the urge to touch the surface, and do not copy the pattern onto your own skin, clothing, or notes in a way you cannot later explain. If you wish to record them, draw them in your notebook from a distance, using the light.

Do not stay long in the hollows. They are restful, and the stillness is strongest inside them. Keep your time short and your exit in sight.

## Mental Preparation

The Sunken Mat asks you to hold still. The longer you stay, the more the stillness pulls at you, and the more the basin feels as if it has always been this way and always will be. This is the most dangerous part of the visit, because it makes you want to keep walking, keep looking, and stop measuring time.

Remind yourself of your turn-back rule. Remind yourself of the time. Remind yourself that you came from a rim, and that the rim is where you left it.

Some visitors describe the pressure as a sense that the place is waiting for something, or that they are becoming part of it. This is a feeling. It is not evidence. Name it, note it, and go on with the plan.

Expect to be tired afterward, and expect your sense of time to be off for an hour or two. Your body will want to lie down. Do not, until you are back on firm ground and have had water and food.

## Returning

Follow your tether back to the stake. Coil the line as you go. When you reach the rim, stop before you step up, and take a last look at the basin. The path you made will be closed, and the ridges will be as they were.

Step up onto the raw crust carefully. The rim is firm, but the transition is sudden, and your legs may feel unsteady after the soft mat.

Before you leave the rim, brush off your boots and clothing well away from the mat. Seed dust and small fragments of stem cling to fabric. Check your bag and your pockets. Leave nothing behind, and bring nothing out that you did not bring in, except your notes, your photographs, and your memories.

Do not return to the basin on the same day. Let your body recover, and let your sense of time settle. If you find yourself wanting to go back soon, that is a sign to wait.

## After

Keep a written record of your visit while it is still fresh: times, routes, landmarks, creatures, and anything you felt. The basin does not keep its own record.

Tell your companions and the people who know where you went that you are back. Share what you saw, but be honest about what you could not describe. Many visitors find the basin hard to explain, and it is fine to say so.

Watch your body and mind for a few days. The heat and glare can leave you with headaches or photosensitivity. The stillness can leave a lingering quietness, which may fade over time. If it does not fade, or if you find yourself listening for drips, seek help from someone who has visited before.

The basin will continue to sink, and to grow, and to be in the middle of becoming. You will leave it in the same state you found it, and that is the right way to visit.
