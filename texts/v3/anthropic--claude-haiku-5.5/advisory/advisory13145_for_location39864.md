# Preparing for the High Tableland

This is a place where the ground is still young, the water is in charge, and nothing hurries. Plan for a visit that is slow, wet, and quiet, and expect the main hazards to be the terrain and your own body rather than any living thing.

## Arrival and Orientation

Expect to arrive on or near the plateau surface, not in a valley, so there is no obvious downhill to walk toward. Fog will usually hide everything beyond a few hundred meters, and distant features will look like pale gradients rather than landmarks. Before you move, fix your position relative to something stable, such as a large lava ridge or the edge of a basin, and write down how you got there.

Do not rely on a magnetic compass without checking it. Young basalt is often magnetic and can deflect a needle locally, especially near ridges and exposed slabs. A satellite fix may work intermittently, since the cloud is thick and dense canopy blocks much of the sky. Bring a physical map, a paper log, and a method of marking your route, but expect any markers to be overgrown or mineral-coated within a season. Tie flagging to rock or to the tree ferns, not to the shrubs, whose roots move across the ground.

The sun is a bright coin, but it is not a reliable guide to direction or time. Its light arrives evenly, so shadows are weak and mostly absent. Use a watch and keep it on your own clock, not the sky's.

## Climate and Kit

The air is warm, around the temperature you would expect at lower elevation, but it is saturated. You will not need insulation for warmth, but you will need to manage wetness. Everything you bring will become damp, and much of it will stay damp.

- **Clothing:** Choose light, quick-drying layers in materials that resist moisture. Avoid cotton. Expect to be soaked by drip alone, even without rain. A loose waterproof shell helps with the constant falling water from the canopy, and a hood with a brim keeps the drips off your face.
- **Footwear:** Wear waterproof boots with deep, grippy soles. The lava slabs are sharp and vesicular, and the moss gives underfoot like a mattress, so the feet can slide or sink. Bring spare socks in sealed bags.
- **Electronics:** Seal everything. Use dry bags for devices and bring silica gel for enclosures. Mineral-laden water and mist can leave residue on optics and seals. Clean lenses often.
- **Gloves:** Thin gloves protect against lava edges and tree fern spines. Keep them on when handling rock.
- **Sun protection:** The cloud diffuses light but does not stop ultraviolet. You can burn within an hour without feeling the heat. Wear a brimmed hat and reapply sunscreen after the pouring water cycles through your clothing.

## Health and Altitude

At around four thousand meters, the air is thinner. The warmth can mislead you into thinking the place is easy. Walk slowly, rest often, and drink water consistently, even though everything around you is wet. Your body loses moisture through breathing and sweat in humid air more than it seems to.

Know the signs of altitude sickness: headache, nausea, unusual fatigue, shortness of breath when resting. Descend if they persist. If you use altitude medication, consult a clinician before travel. Do not rely on shelter in the fog; hypothermia is unlikely, but heat and exhaustion can build quietly.

Watch for gas. Volcanic lakes and fissures can hold dissolved carbon dioxide, which can accumulate in still air or in low hollows. Do not sit or sleep in the lowest basins or in hollows where the fog pools and does not move. If you feel dizzy or short of breath in a low spot, climb out immediately.

## Water, Stone, and Steam

The stratified basins look calm, but their water layers drift. Do not wade into them. Warm and cool bands can change the water's density and temperature in ways you cannot see from the surface. Keep to firm ground and watch the mineral lace, which tends to form where the layers meet. Thin skins can crack under weight, and the water beneath may be deeper and colder than it appears.

The mineral terraces are beautiful and fragile. Each rim is a crust of travertine that can crack under a boot. The living green beneath the water is part of the structure, and walking across it damages the growth. Stay on the bare ground or on established paths, and never step onto a terrace rim, even when it looks solid.

Seepage through the young stone can be hot. In some fissures the water still steams from recent heat. Test any water before touching it with a gloved hand or a probe. Do not drink from standing or seeping water without filtration and treatment, since dissolved minerals and possible microbes are both present.

## Vegetation Etiquette

The plants here are slow, dense, and in some cases unusual. Do not break branches for samples or move epiphytes from the trees they cover. The rosettes hold rainwater, and tipping them out kills the small pool ecosystem they support.

Tree ferns grow in standing water on the lower slopes. Their trunks are strong, but their strap-fronds drip constantly, and their spines can cut skin. Wear gloves when you brush past them.

The broad-leaved shrubs at the basin edges need careful timing. At dusk their leaves close and release their held water in a synchronized shower lasting about a minute. Do not stand beneath them at that hour. Plan your return route so that you are clear of the basin slopes before dusk, or wait in an open area well away from the shrub groups. The rain is not dangerous in itself, but the sudden volume of water and the rush of sound are disorienting. They also soak your gear.

The pale root mats bind the young soil. Walking on them compresses the mats and can tear them. Keep to the surface of the moss where it is firm, and avoid areas where the roots are exposed.

## Fauna

Animals are sparse, and most are harmless to visitors, but a few details matter. The small translucent amphibians sit motionless on leaves and are easy to miss. Do not touch them. Their skin may be sensitive or secrete compounds, and handling stresses them. Give them room.

Insects drift in ones and twos. They are not aggressive and do not swarm, so you need little protection beyond the usual.

The large grey-winged birds fly above the cloud line. You will probably see only their shadows moving across the white. Do not try to follow them into the fog.

There are no obvious predators. Do not let the absence of threat make you careless about the terrain and the water, which are the real hazards.

## Sound and Silence

The soundscape is mostly water: drips from leaf to leaf, seepage hissing through stone, and the rare synchronized rain of the shrubs. The silence between these sounds is very deep. Speak quietly, and keep equipment noise to a minimum. Shouting or loud machinery carries far in the still air, and it breaks an atmosphere that the place seems to depend on.

Wind is almost absent. If you hear a sudden gust, it is likely a shrub releasing water or a large bird passing above.

## Pace and Time

This place does not respond to hurry. Plan your visit with generous time, and expect to move more slowly than you intend. Your sense of duration may shift. Days can feel long, and the changes you notice will be cumulative: a pool has spread its lace across a valley, a terrace has grown a few millimeters, a basin has turned over.

Keep a written journal. Record dates, positions, water temperatures where you can measure them, and changes you observe. Photographs are useful, but the fog flattens detail, so describe what you see in words as well. If you return, your notes will be the best way to compare what has changed.

Expect that some of your traces will disappear. Footprints in moss recover slowly. Markers will be overgrown or mineral-coated. Items left in water may be encrusted with travertine or covered with growth. Nothing you leave will remain as you placed it for long.

## Leaving

Do not remove stone, plant material, or water samples beyond what is essential and permitted. Pumice and basalt are part of a young surface that is still forming, and taking pieces alters the processes that are working on it.

Before you depart, check your gear for mineral residue, mold, and moisture damage. Dry everything that can dry. Repack in sealed bags. Make sure you have recorded your exit point and the time of your departure, since the fog can make it hard to confirm later what you did.

## Checklist

- Waterproof, quick-drying layers and a brimmed hat
- Waterproof boots with deep treads, spare socks in sealed bags
- Gloves for lava edges, fern spines, and probing hot water
- Sealed electronics with desiccant, lens cleaning kit
- Physical map, paper log, compass (checked for local deflection)
- Water filtration and treatment supplies
- Altitude medication if prescribed, with a plan for descent
- Sunscreen for diffuse but intense ultraviolet
- Watch on your own clock, not the sky
- Field notebook for dates, positions, and observed change

The high tableland rewards a visitor who accepts its pace. Move slowly, respect the fragile crust, stay out of the basins and off the terraces, keep clear of the shrubs at dusk, and let the silence do what it does.
