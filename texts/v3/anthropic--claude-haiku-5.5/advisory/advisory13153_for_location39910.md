# Preparing to Visit the Sunken Basin

## Arrival and Access

You will arrive on the floor, about sixty meters down, so plan for a vertical problem before anything else. The walls are raw, pale fracture faces, and the ledges and scree fans are loose. Nothing has weathered, so there are no natural handholds you can trust. Test every stone before you put weight on it. Expect small falls of rock at irregular intervals, and when the quiet suddenly breaks with a dull crack, move away from the wall under it and wait for the patter to stop.

Set your own anchor point before you descend and before you leave. Ideally, arrive with a fixed rope or a reliable way to rig one. If you arrive by a method that drops you in, know beforehand how you will climb out, because the scree gives way under a climber's feet and the basin offers no easy ascent.

## Clothing and Gear

The air is roughly greenhouse-hot and saturated, so dress for heat and wet at the same time.

- **Clothing:** Light, breathable layers that dry quickly. Avoid cotton, which holds water and chafes. Long sleeves and long trousers protect against the vines and the spongy humus, which can conceal sharp debris.
- **Footwear:** Sturdy boots with deep, grippy soles. The moss mats are slick, the concrete is slick, and the scree shifts. Bring a spare pair of socks in a sealed bag.
- **Hands:** Gloves are essential for handling rusted metal, vines, and the bloomed orange surfaces of the vessel.
- **Head:** A brimmed hat or hood helps with condensation, which drips constantly from the walls and above.
- **Eyes:** Bring glasses that resist fogging, or be prepared to clean them often.

Carry a water-resistant pack and keep electronics sealed. Condensation will find every seam.

## Light

The seam overhead provides a pale, diffuse gray glow. Most of the basin sits in shadow, and the bioluminescent fungus gives a cold blue that shows shapes but not text. Bring your own light source, preferably two, with different beam widths. Use a warm white light if you can, since the blue fungus washes out fine detail.

Let your eyes adapt for ten to fifteen minutes before you move through the deeper shadows. Do not rely on the fungus for navigation near the pools or the machinery.

The clouded green lamp on its bracket is not a light source and will not help you. Do not expect it to switch on.

## Heat, Water, and Hydration

The heat seems to rise from the rock, and the deepest part of the basin is warm enough that you should keep your hands off the stone when you can. Drink water steadily. Bring more than you think you need, since sweat evaporates slowly in this air and you will feel damp and tired without feeling thirsty.

Do not drink from the pools. They are still, dark, and covered in an iridescent film. Do not drink the condensation either, even though it looks clean and runs in purposeful lines along the concrete channels. Bring sealed water and treat anything you carry in with the same caution you would apply to the basin.

Expect little or no food from the environment. The plants are abundant, but you have no reason to believe they are safe to eat, and the dead matter dissolves into humus rather than decaying in the familiar way. Pack food that is sealed, dry, and stable in heat.

## The Pools and the Plants

The pools are the most important hazard to plan around. Some sit in old settling tanks with cracked, spilling rims. Others fill the bowls of collapsed foundations. Their edges are fringed with pale, fleshy reeds that lean over the water. Keep a clear body length between yourself and any pool, and do not kneel at the edge. Do not touch the film, the reeds, or the water. Water levels rise by hairline increments, so the edge you stood on yesterday may be closer now.

The ferns are the tallest things in the basin and grow in dense, regular stands, often where pipe trenches used to run. Walk around them rather than pushing through, since their pale, translucent fronds may conceal footing and their roots run over buried conduits that can shift. Do not assume the mats of moss or fungus are solid. Probe them with a pole before you step.

The vines are slow but heavy. Some hang from the catwalks in long curtains and wrap the machinery. Do not pull on them. Their tension has been building for decades, and a vine that looks loose may be holding up a rusted section.

## The Hum

Near the base of the pipe that climbs out of the basin there is a faint, low hum. You may feel it in your chest before you hear it. It may come from the pipe, the warm rock, or the pressure vessel, and nothing else in the place moves with it.

Keep your exposure short. Do not put your hands on the pipe or lean against the vessel. If the sensation becomes uncomfortable, move back toward the ferns or the walls. Note whether it changes as you move, since that may tell you something about its source. Do not try to shut it off.

## Silence and Sound

The basin is almost completely silent. There are no insects, no birds, no movement in the undergrowth. Your own footsteps, breathing, and voice will sound strangely loud. Speak little, and speak quietly. Some travelers find that the silence grows heavier the longer they stay. If you feel the urge to fill it with talk or music, resist it. The sound of drips and the settling of vines will be enough.

## The Traces of People

The control booth, the bench, the sign, and the lamp belong to a human past that the basin has absorbed. Look at them, but do not expect to learn much. The sign has one legible line of safety instruction, and the rest is gone. Do not sit on the bench, which may have lost its bolts, and do not sit in the booth, whose window is cracked. Take photographs if you wish, but do not expect them to show the place as it feels.

## Mental Preparation

Nothing happens here. The basin changes in decades, not minutes. The vines extend a few centimeters a season, the pools creep upward, the rust flakes away, the walls fracture a little more. If you expect an event, a discovery, or a threat, you will be disappointed, and the disappointment can make you careless. Plan your visit as an observation. Measure what you can, sketch the layout, note the water lines, and leave.

Some visitors find the place peaceful. Others find it oppressive, because its stillness seems to wait for something it never receives. Decide in advance how long you will stay. A few hours is usually enough to see the basin. Staying overnight is possible only if your extraction route is secure and you have accounted for the heat, humidity, and falling rock.

## Exit

Plan your ascent before you descend. The walls are unstable, the scree is loose, and no path is marked. If you arrived by a fixed rope, check it for damage from wet rock and vines. If you have no fixed line, you must rig one before you leave the floor. Do not rely on the pipe for support.

Do not leave anything behind that might change the basin in ways you cannot predict. Do not take souvenirs from the machinery or the plants. The place has been arranging itself for a long time, and disturbing it adds to a process you do not understand.

## Checklist

- Rope, anchor, and a tested ascent plan
- Sturdy boots, gloves, long-sleeved layers, and a hat
- Two light sources with warm white beams, plus spare batteries
- Ample sealed water and dry, heat-stable food
- Waterproof pack and sealed electronics
- Pole for probing moss and fungus mats
- Clear limit on time spent near the pools and the hum
- Rule of silence: speak little, touch nothing unknown

Visit carefully, expect nothing to happen, and leave before the quiet starts to feel like it is waiting for you.
