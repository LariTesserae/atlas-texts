# Preparing to Visit the Drowned Terraces

## What you are actually going into

You will not arrive in a town so much as a wet, green argument between a people and their valley. Expect to spend most of your first day feeling that the place is slightly larger, wetter, and quieter-than-loud than you planned for. The fog is not weather you can wait out. It is the room you live in. Plan to stay several days at least, because the first day is spent adjusting to the density of the air and the lack of distance, and the second day is when you start to see the layers.

Before you go, accept three facts. Nothing here is dry for long. Nothing here is flat. And you will rarely see more than forty paces ahead.

## Clothing and gear

**Waterproofing matters more than warmth.** The air is cold and thin at this elevation, but the real enemy is the damp that gets into everything. Wear layers that dry fast: wool or synthetic base layers, never cotton, which will stay clammy and cold for days. Over that, a hood that covers your ears and the back of your neck. The fog beads on hair and drips inside collars all day.

**Footwear is the single most important choice.** The stone terraces are smooth where feet have worn them and slick with moss elsewhere. The lowest step of every terrace is a silt-bottomed puddle you will have to wade. Bring boots that are high enough to keep silt out, with a tread that bites on wet black stone, and a spare pair or at least a set of dry socks you keep sealed in a bag. Bridges are slightly springy and can be slick. Walk low, with your weight centred, and do not hurry across them in the rain.

**Carry two waterproof bags and assume your gear will get wet anyway.** Keep anything you cannot afford to lose, including your route notes, medicine, and any means of return, in a sealed container inside a second one. Don't rely on paper maps. Ink runs in this humidity, and in any case the terraces shift by inches each season.

**Bring a long cord or light rope.** The bridges are good, but the stone channels are narrow and the drops between terraces can be sudden. A cord also helps you find your way back to a house when the fog swallows the path.

## Navigation

Compasses and landmarks are unreliable here. The fog hides the valley walls, and the sound behaves strangely, so a voice two houses away may seem to come from directly underfoot. Use water instead. Water always runs downhill, and the channels the people cut run in consistent directions. If you know which way the nearest channel flows, you know which way is down toward the lake bed, and therefore which way is out of the bowl.

Count terraces, not paces. Each shelf has a rough number, and the people will tell you which level they live on if you ask. Keep a tally in your head of how many bridges you have crossed. And learn the sound of the rain's arrival: when the roaring wall starts up the valley, you have perhaps several minutes to get under a roof or onto a platform, because the path you were on will be a sheet of runoff within the hour.

## Sound and sleep

The place is never quiet. Water is always running, the frogs call from every pool, and birds clatter from six directions at once. Pack earplugs if you need them to sleep, but do not rely on them for safety. Listen for the people's work-songs and the bells. The bells are hollow timber, tuned to the valley, and they ring near dawn when the wind moves through them. If you wake to them, you are probably safe. If you hear a crash or snort from the upper slopes at night, stay on your platform.

The frogs fall silent in places, and that silence moves. If you find yourself in a patch where the chorus has stopped, do not follow the silence and do not stay in it. Walk out the way you came, toward the sound.

## Speaking and being heard

People here speak loudly, partly because of the water and partly out of habit. Do not take this as anger. Match their volume when you want to be understood, and lower it when you are alone with someone, because the fog carries voices and a quiet talk can still reach the next house.

Remember that privacy is a function of distance. Houses are spaced so that nobody's wall touches a neighbour's. Do not wander onto a platform uninvited, and do not cross a bridge toward a house that seems to be occupied without calling out first. A greeting shouted across the mist is the normal way to announce yourself.

## Food, water, and the body

Almost everything is wet, and this includes the water. Assume that every puddle, channel, and leaf-cupped rainfall carries something. Boil or filter all drinking water, and be cautious with the silt-bottomed pools, which look clear until you stir them. The locals drink from the upper channels and cook with boiled water.

The food is simple: sweet tubers and a dark leafy green that tastes strongly of iron. It is good but unfamiliar, and the iron taste can be surprising. Bring your own dried rations for your first few days, in case your stomach objects, and eat small portions of local food until you know how it sits with you.

Expect your skin to stay damp and your feet to soften. Dry your feet at every opportunity. Watch for fungal and skin trouble, and treat cuts quickly, because moss and standing water are good at turning a scrape into an infection.

The salamanders are harmless and will ignore you. The water striders are harmless too. Do not handle the pale green light that rises from the moss at night. It is not hot, but the people leave it alone, and they are probably right. The large animals on the upper slopes keep to themselves, so give them the same courtesy: stay below the thinning trees and make noise when you move.

## Things to bring that you might not expect

Bring a few small gifts that are useful and not metal, since there is no metal here and it may be rare or odd to them. Good options include good cord, sealed salt, dried fruit, or well-made tools of bone or stone you have acquired elsewhere. The people value work and usefulness more than ornament.

Bring something to write with that will survive wet conditions, such as a pencil, since ink runs and paper swells. Keep a field journal in a sealed case. If you have a means of marking your route, such as tied cords or cut marks on a bridge rail, use it sparingly, and only where the locals do not object.

Do not bring anything that depends on a stable reference point. The terraces move. A path you walked on Monday may be a channel by Friday. Treat every landmark as provisional.

## Preparing to return

This is the part to think about before you arrive. Your means of returning to your own world is tied to a fixed point, and the valley is always reworking itself. Choose and memorise your return point on your first day, and check it every morning. Ask the locals which terrace has been stable longest, and note their answer, but expect that even stable things will shift by inches over a season.

If you cannot find your return point, do not panic and do not wander at night. Find the nearest running water, follow it downhill, and ask for the people by calling loudly. They know the valley, and they have been repairing it for longer than anyone remembers. They will help you if you ask plainly and do not disturb their channels.

## The strange part

Some things here are not explained, and the people prefer to leave them unexplained. The pale columns that hang in the fog above the lowest pool are not smoke, and they will not be burnt by anything you do. Do not try to touch them or walk into them. The trees along the eastern wall grow in a curve that does not match the wind, and the people step around them. Some channels are left unfinished on purpose. Treat these as the valley's own business.

If you feel a pull toward a particular spot, a sense that the water wants you to go somewhere, be suspicious of it. Mention it to the people before you act. They will not be surprised, and they will tell you whether to follow it or leave it.

## Last advice

You will want to be useful here, and the people will appreciate help with repairs, mulch, and channel-digging, but understand that the town is always partway through a repair that began before you arrived and will continue after you leave. You are a brief presence in a long conversation. Listen more than you speak, move more slowly than you think you should, and let the valley set the pace.

Above all, expect the fog to change how you feel about distance. Things close at forty paces will feel near, and things you cannot see will still be there. Go carefully, come back to your platform before dark, and remember which way the water runs.
