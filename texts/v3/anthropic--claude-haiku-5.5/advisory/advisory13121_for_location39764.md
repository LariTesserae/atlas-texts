# Preparing to Visit the Held Plateau

Go slowly, dress for wet heat, and expect the place to be less dangerous than it looks and more disorienting than you'd guess. Most of the risk comes from the ground, the moisture, and your own sense of time, not from anything that will chase you.

## Before You Arrive

**Anchor yourself.** The plateau's clock runs slow, and it doesn't care about yours. Before you step through, set a firm return time in your home reality and tell someone there exactly when you expect to come back. Many travelers who lose track of time here don't notice until hours have passed, because the light never changes. The canopy holds a permanent late-afternoon or pre-dawn glow, so your body will want to call it evening, then morning, then nothing at all. Wear a mechanical watch if you have one. Electronic timepieces tend to fail in this humidity, and phones will fog and corrode within a day.

**Plan for the damp.** This is the most important practical point. The air is saturated, nothing dries, and everything you carry will be wet within the first hour. Bring:

- A waterproof pack, or a dry bag with a second dry bag inside it for anything you can't afford to lose: maps, medicine, documents, spare socks.
- Lightweight, quick-drying clothing in breathable synthetics. Cotton holds water, chafes, and stays cold. Wool is better than cotton but still heavy when soaked.
- Waterproof boots with ankle support, and a second pair of socks you keep sealed away until you need them.
- A thin rain shell. The dusk drizzle lasts for hours, and the canopy drips heavily even when no rain falls.
- Gloves that let you grip bark, rope, and wet stone. The bark is smooth and cool, and your hands will slip.

Expect your skin to stay damp. Keep a fungal treatment on hand, since warm, wet feet and skin invite infection. Apply a barrier cream to heels, armpits, and the skin under your pack straps.

**Carry more water than you think you need, and treat all of it.** Water is everywhere here, but it sits in basins under a living mat and is full of microbes, dissolved organics, and possibly things you won't want to meet. Bring purification tablets or a filter rated for silt and biological contamination, and boil or treat every drop you drink, including the drizzle collected on leaves. Don't drink from the basins at all unless you are prepared to be sick for a week.

**Pack light for footing.** The mat is a sponge. Heavy loads sink you into the peat, and you may find yourself knee-deep in water between one step and the next. Keep your load close to your body and under control. Carry a long pole, a walking staff, or a collapsible probe. Test every step before you commit your weight.

**Bring cordage and a marking system you can recognize.** Nothing on the plateau is labeled, and the canopy absorbs old marks quickly. Bring bright, durable tape or flagging, and a compass that you have checked against a known reference. The canopy hides the sun, so a compass will serve you better than the light, though magnetic readings may drift in the mineral-rich rock near the basins. If you use a map, sketch it on waterproof paper as you go.

## Physical Hazards

**The mat is alive.** It is warm and gives back pressure. Don't assume it's stable just because it looks like ground. Some sections are thick, springy peat that can hold your weight. Others are thin crusts over open water. The surface can look identical in both cases. Watch for subtle signs: a faint sheen of standing water in the moss, a sudden darkening of the surface, a smell of sour decay. If you break through, don't thrash. Lie back, spread your weight, and work your way out slowly. Keep a rope tied to your waist if you are traveling alone.

**Slow does not mean harmless.** The beetles, the pale segmented creatures, and the large drifting shapes all move without hurry. Most will ignore you. Don't assume that means they can't hurt you. Some may have defensive secretions that cause burns or rashes. Some of the translucent winged forms may release fine spores or dust when disturbed. Don't touch anything without checking first, and don't press your hands into the moss without gloves. The mat is warm because it is metabolically active, and it may be sensitive to contact.

**Bark and sap.** The trunks are cool and pale, and the veins under the bark carry fluid upward at a steady pulse. Cutting or scraping the bark will likely release sap, which may be sticky, irritating, or toxic. Don't climb the columns. Don't lean on the cords and braids, since they creak under load and some have already given way. The deep creaks you'll hear are not warnings, but a falling limb is still a falling limb. Stay out from under the arches when you hear one.

**Glowing fungi and moss.** The luminescence is beautiful and mildly hypnotic. Don't rub it on your skin or your eyes. Don't eat it. Some of the glow brightens as you approach, which can make it hard to tell how far away it is. Give it a wide berth.

**Mist and drizzle.** The dawn mist is thick and low and will obscure the basins, the rock, and your footing. Don't walk in it without a rope and a plan. The dusk drizzle lasts for hours and soaks everything, so plan to be sheltered by then, under a thick section of canopy or on a high, dry hummock of rock.

## Navigation and Orientation

The plateau has no clear edge, and from inside, its level top looks the same in every direction. Orient by three things: the rock outcrops, the trunks, and the old built remains.

The rock outcrops are the most dependable. The pale stone breaks through the green in places, and its shape gives you a fixed reference. The built remains are useful but unreliable. Footings, channels, and columns can be difficult to find once the green has swallowed them, and the regular spacing that makes them look deliberate can also mislead you. Don't assume that a line of footings continues just because it did a few meters back.

The trunks will make you feel lost even when you aren't. The canopy is continuous, so you can't use the sky to navigate, and the glow never shifts to indicate direction. Pick a bearing, follow it with markers, and check it often.

If you get lost, don't panic and don't wander. Stop, mark your position, and think. The plateau is large, but it is not unlimited, and the rock rises above the lowlands. If you can find an edge where the canopy thins and the rock slopes down, you can use the slope to descend and reorient.

## Ethics of the Built Remains

The footings, channels, and columns are the only signs of making here, and they may be the only signs that anyone was ever here at all. Don't dig them out, don't cut the trunks that have grown through them, and don't carry off any stone, however small. Even if the remains are not what they seem, disturbing them changes the balance of a place that has held itself together for an age. Photograph them if you must, but leave the ground as you found it.

Also don't try to decide whether the remains were built. The plateau keeps that question open, and so should you. Treat them as you would a sleeping stranger: observe, don't disturb.

## What to Expect Inside

Your sense of time will fail first. Hours will pass as if they were minutes, or the reverse. The steady pulse under everything will fade into the background, then return suddenly when you stop moving and listen. The drip of water into the basins will become so constant that you'll stop hearing it, and when you finally notice the silence where it used to be, you'll know you've been here too long.

The place will not feel hostile. It will feel complete. You may find yourself wanting to stay, to sit on a hummock and watch the mist rise, or to lie down on the warm mat and let it hold you. Resist that if you have somewhere to be. The plateau moves at the pace of erosion and growth, and an afternoon there is a moment to it, but it will be a long time before you can say you've returned.

Leave on schedule. Follow your markers back. Shake the moisture from your gear before you pack it, and expect that your boots will still be damp a week later.
