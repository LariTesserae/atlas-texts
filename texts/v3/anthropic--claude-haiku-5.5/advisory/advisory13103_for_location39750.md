# Preparing to Visit the Low Grey Reach

## Before You Go

Learn the place as well as you can before you step onto it. The Reach changes, and it does not explain itself, so your preparation is what you will rely on when the ground and the sky stop agreeing with each other.

Decide on your turnaround point before you leave the last rise. Pick the furthest pump you will approach and the time by which you will start back. Write both down and give a copy to someone who is not coming with you. Plan for a return trip that will feel longer and more confusing than the outward one.

Go with at least one companion if you can. Your companion is also your best check on memory, and the Reach is very good at making one person feel sure of something the other knows is wrong.

## Clothing and Body

The Reach is wet in a way that defeats ordinary rain gear. Water condenses out of the air onto everything, so you will be damp within an hour whatever you wear. Plan for that.

Wear wool or synthetic layers that keep their insulation when soaked. Cotton will kill your body heat. Carry a waterproof outer shell, and expect the inside of it to be wet too.

Your feet need the most attention. The ground takes water from below, so boots must be waterproof, but they also need to come off quickly if you sink into a hollow. Choose boots you can pull free of mud without losing them, and carry a spare pair of dry socks in a sealed bag. Wet feet in cold conditions lead to injury faster than anything else on the plain.

Bring a probing pole, two to three meters long, with a cross-piece near the tip. Test every hollow before you commit weight to it. The crust plates look solid, but many have water under them, and some will fail without warning.

Eat and drink more than you think you need. The cold burns energy steadily, and dehydration is easy to miss when you are already soaked. Carry hot food in insulated containers if you can keep it hot.

## Equipment

Everything you carry will fog, bead, and corrode. Seal your electronics in dry bags with desiccant, and check the seals every hour. Expect screens to fail and batteries to drain faster than usual. Keep at least one analog backup for each critical item: a paper map, a mechanical watch, a notebook with a pencil (ink can run in this air).

Lubricate every metal joint and hinge with oil before you leave, and wipe down your blades and fastenings often. Rust sets in quickly, and the iron of the pumps is not the only metal that will oxidize.

Do not rely on a magnetic compass without testing it first. The iron columns are large and numerous, and near them the needle drifts or stalls. Use a compass only at a distance from the pumps, and cross-check it against the conveyor ridges described below.

Carry a rope of at least thirty meters, tied to yourself and to your companion, and attach it at intervals to something you can see. You will need it for the crossings and for the moments when you lose sight of each other in the haze.

Bring a counter that you can click by hand, and a timer you can read without looking at the sky. You will use these for the pump rhythm, and you will need them more than you expect, because the sounds of the Reach will not give you reliable time.

## Navigation

The sky is a grey ceiling with no sun shadows, so you cannot navigate by the sun, and the haze makes distant landmarks unreliable. The far pumps seem to move nearer and farther, and you should not trust their apparent distance.

The most reliable direction on the plain is the conveyor ridges. The fused rubble ridges carry sediment across the flats, always in the same direction, always toward a low point. Learn which way they move, and you will always know which way is down-basin. The way back is up-basin, against the flow. Walk along a ridge when you can. The ridge is slow, but it does not change direction.

Mark your route with a bearing from the nearest pump, using your compass away from the iron. Record the bearing and the number of pump exhales you counted between two fixed points. When you return to a point, check the bearing and the count again. If they disagree, you have a problem, and you should stop and reassess before moving.

Do not trust the cracks in the crust. The polygons rearrange, and a path that was clear when you crossed it may have become a wall of new fissures. Step on the plates themselves, not in the seams. Keep your path on plates you have already tested and remember by their shape.

## The Pumps

The pumps are the most dependable thing on the plain, and they are also dangerous to stand near. Each one draws water through banded pipes and exhales in bursts that carry for kilometers. The steam is hot enough to scald and the pressure can knock you down.

Before you approach a pump, watch it for two full cycles. Count the interval between exhales. Note the length of the burst and how long the piston takes to fall. Most pumps keep a steady rhythm, though some seem to follow the swarms instead of the reverse.

Stay at least ten meters from the outlet of any pump, and do not stand in the line of its pipes, which run under the ground and surface in unpredictable places. Do not block a pipe, press on a gear, or try to stop a piston. They are doing work that appears to matter, and you have no way of knowing what happens if the work is interrupted.

Cross the open flats during the pump's drop phase, when the pressure is low and the exhale is distant. Cross quickly, and keep your head down, because the steam can carry grit.

## The Fauna

The animals will occupy every space you could stand in. There is no empty ground. Expect to be surrounded, and do not panic when you are.

The water shoals are dense, armored, and move in sheets. Stay out of the lakes deeper than your knee. If you must cross water, do it on a visible channel and keep your rope taut. Do not reach into the water, even to steady yourself. The shoals will part around you, but they will also close in around a hand that stays still too long.

The heavy grazers on the crusts make a tearing sound as they feed, a wet ripping like paper. This is not a warning. They are slow and they are many, and they will not change their course for you. Walk around the edge of a herd, not through it. If one stops grazing and lifts its head, step back slowly.

The glass-winged swarms hang in columns above the flats. They will part for a pump exhale and close again after. Keep your eyes and mouth closed during the swarm's passage. Do not swat, and do not try to catch one. Their wings are very sharp and the air is thick with them.

Predators are the real hazard. You will see them only in flashes: a long body under a lake surface, a jointed shape at a channel's edge, a ripple beneath the crust. Learn to read the chorus. When the chorus drops suddenly, a predator is near, and you should freeze, check your footing, and move only when the sound returns. A stuck chorus that continues after its creatures have gone quiet is worse. If you hear the chorus and see no animals, do not assume they are gone.

Do not take anything from the plain. The slick in the basins may warm when you touch it and then cool to nothing, and you should leave it alone. Stones and crust fragments are carried by the conveyors toward the low point, and removing them may disrupt a process you do not understand. Carry no living specimens. There is nothing green here to bring home, and nothing dead that should be brought either.

## Sound and Time

The sounds of the Reach arrive out of order. A thud may come before its piston moves, and a chorus may keep singing after its singers have stopped. Do not use sound to judge timing.

Use your counter for the pump rhythm and your timer for elapsed time. Check your watch against the counter every hour. If the two disagree, trust the watch and treat the counter as suspect.

Expect the sounds to linger. A specific noise may repeat itself in your ears for minutes after it has ended. Do not mistake this echo for a new event. Say your own position aloud at regular intervals, so your companion can confirm where you are.

## Reflections and the Frayed Edge

The lower lakes sometimes show a different sky, a different light, occasionally a different set of machines. These reflections are not reliable views of the present plain, and they are not necessarily views of anywhere you can reach.

Do not stare into still water for long. Glance at it, note what you see, and look up. If a reflection shows a path, a person, or a structure you do not see directly, do not step toward it. The reflected version may be real, and it may not be on the plain you are standing on. Keep your feet on the ground you can see.

If you see a machine smoke bending around nothing, do not follow the bend. Note the direction and continue on your planned route. The bend is the Reach declining to hold still, and following it is how travelers lose their way.

If the crust rearranges under your feet or the cracks form a pattern you do not recognize, stop. Sit down if you can, and check your bearings against the conveyors and your recorded counts. Do not walk until you can say where you are and which way is up-basin.

## Memory and Records

You will not come away with a clear memory. The Reach does not allow it. You will remember the rhythm of the pumps, the wet cold, the packed and clamoring life, and the sense of a single slow engine grinding the land. You will be less certain of the order of events and of the exact shape of what you saw.

Keep a written log throughout. Write during rests, not while walking, and write with a pencil. Record the time, the pump count, the bearing, the conveyor direction, and anything unusual. Be specific. Note the number of exhales between landmarks, not just that you saw a pump.

Photographs and recordings are less reliable than you might expect. Some will show the plain as you saw it, and some will show it as it was not. Take them, but do not rely on them. Check them against your log before you trust them.

When you talk about the trip afterward, talk with your companion. Compare your accounts and note the points of disagreement. Do not assume that the version you hold more confidently is correct.

## Leaving

Know your way back before you go deep. Keep the conveyor direction in your head and walk up-basin when you turn. Do not trust your memory of the path you took. Trust the log and the counts.

Keep to your turnaround time. If you reach it and feel you can go further, that feeling is the place talking, and you should not follow it.

When you climb back to the last rise, look back once. The plain will look flat and grey and far smaller than you remember, and you may be unable to pick out the pumps. That is normal. Turn around and walk down the far side without stopping to look again.

## Checklist

- Turnaround point and time written down and left with someone
- Companion confirmed, rope attached
- Waterproof boots, dry socks in sealed bag, probing pole
- Wool or synthetic layers, waterproof shell
- Electronics sealed with desiccant; analog backups carried
- Compass checked away from iron; conveyor direction learned
- Pump rhythm timed and counted before approach
- Hand counter and watch synchronized
- Pencil and log, not ink
- Nothing taken from the plain, no specimens carried
- Eyes off reflections, feet on tested plates, rope taut near water

The Low Grey Reach asks very little of you except attention, and it punishes inattention with a wet, cold, disorienting slide into a place where the ground is not quite where you left it. Prepare for that slide, and you will be able to walk out of it.
