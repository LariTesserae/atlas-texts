# Preparing for the Plain of Settled Stone

This is a place where the land is slowly losing its height and the water is slowly taking it. It is not dangerous in the way a cliff or a storm front is, but it is unforgiving of carelessness, and it is easy to underestimate. Prepare for three things: wet, heat, and sameness.

## Before You Go

**Anchor your arrival point.** The plain is nearly featureless. The stone domes are the only landmarks, and from ground level several look alike. Reflections double the sky and the hills, so the horizon can seem to fold back on itself. Before you step off the transit point, record its position relative to the nearest dome and its shape. Carry a beacon or marker that you can reactivate, and set a return window. People have lost hours here walking in what felt like a straight line and was a wide circle.

**Expect the compass to lie.** Iron-rich veins run through the exposed layers and the clay. A magnetic compass will drift and sometimes swing badly near the domes. Carry a compass that you trust, but confirm bearings by the sun, which is easy to read here, or by the cloud banks, which build over the plain at a predictable hour.

**Check your biological clearance.** The water, the mud, the reeds, and the animals all carry an ecology that has never met yours. Bring a full decontamination kit, and plan for a quarantine and scan on return. Do not assume the water is safe because it looks clear. It is clear the way strong tea is clear, and it carries unknown organisms.

## What to Wear

**Footwear is the most important choice you will make.** Choose tall, sealed, waterproof boots with a firm, non-slip sole. The crust rings like pottery and can be brittle and sharp where it has broken, and the mud beneath is cracked and soft in places. Add gaiters or sealed leg wraps, because the water comes up over the ankle and the lower shin without warning. Carry a second pair of dry socks in a sealed bag. Wet feet for a full day here will cause blisters, rot, and worse.

**Dress for warm, wet air.** Midday temperatures are high, but the heat is held in the air rather than blown off by wind, so you will sweat constantly without feeling a breeze. Wear long, loose, light-colored, quick-drying layers that cover your arms and legs. The sun is unshielded and bright, though not harsh, and it will burn you through thin cloth on a long day. Bring a wide-brimmed hat with a chin cord, because the fog and the mornings will soak an unbrimmed hat, and the afternoon storms can come on quickly.

**Protect your eyes.** The water throws the sky back at you, and the light is even and bright from every direction. Polarized or dark-tinted glasses are essential. Without them, the glare off the sheet will give you headaches within an hour.

**Bring a waterproof outer layer.** The morning fog beads on everything, and the evening storms drop grey columns of rain you can watch walking across the plain. A hooded shell that packs small is worth more than an umbrella.

## Water and Food

**Assume every drop is contaminated.** The water is warm and stained brown-black with tannins. It will be body temperature by midday, which is ideal for growth of anything you bring with you or that already lives in it. Carry sealed water for the whole trip, plus a filter and a chemical purifier for emergencies. Drink a litre more than you think you need, and watch for signs of heat illness, because you will not feel as thirsty as you should. Electrolyte tablets will help.

**Eat only what you bring.** Do not eat the shrub leaves, the grasses, the reed shoots, the fungal rings, or any animal you catch. The shrubs' waxy leaves and the lichen on them are not food, and the fungi may be lethal. Pack energy-dense food that survives heat and humidity, such as sealed bars, nuts, and dried fruit in sealed packets. Keep everything in airtight containers, because the damp will reach it.

**Do not wade deep.** The sheets of water are only a hand or two deep, but the reed stands grow where the water deepens, and deeper pools hold large animals that turn beneath the surface and send ripples across the sheet. Stay on the crust, the tussocks, or the dry mud. If you must cross open water, do it on a stick-tested line, slowly, in the morning, and never alone.

## Terrain and Timing

**Watch the crust and the mud.** The clay crust can look solid and then give way over a pale layer beneath. Test each step. The mud cracks and curls when the water retreats, and some patches are soft under a thin skin. Walk with a long pole, tap ahead, and keep your weight low.

**Stay off the domes when they are wet.** The stone tops are bald and smooth, but they shed plates in thin sheets when the heat swells them, and the north faces hold moss and seep water that run down like curtains. Climbing a wet dome is a common way to get hurt. The domes are useful for sight lines and as refuges from standing water, but treat them with respect, and approach the dry, south-facing sides only.

**Plan around the weather.** Mornings bring fog that will cut your visibility to a few metres and soak your gear. Mid-morning, once the fog burns off, is the best time to move. The afternoon is hot and still. The cumulus builds through the afternoon and drops rain in the evening. Lightning on a flat plain is dangerous because you are the tallest thing for kilometres, so be off open ground and away from standing water before the towers build. Watch the clouds, which will show you the storms long before they arrive.

**Use the cloud banks as your clock.** When the fog is gone and the sun is high, the cumulus line will show you the direction of the storms and the time you have left.

## Sound and Sleep

**The frogs will define your evening.** As dusk falls, the frogs begin across the whole plain. Each pond has its own chorus of trills, hammers, and croaks, and together they hum loud enough to keep you awake. Bring earplugs and a sleep mask. Insects drone from the reeds all night.

**Bird calls and sudden cackles will startle you.** Wading birds lift off in great flocks when something disturbs them, and the sound is enormous. Do not mistake the sound of a flock for an attack, and do not walk toward the flock to see what startled it.

**Keep your distance from the grazers.** The four-legged animals that crop the tussocks are not hostile in any obvious way, but they are large, and their trails are everywhere. Do not block a trail, do not approach young, and do not feed them.

## What to Carry

- Tall, sealed, waterproof boots with a non-slip sole, plus gaiters and dry spare socks
- Lightweight long-sleeved clothing in light colours, a wide-brimmed hat with a chin cord, and a waterproof hooded shell
- Polarized or dark-tinted glasses, and sunscreen rated for high, even UV
- A long walking pole for testing crust and mud
- Sealed water, a filter, chemical purifier, and electrolyte tablets
- Energy-dense sealed food, all airtight
- Beacon, marker, and a compass you trust, with a sun-reading method as backup
- Earplugs and a sleep mask
- A dry bag for electronics and documents
- A decontamination kit and a full quarantine plan for return

## The Mental Side

The plain asks you to slow down. Nothing here rushes. The land settles over centuries, and the marks of last century look like the marks of last week. You may find yourself bored, then unsettled by how little changes, then calm. That calm is useful, because it keeps you careful. Keep your attention on small things: the water level, the trail you are following, the cloud line, the sound of the frogs changing as the sun drops. Take regular checks on your position, your water, and your body. The plain will not hurry you, and it will not hurry to save you either. Go slowly, stay dry where you can, and leave the place as you found it.
