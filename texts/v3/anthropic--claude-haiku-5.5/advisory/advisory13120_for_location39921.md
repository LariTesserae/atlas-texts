# Preparing to Visit the Drowned Shelf of Hesk

## First, Understand What Kind of Place This Is

Hesk is not hostile in the way a fire or a storm is hostile. It has no interest in you. Its dangers come from scale, density, and indifference, not malice. The right attitude is that of a careful visitor to a deep cave or an old cemetery: move slowly, expect nothing to acknowledge you, and do not assume anything will make room for you. Almost everything that goes wrong here goes wrong because a traveler assumed the place would behave like a room.

Plan for a stay of hours at most, even if you have the means to stay longer. The cold is patient, the pressure never relents, and fatigue creeps in quietly. A short, deliberate visit is safer than a long one.

## Your Body and Your Vessel

You cannot breathe Hesk's water as it is, and you should not try. Before you go, decide how you will be sealed or supported, and test that method thoroughly in less extreme conditions. Consider these points:

- **Pressure.** The weight of the overlying ocean is immense and constant. Your enclosure must hold against it with a comfortable margin, and you should have a clear understanding of how your seals behave under sustained load, not only at the moment of entry.
- **Cold.** The water is near freezing and far colder than any shallow sea. Bare skin will lose heat faster than you expect, and the cold does not announce itself with pain. It takes away dexterity first. Insulation must be thick and dry, and you should have a way to monitor your own core temperature.
- **Buoyancy and weight.** Silt and sediment are heavy and clinging. Ballast that works in clear water may sink you into the shelf or make you tip in the currents. Test your neutral buoyancy with a full kit and with sediment on your gear, since it will accumulate.
- **Movement.** The water is thick with particles, and your limbs will push through a dense medium that resists quickly. Quick movements stir silt and cut visibility. Practice slow, deliberate motion until it feels natural.

Bring redundancy for anything you cannot repair in the water. A failed seal here is not a minor leak.

## Light and Sight

The only light is what things make themselves, and it is dim, grey-blue, and pulsing. Your eyes will need a long time to adapt, so do not expect to see well immediately after arrival. Give yourself several minutes of stillness before moving.

Bring your own light, but use it sparingly and at low intensity. Hesk's glowing filaments and mats seem to respond to nearby light, and a bright beam can trigger flaring that washes out your view of everything else. A dim, warm-spectrum lamp, or a steady low glow, is better than a searchlight. Keep it pointed at the ground when you can, because the silt will scatter a beam back into your eyes and blind you.

Expect your visible range to be very short, often less than an arm's length in active areas. Your sense of distance will be unreliable, since everything is suspended in a dense field of near-things. Train yourself to judge distance by motion and by touch. Learn to trust your instruments over your eyes.

## Current, Shear, and Boils

This is the most dangerous feature of the shelf. The long cold drift and the rising warm water from the vent meet in violent shears, and eddies spin off into the dark. Currents come from several directions at once, and they change.

- Before entering any open area, look for the faint columns of silt rising in slow plumes. These mark upwelling or shear zones. Avoid them. Do not try to pass through a column; it will tear at you and deposit you somewhere unplanned.
- Stay low and on the lee side of the swells when you can. The rounded ridges break the current and create calmer water in their shadow, though the eddies behind them can pull unexpectedly.
- Carry a means of anchoring yourself, such as a line or a grip you can deploy quickly. Do not anchor to the stalks or the mats. They are fragile, and the stalks may be the only one of their kind for many body-lengths.
- Know that a calm patch can become a current within moments. Keep track of where you came from and where your exits lie relative to the drift.

## Silt, Sediment, and the Changing Ground

The pale silt on every surface lifts at the slightest touch. A single careless contact can reduce your visibility to nothing for several minutes. Move in a way that minimizes contact with the floor, and if you must kneel or brace, do it gently, with your largest surfaces rather than your fingertips.

Sediment slides are real. Flanks of the swells can give way with a soft whisper, carrying silt and anything resting on it downslope. Do not stand at the base of a steep bank or on a freshly exposed ridge. Do not assume yesterday's map of the ground is still accurate. The shelf rearranges itself, and ridges can appear overnight.

## Navigation Without Landmarks

Hesk offers no fixed points. There are no walls, no roads, no markers. The few shaped stones are not reliable guides, since they are half-buried or softened and the water is shifting them. Do not depend on any single feature to find your way.

Instead, build a navigation method from things that persist:

- **Bearings from the vent.** The orange pulse is the only warm point you can see from a distance, and it is the most stable reference you have. Keep track of its position relative to you, and remember that it is far enough away that small movements will not change your bearing much.
- **Your own track.** Use inertial sensors, a compass if your environment allows one, or a logged path. Note your depth, your heading, and your speed at regular intervals.
- **Tethers and markers you control.** If you place a marker, use one you can retrieve. Do not leave anything behind. Anything dropped will be absorbed by the silt within a short time, and anything that is not retrieved becomes an unknown hazard for the next visitor.

## Sound and Communication

Most of what you will hear is below the threshold of hearing. The pressure hum, the ticking of cooling stone, the distant knock of a shifting boulder or the vent, and the whisper of sliding sediment all reach you as vibrations in your body or your enclosure, not as sound in the air. Consider installing a vibration sensor. A distant knock may be the only warning of a slope giving way.

Do not expect your voice to carry far. Radio or acoustic signals will be dampened and distorted by the dense medium. Plan communication with your base or companions around short, repeated messages rather than continuous conversation, and agree on signals in advance for "stop," "return," and "emergency."

The thick silence between sounds is real. Some visitors find it comforting. Others find it oppressive. Know which you are, and plan for the second case.

## Life Here

Hesk's life is sparse and slow. The pale bacterial mats in the lee of the swells, the honeycombed cushions that pulse with their own light, and the sea-glass stalks rising from cracks are all extremely fragile in relation to their rate of recovery. A stalk may take a very long time to regrow after being damaged, and its roots are so thinly spread that losing one may mean losing the only example in a wide area.

Do not touch the mats or cushions. Do not anchor to the stalks. Do not collect samples unless you have a specific, justified scientific reason and the means to preserve them without disturbing the ecosystem. Even then, consider whether observation is enough.

The animals you may see are slow and largely unaware of you. The translucent filter-feeder the size of a hand, the larger dark shape far off, and the things that surface briefly from the silt and sink again are not interested in you. Give them space. Do not chase, herd, or photograph with flash. Keep your distance, and let them keep their business.

## The Stone Shapes

A few flint-grey nodules show signs of having been flaked by something with purpose. Their edges are too regular to be an accident of pressure. Whatever made them is long gone, and the water has been softening them for an age you cannot measure.

Treat these stones with respect. They are the only evidence here that anything once made something on purpose. Do not remove them. Do not alter them. You may document them from a distance, noting their position and orientation, but leave them where they lie. A stone moved from its place is a loss of information, and it is the sort of harm that cannot be undone.

## The Vent

The orange pulse at the lower edge of sight is the only warmth on the shelf, and it is not for you. Do not approach it. The chemistry near the vent will differ sharply from the surrounding water, and the warm rising flow can create shears more violent than anything on the shelf. The heat will not warm your enclosure in any useful way; the temperature gradient will likely stress your seals and confuse your instruments.

Observe the vent from as far away as you can, and treat its distant pulse as a navigational reference rather than a destination.

## Psychological Preparation

The density of the water, the lack of horizon, the endless drifting snow, and the feeling that everything is touching you can wear on a person. Many visitors describe a sense of being pressed upon from all sides, and a sense that the water itself is attentive, even though nothing here is. This is a perceptual effect, but it is still real.

Prepare by practicing sensory isolation in a controlled setting. Learn to recognize the early signs of disorientation, such as a sudden urge to move fast, a conviction that you know where you are when you do not, or a feeling of being watched. When these arise, stop. Stillness is your most reliable tool here. Hold position, check your instruments, and breathe slowly until the feeling passes.

Accept in advance that you will not understand most of what you see. The shelf is not a puzzle built for you to solve. It has been shaped by ages it does not remember, and it will not explain itself.

## Time

Time may feel strange here. The slow rearrangement of the shelf and the pulsing light can make minutes feel like hours or hours like minutes. Your body clock, if you have one, will drift. Keep a reliable timekeeper that does not depend on light or on your sense of rhythm, and log your time at intervals.

## Exit and Return

Plan your exit before you enter. Know the direction you came from, your bearing to the vent, and the route back to your entry point. Know where the drift is strongest so you can avoid being carried away from your path, and where the shelf is calmest so you can rest.

Set a turnaround time and keep to it. Set a fuel or air margin that allows for a slower return than your inbound trip, since currents may push against you on the way back. If your way out is disrupted, your emergency plan should not depend on the shelf's features, since they may have changed.

When you leave, leave everything as you found it. Hesk will not notice your absence. The silt will settle over your tracks within a short time, and the shelf will keep shaping itself without reference to your visit. That indifference is not a reason to be careless. It is the reason to be careful: nothing here will correct your mistakes for you.

## Checklist

- Sealed enclosure rated for sustained, well beyond expected pressure, tested under load
- Insulation adequate for near-freezing water, with core temperature monitoring
- Neutral buoyancy checked with full kit and sediment load
- Low-intensity light source, angled downward when possible
- Vibration sensor for detecting sediment slides and distant knocks
- Compass or inertial navigation, with a logged track
- Timekeeper independent of light and rhythm
- Anchoring line or grip, not to be attached to living structures
- Short-message communication protocol and agreed signals
- Retrieval plan for any marker placed; nothing left behind
- Turnaround time and return margin, with allowance for slower return
- Emergency protocol that does not depend on landmarks
- Prior practice in sensory isolation and stillness

## Last Advice

Go slowly, touch nothing that lives, leave the shaped stones where they lie, keep the vent at a distance, and trust your instruments over your eyes. The shelf is old, the silt is restless, and the water keeps moving. Your job is to pass through it carefully and leave it as it was, which is the only thing this place would ever ask of you if it could ask anything at all.
