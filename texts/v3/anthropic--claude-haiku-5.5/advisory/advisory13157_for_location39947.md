# Preparing to Visit the Stepped Plateau of Arrin Tahl

Think of this place as a workshop where the floor is still being poured. Nothing here will hold you up for long without your attention, and almost everything will change while you're on it. Prepare for a cold, wet, unstable, thin-aired world where you'll have no help, no shelter, and no reliable map.

## Before You Arrive

**Acclimatize your body if you can.** The plateau sits high enough that every breath is a little less than you're used to. Spend a few days before you cross, if your travel allows, doing sustained light exertion like walking hills, climbing stairs, or hiking with a load. Hydrate well beforehand. Thin air dries you out even in saturated fog, because each breath costs more moisture than you notice.

**Know your limits for cold, damp weather.** The cold here is not dramatic, but it is relentless. Air and surfaces stay wet, and your clothes will never truly dry. Wet cold drains heat faster than dry cold at the same temperature, so hypothermia can develop slowly and in mild-seeming conditions. Learn to recognize its early signs in yourself and your companions: clumsy hands, slurred thinking, uncontrollable shivering that stops, and unexplained irritability.

## What to Wear and Carry

**Layers that shed water and keep warmth when wet.** Wool or synthetic base layers work; cotton does not. Add an insulating middle layer and a waterproof shell with a hood, since the fog precipitates constantly on every surface and your hood will collect the drip. Wear gaiters over your boots, because mud and meltwater will find any gap.

**Footwear with serious grip.** The bedrock is pale, sharp, and slick with condensation, and the scree slides. Choose boots with aggressive lugs and stiff soles that can grip edges. Carry spare insulated socks in a sealed bag, and change them at each rest.

**Hands and head.** Bring gloves that work when wet and a second pair kept dry. Bring a warm hat. Your head will sweat and then chill, so keep a second dry layer in a sealed container.

**Gear that tolerates static and grit.** The air builds static before storms, and metal objects can tick or spark. Keep your electronics sealed in padded, non-metal cases, and expect them to fail or behave erratically. Mechanical watches and compasses may be unreliable near the grid's hum.

**Respiratory protection.** The fine dark grit that glitters on the snow may be industrial particulate carried on the wind from the basin. Bring a fitted mask with particulate filters and a cloth to wrap your face in high winds. Do not breathe deeply when the grit is falling.

**Water.** Do not drink the meltwater, tarns, or runoff untreated. It carries iron and probably other things you can't taste, and the metallic taste in the air may be a hint of it. Carry sealed water from outside, plus a filter and a purifier you trust, and treat everything else as unsafe.

**Food.** Pack high-calorie, dense food that tolerates damp and cold, and keep it sealed. There's no settlement, so plan to carry all of it in and out. Expect to eat more than usual, since cold and altitude both burn energy.

**Navigation and marking.** The terrain changes faster than any map can record, so don't rely on a map alone. Carry a compass you've tested, but expect it to drift near the grid. Bring bright, non-metal markers, such as fabric strips or cord, to mark your route, and check them often, since fog and landslides can bury or erase trails.

**Light.** The overcast makes days dim and nights darker than you'd expect. Carry two independent light sources with spare batteries, and remember that battery life drops in cold, wet air.

**Basic repair and rescue.** Pack a repair kit with tape, cord, and a needle, plus a bivy shelter, an emergency blanket, and a way to signal, though signals may carry poorly through fog and sound may be muffled. A rescue is unlikely, so assume you'll need to take care of yourself.

## On the Ground

**Stay out of the gullies' edges.** The lips of the channels are undercut, and the drop-offs collapse without warning. Fresh landslides leave loose, angular debris that can give way under your weight. Walk on bedrock slabs where you can, test footing before committing, and never stand where the wall is overhanging the water.

**Treat the tarns and marshes with caution.** The spongy low ground looks like a safe rest but may swallow you to the knee. Meltwater skins over with ice at dawn, so avoid crossing at night or early morning. The water's edge is also where the lean predators ambush the grazers, so the same spot that looks peaceful at dusk may hold something hunting.

**Be careful near the cliffs.** The ledge-nesting birds crowd the edges where the shelf meets the drop. They are territorial and may dive at intruders near their nests. Keep your distance from the edge, and don't handle eggs, chicks, or the pale, bright remains you see on the rocks.

**Don't approach the burrowers.** Their thick-furred bodies and gritty mounds look harmless, but they defend their tunnels, and the tunnels collapse in the thaw. Stepping onto a mound can drop you into a hollow.

**Avoid the grazers' lines.** The slow, spaced rock-grazers keep to routes across the lichen. Don't block them or cross between them, and never assume a lone animal is alone.

## Timing Your Movement

**Mornings are fog and ice.** Expect poor visibility and frozen pans. Move carefully, and don't rely on sight to judge the ground.

**Midday is when the land thaws.** The mud opens, the streams run, and the slopes loosen. This is when you're most likely to slip.

**Afternoons can turn sharply.** Rain or sleet can arrive without warning, and every downpour cuts the channels deeper and shifts the scree. Watch the sky and the wind. A sudden shift in static or a change in the rhythm of the distant pulse may be a warning.

**Dusk is orange and dim.** The eastern glow from distant burning gives a false sense of warmth. Plan to be in shelter well before the light fails.

**The eastern basin is not a destination.** The smoke column and the pulsing machinery are a long way off, but the wind carries their effects. Don't walk toward the haze to investigate the source. It is unseen for a reason, and no one is there to receive you.

## Coming and Going

**Anchor your return.** This plateau is still forming, and the route you walked in may not be there when you come back. Before you leave any point you intend to return to, mark it clearly and record its exact relation to fixed features you can trust, such as a large boulder or a rim of bedrock. Expect your markers to be buried or washed away, so have a second set.

**Time may not follow your expectations.** Multiverse travel can shift how time feels or passes relative to home. Keep a written log of your departure and each day's events, and set a firm turnaround time, so you don't stay longer than you planned.

**Leave no trace, and take everything out.** Carry out all waste, including any food packaging, and avoid disturbing the scree, the lichen, the grass tufts, and the animals' territories. The plateau is only beginning to build its soil, and anything you leave will last.

## What to Leave Behind

Leave metal-heavy equipment that won't work near the grid's hum. Leave anything you can't carry out. Leave the expectation that this place is waiting for you. It isn't, and it will not hold still to be understood.

If you walk here, you'll find very little that's finished. The stone is raw, the channels are still lengthening, and every animal and plant is adjusting to ground that has only just decided to exist. Your job is to be a careful guest in a place that's still making up its mind.
