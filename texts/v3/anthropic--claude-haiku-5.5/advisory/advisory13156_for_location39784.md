# Preparing to Visit the Weeping Shelf

## Before You Go

Expect to be cold, wet, and dim for the whole visit. The Shelf is not dangerous in a dramatic way. Its hazards are the ordinary ones of cold, slippery stone, and fine-scale things you won't notice until you're already in trouble. Plan for those, and for a place that moves at a pace that will make your schedule feel absurd.

Do not treat old maps as reliable. Basins swell and drain, towers settle, and valleys shift position over decades. If you have a map, assume it is wrong in the details and right only about the broad shape of the terraces. If you can, get a survey from a recent visitor, and expect to find that some of their landmarks are gone.

Plan your exit point before anything else. Identify at least two stable, large-scale features near your arrival point, such as a tall tower with a distinctive tier, a major bridge, or a lake with a particular shoreline, and make sure you can find your way back to your entry site from each. The mist will hide your arrival point within minutes.

## Clothing and Gear

**Clothing.** Wear waterproof outer layers that cover your neck, wrists, and ankles. Mist beads on everything, and water will find its way through any gap. Use wool or synthetic base layers, never cotton, which holds cold and moisture against the skin. Bring a hood that you can cinch tightly, and keep your core warm above everything else. Hypothermia is the most likely serious problem here, and it can creep up while you're busy watching the blue pulses.

**Footwear.** The stone is glossy in places and rimed in others, and the crust flexes underfoot. Choose boots with aggressive soles that grip wet rock, with a stiff midsole so that a flexing crust doesn't roll your ankle. Ankle support matters, because the terraces are stepped at uneven heights. Bring a spare pair of dry socks, and carry them in a sealed bag.

**Hands.** You will want gloves for grip and for protection against the sharp fractures and the mineral films. Choose thin, grippy gloves that keep your fingers functional, and take them off only to handle something specific. Wash your hands thoroughly after each contact with a seep, a lake, or a crust, since the residue is unknown and may be irritating.

**Eyes and face.** Wear glasses or goggles. Mist collects on eyeglasses and makes them useless, but goggles seal against the beading. A light scarf or buff over the mouth and nose helps against fine spray from the cascades and against any airborne particles from crust fracturing.

## Light

This is the single most important preparation. The only illumination comes from the structures and the creatures, and between their pulses the darkness is nearly total.

Bring at least two independent lights, one a headlamp and one a handheld, with spare batteries. Choose a light with a dimmable or red mode, because your white beam will wash out the blue seams you need to read and will be visible from far away, which may draw attention you don't want. Test every light before you go; the mist will reduce output and fog the lens.

Give your eyes time to adapt. After you step out of a bright pulse zone, wait at least a minute before judging distances or footing. Keep one hand on a solid surface when crossing dark gaps.

Do not rely on the blue pulses to show you hazards. They light the surfaces of the structures, but they don't reveal the thin filament bridges, the crust edges over fissures, or the wet glossy patches on the stone. They show shape, not safety.

Consider bringing a light-sensitive marker or a reflective strip. Mark your route with something that will not leave residue on the crust. Chalk and paint will be absorbed or will flake off within days, and the crust will bury anything you leave, so mark only what you can retrieve.

## Navigation and Sound

The mist will swallow sight within a few meters. Sound is no better. Every noise echoes and returns doubled, so the source of a sound is almost impossible to locate by ear. Don't use hearing to find direction.

Use a compass, but test it first. The mineral content and the structures may distort magnetic readings, so calibrate against a known bearing and check it regularly. Count your paces, and record them as you go. Step counting is more reliable than memory, which tends to confuse distance here.

Set a pace and stick to it. Walking blind in mist makes it easy to drift in a slow curve and believe you're going straight.

## The Fauna

The creatures are the dominant presence on the Shelf, and most are harmless to someone who keeps a respectful distance. The rules below are about avoiding accidents and avoiding disturbance.

**Small segmented rafts.** These cling to vertical surfaces and pour over edges in shifting clusters. They are not hunting, but a raft that touches your skin or your gear will spread over it. Do not touch them. Step around rafts rather than through them, and watch your footing on any surface where they are pouring, because they make it slick. Keep your food sealed in case they respond to organic smells, which is unknown.

**Heavy shelled grazers.** These are low and boulder-heavy, and they move in slow grinding lines across the terraces. They leave deep grooves in the stone, and they don't alter course for obstacles. Learn the direction of their grooves and stay out of their path. If one approaches, move to the side and let it pass. Do not block it and do not stand between it and a ledge.

**Tall jointed walkers.** These sway like reeds and move on long legs. They seem indifferent to people, but their joints are strong, and they can knock you down without intending to. Give them a wide berth, and move calmly. Avoid sudden movements, which might be read as threats.

**Hanging colonies.** The colonies under the arches pulse with blue light, and their chorus rises and falls in waves. Do not stand beneath them for long. Do not disturb them with light, noise, or touch. The chorus may be a signal, a state, or simply their normal rhythm; you won't know which, so don't try to interpret it by provoking it. Keep a clear route around the colony's footprint.

**General rule.** Never place your hand or foot on anything that is moving unless you know what it is. The Shelf is patient, and most of its inhabitants will simply continue their business around you. A few will not notice you at all until you are in their way.

## Water

Everything here drips, and the seeps and pooled lakes are all mineral water of uncertain composition. Do not drink the local water, even after boiling, unless you have tested it with a kit you trust. Bring all the water you need in sealed containers.

Do not wade into the lakes. The salt crust over the surface may hold weight in some places and crack without warning in others. The rime fringes break and fall as the temperature shifts. Stand well back from the edge, and never rely on the crust for footing.

## Structures

The towers and bridges are not built for human use, and most of their spaces are closed to you in any practical sense. Do not enter shafts, chambers, or galleries. You cannot know what they do or whether they will change while you're inside.

Do not lean on the ribbed surfaces. Many of them have been smoothed by long use, and the smoothing suggests that they bear loads you can't see. Do not touch the blue seams. They may be warm or cold, and their pulse may change in response to contact.

The filament bridges are the most dangerous structures on the Shelf. They are hung in catenaries so fine that they vanish in mist. Never walk under or beside one without checking for it with your light, and never assume the space beneath a bridge is clear. The wire can cut through clothing and skin, and it can be cut by the same misjudgment that catches a careless arm.

Ramps lead to ledges that serve no obvious purpose. Do not climb them. The ledges may be the places where the Shelf's inhabitants gather, or where its maintenance happens, or they may simply be old. You don't know which, and the cost of guessing wrong is high.

## Time and Pace

Nothing here rushes. A visit that feels like an afternoon may be measured in hours of waiting for a grazer to pass or a raft to clear. Plan for this. Give yourself more time than you think you need, and set a firm turnaround time before you enter the terraces. Keep a timer on your person and check it at regular intervals.

Be wary of the slowness itself. The pulses, the drip, and the hum are hypnotic. Visitors who stop to watch them for too long lose track of time, cold, and direction. Set regular check-ins with yourself: check your body temperature, your footing, your bearing, and the time, every fifteen minutes.

## Companions and Communication

Do not go alone. Travel with at least one companion who is within sight or call at all times, and agree on signals in advance, since voices carry and echo unreliably. Simple hand signals work better than whistles, which the echoes will distort.

Test any communication device before you go. The structures' hum and the mist may interfere with radios, and the metallic architecture may block or reflect signals. Plan for the possibility that you'll have no contact with the outside world once you are on the terraces.

## Health

Watch for cold injury: numb fingers, shivering that stops, confusion, and unusual drowsiness. Warm up before you are cold, and eat regularly.

The mist and fine particles from fracturing crust can irritate your lungs and eyes. Cover your face when the air is thick, and rinse your eyes after exposure.

Skin contact with seep water or crust residue may cause irritation. Wash the affected area, and do not rub your eyes or face until your hands are clean.

Dark adaptation and blue light may cause headaches, dizziness, or nausea in some people, especially when the hum is strong. If you feel unwell, sit down in a dark, quiet place, and wait before moving.

## Leaving No Trace

The Shelf accumulates. Crust grows over the stone, and anything you leave will be buried in months or years. Take nothing you can't carry out, and leave nothing you can't retrieve. Do not collect samples of the crust, the lattice, or the creatures, since you have no way of knowing what disturbance they will cause.

Photograph carefully. Flash may be harmless, but it will flatten the blue light and may disturb the colonies. Use the lowest light setting your camera allows, and avoid pointing it at the creatures for long periods.

## The Feeling of the Place

You will feel small. That is the correct response. Everything here was underway long before you arrived and will continue long after you leave. You are a damp, temporary visitor, and the Shelf will not notice whether you return.

Prepare yourself for that. The visits that go wrong are often the ones where the visitor forgets how little they matter to the place, and begins to act as if the towers are waiting for them or the creatures are watching them. They are not. Move carefully, keep your distance, and let the enormous slow processes proceed around you. Then leave on time.
