# Preparing to Visit the Cinder Bowl

The Bowl is not hostile in any way you would recognize. It doesn't chase you, and most of what lives there will never notice you. The danger is that it is dense, hot, and constantly moving, and its ecology is built around the assumption that anything large enough to matter will be eaten or absorbed. Prepare for a place that is alive everywhere at once, and assume that the ground, the air, and the water are all part of the same system.

## Before You Go

**Find your anchor and your way back.** The rim is your only reliable landmark. The bowl's light gives no sun, no shadow, and no strong sense of direction, and the terraces and mounds shift their apparent shapes as the vents change. Mark your return path before you descend, and carry a second marker you can drop or fix to the rim itself. Do not rely on memory or on compass readings, which may drift near the vents.

**Choose your arrival point deliberately.** The rim has several possible entry slopes. The western slope is nearest the broken ring and has the fewest vents, which makes it the most forgiving. Avoid the eastern side, where the vapor is thickest and the mounds are highest.

**Know the thin air.** The air is hot and sparse, and your lungs will feel it within minutes. Practice slow, deep breathing before you go, and pace your movement. A traveler who exerts hard in the bowl will tire quickly, overheat, and begin to panic about breathing, which makes everything worse. Bring a cloth or mask for your mouth and nose. The fine glassy grit and the ash in the overcast are sharp, and the sulfur smell that sits under the fruit-rot scent can irritate your airways over an hour or two.

**Protect your eyes and skin.** The light is dim and the dust is abrasive. Goggles or a sealed visor are better than sunglasses, since the dusk never brightens and your eyes will be straining the whole time. Cover your skin fully, especially your ankles and hands. The glassy grit cuts, and the disc creatures' edges have a sting that can raise welts. Heavy boots with thick soles are essential, because the crust is hot and the mounds crackle and give underfoot.

## What to Carry

**Light.** The bowl does not produce its own illumination in a useful way, so bring your own. Test any flame before you rely on it. Sulfur in the air can make fires burn oddly, and a torch that works on the rim may sputter below. Cold lights and sealed lanterns are safer. Keep spare power or fuel, because you'll want to see the grooves on the stones and the mouths of the craters.

**Water.** The only moisture is the vent film, which is brackish, warm, and mineral-heavy. Do not drink it untreated. The hollows where the animals crowd are also not a place to collect water, since you will be competing with thousands of mouthparts and you will be pulled into the wave cycle. Bring far more water than you think you need, sealed and cool, and expect your intake to be higher than usual because the heat is constant.

**Containers.** Nothing in the bowl is metal, wire, or fired clay, and the land tends to reclaim unfamiliar material. Bring sealed containers for anything you intend to take out, and keep them closed in the basin. Do not leave gear lying on the crust. If you set something down, it will be covered by shifting bodies within minutes, and by the time you return it may be half-absorbed into a mound.

**Tools for careful movement.** A long staff or probe helps you test the crust before stepping, identify craters from burrowing brass forms, and nudge disc creatures without touching them. Carry rope, not for climbing but for retrieving yourself from a crater or a pit if you go through. Carry a flat board or a light sheet of material to spread your weight across soft mound surfaces.

**Food.** Eat before you go, and bring only what you can keep sealed. Nothing in the bowl is safe to eat as far as anyone knows, and the animals have no edible tissue you should consider. Plan on a short visit and a substantial meal on your return.

## Understanding the Creatures

Think of the fauna as a single organism with many parts, not as separate animals that will attack you one by one. Their behavior is driven by heat, moisture, and the pull of the vents. Most will move away from you if you are slow and steady, because you are another large mass in their way.

**Walkers and scrapers.** The small jointed forms in dense ranks will not bite you unless you press against them. They will crowd your feet if you stand still on the mounds, and they will lift and shift as you pass, leaving grit on your boots. Keep moving and do not kneel.

**Discs.** The flat, lichen-like creatures on the crust are the most dangerous. They wait motionless and fold over anything small that gets too close. A person will not be fully folded, but a foot or hand left in their path can be seized and covered. The crust under a folded disc hisses and darkens, and it stays hot for some time afterward. Never place your body on bare crust near a disc, and step around them with a probe rather than your boot.

**Brass burrowers.** The fat segmented forms tunnel under the mounds. Where they move, the ground heaves and collapses. Watch for fresh cracks and soft, sinking patches, and do not stand on a mound whose surface seems to breathe. If you feel the crust move beneath you, step sideways toward a stable terrace and keep your weight low.

**Paper flyers.** The pale, folded fliers spiral above the vents and settle in clusters. They are harmless in themselves, but their settling stirs dust, and they will drop onto you in numbers if you stand under a vent during a peak. Keep your visor down near the vapor.

## Timing Your Visit

The bowl runs on a cycle you can learn within a few minutes. Watch it from the rim before you commit to descent.

When the vents swell and push their vapor high, the windward creatures gather around the hot cracks and the cooler flank empties. This is the time to move along the cool side, not the windward one. When the vents falter, a wave rolls toward the moist hollows and the crowds at the water's edge thicken into a seething mass. Do not be caught inside a wave. If you see one coming, get onto a raised terrace or a high mound's shoulder, and wait for it to pass. Standing in its path will bury you in the moving crowd.

Plan your time in the basin around these swings. Arrive when the vents are easing, work the cool flank through the peak, and be on the rim before the next wave builds.

## Interacting with the Built Structures

The six standing stones and the archway are the only signs of intelligence here. They are fragile in a way that has nothing to do with force. Their grooves are filling with the bodies of the animals, and the stones are becoming part of the living mass. Do not chip, scrape, or rub the grooves, and do not try to pull the stones free. Even if you can reach them, moving them may disturb the creatures attached to them and bring the wave cycle down on you.

The archway at the centre is the most dangerous and the most interesting. The keystone is missing, and the passage beneath is dark. The tapping from inside is slow and regular, and it does not seem to be a warning. Do not enter the passage on a first visit. If you feel compelled to look inside, do so only from the threshold, with light and a rope tied to a fixed point outside. The tapping is the most significant sign of the Bowl's deeper life, and it deserves respect rather than investigation.

Do not remove shells, grit, or anything else from the basin. The shed skins and cemented bodies are the bowl's structure. Taking them is like taking pieces of a living wall, and you may find that what you carry out is changing in your pack.

## Dangers to Watch For

Most injuries here will come from three things: heat, falls, and getting caught by a wave.

Heat builds quickly. Rest in the shade of a stable mound or a terrace, drink regularly, and leave before you feel dizzy. If you notice your breath shortening or your thinking slowing, withdraw immediately, toward the cool flank and then up the slope.

Falls happen through the crust, into craters or thin places above burrows. Keep your weight on terraces where the rock is fused and solid, and avoid the crackling mounds unless you have a board or a clear sense of what is underneath. If you go through, do not thrash. The mounds will pull at you, and struggling only widens the hole. Use your rope and call for help.

Waves are the most sudden threat. Never linger near the moist hollows. If you are caught, move diagonally away from the crowd's center rather than straight back, because the edges are thinner and easier to escape.

## Leaving

Climb back toward the rim before the vents peak, and keep to the cool flank as you go. The vapor will rise past you, cool at the top and warm at the bottom, and the heat will follow you for some distance before it lets go. Do not look for a dramatic moment of escape. The bowl will not release you so much as lose interest in you as you get higher.

When you reach the plateau again, check yourself and your gear before you do anything else. Brush out your boots and clothing in open air, rinse your eyes, and inspect for welts or cuts from the discs. Check your containers and seal anything you carried. Even if nothing seems wrong, note the time and the state of the vents. You will want that information if you return.

The Bowl will still be awake when you leave it, and it will be awake when you come back. Go prepared, go briefly, and treat it as a living body rather than a place you can conquer or fully understand.
