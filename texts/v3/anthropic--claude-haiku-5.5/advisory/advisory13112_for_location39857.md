# Preparing to Visit the Singing Shelf

## Arriving and Getting Your Bearings

The approach is a climb. The plateau rises in steps, so you will likely arrive from below, and the first thing you'll notice is the thinning air. Take the climb slowly. Your heart will work harder than you expect, and headaches, shortness of breath, and a fuzzy head are common for the first day or two. Drink more water than feels necessary, eat before you start, and don't plan strenuous exertion on your first afternoon. If you're arriving by any method that skips the climb, give yourself a full day of acclimatization before you attempt anything physical.

Once you reach the top, there is nothing to orient you. No roads, no markers, no structures. The pillars look alike from a distance, and up close they look alike too. Before you leave any open area, fix a few reference points: the far line of the eastern cliffs, the gorge's rising mist, a particular pillar with a distinctive shape. Carry a compass and do not rely on the sun for direction, since it sits so high and so bright that its position is hard to read, and it moves more than you'd guess. Bring a notebook and mark your route with cairns or with a colored cord. The stone won't hold a mark, but you can leave one for yourself.

## Light and Eyes

This is the hardest part of the environment for most visitors. The tuff is pale and reflects almost as much light as it takes in, so the whole shelf is bright in a way that is uncomfortable within the first hour. Bring the darkest lenses you own, preferably wraparound, with side shields, since light bounces off the stone from below as well as above. Snow goggles or welding-grade lenses are better than sunglasses. A wide-brimmed hat helps but doesn't solve the problem.

The shadows are the other half of the trouble. They are short, black, and deep, and walking from glare into the shade of a pillar means being nearly blind for several seconds. Don't rush into shadows, and don't walk into them while looking at the bright ground. Stop at the edge, let your eyes adjust, and then step in. Carry a headlamp with a red filter so you can still see your footing in the shade without ruining your night vision.

Dusk is the best time to see the stone. The amber light makes the pillars glow from inside, and it is the one time the shelf looks warm. It is also the time when your depth perception is worst, so keep moving slowly if you're near any drop-off.

## Water, Mist, and Slick Stone

Expect water in three forms, and plan for each.

The first is the meltwater that runs across the plateau in thin threads and pools in basins of bare rock. It looks like weak tea because of the tannins or minerals it carries. Do not drink it untreated. Filter and purify everything, and carry more than you think you'll need, because the threads dry up unpredictably and the basins that look full at noon can be empty by evening.

The second is the afternoon drizzle. Every day, moisture climbs out of the eastern gorges and wraps the pillar forest in a fine mist that beads on the stone. It is not heavy, but it is constant for about an hour, and it will soak through an unprotected coat. Wear a light waterproof shell that breathes, and keep your electronics sealed. Plan to be sheltered or moving toward shelter in the afternoon, and keep your camera covered.

The third is the dew. Every surface is slick and dark before dawn and for an hour or two after. The tuff becomes like wet soap, and the lichen on the shady north faces is slippery enough to lose your footing on. Walk on the grass where you can, or wear boots with deep, soft lugs. Don't climb pillars in the morning. Wait until the sun has dried the stone to pale again, and remember that the drying happens unevenly. A patch can look dry while the crack beside it is still wet.

## The Sound

This is the thing that surprises people most, and you should prepare for it as if it were a physical hazard, because it is one. The pillars sing all the time, and in a strong wind the chord becomes a pressure you feel in your chest and your sinuses. Some tones are low enough to make your ribs hum. Others are thin and high enough to set your teeth on edge. Over this come the bellowing grazers, the wingbeats from the gorge, and the constant clatter of birds breaking shells on the cliffs. And beneath everything is the waterfall, which you can feel through your boots when you stand near the rim.

Bring earplugs rated for high-frequency sound, and a second pair for sleeping. Many people find their ears ache after a full day, and some get a lingering ringing that lasts into the next morning. You may also find the constant sound wears on your attention, making it hard to think or to hear other things you should be listening for, like the approach of a predator. Give yourself quiet time away from the open pillars, preferably in a sheltered spot on the leeward side of a stump or a broken pillar where the wind is lighter.

Be careful with the hollow pillars themselves. Do not put your mouth or any object into the tubes and do not strike them. The resonance is delicate, the tubes may be fragile, and you have no way of knowing what a change in one pillar's note might do to the others. Simply listening is enough.

## The Animals

The grazers are the largest creatures you'll meet, and they are not hostile, but they are very large and they are watching you. Keep at least fifty meters from any herd, and do not walk between a herd and the lee of a large pillar, since that is where they gather and where the grooved paths converge. The paths are worn into shallow grooves between the pillars, and they are used every day. Don't stand in them and don't block them. If a herd starts to move in your direction, step aside and let it pass. Their horns curve back over their heads, and a grazer's head can come up faster than you expect.

The long, low predators are harder to see. You'll sometimes catch a movement at the edge of the grassland, and the herds will watch the same spot intently before going back to grazing. Learn to read this. When the herds stop feeding and turn their heads together, something is nearby. Move toward a pillar, get your back to stone, and wait. Don't run unless you're already in cover, because running is what draws attention.

The cliff colonies are dense and noisy, and their ledges are crowded. Do not approach the nesting edges or the shell-breaking heaps at the base of the cliffs. The birds are protective of the anvil stones, and they drop shells and eggs from the ledges above. Wear a hard hat or at least a thick cap if you walk beneath the colonies, and keep your head covered in the gorge area, where the drops are heaviest. The shell heaps themselves are not tourist sites. They are working tools, rubbed bright by generations of use, and disturbing them is both dangerous and rude.

The small animals are easy to overlook. Lizards blend into the lichen. Beetles graze on the algae in the dew. Rodents dig through the white dust drifts in the lee of the pillars, and the drifts are unstable, so a hand or a boot can sink into a hole you didn't know was there. Watch your footing in the dust.

## The Plants

The vegetation is tough and slow-growing, and it is easy to damage without meaning to. The cushion plants are tight domes of grey-green leaves, and each one may be decades old. Do not step on them, lean on them, or pull them up. The tussock grasses in the hollows around the pillars are the grazers' food and shelter, so leave them standing. The silver-leaved shrubs in the lee of the large columns hold the snow that feeds the plateau's few pools, so cutting or trampling them has effects that extend beyond the individual plant.

The lichen is the most fragile thing on the shelf. It grows in rings and rosettes on the stone, and it grows very slowly. Don't touch it with bare skin, and don't scrape it. Your oils and your weight can kill a patch that took a century to form. Take photographs if you want a record. Don't take samples.

## Night

The shelf does not go quiet at night. The wind keeps moving, the chord continues, and the waterfall roars on under a sky so full of stars that the dark looks like frost on glass. Sleep is possible, but you will need to plan for it. Choose a sheltered camp on the leeward side of a large pillar or in a stump's lee, away from the grazers' paths and from the cliff edges. Pitch your shelter so the door faces away from the wind, and bring a sleeping mat, because the tuff is cold through the night and the dew soaks everything.

Don't sleep inside the hollow pillars, even though some of the tubes are wide enough to crawl into. They amplify the chord, so you will hear every change of wind as a physical event inside your skull, and you may not sleep at all. Some tubes may also be unstable or may hold moisture and small animals. If you need the shelter, use it briefly and leave.

Expect the night to feel unchanging in a way that is hard to explain to anyone who hasn't been there. The sound is the same, the stars are the same, and the only sign of time is the slow shift of the sky. Many travelers find this calming. Some find it disorienting, and it can make the hours feel longer or shorter than they are. Keep a watch and check it. Write down when you wake up.

## What to Bring

Pack for cold, wet, bright, and loud. A lightweight waterproof shell, a warm layer for the night, a hat, goggles with side shields, a red-filtered headlamp, earplugs (two pairs), a water filter and extra purification tablets, boots with deep lugs, a compass, a notebook, a cord for marking routes, a first-aid kit with altitude-sickness medication, and a sleeping mat. Bring a hard hat or cap for the cliff areas. Keep electronics in sealed bags. Bring food that doesn't need cooking if you plan to be out in the drizzle, and remember there is no wood or fuel on the shelf, so bring a stove and fuel for the whole trip.

## A Last Note

Nothing here was built to receive you. The shelf has no shelters, no paths, no signs, no water supply, and no help. It runs on a long cycle that has no interest in visitors, and that indifference is part of what makes it both beautiful and dangerous. You will be a small, warm, noisy thing in a place that has been making the same sound for longer than anyone can count. Go with humility, move slowly, leave the stone and the plants as you found them, and expect to be changed a little by the sense that the place is holding its breath and will go on long after you leave.
