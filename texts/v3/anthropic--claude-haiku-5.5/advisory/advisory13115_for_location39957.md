# Preparing to Visit the Shelf of Weeping Stone

This is a place you enter by walking into a wet green wall of sound and life. It is cold, loud, and at altitude, and nearly everything in it is alive and growing. You will not be able to stay out of the plants, the water, or the animals, so prepare to work with them. The people here have lived with this environment for generations, and most of what keeps a visitor alive is simply following their habits.

## Before You Go

**Timing.** Plan around the storm. Each season a storm tears down limbs and carries off a bridge or two, and the people rebuild before the next one comes. Arrive in the days after a storm, when the bridges are being patched and the market is busy with repair crews, and leave well before the next season's storm is due. If you arrive during a storm, go to the lowest terrace and stay there until it passes.

**Clothing.** The air is cold and saturated, and it stays cold after dark. Dress in layers that keep their insulation when wet: plant-fiber wool, felted cloth, or synthetic-equivalent fabrics if your home universe has them. Cotton holds water and will chill you. Bring a waterproof outer layer that covers your neck and hood, since mist drips off every leaf. Long sleeves and long trousers are essential, both against the sun-warmed stings of insects and against leeches on the wet paths.

**Footwear.** The ground is never dry. Stone is slick with moss, roots are slippery, and the bridges are rounded and sometimes soft. Bring boots with aggressive tread and high ankles, and carry a second pair. Gaiters or trouser ties that seal the boot top will help keep leeches out.

**Insect protection.** Pack a head net, fine-mesh gloves, and a repellent that works on midges. The midges rise at dusk in clouds and will get into your eyes and nose. Locals burn smoke-pots, and you should carry one or two of your own. Do not rely on the smoke alone; swarms of moths and small birds are dangerous in numbers.

**Light.** The canopy keeps the ground dim even in the day, and the cloud makes the light flat. Bring a reliable lamp with spare fuel or batteries. Glowing beetles give a faint green light in the shade, which is useful for finding your way but not for reading or working.

**Supplies they lack.** This is a metal-free culture. Ask yourself what would be useful to them and what would be disruptive. Useful goods include salt, dried fruit, medicines, fine cord, bone or hard-wood needles, and sharp-edged stone or flint if you can obtain it legitimately. Trade goods that the terraces cannot make for themselves will carry the most weight. Be cautious about introducing metal, because it changes the economy and power balance around the springs in ways that are hard to undo.

**Seeds and organisms.** This is the most important precaution. The forest takes back any walkway left unwalked, and it will colonize anything that gets a foothold. Brush your boots and clothes thoroughly before entering the cirque. Do not bring plant material, soil, or live cuttings, and do not carry seeds in pockets, pack seams, or hair. An introduced species could spread faster than the people can manage.

## Arrival

Most visitors arrive either by the river raft or by walking up from the lower slopes. The river falls out of the notch into cloud, so the raft is the easiest way in, and the market on the lowest terrace is the usual landing point. Do not attempt the notch on foot or by any improvised means. If you arrive from a height, expect to come down through cloud and land in wet vegetation near the water.

Once inside, the first hour is disorienting. The bowl is about four hundred paces across, but the vegetation makes it feel smaller, and the sound is constant. Take your time. Breathe slowly, because the air is thin and you will tire quickly on the stairs and climbs. Drink more water than you think you need, and rest in the shade before you climb to the upper terraces.

## Moving Through the Terraces

The eleven terraces are retaining walls of old, weathered stone. Do not lean on them, climb their faces, or dig into them. The balance between wall and pressure has held for generations, and small disturbances can cause bulges to fail. Walk the level strips behind the walls, and use the ladders and bridges the locals use.

**Bridges.** The footbridges are living roots, thick as a thigh and thickening each year. They are safe in ordinary conditions, but they are rounded and slick, and they sway with the weight of crowds. Cross them at the speed of the people around you. Do not stop in the middle of a bridge to take a photograph or look at the view. If you see crowds of tree-climbers crossing, let them go first and do not block them.

**Ladders.** Climbing shoots pruned into rungs are sturdy when properly grown, but they are old and damp. Test each rung before you trust it, and keep three points of contact at all times.

**Paths.** The paths are maintained by walking. Stay on the marked routes. Stepping off them into the undergrowth is not dangerous in itself, but it can hasten the forest's return and may trample grazers or nesting space. If you must leave a path, go slowly and watch where you put your feet.

**Canopy.** Large limbs crack and fall at unpredictable intervals, often with a heavy wet sound and a burst of scattering animals. When you hear a sharp crack in the canopy, step quickly into an open area or under a solid roof. Avoid standing directly under the largest trees during wind, and never shelter under a dead-looking limb.

## The Fauna

The animals are overwhelming in number but rarely dangerous one-on-one. The nectar birds are beautiful and harmless. The moths are slow and will not attack you, but do not press against their resting walls, and do not wave your arms at them. A swarm of moths or small birds that is startled can strip exposed skin and eyes quickly, so keep your face covered and move calmly.

The tree-climbing animals with long tails move in crowds across the bridges and will ignore you unless you corner them. Do not feed them. The frogs are everywhere, and their chorus is constant. Leeches gather on wet paths in the morning, so tuck your trousers into your boots, check your legs every few hours, and remove leeches by sliding a fingernail under the sucker. Do not pull them off, because that tears the skin. Salt, if you have it, will make them let go.

Under the litter, things with many legs move constantly. Do not put your hands into litter or into the fungal shelves. The grazers on the shelves scatter when approached, so you will rarely meet them, but do not reach under a shelf to grab anything.

## Food and Water

Water is everywhere, and it runs from the walls, the trees, and the stone. Locals drink from the springs, and some of these are controlled and used by specific households. Do not drink from a spring without asking, and do not foul or dam any water source. Whoever controls the springs controls the terraces, and disrupting a spring is a serious offense.

Food is dried fish and fungus, bread-like starch from a root, and fruit from the lower terraces. Many fungal shelves are edible, but the local varieties are known to locals and not to you. Eat only what you are given by someone who knows you are eating it, and do not harvest fungus yourself. If you have a sensitive stomach, bring your own food, and keep it sealed away from insects.

## Sleep and Shelter

Houses are living lattices of woven stems with a clay skin. They creak, shift, and grow. Sleep on a raised platform or mat rather than directly on the floor, since the ground is damp and the floor is often wet. Keep your bag and clothes off the walls, because the moths and beetles like to rest there. A house left untended collapses into the undergrowth, so if you are staying in one, expect that someone is maintaining it and offer to help if you can. Sleeping in a house will be loud: frogs, birds, drips, and falling limbs continue through the night. Earplugs are useful if you can tolerate them.

## Etiquette and Work

The town is always in motion. People are cutting, planting, re-weaving, patching walls, and harvesting. If you are going to take wood, replant at least as much as you take, or better yet, let someone who knows the process do the cutting. Offer to help with real work: carrying, weaving, tying, or cleaning up. Watching someone's work for too long can seem like judgment, so be respectful of their routine.

The market on the lowest terrace is loud, quick, and sometimes aggressive. Bargaining is expected, and it is often done with raised voices and gestures. Do not take offense at volume. Watch what others are doing before you enter a bargain. Do not pick up goods without intent to buy. If a dispute breaks out, step back and let the people involved settle it.

Do not touch the walls or the springs without permission. Do not enter houses uninvited. Do not cut, pull, or pluck living plants, and do not take anything from the cliff face, even if it looks dead. The place is built on a balance, and visitors who change it without understanding it tend to cause damage that takes seasons to repair.

## Health and Safety

Cold, wet, and altitude are the main physical threats. Watch for hypothermia, especially after dark and in the afternoon mist. Change out of wet clothes as soon as you can, and keep a dry layer for sleeping. Watch your breathing on climbs, and stop to rest if you feel dizzy or short of breath. Fungal infections are common on wet skin, so dry your feet daily and treat any rash early. Insect bites should be cleaned and watched, since infections spread quickly in damp conditions.

Keep a small kit with antiseptic, bandages, medication for allergic reactions, and anything specific to your body. Carry a whistle or other signal if you plan to go off the main paths, and always move in a group where possible. The smoke-pots are not decoration; keep one lit near you when moving through the dusk midges.

## Departure

Plan your departure before the mist rises or the afternoon storm builds. Check your body and gear for leeches, seeds, and insects before you leave the cirque. Brush your boots and clothes thoroughly, and do not carry anything out of the terraces that was not given to you or bought openly. Thank the people who helped you, especially the ones who showed you the bridges and the springs.

The Shelf of Weeping Stone will not remember you, but it is a place that depends on careful, continuous attention. The people there have made a stable life out of constant small motion. Visit gently, take little, and leave the terraces as you found them, still growing and still being mended.
