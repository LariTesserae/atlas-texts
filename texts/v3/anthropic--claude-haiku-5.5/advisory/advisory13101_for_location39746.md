# Preparing to Visit the Sunken Basin

You will be going down into a place that is still being made. The rock is building itself around the people who live there, and everything you bring will be slowly absorbed into it. Prepare for heat, wet, noise, and the particular unease of standing on ground that is not yet finished.

## Before You Go Down

**Your return point.** The rim is the exit, but the way up is not fixed. Routes change as terraces thicken and seeps shift, and a path that was clear last season may be a crust-field now. Before descending, ask a local guide for the current route, and ask them to show you the landmarks: the tallest pipe bracket, the pump shed with the blue door, the vent that always leaks. Memorize them. Your sense of direction will fail in the echoing dim, and the walls will seem to speak from the wrong side.

**Your timing.** Visit in the early morning if you can. The pools steam most in the cool hours, the light is strongest through the upper fractures before the heat builds, and the bats are still roosting. Avoid dusk unless you want to be inside a cloud of midges and a wave of bats at once, which some people find thrilling and most find exhausting. Plan to stay no longer than a few hours on your first visit. The heat accumulates in the body faster than you expect.

**Your footing.** Bring boots with thick soles, deep lugs, and a closed toe. The travertine is not uniformly hard. Some of it is glassy and sure. Some is crust over warm water and will crack under you without warning. Some is still soft, a surface that takes your boot print and slowly fills with seep. Never step onto a new terrace without testing it with a pole or a stick. Keep one hand free for the cliff brackets, which are the most reliable things to hold, and never trust a pipe that is half-encased in white growth, since the growth may have locked it to rock that is also moving.

## What to Wear and Carry

**Clothing.** Wear light, loose, quick-drying layers in a material that does not hold water against the skin. Cotton will sit wet on you all day and chafe. Synthetic fabrics are better, though they will smell after a day. A wide-brimmed hat helps with the dripping from the cliffs and the falling flecks of guano. Wear long sleeves and trousers tucked into your boots, both for the midges and for the sharp edges of the iron.

**Eye and ear protection.** The machinery is loud and the echoes arrive half a second late, so every sudden sound seems to come from the far wall. Bring earplugs for the pump sheds and a light pair of muffs for the upper terraces. Wear sunglasses or tinted goggles when you come up into the shafts of light, which are much brighter than the floor and can make you flinch and lose your footing.

**Breathing.** The guano banks smell sharp and can irritate the throat, and the bat colonies stir the air in waves. A light cloth over the nose and mouth is useful in the roosting galleries. Keep a water bottle, but treat any water from the pools as unsafe. Drink only from sealed supply taps in the settlement.

**Carry.** Bring a headlamp with a red setting, which the local creatures will not react to as harshly as white light. Pack spare batteries, a small first-aid kit with antiseptic and blister dressings, electrolytes, salt tablets, a notebook in waterproof wrapping, and a pole with a metal tip. Bring a soft bag for anything you intend to leave, and do not bring anything you cannot lose.

## The Heat

The heat is not dry. It is close and wet, like being inside a mouth. Sweat stays on the skin and does not cool you, so your body will overheat faster than you think. Drink small amounts often. Rest in the shade of the sheds every half hour. Watch for dizziness, confusion, a sudden lack of sweat, or a rapid pulse. If you feel any of these, sit down at once, in the coolest place you can find, and tell someone. The residents know the warm seeps and the cool drafts from the shafts, and will guide you to them.

Be especially careful around the warm pools. The water is pleasant and tempting, but it is heated from below and may carry bacteria and unknown organisms. Do not put your hands or feet in it. Do not swim. Do not let the frilled, blind creatures near your skin, even if they look harmless. The residents keep the sumps clear for the pumps' sake, and they do not like visitors to disturb them.

## Creatures and Animals

Everything here moves. Moths the size of your palm will be drawn to your lamp and will settle on your clothes and face. Let them go. Do not swat, since their dust can irritate the eyes and their bodies are fragile. Midges rise from the pools in clouds at dusk and will settle on any exposed skin. A fine mesh cap or a thin scarf helps. Bats roost in the cliff cracks and come out in waves, often over the low ground. Stay still when they pass; they are not aggressive, but they will startle you badly if you flinch into them.

Birds nest in the pipe-brackets and shed rafters. Their calls are sharp and frequent. Do not approach nests. Do not reach into gaps in the pipes, and do not stand under the brackets where the birds are fledging.

The pale things in the pools move in unison in slow ripples. They are beautiful and unsettling. Watch them but do not touch them, and do not stand close enough to the water for them to rise toward you.

## The Machinery and the Rhythm

The pumps cycle over hours. Their engines swell and fall, and the whole basin seems to breathe with them. Learn the rhythm if you can. When the pumps are at the top of the cycle, the noise is at its worst and speech is nearly impossible. When they fall, you hear the cliffs dripping and the water running over stone, and the silence feels enormous. Plan your conversations and your steps around this.

Do not touch the pumps, the chain drives, or the steam valves. They are hot, moving, and maintained by people who know them well. Stay behind the painted line at each shed. If a valve whistles, step back and wait for it to settle.

## Among the Residents

The people here do not experience the basin as you will. They hear the drip as a comfort, and the echoes as ordinary. They will not hear you when you cry out, so you must learn to signal with your hands and to stand where they can see you. Speak slowly and clearly when the pumps are low, and expect them to speak with their hands near the machinery.

They build with cheap materials and spare parts stacked for need. This is not resignation. It is care. A new shed is a gift of time and labor, and it will be taken back eventually, but people still build it, because the building is what matters. Respect this. Do not photograph their repairs without asking. Do not offer to fix something unless you are asked. Do not criticize the gaps between buildings; they are there so that when the rock pushes, the walls can still stand.

If you are invited to leave something, be honest about it. Gifts that are meant to last will be swallowed by the stone, so bring things meant to be used and then given away: food, tools, a good pair of gloves, a story to tell. The basin will keep what it wants.

## Leaving Your Mark

Nothing stays put here. Do not leave carvings, paint, or markers expecting them to endure. Do not bury anything. Do not wedge objects into cracks, since the growth will close around them and the next flood will move them somewhere else. If you feel the urge to leave a trace, leave it in the mind: write it down, tell someone, and let the place take the rest.

Some visitors want to drop a piece of their own world into the basin, a coin or a ribbon, as a token of having been here. These will be encased within a season. If you want to mark your passage, do it in a way that the residents can keep: help clear a sump, carry a load up the cliff, teach a child a knot. The work will remain even when the rock covers the tool.

## The Stillness

The basin feels like a room someone has left running. Nothing is abrupt, and that is what unsettles people most. Days here pass slowly, the light shifts through the shafts without ever reaching full strength, and you lose the thread of time. Set an alarm on your watch to remind you when to climb. Keep a simple count of hours in your notebook.

If you feel anxious, sit with the drip for a while. The residents say it helps. If you feel lost, find the nearest bracket of pipe and follow it; it leads somewhere. If you feel the pull of staying longer than you planned, remember that the stone will be here next season, and so will the people. You can come back.

Go down carefully. Come up slowly. Take the sound of the cliffs with you when you leave, and you will find you cannot quite stop hearing it.
