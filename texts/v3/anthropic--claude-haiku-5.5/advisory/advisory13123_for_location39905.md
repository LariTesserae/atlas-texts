# Preparing to Visit the Sinking Room

## What You Are Walking Into

The Sinking Room is a collapsed cavern about forty meters deep and the width of a village square. It is warm, wet, dim at the bottom, and full of life that is careful, patient, and very interested in what is near it. Nothing here is hostile in the ordinary sense. The danger is mostly in how easily a visitor can damage something, get hurt in slick terrain, or lose the thread of time. Prepare for a long, slow visit, not a quick look.

Most of the preparation is about three things: keeping your body safe in heat and humidity, keeping the place unchanged by your presence, and keeping your own sense of where and when you are.

## Before You Go

**Research the timing.** The grove is best understood at its rhythms. Plan to arrive in the late morning, when the sky-ring is bright and the shafts of light are strongest, and plan to leave before the amber hour, unless you have specifically prepared for the odd hours described below. The frogs begin at dusk, and that is a fine sound, but it means dusk is when the place changes character.

**Check the weather above.** Rain falls straight through the ring and the basin fills like a bowl. The seep from the north wall runs faster after rain, and the pool's level climbs about an inch an hour. Do not enter during or within a day of heavy rain. Ask someone who has been on the plain above, or watch the sky for at least a full day before your descent.

**Tell someone your schedule.** Give a specific return time to a person on the surface or in your home world. Cavern basins like this one can disrupt signals, compasses, and sometimes the ordinary sense of how long you have been gone. If you have a way to mark a fixed point of contact, such as a rope or a timed check-in, use it.

**Plan for a long climb out.** The stair is forty meters of rising stone, wet and uneven, and the air is heavy the whole way. Most people underestimate the return. Budget at least as long for the climb as for the descent, and more if you are carrying weight or are tired.

## Gear

**Footwear** is the most important choice you will make. The limestone is smooth where water runs and rough where it doesn't, and the moss hides both. Wear boots with aggressive treads that you can clean thoroughly. Bring a second pair of socks, and assume your feet will be wet all day.

**Clothing** should be light, long-sleeved, and quick-drying, in a neutral color that does not attract insects or startle the small animals. Cover your neck and wrists. Avoid cotton, which will hold water and chill you when you climb out. Gloves should be thin enough to feel the stone, since you will need to brace yourself on wet walls.

**Biosecurity** matters more here than almost anywhere. The grove is a closed system of interlocking lives. The strangler figs, the eggs in the pool, the spiders, and the beetles on their worn route are all part of a balance that a foreign seed, spore, or insect could break. Before you descend:
- Brush and inspect your clothing, boots, and pack for seeds, soil, and organic debris from your home world and from the surface.
- Do not bring any plant material, food scraps, or live animals.
- Pack everything in sealed containers so that nothing can escape unnoticed.
- Plan to inspect yourself and your gear at the top of the stair before you return to the world you came from.

**Light.** Bring a headlamp with a red or amber filter. White light disturbs the animals, washes out the faint glow of the moss, and makes the pool hard to read. Keep it off when you can, since the grove's natural light is enough for most of the day.

**Water and food.** Bring everything you will eat and drink, sealed. Do not eat fruit, fungi, moss, or anything else you find growing. Do not drink from the pool or the seeps, even though they look clean. Carry enough water for the climb plus a margin, because heat will drain you faster than you expect.

**Insect protection** is useful, but choose repellents with care. Strong chemicals can kill the midges and small insects that feed the fish and the spiders, and they can wash into the pool. Mosquito netting over your face is a better choice than spray.

**Medical kit.** The grove will test your skin. Pack antiseptic, bandages that stay on in wet conditions, antifungal cream, and something for stings and bites. Scrapes that break the skin can become infected quickly in standing water. Carry a small waterproof case with a basic first-aid kit, and check it before you go.

**Emergency gear.** Bring a sealed emergency blanket, a whistle, and a signaling light. Assume your compass may behave strangely near the basin, and do not depend on it alone. Mark your route if you can do so without leaving marks on the stone or the plants.

## Moving Through the Grove

**Stay on the worn paths.** The moss covers loose stone and root. Where there is no clear path, test each step with your weight before you commit. Look for the places where animals have already walked, because they know the ground better than you do.

**Keep your hands off the walls and the plants.** The ferns, vines, and stone are alive in layers. A hand on a fern can crush a colony. A hand on a wall can dislodge a spider's nest or a snail that has taken years to settle. If you need to steady yourself, use a trunk you have checked for loose bark, or brace with your forearm rather than your palm.

**Respect the pool.** The shallow black water looks like an easy crossing, but the bottom is soft and the eggs under the lily pads are fragile. Do not wade. Do not reach into the water. The long, pale shape beneath the altar is not something to test. If you need to observe the pool, do it from the bank, keeping your distance.

**Do not touch the slab.** The altar, step, or marker in the middle of the pool is held together by the fig that grows through it, and the fig is holding it up. Pulling roots, moving stone, or leaning on the slab could bring it down or tear the tree apart. Look at it from the bank and leave it as it is.

**Do not disturb the beetles.** The line of small bright-backed beetles along the north ledge follows a route that has been worn into the stone over a very long time. If you put a hand or a stone in their path, you break the line, and you may break something larger than you can see. Watch them, but step around their path and do not add anything to it.

**Do not build.** The square-cut footings on the ledge look like the beginnings of something. You may feel the urge to finish them, or to set a stone on top, or to mark them with your own sign. Resist it. Whatever this place once was, it is no longer a place that wants visitors to build, and anything you add will be treated like any other foreign thing that enters the grove.

## Light, Sound, and Time

The sky-ring is your clock. The shafts of light crawl across the grove over the course of the day, and the pool reflects them in pieces. Use them to track time, and check them often, because the grove has no clocks and your sense of duration will drift.

The quiet is part of the place. Speak softly. Move slowly. The grove responds to sudden noise by going still, and when many small things stop at once, the silence can feel like a warning. You do not need to whisper, but avoid shouting, clapping, or dropping gear. Your footsteps will carry through the moss, so walk with care.

Expect the frogs at dusk. Their calls will rise after the bats leave the ceiling. This is a normal change, not an emergency, but it marks the end of the day's usable light and the beginning of the cooler, stranger hours.

## The Odd Hours

Sometime in the amber light, when the air is still, the moss along the walls will begin to glow. The pool may reflect a deeper blue than the sky, with a pattern in it like the ghost of a much larger room. The beetles will stop. The bats will return to the ceiling in silence. The whole grove will seem to listen.

This is the most important part of the visit to prepare for, and the hardest to describe. In these moments your own sense of what is above and below can slip. The reflection may seem to show a ceiling much higher than the sky-ring. You may feel that something above is looking down.

If you are present during the odd hours, do the following:

- **Stop moving.** Stand still and let the grove finish its listening. Movement during this time seems to draw attention and can make the light flicker.
- **Look at your feet, your hands, and the stone close to you** rather than the pool's reflection. Keep one reference that belongs to the grove itself: a fern, a root, a crack in the wall.
- **Do not step into the reflected light.** If the glow appears to brighten where you intend to walk, wait.
- **Keep track of your own body.** Breathe, feel the wet cloth against your skin, and count your heartbeats if you need something to hold on to.
- **Remember how you arrived.** The stair is your way back, and its stones are the only solid path out.

When the light fades and the bats come back out, the grove will exhale. The sound will return, and the place will feel ordinary again. Do not assume that this means you are fully back, though. Check your gear, your count of the hours, and the position of the sky-ring before you move.

## Weather and Water

Rain is the most serious natural risk. It falls through the ring, the seep swells, and the pool rises. If you see clouds gathering above the basin, begin your climb at once. Do not wait to see whether the rain will stop. Water moves faster on the stair than you can, and the walls become slick enough to slide on.

If rain catches you below, move to the highest dry ground you can reach, away from the pool and the seep. Stay low and keep your gear dry. Wait for the water level to fall, which will happen over an hour or more as the stone drains.

## If Something Goes Wrong

**Injury.** Clean and bind any wound at once. Do not wait for the climb. If you cannot walk, stay where you are, signal, and conserve your water. Someone on the surface needs to know where you are, so be sure your check-in plan includes a rescue trigger.

**Disorientation.** If you lose track of the stair, stop, sit down, and look for the sky-ring. The stair runs up and out from the western side of the basin, along the same wall the vines hang from. Follow the wet steps upward, keeping the walls on your left hand.

**Heat exhaustion.** Drink, rest in shade, loosen your clothing where you can without exposing your skin to insects, and wait until your breathing settles before climbing again.

**Strange perceptions during the odd hours.** Stop, breathe, and hold on to a fixed object in the grove. Wait for the glow to fade. If the feeling persists, move slowly toward the stair and climb, keeping your eyes on the steps rather than the walls or the reflection.

## Leaving

Climb slowly. The stair is slippery, and your legs will be tired. Stop often and let your breathing recover. At the top, before you rejoin the surface, stop and inspect yourself and your gear. Brush off seeds, soil, insects, and any plant material. Check your skin for ticks, bites, and scratches. Leave nothing behind, and take nothing away except your own impressions, written down while they are fresh.

The grove will go on without you. The fig will keep tightening, the seep will keep running, and the beetles will keep walking the line along the ledge. The best visitors are the ones who leave the Sinking Room as they found it, and who come back up the stair carrying a sense of how much life can exist in a place that the rest of the world has forgotten.
