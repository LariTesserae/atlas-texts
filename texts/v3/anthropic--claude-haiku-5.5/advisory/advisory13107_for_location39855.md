# Preparing to Visit the Weeping Shelf

The Shelf is not dangerous in the way most hostile places are. Nothing here is actively trying to kill you, and most of its risks come from the ground moving, the water arriving without warning, and the place being very good at absorbing whatever it touches. Prepare for a landscape that is always changing, and plan your visit around its rhythms instead of your own.

## Before You Go

**Understand the rain.** The most important thing to learn is the rain cycle. The downpours come in pulses, not on any schedule you'll recognize from the seasons. Each lasts ten to twenty minutes and comes with little warning, though you may notice the air thicken and the drainage sound rise through the stone just before. Between pulses the shelf drains with a rushing, bubbling noise. Learn that noise before you arrive, because after a downpour it will be the only signal that the water is leaving the basins you're standing in. Keep a count of pulses, so you can estimate when the next one is due. Your best protection is timing: plan movement for the drained intervals and sheltering for the wet ones.

**Know that the light won't help you.** The sky is a bronze-gray haze with no real sun, and the light comes from no direction. Shadows are soft and brief, so they can't tell you the time or your heading. Solar chargers will underperform, and the diffuse light will strain your eyes over long periods. Bring a lamp with a strong, directional beam, a compass (test it on arrival, since the iron in the plinths may throw it off), and a timekeeper that doesn't rely on the sky. Track the day by counting rains.

**Dress for wet heat.** The air is warm, heavy, and saturated, and everything you own will become damp within hours. Wear lightweight, quick-drying layers. Bring dry storage in sealed bags, silica or equivalent desiccant, and a spare set of everything that matters. Expect mold and fabric rot on anything that stays damp overnight.

**Footwear is critical.** The ground is a hardening mineral crust that cracks and gives under weight before it sets. Wear boots with wide, soft soles that spread your load, and bring a long pole to probe the crust before you step, especially near pool edges where the crust may be thin. Carry a rope or harness in case you break through.

**Prepare a death protocol.** This is the least comfortable preparation and the most important. Anything that dies on the Shelf is absorbed by the mineral film within days, which means there is no body to recover, bury, or return. If you are injured or killed here, your team may never find a trace of you. Before you go, arrange a check-in schedule with a contact who knows exactly where you are, what time you're expected back, and what to do if you don't return. Leave identification with that contact, not with you. Anything you carry that you cannot afford to lose may also be lost into the stone, so keep the critical items in duplicate in a secure place outside the Shelf.

**Pack for food and water with suspicion.** Pool water carries silt, dissolved minerals, and the film, and you should not assume it's safe. Bring sealed rations and treat any water you collect with a full purification process. Do not eat the crustaceans, the mat bulbs, or the reed-plants. You don't know what they are, and you don't know what the film has been doing to them.

**Bring earplugs, and bring them for sleep, not for daytime.** The Shelf is loud from morning to night. The clicking, the whistling, and the booming are constant. Earplugs will help you rest, but in daylight they'll hide the rain drainage and the approaching flocks, and the booming carries information you need. Use them selectively.

## Arrival and Orientation

The Shelf is a broad, tilted table about four kilometers across, with cliffs that look freshly broken. Arrive at the highest accessible point you can, because the water will collect in the low places, and the basins that fill fastest are where you least want to be. Survey the terrain from the edge before you step onto the open stone, and note which ledges have slumped recently, which pools are widening, and where the water is channeling.

Expect fog in the mornings. It rises from the cracks in the stone and can reduce your visibility to a few meters. Burn-off comes by midday, and the fog returns at dusk. Plan your movements around these windows, and don't rely on landmarks you saw in the morning, because the ground will have shifted by afternoon.

Mark your route with tall, flagged poles, not cairns. Stacked stone will be absorbed into the rock or buried in the swellings that grow around fixed objects. Set your markers in stable ground, away from pool edges, and check their positions each time you pass. Your path back should be two or three independent references, such as a compass bearing from a known cliff face, a flagged pole line, and a memorized sequence of rain pulses.

## Moving Across the Shelf

**Stay back from the cliffs.** The edges are unstable and can slump without warning, especially after rain or during the booming. Keep at least ten meters from any ledge.

**Avoid the pool centers.** The silt is churning and the crust is thin. The water rises and falls, and the edges can flood within minutes of a downpour.

**Don't step on the film.** The gray film on the pools and ridges responds to disturbance. When you break its surface, it flows toward the disturbance and knits itself closed, leaving a harder, smoother surface behind. If it flows over your boots, don't try to pull free quickly. Lift slowly and move in a wide arc, and assume it will keep its shape after you leave. Do not touch it with bare skin. The film is not known to be harmful to humans, but it is known to draw dead matter into the stone, and you should assume it will do the same to a dead traveler.

**Stay off the mats.** The pale mats around pool edges drift toward the water over time, and their studded bulbs fill and empty with the rain. Walking on them crushes the bulbs and may draw the mat toward you. More importantly, the stone-colored hunters move low through the mats, and they are very hard to see in the bronze light.

**Move at a steady pace.** The shelf is not hostile to quick movement, but sudden motion near the water tends to set off the crustaceans, and a silenced pool is a signal of danger. When the ticking stops abruptly, stop with it. Wait, watch, and listen for the rattling wave that follows. Then move on.

## The Living Things

**The crustaceans.** The pools are full of slate-colored, many-legged creatures that click continuously. They are not aggressive toward you, but they are very numerous, and they will swarm if you enter the water. Keep out of the pools. When they go silent, treat it as a warning, not a sign of peace.

**The flocks.** Membranous, iridescent gliders ride the updrafts from the cliffs. They call in long, descending whistles. They are not known to attack, but their wings are fragile and their numbers are large. Avoid walking beneath their flight paths near the cliffs, where the updrafts can push loose debris down on you.

**The hunters.** They are stone-colored and low, and they move through the mats tracking the crustaceans. They are rarely seen, and you may not know one is near until a pool goes silent. Keep away from the mats and the pool edges, and do not crawl or lie prone on the open stone. Stand, keep your head up, and move in the open.

**The booming.** A deep pulse arrives every few minutes from somewhere under the Shelf, and it shakes loose pebbles from the ledges. The source is probably the creatures in the fractures that feed on the water. Do not put your ear to the ground or try to locate the sound by its vibration. Stay clear of ledges during the booms, and keep your helmet on.

**The dusk chorus.** At evening, the calls of the flocks and the rhythmic din from the stone merge into a volume that can be painful. Plan to be sheltered before it begins, and bring hearing protection for it. Be prepared, though, for the fact that the chorus will mask the sound of rain drainage and approaching hazards. You'll need to rely on the rain count and your timing, not your ears, during the chorus.

## The Built Traces

**Plinths.** The dark metal plinths are scattered across the shelf, some knee-high, some shoulder-high. Their etched patterns shift when you look at them directly, which can cause eye strain and mild disorientation. Look at them from the periphery, and don't stare. Do not touch them. Do not sleep within several meters of them.

**Ring ruins.** Collapsed plinths form rings whose interiors are filled with the film. Do not enter them. The film inside is dense and will respond to you more strongly than the film elsewhere.

**Condensers.** The lattice spires rise eight or nine meters out of the stone and collect fog in their filaments, which drain into troughs at their bases and feed the pools. They are beautiful and delicate, and they are probably the most important working structures on the Shelf. Stand beneath them if you need shelter from the fog, but do not touch the filaments. The dull, brittle spires have stopped working, and the filaments on the active ones may respond to contact in ways you can't predict. Note the lean of each spire. They are tilting a few degrees each season, and a spire that has leaned far enough can fall across your route.

**Grooves.** The concentric grooves radiating from the larger pools are too regular to be natural and too weathered to be recent. They are widening. Do not stand within them. Treat each groove as a boundary, and assume the pool it radiates from will expand to meet it. If you are crossing a groove, do it quickly and at a right angle, and do not linger.

## What the Place Does to You

The Shelf is always making noise, and it has no silence. Prepare yourself for sleep disruption, for a background sense of constant motion, and for the feeling that the place is aware of you. The film responds to touch, and the Shelf seems to notice what moves across it. This is not necessarily hostile, but it is attentive, and you should behave accordingly: move deliberately, avoid unnecessary contact, and do not treat the ground as inert.

Keep visits short at first. Plan your time in terms of rain cycles, not hours. A good first visit might cover one full rain pulse and its drainage, with a return before the next. Longer stays require more protocol, not more courage.

Expect to feel small against the open stone. Each living thing here is isolated, weeping, listening, and visibly turning into the land. You'll be doing the same thing, in your own way, and the land will record it.

## Leaving

Plan your exit before you need it. The ground that brought you in will have shifted by the time you return, so navigate by your flagged route and your compass, not by memory. Check your contact schedule. Leave the Shelf the way you came in, and do not cut across the cliffs. If you are caught by a downpour on the way out, move to the highest stable ground you can reach, away from pools and ledges, and wait for the drainage to tell you the water is leaving.

Do not leave anything behind. Anything you drop will be absorbed into the stone within days, and the Shelf will keep it.
