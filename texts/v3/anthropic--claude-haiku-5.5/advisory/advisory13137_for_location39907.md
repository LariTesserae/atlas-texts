# Preparing to Visit the Sump at Kettering Cut

## Before You Go

**Understand what kind of place this is.** The Sump is not a ruin you explore. It is a working system that has absorbed its own machinery, and it runs on living pressure. You are a guest in a machine that is still in operation. Approach it the way you would approach an active industrial site, with the added caution that the machine is alive and has been running long enough to be indifferent to visitors.

**Check the date.** The basin flowers roughly every forty to fifty years, a pale bloom that covers the canopy for a week. Visiting during the bloom is possible and may be worth it, but expect the water to be higher afterward and the knocking to have slowed. If you arrive and the canopy is pale and dropping petals, wait at the rim until it finishes. Walking into the basin mid-bloom means walking through falling pollen, and you will not want to breathe that air for long.

**Decide what you are there for.** Visitors who come to look, to record, or to sit with the place tend to have the best experience and the fewest problems. Visitors who come to take something, whether a specimen, a gauge, a length of pipe, or a cutting of the reeds, tend to have a bad time. The basin compensates for losses, and the compensation is not always gentle.

**Tell someone where you are going.** You will have no signal, and the rim is the last place where anyone can reach you by ordinary means. Leave a return time with someone who can raise an alarm, and agree on what "alarm" means. Given the uncertainty about what this place does with time and distance, a fixed return point helps.

## What to Bring

**Footwear.** Wear high, closed boots that you can wade in, with soles that grip wet concrete and creeper mat. The creepers sink under weight and seep cold water through leather within minutes, so waterproof boots are better than breathable ones. Carry a spare pair of socks in a sealed bag. Your feet will be wet for the whole visit.

**Clothing.** Wear long sleeves and long trousers. The reeds have edges, the iron has rust that cuts, and the aerial roots can scrape bare skin. Fabric should be tight enough to avoid snagging but loose enough to move in the heavy air. Dress in layers you can shed. The air is warm and wet, and you will sweat heavily without feeling it, since the humidity keeps evaporation from cooling you.

**Light.** The light is good but unreliable. Shafts of sun help during the day, but the canopy gets dark quickly once the sun drops below the rim, and the mist thickens in the evening. Bring a headlamp with a red or amber setting. White light draws the flies and seems to excite the bubbling in the dark water.

**Water and food.** Bring more water than you think you need, and do not drink any water from the basin. The standing water is warm and the intake current is unknown. Bring sealed food and eat at the rim before descending, since eating among the fungus and damp leaves is unpleasant and risks contamination.

**Breathing.** The air is thick, and some visitors find it hard to breathe for the first hour. Move slowly, rest often, and sit down when you feel dizzy. A light cloth over your nose and mouth helps against the fungal spores and the rising mist. Don't wear a sealed mask. You need to feel the air moving.

**Tools.** Bring a long pole, a walking staff, or a sturdy rope. The pole is for probing the creeper mat before you step on it. The rope is for a line back to the rim if the mat gives way. You should not carry a knife or saw for cutting. The basin does not appreciate cutting, and you will be tempted to take a cutting from something that looks interesting. Don't.

**Something to write with.** Waterproof paper and a pencil work better than anything electronic. Moisture and interference make screens unreliable. Record times, sounds, and the readings you can see on the gauges. Do not try to clear the frost from the gauge faces or adjust them.

**Recording equipment.** You may bring cameras and recorders, but test them before you descend, and expect them to fail. The knocking and hissing may be recorded as sound, but the basin's texture, the warmth, and the sense of its rhythm rarely survive a recording.

## Navigating the Basin

**Use the old plan.** The works are still visible as a map in the growth. Channels show as denser green, sluice gates as dark knots of woody stem, pump halls as thick climber tangles. Study this from the rim before descending. The outline of the old works is your only reliable navigation, because the canopy hides everything else.

**Stay on the lines.** The concrete channels are the most stable ground you will find. The creeper mat over open water is the least stable. Where a channel edge is visible as a pale ridge, walk along it. Where you have to cross open green, probe first and keep your weight low and spread. If the mat begins to sink faster than you can step, go back the way you came without stopping to consider it.

**Avoid the intake.** The main current rises from the deepest part of the old intake, at the dark center of the water. It is steady, visible, and strong. Do not enter the water near it, do not reach toward it, and do not follow its current upstream. The source beyond the rim has never been found, and nobody who has tried to follow the current has come back with an explanation.

**Respect the pump housing.** The knocking is loudest here and comes about every four seconds. Do not try to time your steps to it. Visitors who fall into its rhythm lose track of their own pace and often find themselves further from the rim than they meant to be. Keep your own count, or keep your breath independent of the beat.

**Watch the split pipes.** Where a pipe hisses under pressure, the joint is failing, and the growth around it is working to seal it. Do not stand directly in line with a pipe joint. Under pressure, a split can release a jet of warm water, and the swelling of pale growth that seals it is fragile underfoot.

**Expect falls from above.** Stems snap and leaves tear free every few minutes, falling with a crash through the canopy. Keep to cover where you can, and do not stand beneath the largest reeds, which are the most likely to fall. A stem can weigh more than a person.

## Conduct

**Do not touch the machinery.** Valves, pistons, and gauges are threaded through the living growth. Touching them can disturb the connection between the plants and the works, and you cannot predict what that disturbance will do. The frozen gauges are particularly tempting. Look at them, record their readings, and leave them alone.

**Do not cut or take.** The basin compensates when something is cut, and within a season something else grows to fill the gap. You may not notice the compensation, but the basin will. Don't take cuttings, fungus, seeds, water, or fragments of pipe. Leave everything where you found it.

**Do not feed or harm the fauna.** The animals here are rare, and their absence is part of the place. The heron-like birds visit occasionally and stand for an hour before leaving. Do not approach them, and do not disturb the flies or moths. If a bird wades close, stay still until it leaves.

**Do not call out.** Your voice will carry strangely through the reeds and may not come back the way you sent it. Keep speech low, and keep it short.

**Leave the way you came.** Return to the rim by the same route. Do not take a shortcut across open water because it looks clearer. The basin does not reward impatience.

## Physical and Mental Preparation

**Expect sensory overload.** The sound is constant: dripping, bubbling, creaking, the knocking, the hiss, and a low hum you feel through your feet. The air is heavy, the light is warm and green, and the smell of sap, rust, and rot is strong. Many visitors feel dizzy or disoriented in the first half hour. Give yourself time to adjust before you move far from the rim.

**Expect the rhythm to take hold.** The knocking, the dripping, and the bubbling form a pattern that is easy to fall into. Some visitors find their breathing and heartbeat slowing to match it. This is not harmful in itself, but it makes it harder to notice when you are tired, cold, or lost. Check yourself regularly. Ask whether you know where you are and how long you have been there.

**Expect the place to feel patient.** The Sump has been working for a long time and shows no sign of urgency. Visitors who arrive with a plan and a schedule often find the basin unresponsive to both. Let the schedule loosen. Your return time still matters, but the place itself does not care about it.

**Accept that you may not understand it.** The machinery runs without any mechanism you can name. The plants operate the works, the water rises from an unknown source, and the basin repairs itself. You will not leave with an explanation, and trying to force one tends to produce a headache and nothing else. Record what you observe. Let the rest stay unexplained.

**Watch for multiverse drift.** Because the Sump is a closed system that draws from an unknown source, it may not be fixed in the same place across every universe you visit. Before you descend, confirm the coordinates with your anchor, and check again when you reach the rim. If the bowl looks different from the description, if the water is cold, if the knocking has stopped, or if the growth is not the same shape it was, do not go down. Return to your anchor and reassess.

**Plan your exit before you need it.** Know which channel leads back to the rim, roughly how long it takes, and where the firmest ground lies. If you begin to feel unwell, retreat at once. The air, the rhythm, and the heat can all wear you down faster than you expect.

## When You Leave

**Do not linger at the rim to look back too long.** The Sump is easy to watch and hard to leave. Take one last look, then turn away.

**Check yourself before returning.** Look for ticks, leeches, or spores on your clothing and skin. Check your boots for creeper fragments. Remove them before you go back through your anchor, and do not bring any growth from the basin with you. Plants from one universe can behave unpredictably in another, and this one has a particular talent for compensation.

**Write down what you saw.** Record the readings on the gauges, the state of the bloom if it was happening, the direction and strength of the intake current, and the time the knocking seemed to slow or quicken. Future visitors will need this, and the basin changes slowly enough that a record from one visit can be checked against the next.

The Sump at Kettering Cut is not dangerous in the way that a fall or a flood is dangerous. It is dangerous in the way that any large working machine is dangerous, and in the way that a slow, patient, living thing is dangerous to those who do not respect its pace. Come prepared, come lightly, and leave everything as you found it.
