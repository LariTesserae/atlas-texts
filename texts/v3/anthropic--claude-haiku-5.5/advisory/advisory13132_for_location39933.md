# Preparing for the Hollow Above the Unfinished Shelf

This is a place where the ground, the plants, and even the rules are still being decided. Most of what you bring and how you move will come down to two things: protecting yourself from heat, thin air, and the oily fluid, and not leaving permanent marks on a world that is still setting. Plan for a short, careful visit, and expect the place to be slightly different when you return.

## Before You Go

**Know your return.** You will arrive at a particular point, and that point is the only one you can count on. Distances shift when you are not watching, and landmarks drift. Decide on your exit route before you step off, and fix it in your memory relative to the arrival point, not to any stone or plant. If you come back in a year, the hollow will have rearranged itself. Your arrival point may be the only thing that has stayed put.

**Plan a short visit.** Set a firm limit, such as one hour, and stick to it. The place rewards patience only in the sense that you may notice slow changes. You will not notice much in a single visit, and lingering increases your exposure to the hum, the heat, and the disorientation.

**Tell someone.** Leave a written plan with a contact outside the pocket: when you arrived, your exit route, your planned time, and what you'll do if you're late. Be honest about the uncertainty. Other travelers can help you retrieve a person who has lost track of time, but only if someone knows where to look.

## What to Wear

- **Closed, high-grip boots** with thick soles. The rock is sharp-edged and unweathered, and parts of it are soft. Ridges can cut through thin leather, and fine grit gets into everything.
- **Long sleeves and long trousers** of tightly woven, light fabric. The sun is plain and white, but the heat is intense and the plants are etched with sharp edges.
- **Thin gloves**, preferably nitrile or similar, since the amber fluid is tacky and difficult to wash off in the dry air. Keep them on, and don't touch your face with them.
- **A wide-brimmed hat and sunglasses**, because the light is bright and the air is thin enough that heat builds quickly.
- **Sunscreen** on any exposed skin, applied before you go and reapplied as needed.

Avoid anything you'd mind losing or ruining. The fluid stains and tacks, and the grit gets into seams.

## What to Bring

**Water and electrolytes.** There is no water here, and the amber fluid is not drinkable. Bring more water than you think you'll need, at least one litre per hour, plus salt tablets or an electrolyte mix. The air is hot and dry, and you'll lose fluid faster than you feel it.

**Light food.** Bring something easy to eat and not too rich. Heavy meals are a poor idea in thin, hot air.

**Hand-held sealed containers.** These are for collecting nothing and carrying nothing out. Anything you take may not survive the trip, and removing samples from a place that is still forming may change the place itself in ways you won't predict.

**Rope or cord, about 20 metres.** Tie one end to your arrival point and mark it clearly. Use the cord to find your way back if the landscape shifts. Don't tie it to any plant or stone, since those may move.

**A notebook and a pencil.** Cameras and recording devices may lag or misbehave, just as shadows do. Write things down as you see them, including the time, your position relative to the arrival point, and any changes you notice.

**A small first aid kit.** Include antiseptic, bandages, burn dressing, eye drops, and any personal medication.

**A whistle.** It's useful if you need to call for help. Nothing calls out here, so a whistle will carry clearly.

## Movement and Navigation

**Walk slowly.** The thin air will tire you faster than normal. Pause regularly, and watch your breathing.

**Stay on firm rock.** Test the ground before you put your weight on it. Soft patches take fingerprints and harden around them, so a footprint or handprint may become a permanent feature of the terrain. Don't leave marks you wouldn't want to keep.

**Don't stand in the paths of the pods.** The small cylinders on the shelf open and close in sequence, and their lids move with a valve-like force. Keep clear of them.

**Keep a safe distance from the gear-lattice.** The fused lobes turn slowly when the air shifts, and they click sharply at irregular moments. Don't put hands or tools near them. Watch the direction of rotation, but don't trust it to tell you which way to go, since it changes with your facing.

**Don't time your steps to the pump.** The thick stalk pulses at a regular interval, and the fluid runs faster after each stroke. Standing close to it is not dangerous as far as anyone has observed, but the sudden surge of wet fluid can be slippery, and you should avoid stepping into the runoff.

**Expect distances to shift.** If a stone seems to sit farther to the left, don't argue with it. Use your cord, your notebook, and your memory of the arrival point to correct your position. Walking back toward an object is not a reliable way to find it.

**Mind the shadows.** They trail their objects by a moment. This can be useful for judging your own motion, since if you stop and your shadow keeps moving, you can tell you're still moving. It can also be disorienting, so don't rely on shadows for footing.

## The Fluid, the Plants, and the Air

**Do not drink the fluid.** It leaves a mineral taste, and anything beyond that is unknown. Don't put it on your lips or near your mouth.

**Wash your hands before eating.** The fluid thins into the air rather than evaporating cleanly, so it can settle on skin and food. Use water, not the fluid, and wash thoroughly before you eat or drink.

**Don't touch the plants more than you have to.** The ferns are sharp-edged and etched with fine lines, the broad leaves glisten and drip, and the braided vines wrap the rock and seem to draw from it. Whatever they draw from may include you. Keep your skin off the vines.

**Pace your breathing.** The air is thin and oily-sweet. Breathe slowly through your nose. If you feel light-headed, sit down in a clear, open space away from the plants, and drink water.

**Watch for heat exhaustion.** Symptoms include dizziness, headache, nausea, cold or clammy skin, and confusion. Stop, rest in the shade of your hat, drink water, and get out if the symptoms don't ease quickly.

## The Hum and the Listening

**Expect the hum in your bones.** It's felt more than heard, in the teeth and sternum. Earplugs won't block it. It will not hurt you, but it may make you feel unsteady. If it's strong, stop moving and sit.

**Don't try to match your breath to it.** The hum may shift pitch in step with your breathing, and trying to synchronize may leave you dizzy. Breathe however is comfortable.

**Listen for changes.** The loudest sounds are the drip of fluid, the grate of grit, and the occasional sharp click of the lattice. If the hum rises suddenly or the clicks come in a rush, move away from the lattice and the pods and wait for things to settle.

## Psychological Preparation

The hollow doesn't feel dangerous, but it can feel wrong. Cause and effect come slightly out of order, and the place seems to be deciding what it is allowed to do. That can produce unease, a sense that you're being watched, or the feeling that your own actions aren't quite yours.

Expect this. Remind yourself that the strangeness is a feature of an unfinished place, not evidence of hostility. Keep your pace calm and your movements deliberate. If you feel panic rising, stop, breathe, and check your position against your cord and your notes.

Don't make promises or speak as though your words will shape the place. Don't try to test its rules. You're a visitor, and the most useful thing you can do is observe carefully, leave little trace, and go home.

## Leaving

Return by your cord, along the route you chose before you arrived. Check your notebook against your memory of the path. When you reach your arrival point, pause, wash your hands, drink water, and rest before you do anything else.

Write down what you saw while it is fresh. Note the time you left, the state of the fluid, the position of the lattice, and any changes in the rock or plants. A later visitor, or your future self, may find those notes useful when the hollow has rearranged itself into something you can barely recognize.
