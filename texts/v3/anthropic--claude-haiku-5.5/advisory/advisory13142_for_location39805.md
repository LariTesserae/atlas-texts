# Preparing to Visit the Sunken Pan

The Pan is not dangerous in the way most dangerous places are. Nothing here will chase you, poison you quickly, or collapse on you. The risk is slower. You can lose track of how long you've been standing still, and you can lose track of yourself. Most of your preparation should be about keeping your own time and your own rhythm.

## Before You Go

**Know your return point.** The rim is only a few meters up, but it will look the same from every direction. Before you descend, fix a clear mark on the rim you can identify from below, such as a bolt in a rail, a crack shaped like a letter, or a particular fold of the stenciled warnings. Practice finding it from the floor with your eyes closed if you can.

**Tell someone outside the Pan exactly when you entered and what your return plan is.** This matters more than usual here, because the Pan may not be on the same timeline as the rest of the world. A partner on the rim who knows your expected window can notice if you've been gone far too long, and can sometimes tell you whether you've come back to the right place.

**Bring a non-repeating clock.** Anything that changes steadily and visibly is useful: a candle that burns down, an hourglass you can see from the rim, a sealed container of slowly melting ice, a water clock. Your wristwatch and phone will probably keep ticking, but they may not tell you anything you can trust. Look at something that changes in a way the Pan cannot copy.

**Pack for dry heat and dust.** Bring more water than you think you need, and drink on a schedule rather than by feel, because the dry air hides how much you're losing. Salt tablets help if you're there for long. Wear a dust mask or a cloth over your face. The mineral haze is fine enough to irritate your lungs, and you will be breathing the warm dust that rises from the seams.

**Protect your eyes and ears.** The amber light is not harsh, but it is persistent, and your eyes will ache within the hour. Use tinted goggles. Bring earplugs for the pistons, though be aware that the bass pulse goes through your body and earplugs won't stop you feeling it. What they will do is keep the high rasp of the cranks from wearing down your attention, which is what you need most.

**Wear the right footwear.** The crust cracks like shell and the flakes curl up at the edges. Thick soles and ankle support matter, since you will be stepping on uneven, brittle plates for hours. Keep off the dark seams, which are warm and may give way.

## On the Floor

**Don't stand in rhythm with the machines.** This is the most important rule. The pistons strike every few seconds, and the belts and cranks drift in and out of time. The longer you stand still, the more your breathing and heartbeat will settle into the pattern. Move occasionally, and change your pace on purpose. Count your own breaths in a different pattern, such as threes or sevens, instead of letting them fall into the thud. If you notice that you can no longer tell whether you're breathing or the piston is, it's time to leave.

**Don't count the strokes.** Counting feels like a way to keep time, but it gives the cycle a hold on you. Keep track of your time by your clock and your energy, not by the machinery.

**Keep the rim shadow in view.** The shadow of the rim lies across the floor in the same place it always has. That is why it's useful. Check it every so often. If it has moved, even slightly, something is wrong, and you should head back. If you have lost track of where it falls, you have probably been standing still too long.

**Don't touch the machinery.** The parts look like they can be taken, but they may have fused with the scaffolds, and grasping them may bind you to the cycle. Gauges and dials are tempting, especially the ones that twitch in an identical arc. Don't read them closely or tap them. Their needles don't show anything you need.

**Leave the creatures alone.** The small armored things can be picked up, and they seem harmless, but they carry grit and lichen, and some have plates grown into their backs. Don't let them crawl onto your skin or your gear. The larger forms in the scaffolds unfold when the pistons pass. Stay out of their path. Learn their timing if you want to cross, but wait for the stroke and go before the next one, and never linger in the gap.

**Don't eat or drink anything from the Pan.** The lichen in the pipes looks like something you could chew on, but it is dry, fibrous, and unknown. The dust gets into everything. Keep your food and water sealed.

**Avoid the hoof ring.** Near the rim, the crust has been pressed into a circle of hoof-shaped prints, each deeper than the last, though nothing has been seen to make them. Don't walk in it, don't put your feet in the prints, and don't measure them. If you notice that the prints look deeper than when you last checked, or that you can feel something pressing on the crust under your own feet, leave immediately and don't look back to check.

**Mark your path, but don't rely on stones.** Stacked stones or scratched lines may be absorbed into the cycle, or may disappear and reappear in the same spot each time. A better marker is a changing one: a strip of bright fabric that you can see fade or stay bright, a note with a date written on it, or a small object you can account for. Check your markers against your clock, not against the Pan.

## Signs You Should Leave

- You have stopped noticing the piston strokes, or you notice them only as a steady background to your own breathing.
- You cannot find the rim shadow or it has moved.
- Your clock disagrees strongly with your sense of how long you've been down there, or your partner on the rim tells you that far more time has passed than you thought, or far less.
- You feel the urge to count, sway, or match the belts.
- The hoof prints look deeper.
- Your footprints behind you are deeper than when you made them.
- The crust cracks in a rhythm that feels like it's coming from your own steps.

If any of these happen, don't argue with the feeling. Climb toward your marked point on the rim. Expect the crust to crack behind you in the same rhythm as before, and expect the rim shadow to lie across the floor exactly where it always has. That is not a sign you've failed to leave. It is the normal state of the Pan. Keep walking.

## After You Leave

Check your clock, your partner's account, and your own sense of how long you were gone. They may not agree, and that is not necessarily a problem, but record the differences. Clean the dust from your eyes, nose, and lungs. Drink more water than you think you need. Give yourself time to adjust to ordinary rhythms, such as the ones of breath, footsteps, and conversation that have no thud behind them. Some travelers report that for a day or two afterward, they still feel a faint pulse in their chest, and their heartbeat seems to hesitate before every beat. This usually passes. If it doesn't, seek out someone who has been to the Pan before and can help you listen for the difference between your own rhythm and the one you brought back.
