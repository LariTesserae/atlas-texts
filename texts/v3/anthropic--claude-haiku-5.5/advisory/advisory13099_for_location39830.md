# Preparing to Visit the Planed Highlands

Most places are dangerous in obvious ways: cold, height, predators, bad water. This one is dangerous in quieter ways, and the quiet ones are harder to prepare for. You will be cold and wet for most of your visit, and that is the easy part. The harder part is that the place will sometimes disagree with your senses about distance, time, direction, and what is underfoot. Prepare for the weather, but spend most of your effort on the uncertainty.

## Before You Go

**Know the route and the exit.** Pick your entry point and your exit point before you arrive, and learn where the plateau's rim falls away into the cloud-sea. Do not plan an exit that depends on finding a particular avenue or plaza again. Plan one that you can recognize by terrain: a specific ridge, a river you can follow downhill, a standing stone with a distinctive lichen spiral.

**Tell someone outside the plateau when you expect to return,** and tell them what to do if you don't. Be specific about the time. Your own sense of elapsed time may not be reliable here (see below), so give them a date and a time from a clock they control.

**Plan for a stay longer than you intend.** Weather can hold you for a day. The mist can hold you for longer. Bring food and fuel for at least double your estimate.

**Get a second clock.** Carry a mechanical or solar watch that does not depend on your phone, and a written log. You will want to check both against each other.

## Clothing and Gear for Constant Dampness

The air here is never fully dry and rarely rains hard for long. The problem is not rain. It is condensation, which soaks everything from the inside out.

- **Layers that wick and insulate even when wet.** Wool and fleece work better than down, which collapses. Avoid cotton entirely.
- **A waterproof shell that breathes poorly is worse than none.** You will sweat under it and get cold in the stops. Consider a light, highly breathable shell with a separate rain layer you put on only in the bursts.
- **Two sets of socks, minimum, and a dry pair that lives in a sealed bag.** Your feet will be wet most of the day. Trench foot and fungal infections are the most likely serious medical problems you will face. Bring antifungal powder and check your feet at every stop.
- **Waterproof boots with ankle support and gaiters.** The peat and moss hide holes and the granite is polished slick.
- **Trekking poles, ideally with spikes or basket tips.** Use them to probe anything that looks like moss over stone. Moss is a good disguise for a crack or a sink.
- **Dry bags for everything that matters.** Maps, electronics, your log, spare clothes, your medical kit, and your food should all be sealed.
- **Fire-making tools.** Tinder and matches will go damp quickly. Bring a ferro rod, waterproof matches, and a supply of fine tinder in a sealed tin. Cold, wet nights are much easier with a fire, and there is very little dry wood on the plateau.
- **A bivvy sack or a compact tent.** Sleep on the crowns of the ridges, not in hollows. Mist collects in low ground, and the tarns and courtyard pools are both cold and damp.
- **Eye protection.** Mist beads on glasses and reduces visibility more than you'd expect.

## Water and Food

**Do not drink from the plateau.** The braided rivers run brown with tannin, which is unpleasant but mostly harmless once filtered. The still pools and clear deep waters look safer than they are, and the algae in the courtyard pools glows at dusk for reasons nobody here understands. Carry your own water and bring a filter and a reliable way to boil.

**Do not eat anything you did not bring.** The bitter gourd-shaped fruit hanging under the trees is not known to be toxic, but it is not known to be safe, and the bone-white flowers open for a single day and then fold back into their stems. You will be tempted to forage, especially if you run low. Don't.

**Bring enough calories for a cold, active body.** There is no game. Your food supply is entirely what you carried.

## Light, Orientation, and Navigation

The plateau bends light and distance in ways that will make your eyes unreliable.

**Carry a headlamp with a red-light mode.** The dusk glow of the algae and mirror-pools can wash out white light, and red light preserves your night vision.

**Do not trust a single landmark.** Streets that are a mile long going out may be shorter coming back, and the avenues may briefly stretch to fit a route you didn't mean to take. Navigate by a combination of compass bearing, map, elapsed time, and terrain features that are not part of the built layer. The ridges, the braided rivers, and the rim are more stable than the ruins.

**Mark your path with material you carry.** Use marking tape or small markers tied to your pack, not to the plants, which grow over and into anything. Mark each junction when you pass it, and when you return, check that the marks are where you left them. Do not assume that a return route matches the outbound route, even when it looks identical.

**Watch for shadows.** They sometimes lag their objects by a half-second. This is unsettling, but it is not dangerous by itself. It is a sign that you should slow down, check your footing, and check your companions.

**Compare the sky to your reflection in still water and then look up.** Mirror-pools reflect different skies: unfamiliar stars, or a plateau that is green when the real one is grey. Do not stare at these for long. Treat a reflection that does not match the real world as information, and step back from the water. If you see a reflection that appears to show a doorway or a figure, do not follow it.

## Ruins and the Layered Cities

The built layer is extraordinary, and it is also the most disorienting part of the plateau.

**Do not enter the lower levels alone.** Doorways open onto stairs that lead to older doorways, and walls show several orientation systems at once. Keep a line of sight to the surface or a marked exit at all times, and don't descend farther than you can climb back up in a few minutes.

**Do not disturb the roots.** They lace through the mortar until walls and roots become one structure. Pulling them out can collapse sections you would rather keep standing. More broadly, the plateau has been left in a certain state, and it is best to leave it as you found it.

**Do not take anything.** The clay tablets with scored, almost-meaningful patterns, the adzes, the bone awls, and the stones are all part of a record. They may not survive transit anyway. Photograph and sketch instead.

**Read inscriptions in daylight and again at dusk.** The letters may shift. Record both versions in your log. If you cannot read them, do not guess. Note the discrepancies and move on.

**Expect the terraces to tilt.** Some of the plazas sit at a slope too slight to slide on but enough that your body will insist on correcting for it. This can cause nausea and vertigo, especially if you are tired. Use a bubble level or the horizon to check yourself. Sit down if you feel unsteady, and do not try to walk a tilted terrace on the same day you feel sick.

## Time

Time behaves differently here, and the signs are subtle.

**Moss grows visibly for a few hours and then stops. Ferns unfurl in a breath.** These changes are real, but they are local, and they will make your sense of elapsed time unreliable. Keep your log, record the time at each stop from your second clock, and resist the urge to estimate.

**Water in the courtyard pools sometimes runs backward for an instant.** Watch for it, record it, and do not try to explain it in the field.

## Sound and Communication

Sound dies in the mist. Voices stop a few feet from the speaker's mouth, and footsteps vanish into moss. This is dangerous, because you can lose your companions while standing next to them.

**Agree on signals before you go.** Use visual signals such as colored flags, headlamp flashes, or a set of hand signals, and a whistle for sound when the mist lifts. Test your whistle at the start of your visit to confirm that it carries.

**Use a line between companions in the mist.** A short rope or a fixed length of cord keeps the group together. Count heads at every stop and at every junction.

**Expect silence.** There is no birdsong, no insects, no rustle of small creatures. The silence can be oppressive, and it can make you believe you are alone when you are not. If you need reassurance, a small object that makes a consistent sound, such as a metal cup or a bell, is useful for testing the air.

**Be ready for the deep sound.** From very far away, there is occasionally a low, slow scraping, like stone dragged over stone, or a bell struck underwater. It has no known source. Do not go looking for it. It is the sound of the place doing what it has always done.

## Weather and Rhythm

The weather turns every few hours. The cloud-sea rises in the afternoons, and the rain comes in bursts. Plan your major movements for the clear windows, typically late morning, and use the afternoons for sheltered work: camp chores, log writing, and rest. Be prepared to move quickly when the mist comes down, because your visibility can fall to a few feet in minutes.

Erosion, plant growth, and the shifting rivers are slow, and you will not see them change in a single visit. What you will notice is that the land never stays still. Give yourself time to notice the small changes around you, and use them to check your own perceptions.

## Mental Preparation

The plateau is patient and undramatic. Its weirdness arrives in small corrections: a path that is longer going out, a shadow a half-second behind, a reflection that shows a different sky. The most important thing you can carry is the discipline to write these things down rather than explain them away, and the discipline to stop investigating when investigation starts to pull you off your route.

Prepare for disorientation, not spectacle. You will be wet, tired, and unsure of the time more often than you are amazed. Keep a steady routine: check your gear, count your companions, log the time and your position, eat regularly, and sleep on the ridges. These habits will carry you through the strange parts.

## What to Carry: A Short Checklist

- Layered wool or fleece, a breathable shell, a separate rain layer
- Two sets of socks plus a dry pair in a sealed bag, antifungal powder
- Waterproof boots, gaiters, trekking poles with spikes
- Compass, paper map, dry bags, marking tape
- Headlamp with red mode, spare batteries
- Ferro rod, waterproof matches, fine tinder in a sealed tin
- Water filter, boiling pot, ample water
- Two days of extra food beyond your plan
- Mechanical or solar watch, a written log, pencils (not pens, which may smear)
- Whistle, short cord or rope for linking companions
- Bubble level, small bell or metal cup for testing sound
- Bivvy sack or compact tent
- Medical kit with blister care, painkillers, anti-nausea medication
- Camera or sketchbook for recording inscriptions and reflections
- A small object from home that you can identify without thinking, something you can hold when the place starts to feel wrong

The Planed Highlands will not hurt you if you respect its ordinary dangers and keep your wits about its strange ones. Go slowly, mark everything, write it down, and leave the stones where they are.
