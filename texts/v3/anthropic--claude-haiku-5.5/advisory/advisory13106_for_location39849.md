# Preparing for the Weeping Shelf

## Before You Go

Expect a mild, humid climate at altitude, a landscape that is always wet, and a settlement that is quiet and spread out. Most of your preparation will be about keeping your footing, staying dry enough to function, and not being unsettled by a place that doesn't seem to change.

Travel light, but not underprepared. Nothing here is hard to reach, but the walking is slow and the ground is uneven. Plan for a long day's walk between clusters, with plenty of stops.

## Clothing

The heat is steady and damp, so dress for humidity rather than cold. Loose, breathable layers that dry quickly are better than heavy fabrics, which will hold moisture and feel oppressive within an hour. Wool and some synthetics stay warmer when wet, but the air is warm enough that you're more likely to overheat than chill.

Your footwear matters most. The rock is slick with algae and moss, the causeways are narrow and wet, and the marsh edges can give way without warning. Choose boots or shoes with good grip on wet stone and some ankle support. Bring a spare pair if you can, because feet stay damp here for days. Avoid anything with open seams or thin soles, since the basalt edges are sharp.

A waterproof outer layer is useful, though not always for rain, which seldom falls. The mist condenses on everything and drips from leaves, so a light hooded shell will keep your shoulders and hair from soaking through during long walks under the trees.

## Light and Depth

The white sky has no visible source, and the light is even from every direction. There are almost no shadows, so the ground, the edges of the cliffs, and the surface of the standing water can look like the same flat plane. This is the most dangerous quirk of the place.

Judging distance is hard. A cliff edge that looks ten paces away may be twice that, and a pool may look like solid ground until you step into it. Walk slowly, probe ahead with a staff, and assume that anything dark and flat may be water. Carry a long pole or sturdy walking stick. It serves as a probe and a balance aid.

If you have a camera or sensor that depends on shadow for depth, bring a backup method of navigation. A compass works. A map works if you can read it, and the terrain is distinctive enough to help. Mark your route with cairns or cloth strips if you are going into the tree ferns, because the understory is a permanent green dusk and visibility drops quickly under the canopy.

## Water

Water is everywhere, but almost none of it is safe to drink without treatment. The seep-water is clear and cold, but you should treat it or drink only from the settlement cisterns, which the locals maintain. Ask before you drink from any pool or channel, and never drink from standing marsh water.

Carry more water than you think you need. The air is damp, so you will feel that you aren't sweating much, but you are losing fluids steadily. Electrolyte tablets help on longer walks.

Keep your food dry. Mist settles on everything, and moss grows on forgotten packs within a day. Store food in sealed containers and check it often.

## Silence and Absence

This is the part most visitors find hardest to adjust to. There are no birds, no insects, no animal calls, and no fish in the pools. The plants make no sound because there is no wind. What you will hear is dripping, the faint tick of droplets into water, the thin trickle of the channels, and occasionally a human voice or a stone knocking against stone, arriving a moment after the motion.

Expect the silence to feel heavy. Many travelers find themselves speaking more quietly, then not speaking at all. This is normal, and it's worth noticing, but it can also make the place feel more oppressive than it is. Bring something to fill the quiet if you need it, such as music, a journal, or conversation with your companions, but respect that the locals may find loud noise jarring.

Don't bring animals, and don't expect your own companions to behave normally. Pets and familiars often refuse to cross the causeways or become agitated in the mist. If you travel with a creature, consider leaving it at the edge of the plateau.

Also watch for the absence of change. Nothing in the landscape seems to age, shift, or decay. The pools hold their level, the moss neither spreads nor recedes, and the cliffs don't crumble. This is comforting to some travelers and deeply unsettling to others. If you find yourself losing track of how long you've been there, set specific checkpoints, such as a daily journal entry or a fixed meal schedule, to keep your sense of time anchored.

## The Settlement

The people here live in small, scattered clusters. They are careful, quiet, and practical. Their tools are hand-made, their channels are cut by eye, and their bridges are single slabs. They have no metal, and they treat their work with patience and respect. Expect them to be polite but reserved. They are not hostile, but they don't seem to need visitors, and they may not explain their customs unless you ask.

Some guidelines:

- **Walk on the causeways, not beside them.** The marsh edges are soft, and the locals keep the stone paths clear for a reason.
- **Don't alter channels, ditches, or levees.** The water flow is part of their balance. Even small changes can have effects they will notice.
- **Ask before entering homes or cisterns.** Many of the houses are roofed with living moss and fern-leaf, and the interiors are kept carefully.
- **Be cautious with fire.** Fires are rare here because of the damp, and the pottery is fired in pits.
- **Don't take glass blades or fired pots as souvenirs** without permission. These are made by hand over long periods and are shared within the community.
- **Keep your voice low.** The sound carries across the still air, and the locals speak quietly.

## Food

Food is simple. The settlements grind grain and roots in shallow stone mortars and cook in clay pots. Fresh greens and fern shoots are common, and the water is clean from the cisterns. Expect meals to be plain and filling. Bring your own supplements if you have dietary needs, since the local food is limited.

Don't eat wild plants or fungi without guidance. The moss and liverwort are thick and unfamiliar, and some growths may be harmful.

## Sleep and Shelter

Stay in the settlements if you can. The houses are dry inside, well-built, and warm. If you camp, choose a raised site on bedrock or a dry shelf, not the soft ground in the low places. Hang your bedding to air out each day, because mildew grows fast.

## Departure

Plan your departure as carefully as your arrival. Leaving the plateau means descending the same stepped path, and the gullies and cut edges are just as sharp going down. Take your time. Before you go, check your pack for moss, mildew, or anything you didn't bring with you. Many travelers feel reluctant to leave, and some find that the stillness makes the return journey feel longer than it should. Set a firm departure time and stick to it.

## Summary Checklist

- Supportive, grip-soled boots and a spare pair
- Light, breathable layers and a hooded shell
- Long walking pole or probe
- Compass, map, and marking cloth for the ferns
- Sealed food storage and extra water
- Water treatment supplies
- Journal or other way to track time
- Something to fill the silence, if you need it
- Nothing that requires shadows to navigate
- No animals

The Weeping Shelf rewards patience and careful attention. Walk slowly, drink carefully, keep your sense of time, and respect the people who have made their lives in its wet, still balance.
