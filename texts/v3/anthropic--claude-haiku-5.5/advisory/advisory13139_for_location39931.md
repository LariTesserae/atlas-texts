# Preparing to Visit the Stilled Table

## First, Understand What You Are Walking Into

The Stilled Table is not a dangerous place in the way a desert or a glacier is dangerous. It does not want you dead. It is simply in the middle of an enormous, slow process of undoing itself, and it is indifferent to whether you are standing in the path of that process. Most injuries here come from ordinary decisions made without enough attention to the ground, the water, or the weather. Your preparation should aim less at surviving a hostile world and more at becoming a careful guest in a world that is always moving.

Expect the place to be overwhelming for the first few days. The noise, the wet, and the constant sense that the ground is shifting will wear on you. Many travelers report that the first night is the hardest. Plan for that, and do not treat the disorientation as a sign that something has gone wrong.

## Physical Preparation

**Acclimatization.** You will be at roughly four thousand meters with thin air, so arrive gradually if you can. If you must come straight up, rest for at least a full day before any real exertion, and drink more water than feels necessary. Headaches, nausea, and a racing pulse are common. Descend if they worsen.

**Heat and humidity.** The heat is wet and close, and your body will struggle to cool itself through sweat that never fully evaporates. Work at a slower pace than you think you need, especially in the afternoon when the cloud is thickest. Wear loose, light layers that breathe, and change them often, since damp fabric chafes and chills quickly when the cloud thins and the wind rises.

**Feet.** This is the most important point of all. Everything is wet and slick. Moss, algae, and wet stone are treacherous, and the mud is deceptively deep in the basins. Bring footwear with aggressive tread and a stiff sole, and break it in completely before you arrive. Bring a second pair that you can keep dry. Sandals are acceptable only for camp and the lower terraces, and you should never wear them near steam vents, since the hot ground can burn through thin soles.

**Hearing and balance.** The roar will strain your ears. Carry earplugs for sleep. If you have any issue with vertigo, the constant motion of water and the sight of falls from every rim may make it worse. Practice balance work before you go if you can.

## Gear

**Waterproofing is more important than insulation.** Bring a good shell that covers your pack and your body, and assume everything will get wet anyway. Store critical items in double-sealed bags. Silica or desiccant packs should be replaced regularly. Anything that rusts, molds, or swells should be wrapped in resin-sealed cloth or kept in a hard case.

**Fire.** The wet makes fire difficult, and the hunters here tend it carefully. Bring a fire-starter that works when damp, such as a ferro rod with tinder that has been treated against moisture, but do not rely on it alone. Learn to make a bow drill fire before you arrive. It is a skill the locals respect, and it may be the only thing that works after a long rain.

**Shelter.** Choose a shelter that sheds water rapidly and can be pitched on uneven stone. A hammock system with a tarp is often better than a tent here, since the ground is not reliable. Never pitch within the reach of a slope, under a loosened outcrop, or in a basin that may flood. Look up before you settle in.

**Light.** The milky cloud light is adequate in the day but fades quickly under cover. Bring a waterproof headlamp, spare batteries kept dry, and a small reflective marker. Dusk arrives with a green-gold glow that is beautiful but short.

**Medical.** Prepare for skin infections, fungal problems, cuts that will not heal in the damp, and sprains from unstable footing. Bring antiseptic, antifungal cream, and a strong bandage system that resists moisture. Bring anything you need for steam burns, and for the sudden fevers that can come from water-borne illness. Filter and boil all water, every time, even when it looks clear. The clear basins at dawn are not necessarily safer than the murky ones.

**Navigation.** Maps age badly here. Rivers change courses, slabs slide, and basins swing between lake and mudflat. Carry a paper map of the most recent survey if you can find one, but treat it as a rough guide. Learn to read the big landmarks: the outer waterfalls, the tallest boulders, the bridge, the cairn at the crossing. Note that the cairn has been rebuilt many times, so use its position relative to the ridgelines rather than its exact stones.

## Communication

Speech carries poorly beyond arm's length, so learn hand signals before you go. The most useful are: stop, come here, danger ahead, water rising, go back, and I am fine. Local hunters use a whistling code over water, which carries across the roar. You do not need to master it, but learn a few of the basic calls, and never whistle a sequence you do not understand, since locals may take it as a claim or a challenge.

Establish a signal plan with any companions before you separate. Agree on what to do if one of you is lost in cloud. The standard advice is to stay where you are, make a steady sound, and wait for the cloud to thin. Do not wander to find each other in the wet, because the ground will hide your tracks within hours.

## Safety on the Shifting Ground

**Slides.** Hillside slabs loosen without warning, and the sound of their going travels for kilometers. Learn to hear the difference between the ordinary booms of distant rockfall and the groaning that often comes before a slide. Keep away from the bases of slopes, especially after rain, and avoid camping below any face. If you hear a slide, move uphill or across the slope, not down.

**Floods.** Rain comes as a sudden, total wall, and the rivers swell within minutes. Never camp in a channel or a basin, even one that is dry. Before crossing any stream, check upstream for the wall of cloud and the sound of rising water. If you are caught, climb to high ground immediately and do not try to cross.

**Steam vents.** The hollows hiss and open unpredictably. Stay at least several meters from any vent, and test the ground with a stick before you step near one. Do not kneel or set down equipment on warm stone, even in rain, since the slabs that are warm in wet weather may be hotter than they look.

**Waterfalls and spray.** The mist makes the rocks around the falls extremely slippery. Do not approach the rims closer than you need to, and never try to climb the wet walls behind the spray.

**Bridges.** The rope bridge across the widest gorge has been replaced in pieces and is mostly reliable, but inspect every section before you cross, and cross one person at a time. Do not stop on it.

## Respect and Society

The people here are few, scattered, and practical. Hunting camps sit in the lee of the big boulders, and a few villages are wedged into the terraces below the falls. Approach any camp openly and visibly, by day, and announce yourself with a whistle or a raised hand. Do not walk up on a camp in the cloud, because you may be taken for a threat or a thief.

**Trade.** Metal is rare and treasured. If you carry any, you will be watched closely and your offer will be judged carefully. Small amounts of metal, given as a gift and not a bargain, are generally well received. Do not flaunt it. Other goods that are useful here include fine cordage, resin, waterproof cloth, and good needles.

**Repair, not replacement.** Everything here is repaired, and the ruins you pass were kept alive by constant work. If you are asked to help mend a bridge, a terrace, or a basket, do it. Your labor is one of the most respected things you can offer. Do not build permanent structures of your own. They will not last, and they may be taken as an intrusion on the work of others.

**Ruins and artifacts.** The broken terraces, the cairn, the watchtower, and the scattered tools are not monuments. They are the marks of people who came here for ordinary reasons and moved on. Do not collect artifacts as souvenirs. Do not climb the watchtower's standing wall. If you find something in the mud, leave it where it is or return it to a camp. Ask before you photograph or sketch anything that belongs to someone.

**Stories.** The hunters speak of creatures that live in the hollow stone. Do not press them for details, and do not repeat the stories in front of children. If the locals change the subject, let them.

## Fauna

Most of what you will see is birds, amphibians, and insects. The large crested bird above the falls is harmless, and the heavy amphibians on warm rocks will simply slide away if you approach slowly. Do not chase or handle any animal. You may see shapes in the reeds, splashes in deep water, and tracks of something heavy in the mud at dawn. Keep your distance from all of them. Do not sleep near deep water, and keep food sealed and away from your shelter, since the things that leave those tracks are not interested in your restraint.

## The Strangeness

The unusual signs are real, and you should not treat them as curiosities to exploit. Certain stones ring like bells when struck, and their notes do not match the rock around them. Some slabs of the old floor are warm in the rain. Some basins clear to perfect stillness at dawn and reflect the sky with unnatural sharpness. The trees near the nearest waterfall lean toward a single point as if listening.

Observe these things without interfering with them. Do not strike the bell-stones repeatedly, and do not sit on the warm slabs for long. Do not drink from the clear dawn basins, and do not try to fix the trees in place. You may find yourself wanting to understand these signs. Let the wanting pass. The locals do not know what they mean, and most have stopped asking.

## The Emotional Ground

You will feel small here. You will also feel watched, though you will not be able to say by what. The cloud, the land, or something older than both may be paying attention, and the sensation can be unsettling for days. Most travelers find it helps to keep a simple routine: the same morning check of gear, the same evening note of the day's weather and sounds, the same counted breaths when the roar becomes too much. Routine is a counterweight to a place that is always changing.

Expect grief as well. The ruins are poignant precisely because they are broken and because the people who made them are gone. You may find yourself mourning strangers. That is a normal response, and it is not a reason to stay longer than you planned.

## Timing

The best time to arrive is the drier season, when the cloud thins for a few hours each day and the rivers are lower. Even then, expect rain. The rains bring the most dangerous conditions and the most dramatic beauty. Plan your major movements for the mornings, when the cloud is often thinnest and the ground has had a night to settle. Avoid heavy exertion in the late afternoon, when the heat peaks and storms gather. Plan your camp before dusk, because fog descends quickly after dark and the terrain becomes unreadable.

Allow more time than you think you need. Every route will take longer because of the mud, the cloud, and the detours around slides and new channels. Build slack into every plan, including your return.

## Leaving

Plan your exit as carefully as your arrival. Know which way you will descend, and check its condition shortly before you leave, since the lowest routes are the most prone to flooding. Tell someone at the nearest camp when you intend to go and by which route. Leave no trace of your presence beyond footprints that will be erased by the next rain. Take all your waste with you, including any buried remains of food, and return any borrowed tools or goods before you go.

Before you step back out of the Table, take one last look at the land. It will look different when you return, and it may look different even tomorrow. That is not a flaw in your memory. It is the nature of the place. It is always becoming a slightly different ruin of itself, and you were only ever a brief visitor to one version of it.
