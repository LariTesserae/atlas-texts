# Preparing to Visit the Undecided Hollow

## Read This First

The Hollow is not dangerous in the way most dangerous places are. Nothing here wants you dead, and the eel-things and silk hunters are only doing what they do. The real hazards are slower and stranger: losing track of time, losing track of your own decisions, and getting stuck in a place that has not yet settled whether you can leave. Prepare for those as carefully as you prepare for the physical environment.

Plan a short visit. The Hollow is easy to enter and hard to measure from inside, so decide before you go how long you will stay and how you will know when that time is up. Most travelers who run into trouble here stayed longer than they intended and could not say how long that was.

## Before You Go

**Set your intentions in words.** The chamber is still deciding what is allowed, and a loosely held purpose is easy to lose. Write down, in a sentence or two, why you are going and what you hope to do or see. Read it before you step through and again when you arrive. If your purpose changes while you're inside, notice that and decide whether the change is yours.

**Bring something that does not change.** Pick an object you can verify by touch: a coin with a known scratch, a knot in a cord, a stone of a particular weight. Keep it on your person. In a place where tracks fade and columns grow and recede, a fixed object tells you what you brought, and it tells you whether your sense of what you're carrying is still accurate.

**Agree on a return signal with your companions.** If you go with others, decide how you will check on each other in a place where sound arrives late and shadows lie. Touch works better than sight or voice. A hand on the shoulder with a set number of taps is a good choice. Choose it before entering.

**Write a log, but expect it to be unreliable.** Keep a record of times, places, and events, and keep a second copy somewhere else. The built marks rewrite themselves in small increments, and ink may spread or fade. Take it as a rough guide to your own experience, not a fixed record.

## What to Wear and Carry

**Footwear:** Bring boots with deep, aggressive treads. The loose grey grit slides, and the wet mineral film on the hummocks is slick. Avoid anything with thin soles, since the uneven rock is full of knuckles and broken column shards.

**Layers:** The air is cool, about the temperature of a cellar in early spring, and damp. Wear a wool or synthetic base layer, which stays warm when wet, and a windproof outer layer, since the cracks breathe cold air in slow exhalations. Keep a hood or cap for the dripping ceiling. The drops fall unpredictably, and they are cold.

**Eyes:** The light is strong and has no source you can point to. Bring dark-tinted glasses or a cloth you can tie over your eyes for when the glare gets tiring. Looking straight at the ceiling seam makes it brighter, so you will want to avoid staring at it and to limit how often you glance at it.

**Hands:** Bring gloves that grip. Wet rock and mineral crust are cold and rough, and you will likely need to steady yourself on hummocks.

**Light:** Bring a lamp, but expect it to behave strangely. Shadows lean, stretch, and sometimes fall in two directions. They can lag behind the objects that cast them, so don't use them to judge distance or position. A lamp is still useful for seeing the floor and avoiding the basin's edge.

**Water:** Bring your own, sealed. Do not drink from the basin or from the condensation threads on the walls. Everything here is partly becoming, and you cannot be sure what will happen to a liquid that has not finished being water.

**Food:** Bring your own and eat it outside the Hollow if you can. Do not eat the pale threads or the bulbs of the stalks. They may be harmless, but they grow only where the stone is soft enough to accept growth, and you should not add yourself to that process.

**Tools:** Bring cord, a whistle, a small hammer, and a marker that writes on wet stone. Use the cord to tie yourself to your companions, or to tie a line back to the entry if you can find a place to anchor it. Do not use the hammer on the cairns or the arch, and do not chip at the formations.

## Moving Through the Hollow

**Step deliberately.** Your footsteps echo a fraction of a second before you take them, and the sound can startle you into moving too quickly. Take a breath before each step on uneven ground. Keep your weight low.

**Don't disturb the basin.** Ripples spread from any disturbance and then gather back toward their source, so the basin seems to remember what happens to it. Stay several body-lengths from the edge. Do not throw things in, dip hands in, or lean out to look at the moving sky reflected there. The eel-things rise without warning and sink back with their catch. Treat the margin of the water as a boundary.

**Give the predators room.** The silk hunters drop from the ceiling on lines that catch the light, so look up now and then. If you see one descending, move laterally and do not run. The eel-things are blind and react to vibration, so move slowly and quietly near the water. The grazers are harmless and will scatter from you. Don't chase them and don't herd them.

**Don't finish anything.** The cairns are fused at their contact points, and the half-built arch is missing its keystone. Do not add stones, do not try to complete the arch, and do not reposition anything. Whatever built these things stopped partway, and your help will not be received as help. You may find that anything you add begins to unbecome, or that it remains and becomes part of the place in ways you did not intend.

**Watch your own footprints.** Grazers' tracks fade in a few breaths or last for hours depending on whether anyone is watching them. You may find it useful to leave your own tracks visible, which means your path back will be easier to follow. But a track that persists is also a track that others can follow, so think about who else might be using it.

**Don't stare at columns.** Columns grow when unobserved and recede when observed. This is not a reason to avoid looking, but it means you should know that your attention is part of what happens here. If you need a column to stay in place, look away from it. If you're trying to watch it change, expect it to change less than you hope.

## Time

Time here is not even. A few breaths might be a minute, or it might be an hour, and you may not be able to tell from the inside. Check time by means that do not depend on the chamber:

- Count your heartbeats at intervals, and keep a rough tally in your log.
- Note when each companion's signal is due and keep to it.
- Use the arrival time you wrote down before entering as your baseline.
- Set a hard turnaround time before you go in. When it comes, leave, even if you have not finished what you came to do.

If you lose track of time, assume more has passed than you think. Most travelers underestimate.

## Your Mind

The Hollow is unfinished, and it can make you feel that way too. You may notice your sense of purpose softening, your memories of the outside world seeming less solid, or your decisions feeling as provisional as the walls. These feelings are the place working on you, not evidence that you have changed.

Counter them in simple ways:

- Repeat your stated intention aloud, in your own voice, at intervals.
- Hold your fixed object and describe it to yourself in detail.
- Speak to your companions regularly, and ask them what they remember. Compare.
- If you find yourself unable to recall why you're here, stop moving and wait until your companion's signal reaches you.

Do not try to decide things while you are inside. Make decisions about where to go, whether to stay, and what to do outside, before you enter or after you leave.

## Leaving

Know how you came in before you go in, and mark the way with your marker and cord. The entry point may close, shift, or appear in a different place when you return. If you can't find it, don't panic and don't search at random. Stop, hold your fixed object, call to your companions, and follow the cord or the track you left.

When you find the way out, go through it without looking back at the seam or the basin. If the path feels longer than it should, keep to it anyway. Do not take anything with you that you did not bring in. Objects picked up here may begin to unbecome, and you do not want to carry something back that is partly undone.

On your return, check yourself. Compare your log with what you remember, and note what you have forgotten. Be honest with yourself about how long you were gone. Write down what you saw while it is still clear, knowing the record may drift.

## Last Advice

The Hollow is beautiful in the way unfinished things are: the stone still soft, the water still deciding, the moths folding their wings over glowing threads. You may want to stay and watch it become something. You can't, not really. Visit it, learn what you can, and leave it to keep deciding without you.
