# Preparing to Visit the Terrace Country of Hallen Reach

Hallen Reach is not dangerous in the way most hostile places are. Nothing here will hunt you across the plateau, and the worm-things keep to the crust and the circuit-walkers keep to their routes. The danger is that the place slowly rearranges your assumptions about distance, time, and where you are. Most injuries and most lost travelers come from people who prepared for a hard landscape and were not ready for a landscape that does not hold still. Prepare for the body, then for the map, then for the mind.

## Before You Go

**Acclimatize to thin air.** The plateau sits high enough that your heart will work harder than usual and your breath will feel short on the first climb up the escarpment. Spend at least three days at comparable altitude before you cross over, or arrive at the lower terraces first and climb in stages. Travelers who skip this often spend their first day sitting on a block, waiting for their pulse to slow, and they lose the light they came for.

**Set your body clock to a dim sky.** There is no sharp dawn or dusk here, only a lilac ceiling that brightens and drains. Sleep and wake on a schedule you keep with a timer, not with the sky. Your internal clock will drift, and the place will not correct it for you.

**Fit your boots for salt and plates.** The crust breaks into plates with curled edges. Their points are sharp enough to cut through thin soles and can tilt under a single step. Choose boots with thick, stiff soles, ankle support, and good tread that grips salt without sliding on it. Break them in completely beforehand. Blisters on this ground turn into real problems, because you cannot easily stop and sit down without disturbing the lichen and the silt.

**Plan for cold without warmth.** The wind carries no heat, even when the light looks like afternoon. Dress in layers you can open and close quickly: a wind-resistant outer shell, a thermal middle layer, and a base layer that will not hold moisture. The silt gets into everything, so use tight cuffs and a high collar, and bring a mask or cloth for your face during gusts.

## What to Carry

- **Eye protection.** The fine grey silt rises in ribbons and gets into the eyes. Goggles are better than glasses. Carry a spare cloth to clean them, because they will fog and crust over in the cold.
- **A breathing cloth or mask.** The silt is fine and dry. Sustained inhalation is unpleasant, and the metallic smell of the wind is a reason to keep your mouth covered.
- **Water, more than you think.** There is no water here, and the salt glaze in the hollows is not drinkable. Carry everything you will need for the entire trip, plus a margin. Thin air dries you out faster than you notice.
- **Food you can eat cold.** Nothing here burns, and there is no safe fuel to build a fire anyway. Bring dense, salty, ready-to-eat food. Avoid anything that crumbles into silt.
- **A helmet or hard cap.** Stones occasionally lift from the hollows and hang in the air above you, turning slowly, before dropping without warning. Blocks also settle and fall from the terrace walls. A hard hat is a reasonable precaution in the courts.
- **Ropes and anchors.** The courts are deep, the lattice bridges sag, and the stone is cold and slick with salt. A rope with a stone-friendly anchor will save you from a fall into a court that looks closer than it is.
- **Marking material.** This is the most important item on the list. More on this below.
- **A mechanical timer and a notebook.** Watches and electronics may behave unpredictably near the glass. A wind-up timer you can read at a glance, and a paper log that you update with times and positions, are both more reliable than anything with a battery.
- **A small lamp you do not depend on.** The glass gives off its own light, but it is uneven and sometimes goes dark when the wind rises. Carry a basic light for the inner spirals, and do not rely on it alone.
- **Nothing made of metal you cannot afford to lose.** The place has no metal. Its wind tastes faintly of it anyway, and objects left out for days can take on a dull bloom. Bring only what you can leave behind without grief.

## Navigation: The Hardest Problem

The plain repeats. You will walk west and find the same curled plate, the same chipped block, the same terrace rising on your left, in the same cracks, after you have already passed it. Your footprints will still lead away behind you, which is the trap: the footprints feel like proof of progress, but they can be on a loop.

**Do not trust the horizon.** The distant ridgeline may be near, a band of flickering color, or absent entirely. Use nearby features and your own recorded path, not the far edge.

**Mark everything you pass.** Use bright, durable markers, such as strips of fabric, cairns of stone you bring yourself, or chalk that the wind will not easily erase. Mark every junction. Then, when you see a marker you did not place, stop. You are looping. Record it in your log.

**Keep a path log, not just a map.** Write down the sequence of plates, thresholds, and courts you pass, in order, with times. A loop is only visible when you compare your sequence to a previous one.

**Walk in straight lines when you can, and count.** Count paces between markers. If the count between two landmarks changes on the return, something in the ground has moved.

**Travel with a partner if at all possible.** A second person catches what the first misses, especially the feeling of having been somewhere before. Agree on turnaround rules ahead of time: if you pass the same marker twice, you stop and retrace, you do not press on.

## The Glass

The volcanic glass is the place's most unsettling feature. The rings set into thresholds, floors, and column tops hold light differently. As you cross a court, the glass seems to lag your shadow by half a step, so the shadow arrives first. Some walls cast shadows on the glass where no wall stands between you and the sun.

Do not stand on the glass rings for long periods. People who linger tend to lose track of time and drift into long trances. They describe a warmth and a sense of being counted. Stand on the glass only to cross, and keep moving.

Do not try to pry glass from the walls. Some visitors have tried, and the fragments do not hold their light when separated. They are also cold and heavy and useless as souvenirs.

When the blue-green spirals rise from the deep courts, keep your distance. They drift a few inches above the floor and wink out, and no one has determined whether they are harmless. Walk around them, and do not reach into them.

## Living Things

**The lichen-like mats** cling to cracks where the salt meets the cliff base. They creep a few millimeters a day along mineral seams. Do not touch them, do not scrape them, and do not step on them. They release pale spore clouds when disturbed, and the spores collect in the glass rings and darken them. You will breathe them if you are careless. Keep your mask on near the mats.

**The circuit-walkers** are small, many-legged creatures that move along the terrace walls on fixed routes. They are harmless if left alone. Do not block their paths. If one walks into your boot, let it go on its way. Their circuits can be used as a clock if you learn them, but interrupting them is a bad idea. They pause together during tilts, which is discussed below.

**The worm-things** live under the plates and rise at dawn to bask on the glass. They are long and slow, and they grip their prey in the cracks and drag it down into the salt. Keep off the glass near dawn, and do not stand on the plates where you see a bulge in the salt. If one rises beside you, move away slowly. Do not provoke it.

**The tall pale shapes** on the escarpment are the rarest and least understood. If you see one, do not follow it. Do not try to approach. It walks on too many joints and vanishes behind the stepped walls before you can be sure it was there. Note the sighting in your log and continue.

## The Built Layer

Respect the architecture. The walls are fitted without mortar, and many are close to failing. Do not lean on them, do not climb them without a rope, and do not sit against them for shade, because you will not find any. The rubble that slides into the courts is slowly filling them, and the walls lose a block a month. Stand clear of the bases of walls where fresh rubble is piling.

The doorways are narrow. Expect to pass through one shoulder at a time, and expect to lose sight of your companions for a moment as you do. Call out before you enter a room, and wait for a reply before you proceed.

Do not take anything. The rooms look like someone stepped out and left their things. Those things belong to the place. Leave a marker and take only your log.

## Time

Time is uneven here. A glass ring lit at dawn may still be glowing at noon. A block that cracked this morning may be a pile of silt by afternoon, while its neighbor has stood unchanged for a year. Do not rely on the light to tell you how long you have been out. Use your timer and log.

Plan your trip in short segments with fixed turnaround times. Expect that your sense of elapsed time will be off by a factor of two or more after a few hours. If you feel you have only been walking for an hour and your timer says four, believe the timer.

## Tilts

Every so often a large section of the plateau shifts, and for a few minutes the terrace country seems to tilt about a degree before it settles. You will feel your balance move. Loose objects will roll. Dust may lift.

When a tilt happens, the circuit-walkers pause and turn to face the same direction. Do the same. Stop, hold still, brace your feet, and face the way the walkers face. Do not run during a tilt, and do not try to climb or descend a wall. Wait until it passes, then note the time in your log.

## The Feeling of the Place

The place will try to keep you. It counts you, in a sense: the doorways lead to courts a little farther away than they looked, and the glass rings seem to notice your footsteps. Expect to feel small, and expect to feel oddly included, as though you have been expected.

Work against the pull. Keep your log updated, check your timer, and speak your name and your plan aloud from time to time. Travelers who stop talking to themselves often find they have been standing still for an hour without noticing.

## Leaving

Your exit is the most important part of the trip. Know your way back before you go deep. Your path log is your return route. Follow your markers, not your footprints, because your footprints may lead you in a loop. If you reach a marker you did not place, stop, consult your log, and retrace until you find the last point where your sequence matched.

Keep at least one full day of supplies in reserve. If you find yourself unable to find the way out, do not wander. Stay near the last clear marker, keep your mask on, and wait for the light to drain from the terraces and come back. The loops are slow to close, and a patient traveler can usually find the way out.

When you leave, do not look back at the terraces for too long. The city has begun to forget that it was occupied, and it does not need you to remember it for it. Take your log, your markers, and your memories, and leave the chair where it is.
