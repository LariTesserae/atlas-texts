# Preparing to Visit the Drowned Terraces

This is a place where the ground is still forming, the sea is still deciding, and the people have built their lives around both. Your preparation should be less about surviving hostile conditions and more about not becoming an accidental disturbance. Most of what goes wrong for visitors here comes from a few well-meant habits carried over from somewhere faster.

## Before You Arrive

**Expect slowness.** The place does not hurry, and it will not hurry for you. Plan on a long, unhurried first stay. Trying to see everything quickly will make you clumsy, and clumsiness here can cost more than time. Give yourself at least several days, ideally longer, so that your body and your sense of pace can adjust.

**Learn the three rules before anything else.** Locals hold these close, and visitors who break them are remembered:
1. Do not dredge, drill, cut, chip, or otherwise break into the travertine or the sea-bed crusts.
2. Do not speak carelessly near the calcite basins. Keep sensitive conversation for open ground, and assume that a phrase spoken in a hollow may return to you.
3. Do not bury, submerge, or leave anything in the shallows unless you intend it to become part of the terraces. This includes offerings, gear you have "temporarily" set aside, and the bodies of the dead, which belong to the local burial practice and are not to be touched or moved by outsiders.

**Learn a little of the language of stone.** The people have words for the sea's growth stages: the thin skin, the lamina, the shelf that will hold, the shelf that will crack. You do not need fluency. But understanding the difference between a shelf that is "still soft" and one that is "set" will tell you where it is safe to step and what you are looking at when someone points.

**Prepare to be noticed as a foreigner.** Your clothing, your gait, and your manner will mark you. Locals will likely be patient and courteous, but they will watch you closely for the first few days. Let them. Watching is how they decide whether you are safe to be around.

## Arrival

You will probably arrive on a higher terrace, where the mist is thickest and the light is hardest to read. Do not move immediately. Stand still for a few minutes and let your eyes adjust. The light comes from everywhere, and depth is hard to judge. Edges blur, and a step that looks like three inches may be a drop of six feet.

Check your bearings using the sea, not the light. The sea is the largest continuous feature, and its direction is stable even when the mist isn't. Note where the black mountain knuckles sit relative to the plateau, because they will be your landmarks for the next few days.

Do not announce yourself loudly. Voices carry only a short distance in the damp, and a shout may travel further than you expect, sometimes returning to you in unexpected ways. Wait until someone approaches you, or walk toward the nearest settlement's lowest tier and stop at the first causeway or ladder you see.

## Clothing and Gear

**Footwear is the single most important item.** The basalt is sharp, unweathered, and sometimes still faintly warm. It sheds flakes that cut through thin soles. The travertine is porous and slick with mineral film, and it can crumble under weight. Bring boots with thick, grippy soles, ideally with reinforced toes. Break them in before you come. Bring a second pair, because the damp will ruin one within days.

**Clothing should be layered, light, and sealed against the film.** The mist is warm and wet, so heavy cloth becomes a sponge. Choose fabrics that dry quickly, and bring a loose outer layer that sheds moisture. Long sleeves and trousers protect against the salty leaves of the succulents, which drip a film onto skin and clothes. Avoid anything you cannot easily wash, because the mineral film stains and stiffens cloth over time.

**Eye protection matters more than you think.** The mist scatters light in every direction, and the lack of a sun makes glare hard to predict. Glare is less of a danger than depth-perception failure. A pair of sealed, clear or lightly tinted glasses will help you judge edges and drops. Bring a spare, and keep a cloth to wipe the condensation that forms on the lenses.

**Carry your own water, but treat it carefully.** Local drinking water comes from cisterns and gourds, filtered through reed and sand. Bring a sealed container of your own for the first few days, and avoid drinking from the basins even if they look clean. The calcite water is alkaline and mineral-heavy, and it will not agree with most travelers. Never drink from the sea.

**Pack simple tools, but not metal ones where you can help it.** The people have no metal, and a bright tool or a loose blade will draw attention, and may be seen as an intrusion. If you must carry a knife, keep it dull, wrapped, and out of sight. Carry cordage, cloth, a waterproof bag, and a small notebook and pencil. Write everything down; the mist makes memory unreliable and the shifting light makes time hard to judge.

**Bring a timekeeper and do not trust it completely.** Dawn and dusk look the same, and the only signal is the faint shift from silver to amber to grey. A wristwatch or pocket clock will keep your schedule, but many travelers find that their internal sense of time loosens within a day or two. Set alarms and make appointments with local hosts by counting cycles of the swells, which the people use as a clock. Each long swell takes several minutes to pass, and they count them in sets.

## Health and the Body

**Expect your skin to change.** The sea's film and the leaves' salt will coat you constantly. It takes days to fade, and it can itch, tighten, or cause mild rash in sensitive people. Wash in fresh water as often as you can, and pat your skin dry rather than rubbing it. Apply a light barrier cream if you have one, and avoid touching your face with unwashed hands.

**Watch for fungal and damp-related problems.** Constant moisture encourages infections between the toes, under the arms, and in any cut or scrape. Keep your feet dry when you can, change socks often, and treat small wounds immediately. Lime paste, which locals use on minor injuries, is effective but harsh, so ask before applying it.

**Expect disrupted sleep.** Without a sun, your body's sense of day and night will drift. Many visitors find themselves waking at odd hours or feeling permanently tired. Establish a sleep routine using your timekeeper and stick to it, even when the light seems to say otherwise. Sleep in the upper tiers if you can; the air is drier and the drip of water is less constant.

**Eat simply and carefully.** Most local food is plant-based, reed-rich, and mineral-seasoned, with shellfish from the shoreline. It is nourishing, but unfamiliar. Introduce new foods gradually. Ask about the sea's shellfish before eating them, because some are harvested from shelves that are still soft, and some may carry the sea's bitterness. Do not eat any plant whose leaves are coated in a salty film unless a local has prepared it for you.

**Respect the heavy water.** Swimming is possible, but the sea is dense, bitter, and slow. You will float more easily than you expect, but the film it leaves can irritate your eyes and skin for days. If you swim at all, do it in the shallows, near a local guide, and rinse thoroughly afterward.

## Navigating the Terraces

**Learn the stairs, ladders, and causeways before you trust them.** Rope ladders are strung between ledges and can fray or loosen. Stone steps are sometimes slick with growth. Causeways across the calcite basins are narrow and may be newer than they look. Test each one, and move one point of contact at a time.

**Never step onto a lamina you cannot identify.** The thin, pale crusts on the shelves may look solid, but some are still forming and will crack under weight. If a local tells you a surface is "soft," treat it as impassable. If they walk on it without hesitation, follow their exact path.

**Be aware of the tiers.** Settlements climb in overlapping levels, and doorways connect to other doorways. You may find yourself in a courtyard that is someone's roof, or a stairwell that is someone's home. Ask before you enter any structure, and remember that the "public" spaces are often shared roofs or passageways that residents use daily.

**Do not take anything from the terraces.** This includes fragments of calcite, fossilized root casts, shells, and especially any piece of the crust. The terraces are made partly of ancestors and partly of the sea's slow work. Taking even a small piece can be seen as theft, and if the sea is still deciding its form, interference is considered dangerous.

## Social Conduct

**Speak softly and deliberately.** Conversation here is intimate. People lean in close and murmur across terraces rather than shout. Match this. A raised voice will feel aggressive, and it will carry further than you intend.

**Be patient with work.** Craft here is slow and repetitive, and the people value the repetition as a kind of prayer. Do not try to speed it up, and do not interrupt a worker who is in rhythm. If you want to help, ask first and be prepared to be told to watch instead.

**Respect the dead.** Burials in the shallows are sacred, and the bodies are slowly becoming part of the terraces. Do not walk on burial shelves, do not touch the calcite formations that look like shaped stone, and do not comment on them as if they were ornaments. If you are invited to a burial ceremony, follow the lead of the people around you exactly, and keep your movements quiet.

**Treat hunting with reverence.** Hunting is rare and ceremonial. If you are present at one, remain still, do not speak, and do not offer to help unless you are asked. Afterward, expect a period of quiet.

**Chant only if invited.** The rituals use low, slow chants that rise and fall with the swells. You may be asked to join. If you are, match the rhythm of the people beside you, keep your voice low, and do not improvise.

**Ask before you record.** Cameras, recorders, and even sketching can be unwelcome, especially in ceremonial spaces. Ask permission, and accept a refusal without argument. The mist, in particular, is said to hold sounds and sometimes return them, and recording may feel like taking something that should not leave.

## Sound and Speech

Mind your voice in the basins and hollows. The local belief that the mist "remembers" is not a metaphor to them, and they will be uneasy if you speak carelessly near the calcite pools. Keep names, secrets, and anything you would not want repeated back to you to open ground, away from the water.

Learn to listen as well. The drip of water, the hum of the sea, and the rustle of the reeds form a constant background. Sounds from distant terraces may reach you late or from unexpected directions. A voice you hear may not be where you think it is, and a phrase you hear may not have been spoken recently.

## Time and Pace

The place measures time in generations, and visitors often struggle with the scale. A terrace that grew a new lip during a child's lifetime is, to the locals, simply the world doing what it does. Do not expect to see anything finish, and do not be surprised when a project you were told would take a season takes a decade. Ask about the time horizon for anything you wish to see or help build.

Keep a journal of your days. Without a sun, the blur of mist and shadow erases the sense of progression, and you may find that you have lost a day or two without noticing. A dated entry each morning will keep you oriented.

## Mental Preparation

The Drowned Terraces will feel unsettling even to experienced travelers. The land is raw and unfinished, the sea is thick and patient, and the people live with a sense of their own dead underfoot. Many visitors find themselves increasingly quiet, and some report a sense that they are being watched, or that their own pace has changed. This is normal. Let it happen, but stay grounded: keep your bearings, keep your timekeeper, and keep in regular contact with a local host.

Accept that you will not understand everything. The people's relationship with the sea, the dead, and the mist is deep and layered, and many of its meanings will remain closed to you. Ask questions, but expect to be told that some answers take a lifetime. Do not press for secrets, and do not treat your curiosity as entitlement.

Most importantly, go in as a guest, not a surveyor. You are visiting a place that is building itself out of its own past and into an unknown future. Your job is to walk carefully, speak softly, take nothing, and leave the stone as you found it.

## If Something Goes Wrong

**If you are injured on the basalt or travertine,** signal to the nearest local with a single clear call and sit still until help arrives. Do not try to climb or descend on your own.

**If you fall into a basin or the sea,** do not panic. The water is heavy and will support you, but the film will get into your eyes and mouth. Get out by the nearest shelf or ladder, and rinse thoroughly with fresh water.

**If you become disoriented in the mist,** stop. Listen for the drip of water, the hum of the sea, and any voices. Face the sea, find the nearest mountain knuckle, and wait for the light to shift. Do not walk blindly toward the basins.

**If you feel that your sense of time has slipped,** write down the date, the last thing you remember clearly, and the name of your host. Check your timekeeper and your journal, and speak to someone you trust before making any decisions.

## Leaving

Departure is not as simple as arrival. Take your time. Say your farewells in person, ideally on open ground, and thank your hosts by name. Do not leave anything behind in the shallows, and do not take anything with you that was not freely given.

As you go, you may notice that the mist has changed color, from silver toward grey. That is the hour turning, though you may not be able to say which one. Walk away from the water. The terraces will keep growing without you, one thin lamina at a time, and that is exactly how they were meant to.
