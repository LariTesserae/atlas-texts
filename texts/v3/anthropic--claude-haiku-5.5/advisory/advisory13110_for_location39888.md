# Preparing to Visit the Lowland of Settled Plates

This is a flat, warm, wet, and very quiet place. Most of the danger is in the sameness, which wears down attention, and in the small, constant hazards of a floor that was built and never finished. Prepare for slow, careful walking, damp everything, and the fact that nothing here will tell you where you are or how long you have been here.

## Footwear and body

**Feet and ankles** are the first concern. The seams between plates are a hand's width deep and knife-sharp when fresh, and the water hides them. Wear high-cut, closed boots with thick soles and good tread. Sneakers and sandals will be ruined in an hour and will cut your feet. Bring a second pair if you can, because the first will stay wet the whole time you are here. Wool socks still work well in damp heat. Synthetic socks that hold moisture will chafe.

**Clothing** should be light, loose, and long-sleeved enough to protect against the sharp plate edges and the fauna. Expect the air to feel like thirty-five degrees even though the thermometer reads the high twenties. Linen or thin cotton is comfortable until it soaks through, and then it clings and chafes. Quick-dry synthetics are a better choice, though they hold heat. Keep a lightweight rain shell in your pack. You will not be rained on, but the drip from the stacks and pylons soaks everything at a steady, low rate, and a shell keeps the drip off your back.

**Gloves** are essential. Bring heavy work gloves or grippy synthetic ones. The stacks, gantries, and pump housings are slick with condensation. Ungloved hands will slip on every vertical surface, and the mineral bloom on the fittings can irritate bare skin.

**Eyewear** should be sealed or wraparound if you can manage it. Condensation fogs glasses and beads on lenses. A small cloth and a spare set of frames, if you wear them, will help. The shimmer on the horizon can be a nuisance, so sunglasses with some tint are useful.

## Water and what you touch

Do not drink the water. It is warm, faintly salty, and metallic, and while nothing here announces it as poison, you have no way to know what is in it. Bring all the water you will need, plus a margin. Sealed bottles are better than refilling, because the humidity will leave every container feeling damp inside and out.

Do not let the water sit on bare skin for long. Wash off after crossing the pooled areas, especially where the iridescent films drift. The films look harmless and beautiful, but they are an unknown residue, and you will want them off you.

Do not step deliberately into the moving threads of water in the cut channels. They run toward drains and pump throats, and a small object or a limb can be drawn toward a drain faster than you expect. Stay on the plates beside a channel, not across it.

Avoid touching the pump housings, the cooling fins, and the extrusion towers. The fins sweat and run warm. The towers keep pushing out composite, and the streams cool quickly, but the stream edges are still soft and hot near the source. You will not be burned by the cooled plates, but the fresh extrusion can take the print of whatever stands on it.

Strike nothing. The glassy black bowls and ridges ring when struck, and the scratched lines in them look like the marks of some old, slow tool. Leave them alone. Whatever made those marks did not expect to be interrupted, and you do not need to learn what happens if you do.

## The creatures

The fauna is dense and, as far as anyone can tell, not hostile. They are small, plated, and move in fixed circuits along the seams, conveyors, and gantries. In the crowds they will brush your legs, climb your boots, and drift across your feet. Do not swat, kick, or crush them. Walk slowly and steadily. If you stop, they will pass around you. If you stand in a route for a long time, they will re-route, but they will not attack.

The clicking they make is constant and, after a while, becomes background noise. Do not assume you will stop hearing it. Your ears will adapt, but it will return when you are quiet, and you may find yourself counting its rhythm.

Do not gather them. Their dark masses darken the plates like pitch, and they do not seem to respond to you, but I cannot promise that they will not respond to a disturbance of their route. Keep a path clear and do not block a seam where a column is moving.

## Navigation

This is the hardest part. Every direction looks the same. The horizon is flat and hazy, the stacks look alike, and the plates repeat. There are no signs, no paint, no landmarks.

The seams run in consistent directions, so you can use them as a rough bearing. Note which way the nearest major seam runs when you start and follow it. Remember that a seam can end and a new one can begin at a different angle, so keep checking.

The sun is consistent, and its shadows fall in the same directions across the basin. You can use it to orient yourself, but not to tell time. It crosses the same arc every day, so do not expect noon to mean anything.

Count your steps. Keep a written log, or tally on your glove, of how far you have walked along each seam. Mark your route with something you can find again. Scratches on the plates will not show, since nothing erodes here, but a bright strip of cloth tied to a gantry or a pump fin will hold. Bring tape, paint markers, or a knife you can use to mark a stack, but be careful not to damage the composite in a way that could confuse your own route.

Do not rely on the stacks for orientation. They are all the same, and they are often exactly the same distance apart.

## Time

Time here does not behave as you expect. Nothing ages. The seams stay sharp, the water holds its level, the pumps run at the same speed, and the creatures repeat their circuits unchanged. You may feel that the hours are passing normally and then look at your watch and find they have not moved as much as you thought, or moved more than you noticed. Keep a written log of your arrival time and check it against your own sense of duration at regular intervals.

Pack something that changes visibly and steadily, such as a candle of known length, a slow-burning fuse, or a container of a substance that dissolves at a known rate. These are not perfect measures, but they give you something to check against your memory. Do not trust your body's hunger or fatigue alone, since they may not track any consistent clock here.

When you leave, expect the transition to feel abrupt. Mark the exit point, and when you return to your own world, check your log and your markers against what you remember.

## Psychological preparation

The place will press on your attention. The sameness is restful for a while and then becomes oppressive. Every direction looks like every other, the drips fall on the same plates in the same rhythm, and the machines run as if they will never stop. Expect a dulling, a drift in which you stop noticing things and start walking on autopilot. That is the real hazard. Set regular check-ins for yourself: stop, look up, note the bearing, note the time, note one thing that is different from before, even if it is small.

Do not try to find a purpose in the machines or the creatures. The place is not telling a story. It is holding a state. Trying to understand the pattern can consume your attention and leave you careless.

If you feel the urge to walk to the horizon, recognize it as the place pulling at you. The horizon is flat and endless, and you will never reach it. Turn back when you have covered the distance you planned.

## Checklist

- High-cut boots with thick soles, a spare pair if possible
- Light long-sleeved clothing, quick-dry synthetics preferred
- Heavy work gloves
- Rain shell for the drip
- Sealed water, more than you think you need
- Tinted or sealed eyewear, a cloth, spare lenses if relevant
- Written log for time, bearings, and step counts
- Marking tape or bright cloth for route markers
- A slow-changing timekeeping device, such as a candle or fuse
- A knife or marker for leaving notes, used carefully
- Wet-wipes or towels for washing off films and mineral residue
- A first-aid kit for cuts from seams and bruises from slips
- Something that does not depend on the sun or the clock to tell you the day

Visit briefly, walk slowly, keep your own record, and leave the machines and the creatures to their routes.
