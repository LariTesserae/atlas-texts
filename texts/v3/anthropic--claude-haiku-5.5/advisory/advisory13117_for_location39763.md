# Preparing to Visit the Stepped Highlands of Oruun

The Highlands will not hurt you quickly, but they will wear you down if you arrive unready. Most of the danger is cumulative: heat, wet, altitude, sleeplessness, and the slow accumulation of small mistakes. The place also rewards patience and attention, so the best preparation is partly mental. Plan to move slowly, watch closely, and assume that the land operates by rules you have not yet learned.

## Before You Go

**Learn the approach.** The lowlands end at a basalt cliff, and the only practical ways up are the switchback stairs cut into the western face, the waterfall gullies (which are wet, slippery, and loud enough to hide shouts), and the rare transit routes that use the broken grey arches. Choose the stairs unless you have a specific reason not to. The arches look like bridges but are only partly intact, and the cliff face has already swallowed some of them.

**Acclimatize in two directions.** Your body will face two stresses at once: thin air at several kilometers of elevation and heavy heat that rises from the ground. Spend at least a week at intermediate altitude if you can, then spend your last few days in hot, humid conditions so you learn how fast you sweat and how much water you lose. Work with a physician familiar with altitude medicine if one is available in your origin world. Many travelers fail here not from one dramatic problem but from thirst, electrolyte loss, and a headache they mistake for dehydration when it is hypoxia.

**Test your equipment.** Before you leave, run every instrument, light, and lamp through a full cycle in conditions similar to the Highlands: high humidity, high heat, and a few hours of exposure to a humming source. Some devices may behave differently near the grey columns. Keep spares of anything that stores power or relies on combustion. Remember that fire does not reliably catch in the lower mists, so do not plan on cooking or signaling with a flame. Carry chemical heat packs or electric sources instead, and test them first.

**Set your time.** Dawn runs long, and the haze takes an hour or more to lift. Plan your day around that. The most productive window is roughly the first two hours after the haze thins, before the noon heat becomes punishing. Dusk is loud and beautiful but dangerous, because the predators wake, the insect columns thicken, and visibility drops in the gullies.

## Body and Health

**Heat.** The plateau holds heat even under cloud, so the sky is not a reliable guide to temperature. Wear loose, light-colored, breathable layers that cover your skin against sun and insects. Take shade breaks every hour, longer at midday. Drink before you feel thirst. Carry electrolyte tablets and eat salted food, because sweat loss in saturated air is deceptive: you feel damp and cool even as you lose a great deal of fluid.

**Wet.** Constant condensation means your clothes will never fully dry in the lower forest. Bring two sets of clothing and keep one sealed in a waterproof bag. Feet are the most vulnerable part of the body. Change socks at every rest, check for maceration and fungal rash, and use a drying powder. Trench foot can develop within a day in the marsh.

**Altitude.** The ridges are harder on the lungs. Move slowly, rest often, and treat any headache, nausea, or unusual confusion as a signal to descend. Carry a supplemental oxygen source if your physician recommends it. Avoid alcohol and sedatives, which compound hypoxia.

**Sleep.** The frog chorus starts at dusk and runs through the night. Many visitors find it hypnotic, but it fragments sleep. Earplugs, a sleep mask, and a camp set well back from the reed beds will help. Never sleep in a hollow or low point. The sumps collect water and the marsh rises with the steam vents.

**Insects and parasites.** The insect drone is constant, and the moth columns at dusk can cover your face. Wear a fine head net and insect-repellent clothing. Check your skin every evening for ticks, leeches in the lower pools, and the small burrowing things that some travelers report in the wet moss.

## Navigation

The blocks are tilted so that everything drains toward the sumps. This means you can often orient yourself by water. Follow the flow downhill to find marsh and open water. Do not follow it to find the way out. Marsh is the most dangerous terrain on the plateau: mats of vegetation float on black pools and rise and fall with the vents, so they may bear your weight one moment and swallow your leg the next. Test each step with a pole and never cross a floating mat alone.

The terraced mineral pools are beautiful but treacherous. Their skins look solid but crack under a steady weight. The thin rainbow film can conceal depth.

Mark your route with materials that will last, such as metal stakes or painted stone. Do not rely on cut branches or trampled grass. Plants regrow in their original form within a season, so your trail will close up quickly.

Be wary of relying on the sky for direction. Some pools reflect a sky that does not match the real one. Use the sun, a compass if it works reliably here, or fixed landmarks like the cliff edge and the larger grey columns. Do not rely on shadows for timekeeping on the west-facing slopes, where they lag by about a minute.

Mist can help you read wind, but it often drifts against the expected currents, especially near the domes. Do not navigate by mist alone.

## Wildlife

**Predators** stand motionless in reed shadows for hours. The most common mistake is to assume an animal that does not move is inert. Keep at least twenty meters from any shape in the reeds that holds perfectly still. If you see one, move slowly and keep your back to a solid surface. Do not turn your back to open water.

**Grazers** with armored hides wade the shallows and will charge if cornered between them and deeper water. Give them room, and give way to their paths. Their plates reflect light, so they are visible from far away.

**Filter-feeders** in the deepest pools are slow and mostly harmless, but disturbing the mineral skins can release sediment and foul the water for hours. Do not wade into those pools.

**Beetle colonies** on tree trunks form a living bark. Do not touch them. Their clustered movement can make a trunk look like it is breathing, and disturbing them can provoke a defensive spray that irritates skin and eyes.

**Animals avoiding stones.** Some creatures will not cross certain flat stones, and no one has found the reason. Do not step on those stones, and do not try to test the limit. If an animal turns away from a path, take the same detour. Watching where animals walk is one of the best ways to read the terrain.

**Flowers and moths.** The heavy pale flowers open at dusk and attract dense moth spirals. The scent is pleasant but strong. Do not sleep near the flowers, and do not handle the moths, which may carry irritant scales.

## The Built Remains

The grey columns are the most important thing on the plateau and the least understood. Approach them carefully and with respect.

**Do not touch the hum.** Some columns vibrate at a pitch you feel in your teeth. Prolonged proximity may cause headache, nausea, or a sense of pressure behind the eyes. Keep your distance from the humming ones, and leave the area if the sensation grows.

**Cool surfaces are not safe surfaces.** The grey material stays cool even at noon, which is unnatural in this heat. That is not a sign of harmlessness. Do not lean on the columns or rest against them. Do not try to open them. The cracked, hollow ones are colonized by fibrous plant lattices, and the roots are tough enough to hold your weight, but they are also tough enough to trap a limb.

**Sunken columns in the marsh** are often submerged to the base. Do not dive near them. Water around them may be deeper or more turbulent than it looks.

**The domes** pull moisture from the air and channel it into grooves. The etched patterns are fine enough that you should not try to read them with a fingernail or a probe. Do not drink water that has run through the grooves until it has been tested. Some domes shimmer when viewed directly, so look at them sideways or through a lens. If you feel your attention slipping while you stare at one, look away and walk off.

**The broken arch** across the gorge is slowly being absorbed into the cliff. Do not stand beneath it, and do not climb it. Its stability is unknown.

**Documentation.** If you photograph or sketch the structures, do it from a distance and keep your notes separate from your body. Some travelers have reported that devices stop working near certain columns, or that recordings come out wrong. Keep a backup record.

## The Strangeness

Some phenomena here are harmless and some are not. Treat all of them as data, not as entertainment.

**Frozen steam.** The vents occasionally freeze in midair for a few seconds and then resume. If you are standing near one when this happens, do not panic. Stop moving, keep your eyes open, and count. Do not walk into a frozen plume, because the moment it releases may come without warning.

**Other skies.** Some pools show a sky that does not match the one overhead. Their clouds move the wrong way or take shapes no weather could make. Do not look into those pools for long. Some visitors report a feeling of vertigo or of being pulled toward the reflected sky. If you see one, look up at the real sky and take a few steps away from the water.

**Water rising the domes.** At night, water seems to climb the grooved domes against gravity in silver threads. This is beautiful and unsettling. Do not collect water from these threads. If you want to observe it, do so from a distance with a light that does not blind you.

**Shadows that lag.** On the west-facing slopes, shadows trail the sun by about a minute. This is a good reason not to rely on shadow for timing, and a good reason to carry a clock that does not depend on the sun.

**Regrowth.** Cut plants regrow in the same form within a season. Do not expect to take specimens, collect deadwood, or leave evidence of your passage. The forest will restore itself around you. If you want a lasting record, carry it in your head and in your notes.

## Dynamics and Time

Nothing here changes on a human timescale. The vents pulse over decades. The lichen grows an inch per century. The grey structures decay so slowly that a crack you see this season will look the same when you return in a lifetime. The forest is in equilibrium, and each fallen tree is replaced by another in its place.

This has practical consequences. Do not expect the terrain you survey to be the same when you come back, unless you return within a few seasons, and even then the marsh edges may have moved by a few meters. Plan for the land to look as it does now, and keep your own records accurate. Do not plan around the expectation that a path will be cleared or a hazard removed.

Also, do not assume that a place that looks dead is safe. The forest is patient, and so are the predators.

## Leaving

Descend before you are exhausted. The way down is harder than the way up on tired legs, and the cliff stairs are slick with condensation. Give yourself a full day of margin for the descent, and expect the lowlands to feel strangely cool and dry when you arrive.

Do not take anything from the plateau that you did not come with. Specimens, water, and stones may carry effects that are not yet understood. If you must carry something out, document it thoroughly and quarantine it.

When you return to your origin world, rest for several days, drink plenty of water, and watch your health closely. Some travelers report lingering fatigue, a persistent sensation of humming in the ears, or dreams of standing on a staircase that never reaches the top. These usually fade. If they do not, seek help from people who have studied the Highlands.

## A Final Word

The Stepped Highlands are not hostile in any simple sense. They are old, patient, and indifferent to visitors, and their dangers are mostly the ordinary dangers of heat, water, and wild animals, multiplied by strange rules that do not yet have names. Go slowly, respect the stillness of the things that wait, and treat every puzzling thing as a warning until you understand it. The land will still be here after you leave, unchanged in any way you can measure.
