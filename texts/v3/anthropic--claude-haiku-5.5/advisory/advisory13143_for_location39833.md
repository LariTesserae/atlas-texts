# Preparing to Visit the Weeping Terraces

## The First Thing to Understand

Everything here is wet, and it will stay wet. You will not be able to fix that, only manage it. Most visitors who get into trouble have spent their first day trying to keep things dry and their second day discovering that they can't. Plan for water the way a sailor plans for the sea: assume it will reach everything you own, and make sure the things you can't afford to lose are sealed, wrapped, or kept on your body.

Also understand that the land itself is not a fixed backdrop. The terraces you see today may not be where they were yesterday. A map from a previous visit is a rough guide at best, and a good local guide is worth more than any chart.

## Clothing

Dress in layers that dry fast and keep warm when damp. Wool and synthetic fibres are better than cotton, which becomes heavy and cold within minutes.

- **Outer layer:** a hooded shell that sheds the fine mist and resists light rain. Long enough to cover your hips. The mist beads on sleeves and collects at cuffs, so close the cuffs.
- **Mid layer:** a fleece or thin wool jumper. Expect to be cold at altitude in the morning and at dusk, and sweaty from climbing during the day. Layers let you manage both.
- **Base layer:** wicking fabric against the skin.
- **Hat:** wide enough to shade your eyes from the glare, and warm enough for the frost. A cap with a brim works well. Sunglasses or tinted glasses are close to essential, because the reflections off the wet stone are sharp enough to cause temporary dazzle.
- **Gloves:** two pairs if you can manage it, one waterproof, one warm. Your hands will be wet and cold constantly, and you will need to handle rope, stone, and ice.

## Footwear

This is the most important decision you will make. Choose boots with aggressive lugs and a stiff sole that will not fold on uneven, rounded stone. Stone here can be slick with a film of water that is barely visible. Wear gaiters or tie your trousers tight so the seep does not run into your boots.

Bring a second pair of socks and keep them dry in a sealed bag. Wet feet are the fastest route to misery and blisters, and the cold will make both worse.

Be aware of the mudstone. In the wettest places the surface softens and holds your footprints for about an hour before smoothing them away. Walking through it is fine, but it means the ground can give way under a stride that seemed solid. Test each step, and avoid soft patches that look darker or glossier than the surrounding rock.

## Pack and Gear

Pack light and waterproof. A large dry bag, or two, is worth more than any other single item. Inside it, keep:

- Dry clothing, sealed in a second bag
- A waterproof torch, plus spare batteries kept dry
- A small first aid kit, with extra attention to blister care and treatments for cold injuries
- Water purification tablets or a filter, even though water is everywhere. Much of it is seep water that has passed over lichen, moss, and the bodies of the small pale creatures. Boil it or treat it, or ask locals which channels are safe for drinking
- Food that doesn't need cooking and keeps in damp (see below)
- A knife with a sealed handle, plus a small whetstone, since blades rust fast
- Rope, around ten to fifteen metres of it, both for crossing the steep stepped terraces and for securing yourself on the slumps
- A notebook in a sealed case, and a pencil. Pen ink runs, and paper turns to pulp
- Sunscreen and lip balm. The thin air and strong light will burn you faster than you expect

Leave behind anything made of metal that rusts easily, leather that will stiffen and crack, and electronics you cannot keep dry. Metal is nearly absent here, and locals may be startled by it, which is worth remembering when you show people your tools.

## Food and Water

Pack food that survives damp: hard biscuits, dried fruit, nuts, cured meats, and hard cheese. Avoid bread, which goes grey and soft within a day, and anything that needs fire.

You can cook, but dry fuel is scarce and the wood here is precious. Do not burn timber from the scrub or from the low twisted trees in the lee of the boulders. Use a compact stove if you have one, and expect the wind to make lighting it difficult. Ask a local before you cook anything, because the stoves and cooking hearths in the settlements are shared and kept in a particular way.

Eat the grazers' food only if you are invited. Do not eat the lichen, even the taller ones after rain. Do not eat the cushion-forming mats without knowing which are edible; they look harmless and some may not be. The grey moss grown in trays is cultivated and eating it is generally fine, but ask first.

## Sleep and Cold

Nights are cold, and frost will form on everything. Sleep in a warm, well-insulated bag rated for near freezing. Bring a bivvy or a waterproof cover for the bag, and an insulating mat, because the stone pulls heat out of your body faster than the air does.

Do not pitch a tent on a seep channel, a gravel fan, or the slope below a wall. The places where the ground looks flattest are often the places most likely to shift or flood. Look for a raised area with clear drainage above it and no sign of recent movement. A good local guide will show you where people sleep.

Pay attention to the land at night. Water can cut a new course overnight, so if you hear running water where there was none before, move before it reaches you. A tent in the wrong place can be undermined in an hour.

## Hazards

**Ice.** Each dusk a glassy skin of ice forms on the stone. It breaks at the first footstep, which is startling and makes you lose balance. Walk carefully in the early morning and at dusk. Do not run.

**Slumps and breaches.** Dry-laid walls lean and fail, and the gravel that spills from breaches can bury a foot or a leg in seconds. Never stand directly below a leaning wall. Do not climb on walls, even ones that look solid.

**Channels.** Some seep runs are fast, cold, and deeper than they look. Cross only at marked fords or where locals cross. If you fall in, get out at once; the cold can cause real harm within minutes.

**Uphill water.** At dawn, a thin silver thread sometimes runs uphill over a step of stone. Do not dam it, stand in its path, or try to redirect it. The locals regard it as the water remembering its path, and interfering with it is considered disrespectful at best and dangerous at worst, since it can reverse the flow of channels below.

**Falling stones and birds.** The flocks drop onto terraces and lift again, and occasionally a stone will come loose from a wall. Keep your head covered and avoid stopping under the high walls where birds roost. The birds are not aggressive, but they are numerous and their movement is sudden.

**The lacy moss.** It spreads quickly and vanishes within weeks. Do not step on it deliberately, and do not pull it. Its fine structure is delicate, and locals treat it as a sign of the land's mood.

**Sun and dazzle.** The reflections can cause snow-blindness-like strain. Wear tinted eye protection. If your vision blurs, sit down in shade and rest your eyes before going on.

**Altitude.** The air is thin. Climb slowly, drink regularly, and stop if you feel dizzy or short of breath.

## Manners and Customs

The people here speak low and close. Voices do not carry, so a conversation across a terrace will be unheard. Match their volume. Shouting is a children's thing, used for the grazers, and an adult shouting across a terrace will be seen as rude or alarming. If you need to call someone, walk to them.

Do not take stones from walls, gutters, or troughs. Every stone is placed by someone who expects to need it again. Ask before you move anything, even a loose slab.

Help with repairs if you are invited. Clearing silt from a trough, reseating a wall stone, or carrying water to a family is a good way to earn trust. Do not start repairs on your own, especially on structures you do not understand. A stone set in the wrong place can divert a whole channel.

Do not build permanent structures. The settlement's shape is a record of decisions, and new buildings change the water's path. If you need shelter, use the existing huts or a temporary tent placed with a local's advice.

Respect the grazers. They are herded in small groups and are not pets. Do not approach them without a herder present. Their coats shed water like thatch, and they can be startled by sudden movement.

Ask before photographing people, houses, or the channels. Some of the water's movements are considered private, and cameras may draw attention in ways you did not intend.

## Mental Preparation

Expect the place to feel unstable. Nothing here is permanent, and visitors often find that unsettling. The people who live here are relaxed about it in a way that can seem strange. They treat change as weather: something to plan around, not to resist. Try to adopt the same attitude. Do not expect your route to hold, your camp to stay dry, or your map to be right. Let the day's plans bend.

The quiet is also unusual. Voices die in the wet air, and the constant hiss of water fills every gap. Some visitors find this peaceful, others find it oppressive. Take time to listen. The sounds of the amphibians shifting, the birds clicking, and the ice breaking in the morning are the closest thing the place has to conversation.

Be patient with yourself. The first few hours are usually the hardest, as your body adjusts to the cold, the thin air, and the strange light. By the second day most visitors settle into a rhythm: walking slowly, watching the water, and trusting the ground less and the locals more.

## Leaving

Leave as the light begins to slant, when the birds settle into the high walls and the water keeps running down through its channels. Before you go, check your packs for anything you have brought in, and leave nothing behind but footprints, which will be smoothed away within the hour anyway.

Carry the day's small changes out with you in your memory. The terraces will be different when you return, if you ever do, and so will you.
