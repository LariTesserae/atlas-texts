# Preparing for the Basalt Plateau

This is a place that rewards slowness and punishes haste. Nothing here is dangerous in a dramatic way. The risks come from wet stone, constant noise, and a system that has run for centuries without anyone needing to hurry. Prepare for a damp, loud, mineral-rich environment with a lot of small hazards and very few emergencies.

## Arrival and Altitude

The plateau sits about two thousand meters up, and the climb from the lowland haze can catch travelers off guard. Give yourself a full day to adjust before any strenuous walking. Expect mild headaches, shortness of breath on stairs, and poor sleep for the first night or two. The city's stairs are steep, and the cliff terraces are stepped with long flights, so plan your first excursions on the upper grid, where the ground is flat and the blocks are easy to navigate.

The warm lowland air that feeds the mist also means your body will be working hard to regulate heat in a humid environment at altitude. Drink more than you think you need. But do not drink the water you find on the surface. Seepage carries gypsum, calcite, and iron, and the marsh water is coated with iron bloom. Carry sealed water, or bring a filter rated for mineral contamination and treat everything you didn't bring yourself.

## Clothing and Footing

Everything gets wet here, and most of it stays wet. Mist beads on every surface, the afternoon drizzle is lukewarm but relentless, and the cliffs weep through the morning. Dress in layers you can remove without getting clammy, and choose a waterproof outer shell that breathes. Cotton holds moisture and will leave you cold once the cloud breaks and the air shifts. Synthetics and wool dry faster.

Footwear matters more than anything else you pack. The basalt is smooth and slick when wet. The gypsum lace that coats the cliffs and seepage lines is brittle and can flake underfoot, and the calcified surfaces are slicker than they look. Choose boots with aggressive lugs and a stiff sole, and waterproof them thoroughly. Bring a second pair if you plan to spend days in the marshes, because the spongy ground soaks through leather and the iron bloom stains and degrades it.

Gloves are essential, not optional. Handrails and cable supports are coated in mineral film and will cut or abrade bare hands. Leather work gloves with a grip coating are better than thin gloves that wet through in minutes.

Expect your skin to suffer. Persistent humidity softens feet and invites fungal problems, so keep a foot-care kit ready, change socks at midday, and let your boots dry overnight. Iron bloom and mineral dust stain skin and fabric. Dark clothing hides the staining better than light, and you should treat anything that touches the marsh as permanently marked.

## Sound and Sleep

The plateau's noise is constant. Pumps lift water from the cisterns in a low chugging rhythm that you will feel in your chest before you consciously hear it. Factories grind and hiss along the escarpment, and the cooling towers breathe out a sound like surf. You will not get away from it entirely, and you should not expect to.

Bring earplugs rated for low-frequency attenuation, since standard foam plugs barely touch the deep tones. Bring an eye mask for sleep, because the cloud deck and early-morning mist can make dawn bright and disorienting. Sleeping in the upper blocks may be somewhat quieter than the lower terraces, which sit closer to the engines, but expect noise everywhere.

Conversation adjusts to the noise. People speak close together and lean in, so you will often need to be near someone to be understood. Learn a few basic gestures before you arrive, and agree on signals with your companions for "stop," "wait," and "come here." Shouting across a gorge or a terrace will go unheard.

## Birds, Insects, and the Gorge Creatures

The fauna is dense and at times overwhelming. Swifts and swallows fill the sky at every altitude and nest in the cooling towers and cliff cracks. Their mud-and-slag nests are packed so tightly that the towers look furred. Do not approach nests, do not touch them, and keep your head covered in the gorge mouths where the birds are thickest. At dusk the sky flickers with them and their calls merge into a single shrill texture. Many travelers find this beautiful. It also means you should expect droppings on everything that sits still for long.

The marshes hold grey-brown frogs whose chorus rises after dark until it sounds like a second machine. If you camp or sleep near the marsh edges, you will hear them all night. They are not dangerous. Keep your gear sealed to avoid them.

Large, iridescent beetles move through the cisterns and along the aqueducts, feeding on mineral film and on each other. Shake out clothing and bedding before use. Inspect your pack when you return from the lower terraces. Do not handle beetles, and do not assume their colors are a warning or an invitation. Their defenses are unknown.

The pale, many-legged creatures in the gorges are the most unusual residents. They move along the seepage lines, feed on the gypsum lace, and leave trails of calcified mucus that harden into a crust. They build domed shelters from slag and sediment, and their settlements can look like ruins from a distance. Do not step on a dome. Do not walk along active seepage lines if you can avoid it, since that is where the creatures move and where the lace is most fragile. If you do get mucus on your boots or clothing, do not try to peel it off with bare hands. Bring a stiff scraper and a solvent recommended by the local maintenance crews, and let it dry before you attempt removal.

## Light, Air, and Smell

The light is pale and diffuse under the mist, which is gentle on the eyes, but midday brings sharp contrast when the cloud breaks. Bring sunglasses with a good UV rating and a second pair with a light tint for overcast mornings. At dusk the marshes turn copper, and the reflected light can be strong enough to disorient you on the flats.

Everything carries condensation. Your glasses will fog, your camera lens will bloom, and your electronics will corrode if you let them sit in damp air. Pack electronics in sealed bags with silica gel, and bring lens cloths in quantity. Keep your devices close to your body in the early morning, when the mist is thickest.

The air is warm, wet, and faintly metallic. It carries the smell of hot iron, wet stone, and standing water. Most visitors stop noticing it within a day. If you are sensitive to strong smells or have respiratory issues, bring a light filter mask, especially for the factory terraces. The dry tuff dust that lies on exposed ground can irritate your lungs when the air is not saturated, so wear a mask whenever you walk the bare gravel areas in dry spells.

## Navigation and Infrastructure

The city is layered, and that makes it easy to get lost. The upper level is a regular grid of flat-roofed blocks, and it is the easiest place to orient yourself. The lower terraces are older and more irregular, with aqueducts, cisterns, and factories stacked along the escarpment. Stairs connect the levels, but many are steep, wet, and partly worn away.

Carry a physical map and a compass. Under the cloud deck and in the winter fog, electronic navigation may be unreliable, and the towers can vanish entirely. In winter, plan to stay within sight of a landmark or travel only with a companion and a rope. Do not wander into fog on the flats.

Respect the infrastructure. Cables run between pylons across the gorges, sagging under the weight of moisture. Keep your distance from them, and never climb a pylon or tie anything to a cable. The pumps draw water from the cisterns constantly, so keep clear of intakes and any grates or channels that feed them. The cisterns are cut into rock and are dark, flooded in places, and slippery. Do not enter one without a strong light and a reason. The aqueducts stand on arches that look ancient and sound, but they are patched and repatched, and you should assume they carry weight only where the maintenance crews say they do.

The city is maintained by people who understand its patterns, and it is worth finding them. Ask before you enter restricted areas, observe how they move, and follow their lead on stairs, crossings, and gorge paths. Their knowledge of the seepage lines, the lace, and the weather will save you hours of trouble.

## Food, Supplies, and Shelter

Nothing green grows on the plateau. Bring all your food, and expect moisture to ruin standard packaging. Vacuum-sealed or foil-lined packs with desiccant are the best option. Dry foods that do not absorb water, such as hard grains and nuts, will keep better than bread, fruit, or anything with sugar. Plan for more calories than usual, because the cold, wet nights and the climbs will drain you faster than the warm days suggest.

Shelter should be waterproof, ventilated, and off the ground. The mist condenses on everything, and damp leaks through fabric faster than you expect. Elevated sleeping platforms and an open-front shelter that catches airflow are better than enclosed tents. Dry your gear every day, and carry a small drying line.

## The Pace of the Place

The most important preparation is mental. Nothing on the plateau moves quickly. The cliffs dissolve over centuries, the lace thickens by millimeters each season, and the city replaces one block at a time. The people, the animals, and the stone all wait. Visitors who try to rush through the terraces, rush the marshes, or rush the afternoon drizzle usually end up wet, tired, and frustrated.

Plan your days around the cloud cycle. Mornings are wrapped in mist and the cliffs drip. Afternoons bring cumulus and sometimes sun. Evenings bring drizzle and the frog chorus. Schedule strenuous climbs and marsh walks for the clearer midday window, and save the lower terraces for the calmer hours when the factories are slower and the swifts are settled. In winter, consider not going at all, or limiting yourself to the upper grid, where the fog is less threatening.

Stay for the rhythm if you can. The first day is loud and disorienting. By the third or fourth, the pumps become a tide you stop hearing, the birds become texture, and the mist feels like a natural condition. People who stay long enough often say they start to move the way the place moves.

## Before You Leave

Inspect everything. Check your packs, boots, and clothing for beetles, frogs, and mineral film. Gypsum and calcite will clog zippers, buckles, and seams, so clean them before they harden. Iron bloom will have stained anything that touched the marsh, and it is difficult to remove. Dry your electronics, your gear, and your boots before storing them, or they will grow mildew in the bag.

Leave the plateau the way you found it. Do not take stone, lace, nest material, or anything from the cisterns and marsh. Do not disturb the domes or the shelters of the gorge creatures. And if you can, leave with a clear sense that the place will still be doing what it has always done when you are gone, because it will.
