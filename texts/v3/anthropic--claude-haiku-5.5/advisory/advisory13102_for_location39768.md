# Preparing to Visit the Shelf

## Overview

The Shelf is a high, wet, silent city of stone, now half-returned to the ground. It is not dangerous in the way a predator or a storm is dangerous. Its hazards are slow and mostly physical: slick footing, cold, disorientation, and the constant risk of a wall or ledge giving way. Most of your preparation should aim at three things: staying warm and dry, moving safely on wet stone, and never losing your way. Plan as though you will be cold, wet, and briefly lost at some point, and you will have a comfortable visit.

## Altitude and Climate

The plateau sits around two thousand meters, in cloud for much of the day. Expect cool air, high humidity, and nights that drop near freezing, especially in the season when the stone splits in freeze-thaw cycles. Altitude itself is modest, but:

- **Acclimatize for a day or two** if you arrive from low ground. Headaches, shortness of breath on the stairs, and poor sleep are normal, and climbing the escarpment's stepped approaches will make them worse.
- **Drink more than you think you need.** Cold, damp air hides dehydration. Carry water, but treat everything on the Shelf as unsafe to drink until you have purified it. The cisterns are full, clear, and very tempting. They are also stagnant at the edges, reed-choked, and likely harbor organisms you do not want in your gut.
- **Layer, and keep the inner layer dry.** Wool or synthetic base layers hold warmth when wet. Cotton holds water and will chill you quickly. A windproof outer shell is more important than a heavy coat, since the wind moving through the doorways is cold and persistent.

## Footwear and Movement

The basalt is polygonal and cracked, and the paving is worn smooth in the middle of streets and green and slick at the edges. Every crack holds water, and the moss is treacherous.

- **Boots with aggressive lugs and high ankle support** are essential. Choose soles that bite on wet, moss-covered stone, not just on dry rock.
- **Carry trekking poles** for stairs, sloped streets, and the steep cuts of the gutters. A pole you can plant in a crack is worth more than a sturdy wall, because you cannot trust the walls.
- **Never lean on a wall** that is leaning out of true, slumped, or tilted. Many are, and some still shift with a dull thud when no one touches them. Keep a body-width distance from any upright stone.
- **Watch for the sheet-water.** Cracked spouts throw thin films across streets. These look like puddles but can hide slick stone beneath. Test each step on a dry line if you can see one.

## Navigation

This is the most important preparation, and it is the easiest to underestimate. The city is dense: blocks on blocks, courtyards inside courtyards, doorways that open onto other doorways. The fog, which rolls in each early morning and lingers into the afternoon, erases landmarks. The walls all look alike, and the stone does not change color with distance.

- **Carry a compass and a physical map** you have made or obtained beforehand. Do not rely on a phone. Electronics may fail in the wet, and the Shelf's no-metal character makes some devices feel out of place, though a compass works fine.
- **Carry a reel of cord or flagging tape.** Tie the cord at each entrance you pass through, or mark the first and last doorway of each courtyard with a single strip. Remove markers on your way out; leaving litter on the Shelf is poor manners.
- **Count your turns and doorways.** Keep a written tally, waterproofed, of how many doors, arches, and stairs you pass. This is more reliable than memory in an unfamiliar place.
- **Know the cascade.** The settlement drains downhill through the cisterns and gutters. When you are lost, following water downhill will eventually bring you to a lower basin, and the lowest districts are the easiest to read.
- **Set a turnaround time** and stick to it. The fog returns in the afternoon, and visibility can drop to a few meters within minutes.

## Light

The sky is a pale, even grey-white. Shadows appear only at the base of walls, so judging distance and the depth of an opening is difficult. Stairs and ledges can look flat from above.

- **Bring a waterproof headlamp** with a strong white beam and a red or dim setting. Use it in the fog and under the roofed remnants of rooms, where the dim light becomes a trap.
- **Use a second light source** for emergencies, and keep spare batteries sealed.
- **Plan around the brief breaks in the cloud.** When sun slices through, the wet rock turns silver and the plateau seems to glow for a few minutes. These moments are beautiful, but they are short. Do not let them pull you into an unmapped courtyard.

## Water, Food, and Fuel

- **Carry all the water you need, plus a purification method.** A filter that handles fine sediment and a chemical or UV treatment is best. Boiling works, but fuel is limited (see below).
- **Bring all your food.** The Shelf supports few animals, and they mostly pass through. Do not eat the moss, the lichens, the reeds, or the shrubs. Some may be harmless, but you cannot know, and the lichens grow so slowly that gathering them does real damage.
- **Fuel is scarce and wet.** Juniper and thorn are rare, and the dead wood is soaked. Bring fire starter, dry tinder in a sealed container, and enough stove fuel for every meal and every cup of water you intend to purify.
- **Leave no trace.** Carry out all your packaging and waste. The Shelf is patient and takes back what it is given, but it is slow about it.

## Shelter and Sleep

Roofless rooms are common, and they seem inviting. Be cautious.

- **Do not sleep in any room whose walls lean or have slumped.** A wall that has held for centuries can fall during the night.
- **Avoid camping beneath overhangs** where water drips steadily. The drip is quiet, but it will keep you awake, and the stone beneath is always wet.
- **Choose flat, open ground** on a courtyard with stable walls on all sides, or pitch a tent on the paving well away from the cisterns and the cascade spouts. Expect condensation inside the tent and dew on everything by morning.
- **Plan for cold nights.** A four-season sleeping bag rated well below freezing, an insulated pad, and a warm hat will serve you better than extra blankets.
- **Expect the wind.** It moves through the empty doorways in a low, breathing note that will carry through your tent walls. Earplugs and a sleep mask can help.

## Sound and Silence

The Shelf is the quietest place most travelers will ever stand, and the sounds that break that silence are unnerving.

- **Keep your voice low.** Sound carries oddly through the stone and fades slowly. Shouting is not a good way to find a companion in fog.
- **Listen for the drip, the wind, and the thud of shifting stone.** The thud is short and dull, and its echo takes a long time to fade. If you hear one, do not walk toward it to investigate. Walk away from walls, not toward them.
- **Expect to feel watched.** The empty city, the doorways, and the sense of having just missed someone are powerful, and they can make you careless. Keep to your plan.

## Wildlife

- **Small brown birds** nest in the roofless rooms and forage along the gutters. They are harmless and often the best sign that the place is safe.
- **Many-legged creatures** move under the moss in the mornings. Do not put your hands into moss, and check your boots and sleeping gear before bringing them indoors.
- **Pale insects** hover over the cisterns in the mist. They are probably harmless, but cover exposed skin and use repellent with them.
- **Larger animals** sometimes cross the high streets at dusk. Do not approach them. Be off the open streets by dusk, and carry a light and a whistle.

## Respecting the Place

The builders of the Shelf are gone, and their city is being returned to the ground at the pace of ice and moss. The stones are not yours to move, and their fittings are not yours to take. Do not chip samples, stack stones, or add marks. Photograph if you wish, but remember that the Shelf will look different to the next visitor, and that is part of what it is.

## Multiverse-Specific Preparation

Because the Shelf sits in a place where your home reality's anchors may be weak, plan for the return with particular care:

- **Carry an anchor object** from your home reality that you can hold in your hand throughout the visit. Something personal, something you would notice missing.
- **Record your arrival point precisely**, including the cascade basin nearest to it, the doorway you entered by, and the bearing from the escarpment edge. Your return depends on finding that point.
- **Establish a check-in routine.** Every few hours, write down the time by your own clock, your location, and how you feel. Time may not run evenly here, and a written record will help you notice any drift.
- **Agree on a turnaround rule with companions**, and make sure everyone carries the same map and the same markers. Splitting up in the fog is the most common way visitors get lost.
- **Be honest with yourself about the emotional weight.** Even a short stay in an empty city of stone and water can leave you quiet for a long time afterward. Plan a gentle first day back in your own world.

## Summary Checklist

- Waterproof, layered clothing with a windproof shell
- High-grip boots and trekking poles
- Waterproof headlamp with spare batteries
- Compass, physical map, cord or flagging tape, and a waterproof notebook
- Water purification and all food and fuel
- Four-season sleeping gear and a dry, sealed tinder kit
- Anchor object and recorded arrival point
- Turnaround time and a shared check-in routine

Go slowly. The Shelf rewards patience, and it has been waiting for a very long time.
