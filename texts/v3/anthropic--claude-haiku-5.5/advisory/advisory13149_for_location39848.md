# Preparing to Visit the Sump Plain

This is a place that will not stop moving, make noise, or let you rest easily. Most of your preparation is about your body, your senses, and your sense of where you are. The plain itself is less dangerous than it is relentless.

## Before You Arrive

**Arrive with a fixed reference.** The map you carry will be wrong within a day, and the causeways you walked yesterday may have walked off overnight. Before you go, fix a point you can return to. The most reliable anchors are the oldest structures at the far edge, where the towers stop, because nothing there moves. If your crossing point or return route can be tied to one of those low, sunken buildings, you will always know which way is out. If you arrive by a portal, check whether the portal's anchor is on the living city. If it is, expect it to relocate, and plan to walk to the edge on foot.

**Expect to be wet, and plan for it.** The air sits near saturation, and it rains in pulses, with a permanent drizzle of mist beneath the pumping halls. You will not stay dry. Plan for the cold-wet problem rather than the rain itself: wear synthetic or wool layers, which keep their insulation when damp, and avoid cotton entirely. Bring a waterproof outer shell, and pack everything you cannot afford to lose in double-sealed dry bags. Nothing dries quickly here, and mildew will take anything organic within a few days.

**Bring all your food.** There are no plants, and the fauna are not a food source for travelers. Ration packs with sealed, dry contents are best. Anything that needs refrigeration will spoil, and anything that absorbs moisture will go soft. Fresh food is not worth carrying. Bring more water-purification capacity than you think you need, because the water here is not something to drink on instinct. It runs through the pumping halls, comes out misted, and carries a dissolved mineral film. Filter and test everything. Sealed water from your origin is safer for the first days.

**Protect your eyes and airways.** The mist carries fine mineral particles, and the film it leaves coats every surface, including your skin and your lenses. Bring a respirator with particulate filters, or at minimum a dust mask you can swap often, and wear sealed eyewear. Carry eyewash. Your visor will fog and streak within minutes of arrival, so pack spares and something for cleaning.

## Footing

The ground looks solid from a distance and is not.

Standing water sits in sheets a few centimeters deep, which sounds harmless until you notice that the channels between sinter plates and causeway edges are much deeper, and the sheet water hides their edges. Carry a long pole and test every step. The mud beneath the sheets can grip a boot hard enough to pull it off, so avoid stopping on soft mud, and keep moving when you cross it.

The sinter is sharp. Its plates are crusted and edged with right angles, and they cut through thin soles and gloves. Heavy, high-ankle boots with aggressive soles and cut-resistant gaiters will serve you. Gloves should be thick enough to grip wet rails and lattice without sliding.

The fused black seams are glassy and smooth. They are slippery when wet and should be treated as a surface that can change under you. Do not set down equipment on them. The material is self-repairing, and if you leave a pack or tool in a seam, it may be slowly absorbed as the surface closes over it.

## Moving Through the City

**Never trust a bridge's end.** Many bridges reach into empty air and stop, and they may extend, retract, or reroute while you are on them. Do not step onto an unfinished span. If you must cross, use only spans that connect two completed structures, and check them before you step out. Look for fresh joints, new plate seams, or any sign of recent movement.

**Keep away from the shingle plates.** The outer skins of the towers open and close, and each gap breathes air with a hollow sigh. When the sigh changes pitch, the plates are closing, and they can pinch fingers, limbs, or anything else in the gap. If you hear the sigh rise, move at once. Do not reach into a gap to free something that has caught. Call for help or step back, and let the plates finish.

**Listen for the causeways.** A causeway can lift on hydraulic legs and walk sideways over a night, and you will hear it before you see it. The sound is a rising whine under the machinery noise, then a long grinding as the legs take weight. When you hear it, move to the nearest high ground or to a fixed structure, and do not stand on the causeway that is moving. Plan crossings for the hours when the halls are quiet, if you can identify them, and avoid crossings during or just after a rainpulse.

**Watch the water levels.** When rain backs up into the channels, the sheet water rises, and whole quarters flood. The pumping halls work for a day or two and then the water drops. Before you commit to a route, check the water. If it is climbing, get to the causeway spines, which are raised, and stay there until it falls. Do not wade into flooded quarters to save time.

**Respect the haulers and barges.** The long segmented haulers carry sinter and bulk material along the corridors of standing water. They do not stop for you. Stay well clear of the channel edges when one passes, because its wake will throw you into the water. Do not moor to the bright hooks or board the barges without leave. The hooks are there for the people who work the city, and using them without permission will make your presence known in ways you may not want.

## The Fauna

The noise here is a weather system, and it is also a set of warnings if you learn to read it.

The flocks of pale, long-winged things are sharp and fast, and their rattling calls carry over kilometers. They wheel above the towers in clouds that can dim the sun. They are not known to attack, but a flock that suddenly drops toward you is a flock reacting to something, and the wise response is to move under cover, not to flee in open ground. Carry a hood or a cap with a brim that protects your face from debris they dislodge.

The swimmers in the channels surface with a boom and sink again, and the sound travels far. Keep at least several meters from channel edges. Do not wade near a place where the water is disturbed, and never swim. You will not out-swim anything in here, and the water is not safe.

The armored crawlers along the causeways are the size of dogs, with iron-colored plates and grinding jaws. They graze the sinter and do not seem to notice people, but they do not give way. If one is on your route, step around it at a distance and do not put your hand near its mouth. Their gear-like grinding is a sign that they are feeding, and they are likely to resent interruption.

The insect swarms are dense in the mist, and they are attracted to the film on your skin and gear. Bring head netting and a light repellent you have verified is safe for your physiology. Keep your mouth and nose covered in the thickest swarms.

Do not eat anything you find here, and do not collect samples of the fauna without a plan for handling them. Death in this place is an input, and a body in the water will be taken apart and returned to the lattice. Your gear and your body are not an exception.

## The People You Might Not See

The city shows the marks of hands: hatch-doors, handrails, bright hooks, sealed galleries, and hauler cabs that are closed. The people are not out in the mist, and it is likely that they keep their distance. If you encounter a hatch that is open, or a gallery with a light on, approach slowly, make your presence visible, and wait. Do not enter any enclosed space. Do not attempt to open a sealed hatch or hook a barge. If someone answers, let them speak first.

It is also possible that the work of hands is not entirely human, and that you will be observed by something you do not recognize. Behave as if you are being watched, and keep your movements unhurried and visible.

## Sleep and Time

Most visitors find the first night here the hardest. The noise does not stop, and the sheet water shifts constantly beneath you. Bring earplugs for sleep even if you wear hearing protection by day, and bring a sleeping platform or inflatable mat that holds you above the sinter and the damp. Sleep on high ground in a fixed structure if you can. The oldest buildings at the edge are the quietest place on the plain, and many visitors choose them as their base.

Keep a log. Note the time, the weather, the water level, and the position of your fixed reference. The city does not keep time as humans do, and after a day or two you will lose track of days and hours. A written record is the only reliable clock you have.

Be ready for the psychological effect of a place that has no edge and no interior. The city is always responding to itself, and it can make you feel like a stranger in a process you cannot understand. Stay anchored to your reference, walk often to the edge, and treat the stillness there as a rest rather than a destination.

## Leaving

Plan your return before you start your visit. Your route out should lead to the far edge, where the towers stop, and from there to the anchor you chose before you arrived. Do not wait until you are tired or wet to plan it. Check the water level and the causeway positions before you set out, and allow extra time, because crossings may need detours.

When you reach the edge, the sound will drop to a distant surge, and you will hear the drip of the cracked spouts. Take a moment before you leave. The place does not mind if you stay or go, but you should know what it sounded like when you were still in its noise.

## Summary Kit

- Waterproof, high-ankle boots with aggressive soles and cut-resistant gaiters
- Thick gloves with good grip
- Long test pole
- Synthetic or wool base layers, waterproof outer shell
- Respirator with particulate filters, sealed eyewear, eyewash, and spare lenses
- Head netting and verified insect repellent
- Sealed, dry food rations
- Water purification and sealed backup water
- Earplugs (for sleep and for daytime)
- Sleeping platform or inflatable mat
- Dry bags for all electronics and documents
- Written log and a fixed reference point
- Hood or brimmed cap for flock debris
