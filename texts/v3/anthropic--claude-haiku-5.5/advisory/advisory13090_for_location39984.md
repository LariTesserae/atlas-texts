# Preparing to Visit the Silt Grid

This is a place that is still changing, and most of the preparation comes down to not trusting things that look settled. The grid looks like a city and behaves like a riverbed. The water looks still and is moving. The towers look like buildings and may be deciding what they are. Prepare for a visit that is wet, dim, slow, and quietly unpredictable.

## Before You Go

**Check the water first.** Before you commit to a visit, find out what the region has had recently. Heavy rain means the river is high and undercutting banks, the drums are humming, the towers are extended and active, and the amphibians are loud. A dry spell means lower water, more stable ground, dormant towers, and a quieter, more brittle landscape. Neither is safer in every way. Pick the condition that matches your goals, and don't go in right after a big storm unless you have to, because the river will have rearranged its banks overnight.

**Plan a short visit.** The place is disorienting. Distances are hard to judge in the flat light, sounds arrive from the wrong direction, and the same grid intersections look alike for blocks. Set a turnaround time and a hard exit time, and keep both. Most travelers who get into trouble here stay past their own plans because the place is quiet and not obviously hostile.

**Expect no one.** There is no sign of current habitation, no roads in use, no trade routes, and no rescue. If you are injured or stranded, your own preparation is all you have. Tell someone on your origin side where you are going and when you expect to return.

**Think about what you're visiting.** This is a city in the middle of being unmade, and some of what you'd expect to find, such as intact shopfronts, legible signage, and clear streets, may already be gone or dissolving. Go with curiosity rather than an expectation of ruins you can explore at leisure. Photograph what you see. Take nothing from the cross-sections or the basements.

## What to Bring

**Footwear.** Waterproof boots with deep, aggressive tread are essential. The grid is broken concrete with sharp edges, clay ledges that go slick within minutes of mist, root-buckled pavement, and soft ground in the hollows. Bring a spare pair of socks in a sealed bag, because your feet will be wet for most of the visit. Trekking poles are strongly recommended. Use them to test ground, especially near river cuts and clay ledges.

**Clothing.** Dress in breathable, quick-drying layers. The air is warm and close, so you'll sweat under rain gear. Carry a light waterproof shell with a hood, and a brimmed hat, since the drip from leaves continues for hours after rain stops. Avoid cotton, which holds water and stays cold. Wear long sleeves and trousers, both for the vegetation and because you should avoid skin contact with the silver-green fur.

**Light.** The light is dim and flat, and it never really gets bright. Carry two independent light sources, a headlamp and a handheld, and spare batteries in a sealed bag. Red or amber output is less glaring in the mist and will not wreck your night vision if you're moving between dark tunnels and open ground. At dusk the towers' filaments glow faintly, which can serve as a rough guide, but they are not a reliable lantern and you should not plan to navigate by them.

**Navigation.** Bring a physical map, a compass you've tested on your origin side, and a notebook. Don't rely on electronics, which will likely suffer in the humidity and may behave strangely near the towers. Paper swells and smudges in this air, so keep your map and notes in a rigid, sealed case. Record times, intersections, and landmarks as you go. Use non-metal marking if you need to mark a route: ribbon or biodegradable flagging works, chalk washes out within a day, and metal tags tarnish and tack together.

**Water.** Bring all the drinking water you need from your origin side, sealed. Do not drink from the river, from the puddles in the grid's intersections, from the floating mats, or from the clear troughs at the bases of the towers. The troughs look clean, but they are the towers' output, and what they carry is unknown. Carry a filter and a way to boil water in case of emergency, and treat any water you collect as unsafe until you've confirmed otherwise.

**Tools.** Metal goes faintly tacky in this air, so oil or wax the hinges, zippers, locks, and knife blades before you go. Bring tools made of wood, plastic, or sealed composite where you can. Keep a repair kit for your boots and clothing, a bivouac or emergency blanket, a whistle, and a basic first aid kit with extra blister and wound dressings, since the damp makes minor cuts slow to close.

**Documents and electronics.** Keep anything important in rigid, waterproof cases with desiccant packs. Paper swells, adhesives soften, and seals that were fine on your origin side may fail here. Test everything in the damp before you rely on it.

**Samples and specimens.** Don't collect any. The mineral-crusted trees, the pale mycelium, and the silver fragments in the river are not safe to handle, and taking them may affect the things you're trying to observe.

## The Environment

**Water.** Water here is a pattern rather than a fixed condition. The morning mist will lie in the low streets and burn off by midday, and a settled afternoon drizzle can keep dripping for hours after it stops. The standing water in collapsed plazas is often perfectly smooth and mirrors the sky in a way that looks wrong. Don't step into it or lean over it. You can't judge depth from the surface, and the reflection gives you no information about what's underneath. Stay on firm, raised ground and keep at least a body length from the edge of any hollow.

**The river.** Keep away from the banks. The cuts are clean and vertical, undercut by the current, and can collapse without warning, especially after rain. Many are only weeks old and still saturated. Stay at least two or three meters back from any edge and never stand on a bank that overhangs water. The river is fast in the middle and slack at the edges. Trunks and lattice fragments move with it, and a fall in the current would be very hard to recover from. Don't retrieve objects from the water, including the silver fragments that turn over and sink. You will not be able to reach them safely, and touching them is not advisable.

**Air.** The air carries a sweetish, metallic smell, like old coins in rain. Treat it as a sign of mineral load. If you develop a headache, nausea, a metallic taste, or an unusual pressure behind the eyes, move to open, higher ground and rest before going further. Pay attention to whether these symptoms follow proximity to the towers.

**Ground.** The clay near the towers is stained in rings, and the soil is mineral-rich. Don't dig, kneel, or sit directly on the clay near the towers. Don't pick or eat anything that grows here. The pale, crusted trees near the towers are a warning, not a food source.

## The Towers

The towers are the most important feature of the place and the most important to respect. They are not machines in any sense you'll recognize, and their behavior depends on the weather and on something you can't see.

**Keep your distance.** Stay at least thirty meters from any tower and wider in wet weather, when the filaments extend. The lattice seems to have a boundary, and the vines and roots respect it. You should too. Don't touch the ribs, the filaments, or the silver-green fur, and don't lean on any part of a tower, even briefly.

**Read the state.** A dormant tower has dulled to gray-brown, its channels are dry and cracked, and it gives off a faint warmth, a sense of holding its breath. Dormant towers are less active, but they are not inert. A live tower ripples faintly, its filaments are extended, the troughs at its base are full, and it makes a low, irregular ticking, like a clock with too many hands. Live towers are more active and more likely to respond to you if you come close. Give live towers more room and more time.

**Don't stand in the drainage.** The troughs and the channels that drain them are the towers' way of moving water. Standing in them, blocking them, or placing objects in them may interrupt that process, and you don't know what the towers will do in response. Don't sleep, camp, or set up a base in a tower's drainage zone, even if the ground looks dry.

**Rain and the filaments.** When rain begins, the filaments extend and catch the mist. They run droplets down the ribs into the troughs, and the water drains into the ground. If you are under or near a tower when rain starts, move away from its base promptly and on foot. Don't wait to see what happens.

**Don't assume they are fixed.** The towers rearrange themselves. Ribs thicken, close over breaks, and in at least one case have grown new filaments and reoriented. A tower that was pointing one way when you arrived may be pointing another when you leave. Don't rely on tower positions to navigate over a long period, and don't assume a tower's orientation means anything about the grid below it.

## The Drums and Devices

The low drums half-buried in the pavement hum when rain begins and go silent when it stops. Don't open them, dig them out, or cut into their surfaces. Some have split open to reveal pale, threaded mycelium grown into old copper wire, and those are the most fragile things you'll see here. Don't disturb them, and don't stand on or lean against them while they are humming.

The line between built and grown is thin. Don't assume that anything you find is a tool or a structure. Assume it may be alive, and treat it accordingly.

## Fauna

Animals are scarce and mostly hard to see. This can work in your favor.

**Amphibians.** Their high, brief calls are your best early warning. They stop abruptly when anything large comes near. If you hear the chorus fall silent around you, that means something is approaching, and you should check your surroundings. Don't pursue the frogs or disturb the floating mats where they hunt.

**Birds.** The small long-beaked birds feed low and fast in the fern beds. Let them be.

**Larger animals.** The deer-like or dog-like animals on distant ridges will not come closer and do not look back. Don't approach them. Don't follow them. Don't feed anything, and don't leave food out.

## Sound and Communication

Sound here is strange. A dropped stone can be heard clearly from across a block, while a voice a few meters away can disappear into the damp. Plan around this.

Use hand signals for anything important, and agree on them before you enter the grid. Keep a whistle on a cord, and use it for emergencies only. Tapping, such as striking a stone against a pipe or a metal rail, may carry better than your voice, though you should avoid making noise near the towers unless you have to.

The long, rising and falling tone that sometimes comes from deep in the grid may be the towers, or the wind through a pipe underground, or something else. You don't need to identify it. Note the time and direction when you hear it, but don't go looking for the source.

## Light and Timing

Shadows are soft, distances are hard to read, and the grid's straight lines are misleading. Things that look close are far away, and vice versa. Plan your route with extra margin for the time it takes to cover ground, and assume it takes longer than it looks.

Plan to be back on firm ground before dusk. The towers' glow at dusk is beautiful and may draw you deeper into the grid. Resist. Night in the grid, with its mist, drip, and uneven ground, is much harder than day.

## Leaving

Choose your exit point before you enter. Make sure it is not in a tower's drainage zone and not on a river bank. Aim for ground that is high, dry, and away from the water's edge.

Keep your return route marked in your notebook. Before you head out, check your water, light, and time. If you are running late, leave the grid by the route you know, even if it is longer.

If you feel disoriented, stop, sit on high ground, and write down what you can see: the nearest tower's condition, the direction of the river, the time. Do not keep walking in circles hoping the grid will resolve itself. It won't.

## What to Carry With You Afterward

Visiting this place is likely to leave you with a sense of how quickly a city can be undone and how little of it survives in the form you expected. Give yourself time to process it. The grid is still being remade, the river is still digging, and the towers are still deciding what they are for. You are visiting a place that has only just started becoming something new, and it is not clear what will still be recognizable when it settles. Go in with humility about that, and come out with your notes, your water, and your sense of time intact.
