# Preparing to Visit the Sallow Reach

## What kind of place this is

The Sallow Reach is young, wet, loud, and always moving. Nothing here is fixed for long. The silt shifts, the channels cut new paths with every rain, and the reed-groves migrate. Plan for a landscape that will not match the map you studied, and expect to improvise. The basin is also not hostile in the usual sense. Its dangers are the ordinary dangers of soft ground, standing water, heat, and weather, all amplified by how fast everything changes.

## Before you go

**Check your anchor and your route against recent conditions.** Return points are tied to features on the ground, and erosion can move or bury them. If your anchor is a riverbank or a grove, assume it may have shifted by the time you arrive. Ask anyone who has come back recently what changed. Pick a return point on a Weir, since the Weirs are the most stable things here, and confirm it is still accessible before you commit.

**Know that the Weirs don't all look different.** They are tall, pale, and tiered, and from a distance they blur together. Mark which Weir is yours with a sign you can see from its base, or note the orientation of its lowest tier and where its filament-fields point. Two Weirs a few miles apart can look almost identical in the haze.

**Expect the haze to hide the sun and the horizon.** The sun is a brass disc you can look at without much discomfort, and the light is gold, flat, and misleading about distance. Tinted lenses help with glare and with depth perception in the dim light. Bring a compass you trust and a watch, because the sun will not reliably tell you the time.

**Prepare for the sound.** The insect and bird chorus is a constant wall and can be painful for the first hour. Bring earplugs you can remove quickly. A few people leave one ear open on purpose, because the Weirs' tuning event silences everything, and the silence is your only warning. If you hear the chorus stop, pay attention.

## Clothing and gear

**Footwear is the most important choice.** The pale silt looks solid and often is not. Cracked silt can break into stepped plates over soft mud, and gullies have sharp lips that cut through thin soles and ankles. Wear high boots with a stiff sole and gaiters to keep silt out. Bring a spare pair of socks, because your feet will be wet for most of the day.

**Expect to be wet.** Rain arrives suddenly and often, the reed leaves channel water onto you even between storms, and the shallow skin of water covers most of the ground. Use a hooded waterproof layer that breathes, since the air is warm and humid and you will sweat under it. Quick-drying trousers that don't absorb silt are better than cotton. Keep electronics in sealed cases with desiccant.

**Protect your skin from the fern and the water.** The rust-colored fern mats stain skin and clothing when they are disturbed, and the stain is slow to wash out. Some people react badly to the crushed stems. Long sleeves and gloves are sensible. Don't put your hands in standing water without checking it first.

**Carry a tool for cutting vines.** Vines the width of a wrist climb everything, including the structures. You may need to clear a path or free a boot.

## Water, food, and health

**Drink only filtered or boiled water.** Water is everywhere here, but the basins fed by the Weirs carry condensation that has run over the lattice and through the silt. Use a good filter and carry a reserve of sealed water for the first day.

**Watch for heat exhaustion.** The air is warm and wet and the haze holds the heat in. You will sweat constantly and may not feel how much fluid you are losing. Drink on a schedule rather than when you feel thirsty, and take breaks in the shade of the reed-trees.

**Be careful with food.** The smell of the basin, iron, cut green stems, and sweet rot, comes from the vegetation and the standing water. Don't eat anything you have not identified as safe, and don't assume a fruit-like thing is edible because it smells like fruit. Bring your own food.

**Keep cuts clean.** Silt, rust-fern sap, and standing water all carry bacteria. Clean any break in the skin as soon as you can, and treat it before it has time to swell. Carry antiseptic and a sealed dressing kit.

**Insects and small animals.** The beetles and grazing creatures are mostly harmless to people, but the cicada-analogues and the clicking fern-dwellers are numerous enough that you will get bitten or stung at some point. Use repellent that works on the local species, which you can get from travelers who have been here longer.

## Moving through the basin

**Stay on firm ground when you can find it.** Look for the dark basalt ribs, which are solid, and for the reed-tree clumps, which often stand on higher silt. Avoid flat, pale, cracked surfaces that show standing water at the edges. If the silt gives under your weight, stop and go back the way you came.

**Don't cross channels in flood.** Water runs through the silt with a sound like pouring sand, and the current picks up fast after rain. Banks collapse into the flow with little warning. Cross only where the channel is shallow and slow, and never immediately after a storm. Assume that any bank you are standing on can slide.

**Keep away from the lattice-foot unless you have a reason to be there.** The water near the Weirs does not always flow the way you expect. In places along the strongest lines of the lattice, it runs uphill for a few meters. Predators also hunt along the foot of the structures, and the prey run up the metal to escape them. If you go near a Weir, watch the ground and the lower tiers, and move slowly.

**Be cautious with the Weirs themselves.** The surfaces are clean and faintly warm, and the joints shift slowly through the day, opening and closing. Don't climb them, don't lean on them for long periods, and don't put your hand into the joints. The filaments in the upper tiers are fine and will snag clothing and skin.

**Watch for the tuning event.** Sometimes a Weir's lattice tunes itself and rings at a single pitch for hours. You will feel it in your teeth before you hear it clearly. When it happens, every bird and insect falls silent, and then they return all at once. Use that silence to get your bearings and to note where you are. Don't approach a ringing Weir. Many travelers report that the pitch makes it hard to think and to keep your footing, so if you are near one, move away from it until it stops.

**Electronics may behave strangely near the tuning.** Devices have been reported to glitch or lose their location fix during a ring. Don't rely on them alone for navigation.

## Sleeping and shelter

**Don't camp under the Weirs.** Condensation drips from the upper tiers all night, and the roosting flyers cluster on the lower tiers and move in their sleep. The noise and the dripping make rest difficult, and the ground at the base is often soft.

**Choose high, dry ground away from the channels.** The basalt ribs are a good choice if you can find a flat face. Reed-tree clumps are also good, but check the trunks for hollow segments that might fill with water. Avoid low ground near the old tide line, which is only a few meters above the basin floor.

**Expect rain at night.** Set up a shelter with a steep roof and a drainage plan. Dig nothing into the silt, because it will fill.

## Timing your visit

Travelers agree that the first hour after dawn is the best time to be here. The haze thins, the low light catches the Weirs' tiers, and for a few minutes each lattice glows amber from top to bottom. The droplets on the filaments light up like beads of fire, and the flyers lift off the metal in one long wave. Plan to be at a Weir's base before sunrise, and stay still and quiet while the light comes up.

After that, the sun clears the haze, the light goes flat and gold, rain often begins somewhere in the reeds, and the chorus rises to fill the basin. Plan your movement for the middle of the day around this, and head back toward your return point in the late afternoon, before the rain and the failing light make the silt harder to read.

## Final notes

No one knows who built the Weirs, and the local explanations contradict one another. Some people come here to study the structures, some to watch the flyers, some simply to stand in the warm wet air and listen. Whatever brings you, treat the Reach as a place that is still forming, and let the Weirs keep doing what they do: pulling water out of dry air and sending it down into the silt while the basin grows around it.
