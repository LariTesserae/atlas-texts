# Preparing for the Weeping Terraces

## Arrival and Orientation

Expect to arrive on the rim, not in the basin. The wall is dark poured stone and the open side drops into cloud, so you will almost certainly come in from the rim path or from the cloud side. Before you step off anything, take a full minute to look. The terraces are a map of the basin's current state, and you will want to remember where the open channels run, because they will not be the same tomorrow.

Confirm your exit point before going down. The rim is your only reliable landmark, and in the cloud the basin's concentric rings look alike. If you cannot see the wall, you cannot tell which way is up. Set a return bearing and keep it.

## Clothing and Physical Load

The basin is warm, at about 2,500 meters, but the air is saturated and the cloud is cold on the skin when it condenses on you. Dress in layers that dry fast:

- **Base layer:** synthetic or merino. Cotton holds water and will chill you when the cloud closes.
- **Mid layer:** a light fleece or similar, removable quickly when the afternoon sun breaks through and the heat builds.
- **Outer shell:** a hooded, breathable rain jacket. The drip from the lattice is steady and will soak you slowly even when it is not raining.

Bring a spare dry layer sealed in a bag. You will want it at the end of the day, after you have been in the cloud for hours.

Altitude is modest, but if you come up from low elevations, give yourself a day to acclimatize before attempting the descent into the terraces. Fatigue here comes from the damp and the footing, not just the air, so pace yourself.

## Footwear

This is the most important decision you will make. The ground takes boots in two ways. In some stretches it is glassy and hard, and in others it dissolves into a charcoal silt that clings to everything and turns to paste when wet. Your boots should be:

- **Waterproof** at the ankle, with a sealed seam.
- **Deeply lugged** for grip on the glassy sections, which are slick when wet.
- **Light enough** that you can lift your feet cleanly out of silt without tiring.

Carry gaiters if you have them. The silt gets into everything, and it stains.

Walking poles help a great deal on the ridges, which are slumping, and in the troughs, where the fill gives under weight. Use them to test the surface before you commit.

## The Ground: How to Walk It

Do not treat the terraces as paths. The ridges are newly cut and rounding toward the troughs, so their edges may collapse under you. The troughs are gullied and uneven, and the fill shifts overnight.

Stay on the broad, set sections where the surface is hard. Test each step. Where the fill is loose, move in a straight line rather than picking your way across it, so you do not churn the surface into a pit. When you must cross silt, cross it fast and lightly.

Avoid the channels. The water runs through them, the walls are undercut, and they can give way without warning. If you must cross a channel, cross at a point where the banks are firm and the water is shallow, and check the far bank before you step.

Be aware that the terraces change. A route you took in the morning may be blocked by afternoon. Keep your bearings to the rim, not to features in the terraces.

## Water and Food

Do not drink from the cisterns or the channels without treatment. The water tastes faintly of iron and something sweeter underneath, which is pleasant and misleading. It is warm from sitting in the sun and full of organic growth. Bring your own water, and plan for more than you think you need, since humidity is high and you will sweat more than the air suggests.

If you carry a filter or purification tablets, use them. Boiling is better if you have fuel and can light a stove in the damp, which is not easy. Plan meals that do not need cooking, since open flame in the basin is a poor idea anyway. The lattice and moss are dense enough to catch fire in dry spells.

Pack out everything. The basin feels like it is absorbing what it finds, and waste that is left behind may end up in the fill.

## The Pump House and Machinery

The pump house is the only machinery here, and it is the most dangerous thing in the basin. The flywheel turns once every four or five minutes, and the pistons and brass pipes are under pressure. Surges ring through the pipes, and a burst line can spray water hard enough to injure.

Do not enter the pump house without a clear reason, and if you do, go slowly and do not touch the flywheel, the valves, or the gauges. Several valves are fused shut with mineral deposit. Forcing them can rupture the line.

The leaking pipes have made wet gardens around the building. These are slippery and look soft, but they hide sharp fittings and corroded metal. Do not step on them.

The vines around the pump house are thick and strong. Do not pull on them. They are part of the structure, and disturbing them may bring down a wall or open a leak.

## Biology and Health

The basin is alive in a dense, layered way, and you should expect a range of organisms.

- **Mud and banks:** the cocoa-colored mud has tracks of small creatures. Check your legs and boots for leeches or similar parasites after crossing the wet sections. Tuck trousers into socks.
- **Lattice and moss:** the moss is luminous and pleasant to look at, but do not press your hands into it. Some of the epiphytes have pale, fleshy leaves that drip, and the drip may irritate skin. Wash off any contact.
- **Spores and damp air:** the humidity favors fungal growth. If you have respiratory problems, bring a mask for the terraces and watch for coughing.
- **Insects and moths:** insects are rare, but moths drift through the afternoon light. Most are harmless, but a few may carry scales that irritate eyes and skin.
- **Larger movement:** something moves in the vines and goes silent. Do not approach. Give the vines a wide berth and do not poke into them.

Carry a basic first-aid kit with antiseptic, wound dressings, antihistamines, and any personal medications. Medical help is far from the rim.

## Light and Timing

The light is gray-white most of the day. The cloud sits in the basin and makes it hard to judge distance or depth. Use a headlamp for any movement before midday and during the hours when the cloud is thick.

The afternoon sun is the exception. For about an hour, the cloud thins and long slanted beams light the lattice, and every drip becomes a point of fire. This is the best time to photograph and observe the basin, and the most dangerous time to be distracted. Plan your footing before the light arrives, because the glare off wet surfaces can hide slick patches and the warmth can speed up the silt.

Do not plan to stay overnight. The fill shifts in the dark, channels cut across terraces, and the cloud makes return unreliable. Set a turnaround time and keep to it. Return to the rim before the afternoon cloud closes again.

## Navigation and Memory

Your maps will be out of date by the time you arrive, and the terraces may be different from any description, including this one. Keep a written log of your route, with bearings and landmarks you can trust, mainly the rim, the pump house, and the cistern edges. The pump house is a fixed point because it is the only machinery, and it does not move, though its surroundings change.

Mark your route with something that will not wash away or be absorbed. Flagging tape on the rim works. Cairns on the terraces do not, since the fill sets around them and they sink.

Be cautious about footprints. The fill takes impressions and sets them into new shapes. A trail you leave may become a permanent channel or a ridge, and you may not be able to retrace it.

## Behavior

The basin is making itself, and it may respond to the people who move through it. Some of the moss follows the grid of the ribs so exactly that it looks drawn, and the lattice grows new ribs at its edges. Do not try to influence this. Do not cut moss, pull ribs, or rearrange the fill to make a path. If you want to leave a mark, you will leave it in the fill, and the fill will decide what it becomes.

Be quiet. The place is quiet, and noise carries across the cistern pools and up the walls. The tree frog's two-note cry is a useful signal of how still the air is, and loud voices or equipment will disturb the rhythm of the place.

Do not name features or claim anything. The terraces are changing faster than names can keep up, and naming may fix a shape that is still in motion.

## What to Bring

- Waterproof boots with deep lugs and sealed seams
- Gaiters
- Walking poles
- Layered synthetic or merino clothing, plus a dry spare set in a sealed bag
- Hooded rain shell
- Headlamp with spare batteries
- At least four liters of water per person, plus treatment method
- Non-cooking food
- First-aid kit with antiseptic, dressings, antihistamines, and personal medications
- Flagging tape for marking the rim route
- Notebook for bearings and landmarks
- Camera, if you want one, with a lens cloth for the drip
- A sealed bag for pack-out waste

## Leaving

Give yourself time to climb out before the cloud closes. The wall and rim path are the safest route. Do not hurry on the ridges, which slump, or in the silt, which pulls.

When you reach the rim, look back once. The basin will look the same as it did when you arrived, and it will not be the same. The lattice has grown, the channels have shifted, and the fill has set in new shapes behind you. The place is still making itself, and you have been a small part of that while you were there.
