# Preparing to Visit the Basin of Hollow Stone

Most travelers who go to the Basin of Hollow Stone are surprised by how much of the trip is about managing your body and senses rather than navigating a strange culture. The basin is loud, dry, oily, bright, and enclosed, and it wears you down slowly. Prepare for the environment first and the people second. The people will be easier to deal with once you can think clearly.

## Before You Go

**Know your entry and exit.** The basin is a sealed pocket world, and the fissure in the rim is the only opening you can see. Find out how you will arrive and how you will leave before you step through. Whatever method you use, make sure you know where you will physically appear on the floor. If you arrive in the open, you will be in the glare of the shaft within minutes, so have your eye protection ready before you cross.

**Plan around the forty-day cycle.** The vents swell and fade over roughly forty days, and the plants respond. Leaves open or close, the fog thickens or thins, the city's sound shifts. Ask a local or a previous traveler what phase the basin is in when you arrive, and decide how long you will stay. Many visitors find one full cycle is enough to understand the place without losing their footing in it.

**Set a return date and keep it.** This matters more here than in most worlds. Staying longer is easy, and the basin makes leaving harder over time, as explained below. Choose a date, write it down, and tell someone on your side who will act if you do not come back.

## Clothing and Gear

**Layer long, and expect to ruin some of it.** The grey silt clings to everything, and the resin-oil from the plants soaks into cloth and does not wash out easily. Wear long sleeves, long trousers, and a wrap or scarf that can cover your nose and mouth against the silt. Bring one set of clothes you are willing to throw away.

**Boots with ankle support.** The dry mineral plates have curled edges, and the roads are pale and smooth but lie beside cracked, uneven ground. Ankle injuries are the most common minor injury among visitors.

**Gloves, thick ones.** The understory plants clench and open when touched, and their surfaces are slick with oil. Bare hands pick up residue that is difficult to clean and makes everything you hold slippery. Heavy leather or cloth gloves are best, and remove them carefully before you eat.

**Eye protection.** The shaft of light is intense around midday, and the reflected glow off the silt and leaves is worse. Bring tinted glasses or goggles, and a wide-brimmed hat. The shade under the trees is soft and pleasant, and you will spend most of your daylight time there. Locals move through the shade at noon for good reason.

**Earplugs, with caveats.** Wax or foam plugs help with sleep and with the constant drone when the wind comes down the fissure. But the pod cracks are sudden and loud, like gunshots, and the startle reflex is strong. Plugs can keep you from hearing a warning shout or a cart coming at you on a narrow road. Many visitors wear one plug during the day and both at night. Test this before you rely on it.

**Light for evenings.** The basin dims to amber, then relies on the vent-chimneys for light. Bring a headlamp and spare batteries. The chimneys are useful landmarks, but the lanes between them are dark and steep.

## Health and Supplies

**Water is the first problem.** There is no surface water in the basin, and the plants drink oil, not water. Assume that the local water supply is scarce and that you will need to bring your own. Carry sealed water, enough for several days, and ration it. Do not drink the resin-sap or the oily runoff from the leaves, even if it looks clear. Ask locals where their drinking water comes from before you accept anything.

**Food.** Much of the local diet is plant matter processed in ways that are not always obvious. Bring your own staple rations, especially if you have dietary restrictions, and ask how any local dish is prepared before you eat it. Salt and dry foods are useful because humidity is low and the silt gets into everything.

**Breathing.** The vents hiss continuously and release hydrocarbon-rich vapor. The smell is faint and metallic most of the time, but the low hollows, where the fog pools, can be much stronger. Do not sleep in those hollows or linger there at dusk. Carry a cloth you can pull over your face if the air thickens. If you develop headaches or dizziness near the vents, move uphill into the open air and stay there until it passes.

**Heat and cold.** The vents add warmth, and the chimneys glow, so the basin floor can feel warm even after sunset. Nights may be cooler away from the vents. Dress in layers you can take off and put back on.

**Medical kit.** Include a basic kit with bandages, antiseptic (ideally alcohol-based, since oil is hard to wash off), eye drops for silt irritation, anti-nausea medication, and something for headaches. Oil-based cleansers work better than water on skin and gear.

## The Living Landscape

**Do not cut the plants without permission.** The city has been carved out of the growth, but the plants are the basin's economy, its water, and its breath. Cutting them, even a small branch, will draw attention and possibly anger. Ask before you take anything.

**Leave the fog alone.** The resin-mist sometimes forms slow shapes in the low hollows that look like animals. They dissolve when disturbed, and they are not something to chase or photograph too closely. Do not step into them.

**Be careful with the hum stones.** Some stones in the walls hum when touched, and the pitch changes with temperature. They are interesting, and some residents use them as informal thermometers. Do not press your ear or your whole body against them for long. Prolonged contact seems to produce a ringing that lingers for hours.

**Watch for the burrowers.** The grey burrowing creatures come up at dusk. They are small and slow and mostly harmless, but they will bolt into holes at the slightest movement, and people step on the holes and turn ankles.

## Living Among the People

**The noise will be normal to them and exhausting to you.** Doors bang, wheels grind on stone, bells ring at odd hours, and pods crack overhead all day. Residents live inside this sound without noticing. You will flinch and then feel foolish. Expect it, and don't let it make you snap at people.

**Crowds and carts.** The streets are narrow, and the silt-roads are shared by people on foot and carts pulled by long-legged beasts. Step aside for carts. Stay off the rope bridges unless you have been shown how to cross them. Watch the ladders and ramps; they are steep and often slick with oil.

**Trade and money.** Markets spill into courtyards, and goods are traded in local tools and materials: chipped stone blades, bronze-colored fittings, pottery, woven cloth. Find out what the local exchange is before you buy. Bring small items you can trade if you need to: salt, metal, tools, thread, or medicine. Bargain with patience. Haggling is part of the social rhythm, and rushing it will mark you as careless.

**The bells.** The city's bells ring irregularly from the terraces. They may mark something, but locals do not always announce what. Ask someone to explain the system early in your visit so you are not caught off guard during a gathering or a closing.

**Respect the cycle.** Locals mark the forty-day phases in their festivals, work, and conversation. If you arrive mid-cycle, you may find people preparing for a change, or recovering from one. Ask questions, and listen more than you speak.

## Navigating the Light

**Midday.** Stay in shade, wear your eye protection, and drink water. Most locals rest or work under the trees during the hottest, brightest hours. Move with them.

**Afternoon.** The shaft of light crawls across the floor. Plan your walks around where the light falls, because the reflected glow from the silt and the oily leaves can disorient you.

**Evening.** The walls turn the color of dried blood, and the city glows from within. This is the most beautiful time to be in the basin. It is also when the burrowers come out, the fog begins to gather in the hollows, and the chimneys become your main guides. Keep your headlamp handy and stay on the lit paths.

## The Long Stay

**The silence problem.** The basin's noise seems to come from just behind the ears, and visitors who stay several weeks report that silence begins to feel wrong. When you leave, the quiet may be disorienting. Some people feel phantom sound. Others find it hard to sleep without the drone or the cracks of the pods. This usually fades over a few days, but it can be unsettling, so expect it and plan a quiet landing on the other side.

**Keep your own rhythm.** The basin runs on constant, churning motion, and it is easy to lose track of time. Keep a journal with dates and cycle phases. Eat and sleep at regular hours even when the bells ring at odd times. Take a day away from the crowds now and then, in the shade or on the rim path if you can reach it.

**Stay grounded in your own body.** Watch for headaches, ringing in the ears, skin irritation from oil, and shortness of breath near the vents. Small problems in a sealed place can get worse quickly, and the basin is not a good place to tough it out.

## Checklist

- Entry and exit method confirmed, return date set and shared
- Cycle phase researched; stay length planned
- Long, layered clothing and a spare set
- Gloves, eye protection, wide hat
- Sealed water for several days, plus food rations and salt
- Earplugs (test before you rely on them)
- Headlamp and spare batteries
- Basic medical kit, including oil-cleansing wipes and eye drops
- Barter goods for trade
- Journal and a way to track time

The Basin of Hollow Stone rewards travelers who arrive prepared and slow. Give your body time to adjust to the dust, the oil, the light, and the noise. The city is generous with people who listen, and the basin, for all its strangeness, is a place of constant, careful living. Go with respect, and you will leave with a clear memory of one of the most unusual rooms you will ever stand in.
