# Preparing to Visit the Condensary at Hesk Ardal

## Arriving

Arrive on the rim, not the floor. The rim is the only place where you can see the shape of the cirque and orient yourself before the cloud closes over. From the lowest terrace the walls rise on every side and the cloud lid cuts off the sky, so you lose direction quickly. Mark your arrival point with something that will still be there later: a cairn, a strip of bright cloth tied to a railing, a scratch on a bolt plate. The works has no landmarks you can rely on, and the terraces look alike after the first hour.

Before you go down, confirm your return. The rim stair is the only route out that a person can climb without equipment. The old service track on the north side is listed on the maps but has been slumped and overgrown for a long time, and I would not trust it. If your means of leaving the world depends on a fixed point, make sure that point is somewhere the cloud does not reach, because the mist seems to dull signals and tethers, and some travelers have reported their anchors feeling faint and distant once they are under the lid.

## Climate and Clothing

The cirque sits at about four thousand metres, but inside the bowl it is warm and humid, closer to a lowland rainforest. You will arrive in cold mountain air and step into a sauna within a few hundred metres of descent. Dress in layers you can remove quickly. The sensible base is a light breathable shirt and trousers that dry fast, not cotton. Wear something that covers your arms and legs anyway, because the broad-leaved ferns are covered in fine hairs that itch, and the rusted edges of the frames are sharp.

Footwear matters more than anything else. The terraces are paved in grey stone that the moss has made slick, and the drains and condensate channels run with water at all times. Use boots with deep lugs and high ankles. Carry a second pair of socks in a sealed bag. Your feet will be wet within the first hour no matter what you wear.

Gloves are essential for handling rails and gantries. The metal is warm and greasy with condensate, and some of the stacks are hot enough to blister bare skin. Do not lean on anything you have not tested with the back of your hand first.

## Hearing and the Pistons

This is the single most important part of your preparation. The pistons are very loud, and the sound is not only a noise but a physical blow. It comes through the chest and the jaw, and it can leave you dizzy or briefly deafened if you are standing close to a cylinder when it drops. Bring real hearing protection, ideally the kind that cuts sharp peaks without muting everything, so you can still hear the valves and the falling water, which will warn you of a cycle change.

Learn the rhythm before you try to cross open ground. Each piston drop is followed by the echo from the far wall about half a second later, then a few seconds of valve shriek and steam, then a silence that can last several minutes. Use that silence to move. The birds rise after each blow and settle in the quiet, so you can watch them to gauge the cycle. Do not cross under a gantry during the first ten seconds after a drop, and never stand beside a cylinder during the drop itself. Most injuries here come from people who stopped to look.

## Navigation

The terraces are nine steps descending toward the lowest floor, and each one is a long strip of machinery separated by wide gulfs of fern and mist. You can walk a kilometre without seeing another structure. The most reliable guide is the water. The condensate chutes and sluices all run downhill toward the cascade at the outer lip, and if you follow the flow you will always reach the descending channel that leads out over the rim. Do not follow water uphill. The intake batteries on the top terrace are dangerous and not for casual visits.

Expect to lose time. The loop of nine cycles means that the wandering grazer and the bird colonies repeat their routes on a schedule you can learn, but the pistons do not give you a clock. Carry a watch that you trust, and count the drops if you want to track elapsed time. Many visitors report that the hours pass strangely in the bowl. It is easy to spend an afternoon at one drain.

## The Fauna

Most of the wildlife is harmless and will leave you alone. The lizards bask on the warm pipes between cycles and will bolt if you approach. The slate-coloured beetles cluster around the drains and are not venomous, though they will swarm if disturbed, so do not kneel by a drain with bare hands. The swifts wheel in the open air between gantries and will not come close to you, though they will dive past if you stand in their flight paths during the quiet.

The grazer is the animal to respect. It moves through the lower terraces in the fern-shade and browses the broad leaves. It is large, and it follows a fixed route that loops every nine cycles. Learn its route and keep out of it. It does not seem aggressive, but it will not yield a path, and it has been seen trampling people who stood in the wrong place. If you meet it, stand still, make yourself visible, and back away along the terrace edge rather than turning your back on it.

Do not eat the fruit or the fern shoots without testing. Some of the plants have a sap that causes skin reactions, and the moss on the pipes may conceal growths you should not disturb.

## Water and Food

The water from the condensate drains is cold, pure, and tastes faintly of iron. It is safe to drink after boiling, and most travelers prefer to boil it anyway because the drains carry grit from the old pipes. The channel water is the same supply that feeds the lowland, so do not contaminate it. Use sealed bottles, never tip used water back into a drain, and never use the condensate for washing near the sluices.

Bring your own food. The warm air spoils perishables quickly, so pack dried or sealed rations and eat in the cold stove rooms, not in the open. The cots in those rooms are old and may not bear weight, so test them before lying down, and keep your boots on.

## Shelter and Sleep

The people who kept the works left a few rooms with cots and stoves on the upper terraces, and these are the only shelter you will find. They are damp, with mould on the walls and moss in the corners. Air them before you sleep, check the stove for gas, and close the door against the mist. The sound of the pistons is loud enough that many visitors cannot sleep in the first night. Plan for that. Some find that after a few cycles the rhythm becomes a comfort, and that the long silences between drops are the easiest sleep they have had.

## What to Leave Alone

Do not touch the valves, the gauges, or the piston housings. The works is maintaining itself in a slow balance. The seized parts are being eaten by rust, and the rust is being flushed by the condensate surges. If you free a valve, you may change the timing of the cycle, and the works has run this loop for longer than anyone has counted. No one knows what a change would do, and the people who knew are gone.

Do not dig into the terraces or drill the walls. The retaining plates are holding the cirque together, and the cloud is pressed against them to condense and return the water. Damage to the walls could alter the weather of the whole bowl.

Do not take the pale reed or its seed from the lowland back into the bowl, and do not carry bowl moss or fern cuttings out. Several travelers have brought back samples that did not survive the transition, and the ones that did behaved strangely, growing toward the sound of the pistons even in other places.

## The Question

Expect to be asked, by yourself and by others, what the works is for. Nobody knows. It may have been built to make rain for the lowland, or the lowland may have been designed to be fed by the cloud, or it may simply be a machine that needed to keep running and the rain is only what it does. Visitors who come with a theory often leave without one, and visitors who come without one sometimes find themselves building one by the third day. Whatever you conclude, keep it loosely. The works does not answer the question, and it has been running without an answer for a very long time.

## Leaving

Leave on the same day you enter if you can. Staying longer means the warm air and the rhythm will get into you, and the tiredness that comes from days of fine mist and echoing blows is real. Walk back up along the water, follow your marks, climb the rim stair before the next cycle, and do not look back into the bowl while the cloud is thick. When you reach the top, the sky will be open and cold, and the sound will drop out of your chest all at once. Many visitors say that the first breath of thin air on the rim is the moment they realise how much they had been listening.
