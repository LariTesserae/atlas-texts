# Preparing to Visit the Drowned Hollow

This is not a place you can prepare for in the usual way. Nothing about it is hostile in a hurry, so your preparation should be less about surviving an attack and more about keeping your own rhythm when everything around you is slower than you are, and about not being carried off into the room's slow argument.

## Before You Go: The Body

**Learn to hold your breath, then learn to hold it longer, calmly.** The swell reaches the ceiling on a cycle you cannot time from inside, and when it touches the membrane the water seems to hold there for a stretch longer than is comfortable. You should be able to stay still and relaxed for at least two minutes without any sense of panic. Practice in cold water if you can. Cold shortens your tolerance, and this room is cool enough that it will pull at you. Train until a slow exhale feels like a choice rather than a reflex.

**Get used to cold without gasping.** The water is cool, almost cold, and it does not warm with time. Sudden cold makes people inhale at the wrong moment. Acclimatize in stages: cool shower, cold bath, then open water if you can manage it.

**Accept dark as a condition, not an emergency.** You will rely on touch and sound more than sight. Spend time with your eyes closed, navigating a familiar room by feel, until you can do it without tensing. The bioluminescence will help a little, but it pulses and fades, and your eyes will adjust and then refuse to adjust further. Train yourself to stop asking the dark for more than it gives.

**Know your own pace.** Things here move at the speed of water and stone. You will want to hurry because you cannot see what is coming. Practice moving deliberately: one hand forward, a slow sweep, a pause, then the next. Quick motion stirs the filaments, and the stems close behind you, so haste makes you slower in the end.

## What to Bring

**A line.** A cord of at least twenty meters, tied to something secure at the surface, is the most important item you will carry. Tie it to yourself at the waist and keep a loose coil in hand. The room is a closed cavity, and the entrance, a fissure in the ceiling, may narrow or seal over time. The line lets you find your way back without having to read a room that will not show you the same walls twice.

**Markers that do not drift.** Bring something you can attach to the line at intervals: small weighted tags, knotted loops of different colors, or a few metal rings that you can feel with your fingers. Use them to mark your path and the places you have already been. The membrane will carry loose things upward, so anchor everything.

**A slate and a grease pencil, or a sealed notebook.** Write down the angle of the carved face in relation to a fixed point, such as the edge of the step or the joint between two blocks, before you look away from it. Write down the time, as best you can estimate it, when you enter and when you leave. Your sense of duration will not be reliable. Your notes will be.

**A knife with a blunt-tipped blade, kept sheathed.** You may want to cut through the stems to clear a path. Do not. The plants do not respond to force, and cutting one is a kind of argument you will not win quickly. Carry the knife for emergencies only, such as a stalk wrapped around your line or a limb caught in a mat.

**A light you can switch off.** Carry a small lamp, but use it sparingly. Light in this room changes what the room does. Some of the reflections become sharper when the lamp is on, and the carvings seem to hold their angle longer when watched. Keep the lamp for checking your markers and for reading your notes, and then turn it off so the room can return to its own rhythm.

**Warm dry clothing for after.** You will come out cold, shaking, and disoriented, with your sense of time wrecked. Have a towel, dry layers, and something warm to drink ready at the surface.

## What Not to Bring

Leave anything that rattles, rings, or sinks quickly. Drops ring through the stone for a long time, and loud objects will echo for minutes. Do not bring anything you cannot bear to lose, because the membrane takes what it touches and sometimes returns it changed. Do not bring anything made of a material the water will corrode in an afternoon.

Leave any instrument that measures time by sound, such as a bell or a ticking watch. The room's sound is mostly the absence of sound, and any added noise will be heard far away, in a way you cannot predict.

## On the Things You Will Meet

**The water.** It rises a finger's width over hours, then settles. Track this by marking your line at the surface when you enter and again when you leave. A rising level is not a threat in itself, but it does mean the entrance is changing relative to you. Plan your exit with that in mind.

**The plants.** They grow toward places where water has already stood, so the spaces you have just cleared will fill again quickly. Do not expect a path to remain open behind you. Move through them as you would through tall grass in a dark field: with your forearms, not your fists, and with your face turned slightly away from the stalks. The pale filaments drifting in the water are the most sensitive part of the growth. Do not brush them with your face.

**The membrane.** When the swell touches it and holds, the membrane seems to sink into the water instead of the water rising into it. Do not push upward through the membrane to reach the air. Feel for its edge with a flat hand, slide along it, and find the place where it meets the stone. You may find a pocket of air there, small and still. If you are running low on breath during a hold, that is the place to go, but go slowly, and expect the membrane to press gently against you as you rise.

**The fauna.** The pale creatures without eyes respond to touch and to warmth. Hold your hand still and open, palm down, near the filaments of a creature you see. Do not reach for it. Its fringe will flare and then settle if you do not move. If something moves in the membrane above, do not look at it directly. Look at the membrane generally, and let your attention rest on the whole rather than the part. Things you can't name will become leaves when you focus on them, and you will not be able to tell whether they were ever more than that.

**The carved face.** It may be a face or a root. Its angle changes when no one is watching. Before you look away, note its alignment against a fixed reference, then look away and return your gaze to the water. When you return, check the angle against your note. You will likely find it has moved. Do not try to correct it. Record it and let it be.

**The doorframe.** It stands in the middle of the room, with no wall and no door, pointing through nothing. Do not step through it, and do not reach through it. Its shadow falls in no direction, but the light in the room is nearly nothing, so this proves little. Tie a marker to a line and set it in the doorframe's opening. Leave it there. Its position will tell you whether the room is changing around the frame or the frame is changing around the room.

**The step and the blocks.** The stone step on the east wall is worn into a shallow dish, which may be a seat or a basin. You can rest there if you need to, but do not stay long. The line of blocks under the water may be a path. Walk it with one hand on the wall and one hand in front of you, and count each block as you pass it. If you lose count, return to the step and start again.

**The reflection.** The water shows a ceiling a few inches above the real one, and the reflected stone has no cracks. Do not use the reflection to judge height or distance. Measure everything by touch. Ripples that curl back toward their source are not a sign of danger. They are a sign that the water is paying attention to you, and that you have changed something by being there.

## On Sound

The drops that fall from the ceiling ring through the stone longer than they should. Count the rings as they fade and note how long they last. You may find the count changes over the course of your visit. Do not try to match the rhythm. Let it pass through you, and do not add to it.

The hush of the water is steady. If it changes, if it goes quiet for longer than usual or rises in pitch just before a swell, stop moving and wait. The room is telling you the swell is coming.

## Time

You will lose track of time here. Hours will feel like minutes, and minutes like hours. Decide before you enter how long you will stay, and tie a marker to your line for each interval. When you pass the last marker, begin your exit, even if you feel you have only just arrived. The room is patient. You are not, and the difference matters.

If you find yourself wanting to stay past your limit, or feeling that the water is holding its breath for you, that is the moment to leave.

## Leaving

Follow your line back to the entrance. Expect the path to have closed in some places and opened in others. Trust the line over your memory. Climb slowly and steadily, keeping your face up, and do not hurry through the membrane or the stems. When you reach the surface, do not take the first deep breath too fast. Let your body remember what air is, and let the cold leave you gradually.

Once you are dry and warm, write down everything you can remember while it is still fresh: the swell, the angle of the carving, the rings of the drops, the place where the membrane met the stone, the moment you felt the water listening. Your notes will fade as you do, but they are the only record that the room is not entirely yours to keep.

## A Last Note on Attitude

The Drowned Hollow is young and still deciding what it will become. It does not want you to conquer it, to map it, or to take anything from it. It is more interested in whether you will stay still long enough to notice that it is also watching. Go with patience, with respect for the slowness, and with the understanding that you are a visitor to something that is still learning to be a room.
