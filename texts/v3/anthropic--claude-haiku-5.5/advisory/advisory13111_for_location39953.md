# Preparing to Visit the Shelf Above the Clouds

This is a place that will test your assumptions about solid ground, distance, and direction. Prepare for the physical demands, but also for the places where the world stops behaving predictably. Most of the danger here is not dramatic. It is the slow accumulation of small errors: a step that isn't where you expected, a train that goes somewhere else, a map that is already out of date.

## Before You Go: Mental Preparation

**Accept that the map is a rough suggestion.** Bring a map, but treat it as a record of how the land was once arranged. In the newer districts, especially near fresh basalt, measured positions shift. Learn to hold a rough mental model and update it constantly. Locals do not trust maps, and neither should you.

**Drop your clock-based sense of time.** People here measure time by the stone and the trees. You can keep a watch, and you should, but it will not tell you much about how the day is going. The sky never darkens so much as it gradually grows lamplit, and the light has no direction. Plan in hours and tasks, not in sunsets.

**Expect to be slow.** Nothing here is hurried, and rushing will get you hurt. Set your schedule around a single crossing, a single conversation, or a single climb per day, and leave room for the place to interrupt you.

## Health and Physical Preparation

**Altitude.** The higher shelves are thin-aired. If you come from low elevation, spend at least two days acclimatizing in the lower built districts before going above the cloud line. Hydrate heavily, but be wary of the water itself (see below). Headaches, breathlessness, and poor sleep are common early signs.

**Moisture.** The cloud forest saturates everything. Your clothes will be damp within minutes, and skin will feel perpetually cool and clammy. Wear wool or synthetic base layers, because cotton holds moisture and chills you. Carry two sets of dry clothes in sealed bags. Wet feet are the most common reason visitors cut trips short.

**Dust and grit.** The grey ash crust above the scree line is fine and mineral-sharp. Bring a fitted particulate mask or respirator, and eye protection with side shields. Wash your face and hands before eating, and keep your water sealed.

**Gas.** Vents breathe steam into the cloud, and some lower hollows near the works may hold sulfurous or otherwise heavy gases. A personal gas detector is worth its weight. If you feel dizzy or nauseous in a low fold of ground, climb out immediately. Do not sleep in valley bottoms.

**Footing.** The new rock is sharp enough to cut through gloves. Wear heavy boots with aggressive treads and ankle support, and carry leather gloves that are reinforced at the palm and fingers. Scree fans shift underfoot; move across them on a diagonal, not straight up or down.

**Hearing.** The machine drones and waterfall roar are constant and can cause fatigue over a long stay. Foam earplugs will reduce the drone without isolating you from warnings. Keep at least one ear open when you move through the terraces, since rockfalls and rail traffic are the main hazards.

**Vibration.** Deep tremors roll through the ground several times a day. Do not panic at the first one, but do not ignore them either. If you feel one while on a cliff, a scaffold, or a bridge, move to the stable side of the structure and stay low until it passes.

## Gear

- Layered waterproof and breathable outer shell, hood with a brim
- Quick-drying base layers, plus spare dry sets
- Heavy waterproof boots with deep lugs and ankle support
- Leather gloves, reinforced at the palms
- Respirator with fine particulate filters, plus spare filters
- Personal gas detector and a small first-aid kit with antiseptic for cuts
- Headlamp and a second, larger lamp; you will need light by mid-afternoon
- Water purification (boiling and filtration) and sealed food containers
- Hearing protection and a whistle
- Compass, though keep in mind it may drift near new stone
- A notebook and a pencil that does not depend on ink flow in damp air
- A waterproof, sealed bag for documents and electronics; damp corrodes everything

## Navigation

**Use sound first, sight second.** The rail line, the waterfalls, and the drone of the pumping stations all give you directional information that is more reliable than maps. Learn where the big pumps are and orient yourself by their pitch.

**Read the trees.** Locals navigate by the lean of the trunks, which tends to follow the old slope even when the ground has moved. The bark-stripped trees also mark the routes of large animals, so note where they cluster.

**Expect discontinuities.** Stairs may have more or fewer steps than the building suggests. Rail tracks may climb a gradient you should not be able to reach. A cable car may deliver you to a station that is not the one you left from. When this happens, do not panic and do not try to force the system to behave. Ask a local how they would get back. Usually the answer is to walk a short distance, listen, and find the familiar sound of the works.

**Agree on a meeting point defined by something stable.** If you are traveling with others, do not rely on coordinates or street names. Choose a landmark that has been stable for decades, a particular turbine, a named waterfall, a tower that leans in a known direction, and make sure everyone knows the same sound or sign to signal it.

**Mark your own path.** Carry a roll of bright tape or flagging to mark the junctions you pass, but expect some marks to be displaced or to appear in unexpected places. Re-check them against the sound of the works.

**Watch for upward rain.** Near the edges of the cloud forest, droplets sometimes lift from the leaves and vanish into the mist. This is not a hazard in itself, but it is a sign that the local geometry is unstable. Move carefully and avoid steep edges in those areas.

## Water and Food

Do not drink from streams without treating the water. The lower valleys run in two directions at once in different strata, and what is flowing past you may not be coming from where it appears to. Boil everything, filter it, and use chemical treatment as a backup.

Do not eat the moss, fungus, or fruit of the cloud forest unless a local has shown you what is safe, and even then, start with very small amounts. The local diet is built around the terraces and the markets, not the wild forest. Bring your own food, enough for several days, and sealed containers that keep out damp and insects.

Expect to eat warm, simple, heavy food. The air is cold and the work is physical, and locals tend to favor dense grains, rendered fats, and stews.

## Wildlife

The animals here are abundant and mostly indifferent to visitors, but they are large, and some are dangerous through accident rather than intent.

- **Grazers.** The long-necked, heavy-bodied grazers in the terraces feed on the moss that grows on pipelines. Keep your distance, especially from young animals, and do not block their paths. Sudden movement startles them.
- **Bark-rubbing creatures.** Those in the deep forest rub their flanks against trees, stripping the bark. Do not camp near fresh scarring, and move quietly if you find it.
- **Nesting birds.** The large pale birds nest on cranes and in abandoned foundries. Do not approach nests. Their screams are a warning, and their droppings can be a respiratory hazard in enclosed spaces.
- **Insects.** The luminous insect clouds around the stacks are drawn by heat. Do not stand in them for long. In the mornings, when they fall in drifts against the foundations, do not walk through the drifts without covering your face; they are not venomous, but they clog eyes, nose, and mouth.
- **Nocturnal mammals.** The small, reflective-eyed mammals moving through the ruins at night are mostly harmless, but they will nibble at unsecured food. Keep supplies sealed and off the ground.
- **Amphibians.** The enormous creature in the flooded terraces produces a loud call that carries for kilometers. It is not a threat, but it will wake you if you sleep near water.

## Social Conduct

The settlements are spread thin, and they tend to be closely knit. Families here keep memory of the land in a way that outsiders often do not. A grandmother may remember where a cliff used to stand. If someone tells you how the ground has moved, listen, because that is how they navigate.

Engineers and quarry workers are often under pressure to build things that will be outdated before they are finished, and they will build anyway. Respect the work. Do not criticize a bridge or a tower for being temporary; that is understood.

Do not treat the local sense of time as a quaint custom. It is an accurate reading of the environment. People who ignore it get lost, fall, or are surprised by a shifted cliff.

## What Not to Do

- Do not touch freshly cooled basalt with bare hands, and do not stand on the newest outcrops, which can still be warm and brittle.
- Do not rely on a single route, a single map, or a single timetable.
- Do not sleep in valley bottoms, near pipelines, or under unstable cliffs.
- Do not climb into the cloud forest without someone who knows the trails and the tide of the mist.
- Do not force a train, cable car, or stair to behave in a way it does not want to. Wait, ask, or walk.
- Do not assume the ground is where it was last year.

## Leaving

When you go, take the same rail line you came up, if it is still running in the same direction. Before you board, confirm the station name with someone who lives there, and watch the cloud as the train descends. You will see the new stone disappear into the mist, the machines continue their grinding, and the birds continue calling. The mountain is still becoming itself, and it will not wait for you to finish your visit. Leave with a sense of how small your own timescale is, and carry some of the patience you learned here back with you.
