# Preparing for the Stillhigh

## Who should go, and how long to stay

The Stillhigh is not dangerous in the usual sense. Nothing here will hunt you, poison you, or collapse under you. The risk is that it is easy to stay too long. Its stillness is calming, and the calm can slide into a loss of time and purpose. Go with a fixed purpose and a fixed duration, such as a survey, a sketch, a few hours of quiet, or a single crossing. Decide on your turnaround time before you step off the first crust polygon, and write it down somewhere you will see it. Don't plan to sleep here unless you have experience with stillness-induced disorientation and a partner who will hold you to your schedule.

## Time, and the lack of it

The sun holds its angle all day, so the sky gives you no hours. The stalks fold at dusk, which makes them the only clock on the plateau, but the dusk they keep is more a habit than a sunset. Expect your sense of time to drift within the first hour. Some travelers report that an hour felt like ten minutes, and others that it felt like most of a morning. Bring two independent time sources, such as a wristwatch and a small hourglass or a dated object you can see changing, like a candle burned to a known mark. Check them every half hour and say the time aloud. Your voice, odd as it will sound, will help you stay anchored.

## Breath and body

The air is thin and cool, and your breathing will run quick even at rest. This is not dangerous at a walking pace, but it wears on the first day. Before you go, do some sustained cardio for a few weeks so your breathing recovers faster. On arrival, sit for a few minutes and breathe slowly through your nose before you walk. Drink more water than you think you need, because the air dries you out without your noticing. Wear a light layer, since the cool air holds steady and you will sweat less than you expect, which can mislead you about how much fluid you've lost.

## The dust

Dust hangs in the air in slow glittering veils and never settles. You will breathe it, and it will not clear on its own while you are here. Bring a fitted cloth or mask, preferably one that covers the nose and mouth and can be rinsed or replaced. Keep eye protection too, because the veils catch light and can dazzle or irritate your eyes. Do not wipe at the dust on your skin with your bare hands. Rinse your face and hands with water after each stretch of walking, and wash everything you bring back before you carry it into your ordinary world.

## Navigation: distances and horizons lie

This is the most important practical section. The horizon sometimes doubles, distances stretch and shrink, and a point a hundred paces away may look within reach while the nearer rise seems endless. Do not navigate by eye alone.

- **Mark your path.** Use a thin, bright cord or a row of small stones, placed at intervals and checked against your compass. Do not use paint or anything that stains the stone, since the land has finished changing and you should not add to it.
- **Use a compass, and check it against the sun.** The sun is fixed, so its bearing is a reliable reference. Shadows, however, may lag or fall in the wrong direction, so ignore them when judging direction.
- **Count your steps,** and keep a written tally. Do not trust distance by sight.
- **Carry a map drawn from your own marks,** not from the landscape's apparent layout.
- **If the horizon doubles,** choose the lower skyline as the real one. Everything I've observed suggests it is the one that tracks with the sun.

## The animals

The herds are dense, quiet, and move in loops. The beetles, runners, and grazers are not aggressive, and they are not frightened of you in any way that shows. They simply continue. Walk around them and never through them. If a herd crosses your path, stop, stand still, and let it pass. Do not try to touch, herd, or photograph them up close. The silence of the creatures is deep, and any noise from you will feel like a violation, so move quietly, with soft footsteps on the crust, which rings underfoot.

The winged clouds hang at every height. Keep your head covered and your mouth closed when you move through them, and do not swat. Let them drift across you.

The predators, if any, are shadows that pass. You may see one at the edge of your vision and lose it, or catch a movement in the stalks. Do not run. Stop, keep your breathing slow, and let it go. Running is the only thing here that changes anything.

## The makers' traces

The stone circles, upright notched slabs, chert flakes, and bone awls are the only evidence that someone was here, and they have been left untouched for longer than anyone can say. Do not take them. Do not move them, not even slightly, and do not add to the notches on the slabs or try to read them into a calendar of your own. Some of the artifacts are aligned to the sun or the stars, and their alignment is part of what remains. Photograph or sketch them where you find them, from a respectful distance, and leave them exactly as they are.

Do not step inside the stone circles. Walk around them, and treat the hollows they ring as places of quiet, not exploration.

## The silence and your voice

You will want to speak, shout, or sing, to test the place or to break the pressure that builds in the silence. Resist this. Speaking quietly and sparingly is fine, and it can help you keep your sense of time. Shouting is not. The pressure you will feel, the sense that a sound is about to come, is part of the place's stillness, and loud noise may be what breaks it in an unpredictable way. If you feel the pressure growing, sit down, breathe, and wait. The nothing that follows is not an absence to fill.

## Anchoring yourself

The land has finished becoming, and you are not. Bring something from home that changes in a way you can see: a plant in a sealed glass bottle, a pocket watch with a known drift, a photograph of a person you love that you look at each time you check the time. Keep it on your body. Do not leave it on the plateau. Its purpose is to remind you that the world you came from is moving, and that you are going back to it.

Also bring a written note stating your name, the time you arrived, your planned return time, and the marks you placed. If you are not with a companion, leave this note with someone in your home world, and tell them your turnaround time.

## Returning

Your path back is marked by your own cord and stones. Follow them, not the horizon. When you reach the edge of the plateau, pause and check your compass against the sun again. Then walk back the way you came, counting your steps, and when your tally reaches the number you wrote down at the start, stop. Breathe slowly, rinse your face and hands, and look at your anchor object. Say the time out loud.

Expect to feel a sense of loss on the way out, as though you are leaving something that finished long before you arrived and has agreed to keep going without you. This feeling is normal. Let it pass before you sleep, and drink water.
