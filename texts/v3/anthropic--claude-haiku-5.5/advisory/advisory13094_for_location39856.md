# Preparing to Visit the Sunken Room

The Sunken Room is not dangerous in the way that a volcano or a predator's den is dangerous. It is dangerous the way a flooded cellar is, or an old bridge, or a cold lake: slowly, through conditions that wear you down if you ignore them. Most visitors who get into trouble are not caught by something that attacks them. They are caught by wet feet, a sinking step, a misjudged channel, or a stretch of time spent too long in the cold. Prepare for the environment, and the room will mostly leave you alone.

## Footing and the Ground

The floor is a sponge over new stone, and you should assume that any patch of soft ground might give way. Wear waterproof boots with a stiff sole and a deep, aggressive tread. Sedge-clumps and moss can hide hollows and undercut ground, so probe ahead with a staff before you commit your weight. A staff with a flat foot, roughly the width of a hand, is better than a pointed one, which punches through the sponge and sticks.

Carry at least thirty meters of rope and a few stakes or grapnel hooks. You are not likely to fall far, but you might sink to the thigh in a soft pocket or slide down a wet shelf. A rope tied to a sound trunk, not a fern or a fungal shelf, gives you a real anchor. Do not trust the boulders at the bowl's edge; they shed grit and shift.

Watch the pools closely. Clear, still pools with green bloom look harmless and are often shallow and firm at the margins. The dark, fast channels that run under root-bridges are not. Their current is strong and steady, cold enough to stun, and the root-bridges that cross them are often slick with spray and growth. Cross only where the bridge is thick as your thigh and visibly anchored at both ends. Never step on a bridge that bounces when another creature is moving under it.

## Keeping Warm and Dry

The cold here is patient. It comes up through your soles and settles into your joints over hours, so the danger is cumulative. Dress in three layers: a wicking base, an insulating middle layer that keeps its warmth when wet (wool or synthetic fleece, not cotton), and a waterproof outer shell with a hood that covers your ears and the back of your neck. Gloves should be thin enough to grip rope and a headlamp switch, with a second, heavier pair kept dry in an inner pocket.

Your skin will stay damp no matter what you wear, so plan for it. Carry a dry change of clothes sealed in a bag, and change into it at the first sign of shivering, not after. Wet socks are the most common cause of trouble. Bring two spare pairs and swap them every few hours.

Frost forms on the north-facing roots and the undersides of leaves. Do not sit or lean against rimed growth for long. The warmth you may feel in some of the bark is real, and it is a good place to rest your hands briefly, but do not press your whole body against it. You can warm yourself by touch; you cannot warm yourself by sitting in the cold for an hour and hoping the bark will carry you.

Eat warm, high-calorie food at regular intervals. Carry a flask of something hot, and drink it slowly, because the air already pulls water out of you through your breath even though everything seems wet.

## Light and Sight

Natural light here is weak and comes in shafts. You will spend most of your time in grey-green twilight, and your eyes will need a long time to adjust after a bright column of sun. Bring a headlamp with a dim amber or red setting. White light dazzles the fungal rings and startles the animals that hunt by the glow along the lower walls. The phosphorescence is also your friend. Learn which rings are steady and which pulse, because a pulsing ring usually means something is moving beneath it.

Expect the mist to thicken and clear without warning. A gap in the canopy can turn a short stretch of trail into a blaze of gold, and then leave you blind for a minute or more. Stop and wait when the light changes. Never walk into a sudden column of sun, since the changing light often means a leaf-fall or a shifting branch above you.

Keep a marked route. The room has no landmarks you can trust, because the vault reshapes itself slowly and the water reroutes around its own changes. Mark your path with stones or ribbon at intervals, and pick up any marker you no longer need so you do not leave a trail the room will have to carry.

## Sound and Communication

The room is loud, and the noise is more tiring than it first seems. The drip and the roar of moving water mask voices, and the lattice hum changes pitch with the wind, so a call from across a shelf may simply vanish. Agree on hand signals before you enter. A closed fist means stop, an open palm means wait, a pointed finger means look. If you are traveling in a group, keep it small, two or three people at most, and stay within arm's reach of each other on any stretch with running water.

Some visitors find the constant percussion of dripping leaves maddening after a few hours. Earplugs will dull it, but they also cut you off from the sound of moving animals and shifting water, which are your early warnings. Consider a single thin pair you can remove when you need to listen closely, rather than none at all.

The lattice humming is harmless, but it is a sign of activity. When the hum rises sharply, something is moving through the filaments, often a long-bodied creature feeding on the water-pulses. Give these coils room. They do not attack, but if you break a filament you may interrupt a route the plant has built over generations, and the water it was carrying will flood a shelf that was dry an hour before.

## Respecting the Lattice and the Water

The filaments are fragile. They look like crystal, and they break like crystal, and their routing of water is older than the room. Do not touch them unless you need to, and never lean your weight on them. Keep your hands and pack away from the nodes where water pools, since they are often the most delicate parts of the structure.

Do not block channels. If you find water running into a dead end, do not dam it with stones or moss to make a path. The water will find its way eventually, and your dam will make the room's management more difficult, sometimes with a flood at the point you least expect it.

Do not take samples. The bark, the moss, the grit, and the phosphorescent fungus are all part of living systems that depend on each other, and removing them tends to shift the balance in ways that are hard to predict. Photographs and field notes are the only things you should leave with.

## The Animals

Most of the fauna is shy, and most of it is hard to see. Treat movement at the edge of your vision as a fact, not a threat. Do not chase anything, and do not reach into gaps between fronds or under shelves where animals might be sheltering.

The grooming creatures keep the moss trimmed in patterns. Leave their work alone. If you disturb a pattern, the animals will often rebuild it in an hour, but the disruption may spread to the shelves below as they shift.

The beetles that carry seeds are useful, and they will crawl over your gear. Brush them off gently and let them go. The shell-banded creatures in silver lichen can be nearly impossible to see; when you stand on a shelf, check for the faint ripple of a shell before you put your weight down.

Birds are rare and nest high in the vaults. If you see one, do not look up for long. Its nest is probably above the thinnest light, and it will leave if it feels watched.

## Time and the Slowness

The room will make you feel as if time moves differently here. A morning can disappear into the rhythm of the drips, and a rest on a warm trunk can stretch into an hour before you notice. Set alarms on your watch for check-ins. Agree on a turnaround time and keep to it even if the light is good and you feel strong.

Do not sleep in the room. The ground is too wet, the cold too patient, and the animals move at night in ways you cannot easily track. If you need to stay overnight, set up on a stable shelf well above the water line, with a tarp overhead and insulation below you. Anchor the tent to a thick trunk and not to fungal growth.

Remember that the room is still forming. Its shape will be slightly different when you return, and the water will have found new courses. Note the landmarks that matter to you on your way out, and expect that some of them will have moved by the next visit.

## Leaving

Plan your exit before you go in. Know which direction leads up, and which way the rope is anchored. The climb out is often the most dangerous part of the visit, because you are tired, cold, and carrying wet gear over soft and slippery ground. Take the easiest slope you can find, and test each handhold before you trust it.

When you reach the rim, do not stop immediately. Walk a few hundred paces on firmer ground before you sit down. Check yourself and your companions for cold injury: numb fingers, pale skin, slurred speech, unusual clumsiness. Change into dry clothes, eat something warm, and drink.

The bowl will keep its quiet roar behind you for a long time. Many visitors report hearing it in their sleep for days afterward. That is normal. Rest, and come back when you are ready to see how the room has changed.
