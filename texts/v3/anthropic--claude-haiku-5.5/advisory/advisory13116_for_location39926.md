# Preparing for the High Dry Lands of Ossuary Reach

## Read This First

Ossuary Reach is not hostile in the way a jungle or a sea is hostile. It does not hunt you. It is simply indifferent to anything that needs water, air, warmth, or stability, and most of what you bring with you needs at least one of those. Most travelers who get into trouble here are not attacked. They run out of water, misjudge the altitude, lose their bearings, or stand too long where the wind is about to change. Prepare for the environment, and you will mostly be fine.

## Your Body

**Altitude.** You are high up, and the air is thin. Your body will feel it within minutes: shortness of breath, a pounding pulse, headaches, a dullness in thought. Plan for at least two days of acclimatization before doing anything strenuous. Walk slowly. Rest often. Eat more than you think you need, since altitude suppresses appetite while raising your energy demands. If you develop a persistent headache, nausea, confusion, or a cough that brings up fluid, descend or stop at once. Some visitors feel fine at rest and collapse after a brisk climb up a ridge. Pace yourself to the slowest member of your group, even if the group is only you.

**Cold.** The days are cool, but the nights are cold enough to crack a mug of water. Your body's temperature regulation is weaker at altitude, and the dry air pulls heat and moisture from you constantly. Dress in layers you can open and close quickly. Keep a warm hat, gloves, and a windproof outer shell within reach at all times, not buried in your pack. Do not sleep without insulation. Frostbite can develop on exposed skin within minutes when wind chill is high.

**Dehydration.** This is the most dangerous hazard in the Reach. There is no water in the landscape, and the dry air means you lose water with every breath and through your skin without noticing. You will feel thirsty late and drink too little unless you make a deliberate plan. Drink on a schedule, not on sensation. Carry more water than you think you will use, and treat anything less than a full day's reserve as a warning sign. Watch your urine color and your mouth. Dry lips, dark urine, and a racing heart with no sweat are signs you are already behind.

**Sun and light.** Above much of the atmosphere, sunlight is intense, and the salt flats reflect it upward. Shadows are sharp and cobalt, which is beautiful and disorienting. Wear wide-brimmed hats, UV-blocking eyewear with side protection, and cover your skin. Snow blindness is possible even without snow, since the crystal-laden air and the salt glare do similar damage. Bring a spare pair of eyewear.

**Lungs.** The storm to the horizon drops fine crystalline snow that sublimates before it reaches the ground. Some of that crystal fog will drift over you. It is probably harmless in small amounts, but it is fine enough to irritate airways. Use a cloth or a mask over your mouth and nose when the wind is strong or when you are near the storm's edge. Rinse your mouth and eyes after exposure.

## Gear

**Water.** Bring at least four liters per person per day, plus a reserve of several liters. Carry it in insulated containers so it does not freeze at night, and keep at least one container close to your body. Bring a means to purify or at least filter water if you are going to attempt to harvest dew or meltwater from plants. Dew collection is possible but slow, and the mats you would harvest are living things that depend on it.

**Shelter.** A tent is not optional. Choose a shelter rated for high wind and sub-freezing nights, and anchor it with more stakes than you think you need. Salt plates are brittle and will not hold a stake well, so bring long stakes or weights. Do not pitch in a hollow, where cold air pools and salt can accumulate. Do not pitch near the built forms, which shift.

**Navigation.** Be careful here. The compass may be unreliable. The salt crystals orient toward invisible fields, and there is good reason to think the same fields act on magnetic instruments. Test your compass against the sun and the stars before relying on it, and carry a second, non-magnetic method. Star navigation is excellent here because the sky is so clear and the stars are visible even by day at the zenith. Learn the sky before you go. Carry a sextant or a simple inclinometer if you know how to use them.

**Maps.** Any map you bring will be wrong within days. The built forms change shape, the ground redraws its lines, and the whirlwinds follow channels that do not appear on the surface. Use maps only for regional orientation. Navigate by stars, sun position, and the relative positions of the larger landforms, which change least.

**Marking your route.** Footprints in the salt begin to fill in as you walk. Cairns can be rearranged by wind or by the slow reshaping of the built forms. If you need to mark a route, use several methods at once: a visible colored marker, a note of bearing and distance, and a memory of landmarks you will recognize even if they have moved. Assume that any single marker will fail.

**Communication.** The thin air carries sound poorly. Voices fade within a few dozen meters. Bring whistles, a signal mirror, and a radio or beacon with a long battery life. Agree on hand signals before you lose sight of each other. Be aware that the sky occasionally flickers with pale arcs, and electronics near them may behave oddly.

**Medical.** Bring altitude medication if you can get it, along with pain relievers, anti-inflammatory drugs, oral rehydration salts, eye drops, a burn kit, a splint, and frostbite treatment. Carry a pulse oximeter if you have one. Its readings are useful but not decisive; feel your own condition first.

**Lighting.** Bring multiple light sources with spare batteries. Night is cold and dark, and the sky, though full of stars, offers little illumination on the ground. A headlamp with red light preserves night vision.

## The Ground

**Salt plates.** The hexagonal crust is thin and sharp at the edges. Raised plates can crack under weight, and the hollow ones sing when wind passes over them, which is pleasant until you realize they are sensitive to your weight too. Step on the centers of plates, not the edges. Test before committing your full weight. Wear sturdy boots with good ankle support and ridged soles. Salt cuts easily and dries into a crust that makes wounds harder to clean.

**Hollows.** Low ground collects cold air and salt. It is also where the whirlwinds tend to cluster. Avoid camping or resting in hollows.

**Polished stone.** Where the wind has scoured the rock to satin, it is slippery, especially when frosted. Use your hands and keep your center low.

**Cracks and channels.** The old carved channels are smooth and sometimes undercut. They are good for shelter from wind but may collapse. Do not walk along the lip of an undercut channel.

**Creatures.** The small pale animals are shy and usually dig into cracks when approached. Do not try to catch them. Do not dig into the cracks yourself. Plants should be treated as living things and left mostly undisturbed. Their mats are slow to recover and provide the dew that other life depends on.

## The Storms and the Wind

The great storm wall is roughly forty kilometers away and climbs past the tropopause. Its lightning is silent to you at that distance, but do not assume it is harmless. Storms of this size can move across the plateau faster than you expect, and the fine crystal snow that falls from them can reach you well ahead of the main cell. Watch the horizon constantly. If the wall grows darker, begins to lean, or if the crystal haze thickens overhead, move to shelter at once.

The smaller whirlwinds are more immediate. Some are only a few meters across; others are hundreds. Learn to read their paths. They tend to follow lines in the ground, and those lines may be shown by faint color changes, subtle ridges, or the orientation of salt plates. Stay at least several hundred meters from any column unless you know its track. A whirlwind can carry salt and grit that will strip exposed skin and damage eyes. It can also lift a person off the ground, though rarely.

Wind can change direction quickly here. Do not set up camp in the path of a prevailing gust without checking the local flow over the course of a full day.

## The Built Forms

The domes, towers, and arches are beautiful and deeply strange. Treat them with respect and caution.

They are made of a material that seems to be the same as the ground. They tick as they cool or settle. They sometimes hum from below, and the hum can be felt in your teeth and sternum. Do not touch them without good reason. Some have unhealed cracks or unfinished ribbed edges that are sharp. Others may respond to contact, though what that response looks like is unknown.

Do not shelter in them. They change. A dome you enter may rise, tilt, or fold while you are inside. Several visitors have reported feeling their stomachs turn as a floor shifted under them. Treat every built form as temporary.

Do not rely on them as landmarks. A tower that is your bearing marker today may be a mound next week.

If you find a structure partially built with a rough, open edge, keep your distance. The edge may be in the middle of a change, and changes here can be fast.

## The Sky

The sky is violet and thin, and the stars are faintly visible even in daylight at the zenith. Learn the major constellations for your latitude before you arrive. They will be your most reliable navigation.

The pale arcs in the sky move in directions that auroras never do. They are not dangerous in themselves, as far as anyone can tell, but they coincide with interference in electronics and with shifts in the built forms. If the arcs begin to brighten, expect instrument failure and tread carefully near the structures.

## Psychological Preparation

The Reach is silent and enormous. Your heartbeat becomes the loudest thing you hear. The land changes around you, and nothing you build stays in place. Many visitors report a sense of being watched, which is probably the mind trying to find patterns in a landscape that is actively reshaping itself. Accept the feeling, note it, and keep your attention on your breath, your water, and your footing.

Expect to feel small. That is part of what the place offers. But do not let the sense of scale lead you into rash decisions. The most dangerous moment in the Reach is often the moment you decide you have figured it out.

Travel with at least one other person whenever possible. Agree on turnaround times and distances. Agree on what you will do if separated. Tell someone, on whatever side of the boundary you come from, where you intend to go and when you expect to return.

## Rules of Conduct

Do not take anything that is alive. Do not take salt plates, stone, or the crystal dust unless you have a specific, approved purpose, since the land seems to resist and rebuild what is removed.

Do not try to interact with the built forms in ways that alter them. Observation, measurement, and gentle photography are acceptable. Digging, breaking, or attempting to tap their hum is not.

Leave no trace where possible, though understand that the land will erase your footprints anyway. Pack out everything you bring, including food wrappers and spent batteries.

## Leaving

Plan your exit before you enter. Know where your nearest return point is, how far it is, and how long it takes to walk. Watch the weather for at least a full day before departure. Do not linger past your planned turnaround. The land is patient. You are not.

When you come back, expect to be tired, dehydrated, and mildly altitude-affected for a day or two. Drink water. Rest. Your body will need time to readjust to ordinary air, and you may find yourself breathing deeply without meaning to for several hours.

## A Final Note

Ossuary Reach is one of the most beautiful and unsettling places a human traveler can visit. Its silence, its clean light, its impossible storms, and its slow, patient rebuilding of mountains that wind keeps taking away offer something few other places can. Go with respect for the air, the water, and the ground. Keep your attention on the simple things. The Reach will give you its view if you give it careful attention in return.
