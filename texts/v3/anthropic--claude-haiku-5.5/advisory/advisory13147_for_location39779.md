# Preparing to Visit the Hollow Under the Plateau

This is a place that does not want you, and does not notice you. Its inhabitants are not hostile. They are busy. Your job is to be an unremarkable obstacle that moves out of the way. The preparation below is organized around that: protecting your body from heat, air, and sharp rock, protecting your senses from the noise and glow, and keeping your footing and your bearings in a place with no horizon and no sense of time.

---

## 1. Understand what kind of place this is before you go

The Hollow is young. Its rock is still cooling, its plates are still sliding, and its creatures are still turning the raw material of the place into something. It is closer to a foundry floor during its first shift than to a wilderness. Several consequences follow:

- **Nothing is settled.** A path that is clear today may be blocked by a slumped terrace tomorrow. Treat every route as provisional.
- **Nothing is yours.** You are passing through a production space. Whatever you drop, leave, or set down may be sorted, crushed, or incorporated into the work. Assume the creatures will reclaim anything you leave behind.
- **Nothing here is made for you.** There are no handholds, rails, shelters, or signs. Everything you need must come with you.

Go in with the attitude of a visiting engineer on a working site: careful, quiet, and out of the way.

---

## 2. Heat is the first and constant enemy

The heat does not rest. It rises in columns from the fissures and radiates back off the rock, so the air shakes everywhere and there is no cool corner to retreat to.

**What to bring:**
- **Light, reflective, loose-fitting clothing** in pale colors, layered so you can vent air at the wrists, neck, and waist. Dark fabric absorbs the radiant heat and cooks you faster.
- **A wide-brimmed or hooded covering** for the head and neck. Radiant heat from the walls and ceiling is as dangerous as heat from the ground.
- **Cooling supplies you can actually use:** a wet cloth wrapped around the neck, a sealed bottle of ice or cold packs if your gear allows, and a plan for rotating in and out of a cooler exit if you have one.
- **Heat-rated gloves**, not just for grabbing warm stones but for brushing against walls. Some surfaces are hot enough to blister skin in a few seconds.

**Watch for:**
- Dizziness, headache, nausea, or a sudden stop in sweating. These mean you have been out too long and must leave immediately.
- Your hands and feet getting hot through your boots. The ground radiates as well as conducts. Keep moving; standing still on warm rock is how people overheat.

---

## 3. Your air supply is the most urgent concern

The atmosphere is thin at this altitude, and every breath feels shallow. The vents add sulfur dioxide, hydrogen sulfide, and other gases in quantities that vary from one crack to the next. Most of the danger is invisible.

**Essential equipment:**
- **A full-face respirator with acid-gas and sulfur-compound cartridges.** A half-mask is not enough, because your eyes will be exposed to vent gas and sulfur particulate.
- **A supplemental oxygen source** if you are not acclimatized to altitude. Plan for a steady flow rather than a reserve you hope to stretch.
- **A portable gas detector** that reads at least sulfur compounds and oxygen level, with an audible alarm. Your nose will not be reliable here. Sulfur smell is fleeting and can be deadened by exposure.

**Breathing discipline:**
- Move slowly. Exertion increases oxygen demand in thin air, and the shallow breathing will make you feel as if you cannot get enough.
- Avoid hollows, low spots, and pockets near the vent mouths, where gas collects. The detector will tell you more than your instinct.
- If you feel lightheaded, confused, or unusually calm, stop, put your mask on fully, and retreat upslope to clean air. Those are early signs of gas or hypoxia, and they feel deceptively pleasant.

---

## 4. Footing: the ground will cut you and ring under you

The floor is fresh basalt with edges so sharp they look wet. Terraces have stacked and slipped, and between them lie drifts of black glass scree that chime when you step on it. Crusts of ash crack and lift like old paint. Sulfur and salt crystals grow in needles and fans that snap with a sound like dry sugar.

**Footwear and protection:**
- **Thick-soled, high-ankle boots with cut-resistant uppers** and a rigid midsole. Basalt edges will slice through thin leather, and glass will find any gap.
- **Gaiters or sealed ankle guards** to keep scree and ash out of your boots. Glass grit in your shoes will grind your feet raw by the end of a day.
- **Knee and shin protection** for the times you must kneel or crawl over slumped plates.
- **Cut-resistant sleeves and gloves** for handling anything that needs to be steadied.

**Moving on it:**
- Test every step. Crusts can look solid and give way. Scree can look shallow over a crack.
- Keep your weight centered and your steps short. The terraces are unstable and can shift slightly underfoot.
- Do not stack, lean, or build. Anything you place on the terraces may be treated as debris to be sorted, and anything you lean on may not still be there when you turn around.
- Keep a rope or tether if you can anchor it to something stable, but remember that the rock you anchor to is moving on a timescale too slow to see and fast enough to matter over a long visit.

---

## 5. The noise is not background; it is a physical presence

The Hollow is never silent. The vent roar is continuous, the percussion of shells on rock comes in waves, grinding and sawing come from the walls, ratchet-like clicking repeats for hours, and the hollow amplifies all of it until you feel it in your teeth and sternum.

**Protection and planning:**
- **Good hearing protection**, ideally a level-dependent set of earmuffs or plugs that let you hear voices while cutting peaks. The impacts are sharp and can startle you into a bad step.
- **A signaling plan** that doesn't depend on speech. Hand signals, a light code, a tether tug, or a whistle you can feel rather than hear.
- **A vibration-aware approach:** if the percussion suddenly intensifies, expect a cluster event and find a stable spot, or leave the area.

**Mental preparation:**
- The sound does not change in any way that tells you what it means. Do not try to interpret the rhythms. They are the work, not a message to you.
- Expect the noise to fill your thinking. Some visitors report losing their train of thought after an hour. Write your plan down before you go in, and check it often.

---

## 6. Light, and the problem of seeing

The light is dim, close to darkness. The rock itself provides a dull cherry glow in the cracks, orange at the vent edges, and occasional pale flares where gas ignites and dies. Shadows are deep and shift with the glow.

**Lighting gear:**
- **A headlamp with a red or amber filter** to preserve your night vision and avoid washing out the heat-glow you rely on. Bright white light will leave you blind outside its beam.
- **A backup light** on a separate battery, in case the first fails in a pocket of gas or dust.
- **Glow-stick or chemical markers** to mark your route, placed carefully where they will not be picked up or crushed.

**Reading the place:**
- Use the heat-glow to judge depth. Brighter orange means hotter and more recent cracks. Dull red means older or cooler.
- The fauna's sparks flash and fade. Do not chase them with your light. Sudden bright beams can disturb the movement of large groups.
- Watch the flares. A pale flare is gas igniting, which means a vent is active nearby. Move away from its line.

---

## 7. Navigation without a horizon

The walls curve in on themselves. You cannot see the sky or the far edge, and nothing is landmarked. The plates shift slowly, so the terrain changes too gradually to notice over an hour but enough to mislead you over a day.

**Bearings:**
- **A compass** is useful only if you know its deviation in the local rock, which may be significant. Test it before you leave the entrance, and recheck at intervals.
- **A pre-surveyed map** with the terraces, vents, and entrance marked. Update it as you go, noting the date and time of each mark.
- **A marked trail** using durable, high-visibility markers at each junction. Place them where they will be seen and not crushed. Check them on the way back.
- **A fixed reference point** you can always return to, such as the entrance or a particular vent outside the hollow. Know its position relative to where you are at every moment.

**Pace and time:**
- There is no day or night here. Set timers for your rest and exit points, and carry more than one clock.
- Keep a time log. The fauna cycles through gatherings and dispersals on seasonal scales. You may see waves of activity without being able to tell how long they last.

---

## 8. Living among the creatures

The fauna is crowded, shoulder to shoulder, covering the walls and pouring across the floor in slow, dense currents. You cannot walk without touching them. Many are plated and segmented, built like crushers and bellows, with toothed limbs that work like gears. Some pump heat or gas through their bodies over the vents. They break, sort, lay down crust, and feed on the minerals they release.

**Basic conduct:**
- **Do not touch them unless you must.** Their armor is hard, their limbs are toothed, and some may be hot enough to burn you. A disturbed creature may stop its work or move suddenly in ways that injure you.
- **Step where they are already moving,** not into their path. Wait for a current to pass before crossing. Many will move around you if you give them room.
- **Do not interrupt rhythms.** Pausing a group at a vent or stopping a crusher mid-cycle may cause a cascade of disruption. If you find yourself in the middle of a gathering, move steadily and calmly out of it.
- **Do not collect samples or take anything.** Anything you carry out is material that has already been sorted. Taking it may be interpreted as theft from the work, and it may also contain gas, heat, or sharp fragments.

**Reading them:**
- Groups that gather at a vent are preparing to work. Groups that disperse are finishing a cycle. Your path should avoid the flows toward the vents when they are gathering.
- The clicking and ratchet sounds often precede movement. Treat them as a warning, not as signaling to you.
- Some creatures will lift and pump their bodies over a vent. Keep your distance from the arc of their exhaust.

---

## 9. The vegetation question

There is none. No roots, no stems, no moss, no spores. Any apparent growth is crystalline crust or sulfur bloom, and it does not spread. Do not mistake crust for something living. Do not bring plants, seeds, or any organic material you need to keep alive. Nothing here will nourish it, and it may be sorted or consumed by the creatures.

---

## 10. Water, food, and what you actually need to bring

Water is absent. Nothing here has a liquid cycle, so there is no drinking supply inside the Hollow, no streams to filter, and no condensation to gather.

**Bring:**
- **Enough water for the entire visit plus a margin for emergencies.** Account for the heat, which will cause significant fluid loss even if you rest.
- **Non-perishable food** sealed in packaging that will not attract the sorting creatures. Eat outside the Hollow when possible.
- **Electrolyte supplements** to replace salts lost through heavy sweating.

**Do not rely on:**
- Anything produced locally. The chemistry is heat, gas, and sulfur, and none of it is food or drink.

---

## 11. Emergency and exit planning

Everything here is slow to change, but the parts that move can move dangerously. Plates slip, crusts collapse, vents flare, and gas pockets build.

**Before you go:**
- **Identify at least two exits** and the routes to them, and know their distances from every point you intend to visit.
- **Set a turnaround time** and a hard exit time. Honor them even if you feel you could stay longer.
- **Carry a communications plan** that works without the entrance being visible, such as a relay person outside, a timed check-in schedule, or a beacon. Assume your radio will be degraded by the rock.

**If something goes wrong:**
- **Gas exposure:** Mask up, move upslope to clean air, and report your location. Do not run through gas pockets.
- **Heat exhaustion:** Stop, cool your core, and retreat. Do not keep working.
- **Injury from rock or creatures:** Stabilize, stop bleeding, and move only when you can do so safely. Terraces and scree may not hold your weight.
- **Lost:** Stop moving. Use your marked trail and fixed reference. Do not try to find a shortcut through unfamiliar terrain.

---

## 12. The mental preparation

The Hollow is loud, bright only in fits, and crowded with creatures that never stop working. Its scale and density will make you feel small and unnoticed, which is accurate. Several things will be hard:

- **Sensory overload.** Heat, noise, glow, and motion will press in on you at once. Build breaks into your schedule and practice slow breathing.
- **Loss of time.** Without day or night, you will misjudge how long you have been inside. Trust your clocks, not your sense of duration.
- **Loss of orientation.** Without a horizon, you may feel turned around even when you are not. Trust your map and your markers.
- **The feeling of being a visitor to a workshop.** Nothing here is finished, and nothing is waiting for you. This can feel alienating or, for some, strangely calming. Either reaction is fine. Your job is to remain respectful and to leave.

The most important preparation is to decide, before you cross the threshold, what you came to see and how long you will stay to see it. The Hollow will keep working whether you stay or go. Go in prepared, move quietly, touch nothing you do not need to touch, and leave on schedule.
