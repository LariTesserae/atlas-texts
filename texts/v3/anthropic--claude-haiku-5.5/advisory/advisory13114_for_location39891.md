# Preparation Brief: The Wet Upland, Dark Plateau

## Before You Step Through

Expect no sun, no moon, and no stars. The cloud ceiling is continuous, and there is no natural light source. Bring your own light, but test it before you commit. Light may behave strangely here, and you should know how far a beam carries through the mist before you depend on it. Carry at least two independent light sources, and one that needs no battery, such as a hand-crank or chemical stick, in case the drizzle kills the first.

Plan for total reliance on sound. Your ears will be your primary instrument for the whole visit, so protect them from water. Keep ear canals dry, and if your equipment has any sensitivity to pressure changes, expect fluctuations every time the large engines cycle.

## Your Body in Warm, Saturated Air

The air is warm and dense with moisture, so you will sweat heavily without feeling dry. Your clothing will never dry out. Choose synthetic or treated fabrics that shed water and do not hold it against your skin. Cotton will become heavy, cold when the breeze comes, and prone to rot within a day.

Thin air will make you breathe harder than you expect, especially on the slopes. Pace yourself on the conveyor ramps and the cistern-stairs. Rest before you need to.

Your skin will soften, wrinkle, and invite fungus within hours. Keep feet dry where you can, change socks often, and treat any cracks or abrasions at once. Bring antifungal powder and a sealed kit for wounds. Check your skin for leeches or things that resemble them. Shake out your boots before putting them on.

Drink only water you have sterilized yourself. The streams look clean and run cold, but they come from pumps that lift water from unknown lowlands. Do not assume that the wetness is pure. Hydration is easy to overlook in a place where everything is wet, so drink on a schedule, not on thirst.

## Footing and the Ground

The bedrock is old, smooth, and slick. In the wet it can throw you without warning. The moss is the worse hazard: a deep, soft carpet that looks like solid ground and gives way into hidden pockets of water. Test every step with a probe or a stick before you commit your weight. Do not cross moss-covered slopes alone.

Watch the channels between the rock plates. Water runs in them quickly, and some are deeper than they look. Cross them at the narrowest point, or follow the existing stone bridges, which show signs of use and of repair.

## Navigation by Sound

This is the most important section of the brief.

Sound is the only map here, and it lies. An echo can come from a cliff near you or from a pump several valleys away. A drip that seems to be at your shoulder may be fifty meters off. The machine hum can pass for a heartbeat, and a heartbeat can pass for a machine.

Do not navigate by direction alone. Instead:

- **Count the pump cycles.** The large engines rise and fall on a slow, steady rhythm. Count them to measure time and distance. Each cycle is roughly a unit of the day here, and you should adopt it as your clock.
- **Triangulate with a companion.** Stand apart and call a single word, then compare how it returns. Do this often.
- **Mark your route physically.** Use chalk, cloth, or cut vines. Vines regrow, so mark them with fresh cuts, not tied knots.
- **Keep a written log** of each sound you map and where it came from, and check your guesses against what you find.

Some of the animal calls answer the engines with exact timing, as if they have learned the rhythm. Do not treat any sound as neutral. A call that answers a pump may be a warning, a territorial marker, or a lure.

## The Machines

These are the largest and oldest things you will see. Some are still running, and some have been swallowed by the vegetation so completely that the plants now hold them up.

- **Do not stand beneath the pistons.** They rise and fall with heavy, unhurried force, and their stroke is longer than it looks.
- **Do not touch valves, gauges, or the grilles behind which gears turn.** Valves hiss, and the pressure that moves them can burst skin at close range.
- **Do not cut vines that bind a machine into the forest.** Those vines are likely holding up gantries or sealing gaps in boilers. Removing them may cause structural failure.
- **Do not disturb the pinned gauge.** Its needle sits at a stop, and you should assume that stop means something.
- **Expect pressure changes.** When the big engines cycle, the air shifts. Your ears may pop, and the cloud may thicken or thin.

Some machines may still be tended. If you see recent maintenance, fresh oil, or sharpened tools, assume a person is nearby, perhaps in the dark beyond your light.

## The Huts and the People

Corrugated iron huts appear every few hundred meters. Each has a cistern on its roof and a window with no light behind it.

Approach them openly and make noise before you reach the door. Do not enter a hut uninvited. The windows are dark, but that does not mean the rooms are empty.

The rusted sign is in a script you cannot read. Do not assume it is a warning, a name, or a rule. Photograph it, copy the shapes, and compare them later with what you learn.

If you encounter people, expect them to communicate by sound before anything else: knocks on wood, whistles, calls that answer the pumps. Answer in kind, slowly, and do not mimic a call you do not understand.

## Fauna and the Undergrowth

The frog choruses rise and fall in tides. Learn their rhythm and use it. A chorus that suddenly stops is a warning.

Winged creatures move overhead in groups. When they drop out of range and silence follows, freeze and listen. Their silence means something is close, or that they have left a space open for something else.

Larger animals move with deliberate weight through the ferns. When you hear a heavy step or the rustle of a fern wall being pushed aside, do not run. Move slowly away from the sound, toward open ground or a machine's shelter.

Predators and prey are everywhere. You will hear chases, cut-short screams, and frantic scuttling. Do not intervene. Do not follow a sound toward violence.

## The Fungi and Plants

The fungi shelving up the trunks pulse in a rhythm you can feel. Do not press bare skin to them. Some may release spores that irritate or infect. Keep gloves on and avoid breathing close to the pale tiers.

The vines are thick as a man's arm and drip constantly. Do not pull on a vine to test whether it holds your weight. The fleshy leaves can produce a sap that stings or numbs.

The ferns form arches and walls. Do not sleep under them. Their shelter is dense and wet, and things move through them after dark.

## Gear List

- Two independent light sources, one manual
- Waterproof, quick-drying clothing in synthetic fabric
- Sealed boots with good grip, and spare socks in a dry bag
- Probe or walking staff for testing moss and water depth
- Water sterilization kit and sealed food
- Chalk or cloth markers, and fresh cutting tools for trail marks
- Antifungal powder, wound kit, and tick-or-leech removal tools
- Gloves for handling plants and fungi
- A notebook that resists water, and a waterproof pencil
- Earplugs or a sound-dampening device for the machines' hum, which can cause fatigue
- A sealed pouch for your ears, to keep canals dry
- A timepiece, but treat it as secondary to the pump cycles

## Mental Preparation

The hum is low enough to enter the body. Expect to feel it in your chest and jaw, and expect it to make you tired and disoriented. Take rest periods, and keep to a routine tied to the pump cycles.

Expect the sense that the living and the built have merged. You will not be able to tell where the forest ends and the machinery begins. This is normal here. Trust your notes over your ears when they disagree.

Expect to feel watched, though you may not see anything. Do not respond with aggression. Stay calm, speak quietly, and keep moving.

## Leaving

No exit is marked. The conveyor belts run up the slopes and into the cloud, and the streams run downhill, but neither is a guaranteed way out. The most reliable route is the one you mapped on the way in. Follow your marks, count your cycles, and return the way you came.

If you become lost, stop. Stay near a machine, away from the pistons, and wait for a pump to cycle. Listen for the sounds you logged on arrival, and orient by the ones you are sure of.

The place has its own rules, written in sound, water, and machinery. You will not learn them all. Go carefully, leave what you find as you found it, and do not assume you understand what you hear.
