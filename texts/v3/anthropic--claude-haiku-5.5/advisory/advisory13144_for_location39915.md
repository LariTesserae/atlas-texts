# Preparing for the Hollow Under the Mat

The Hollow is not dangerous in the way most places are. Nothing here is likely to ambush you. The danger is slower and quieter: cold that creeps up on you, a footstep that sinks, a day that becomes a week because the light never changes. Most visitors who get into trouble do so by forgetting that the place is alive, that it is always moving, and that it notices what stays still.

Plan for a visit as if you were going into a large, cold, wet, living thing that has nothing against you and no interest in you either, and that will absorb whatever you leave lying around.

## Before You Go: Timing

Choose your season carefully, because the Hollow changes character with it.

**The long cold (winter).** The mat firms and the drip freezes into grey rime. The bats are gone and the town is quiet. This is the easiest time for footing, since the surface is stiffer, and the least fauna is active. It is also the most dangerous for the body. Cold is the main threat here, and it is constant. If you come in winter, bring more insulation than you think you need, and plan for shorter stays.

**The thaw.** Everything wakes at once, and this is the time most travelers want to see and the time they are least prepared for. The mat floods, the seep rises, and walkways are being lifted or reset. The grey burrower surfaces. Insects boil out of the peat, bats return in large numbers, and fungus fruits on every wall. The noise is constant. Only go during the thaw if you can stay with a host household, and only if you can move on raised walkways without help. Do not walk the open mat during flood weeks.

**The settled season (the grey patience).** After the thaw, growth slows, the rains thin, and the pools shrink to black mirrors. This is the best time for a first visit. The town is most willing to host visitors, the mat is firm but not frozen, and the fauna is active but manageable.

Whatever season you choose, confirm the state of the walkways before setting out. If the town has lifted or rewoven its lattices recently, the old routes will be wrong.

## Getting In and Out

The root stair is the way in, and it is also the way out. Mark it well before you descend. The canopy hides the sky, the basin is shallow but wide, and the mat has a way of making every direction look the same. Carry a marker you can see in grey light, such as a bright cloth tied to the stair's top root, or a pale stone you can check against. Some visitors carry a cord to tie back to the stair on the first day until they learn the layout.

Do not leave gear sitting on the mat. Anything that stays still long enough will sink, grow over, or be taken apart by the crawlers beneath. Keep packs on your back or on raised frames. If you must set something down, set it on a platform or a stone.

Plan your return before you go deep. The sky is hidden, so your sense of how long you have been gone will drift. Keep a timepiece that works in cold and damp, and check it at every walkway junction. The bats begin to stream out at dusk, and the first clicking is a useful signal that you should be heading back to a lit window.

## Footing on Living Mat

The mat is springy, cold, and saturated. It sighs and seeps when you press it, and in some places it gives way entirely. Take it slowly.

- **Stay on woven walkways whenever you can.** They are fragile and sag, but they are the safest route across the basin. Test each lattice section before committing your weight.
- **Spread your weight.** Step with a flat foot, not on the heel or toe. Do not jump, run, or stamp. A sharp impact can open a sinkhole in soft peat.
- **Carry a probe pole.** A straight length of split wood or bone works well. Test ground ahead before stepping on any patch that looks thicker or greener than the rest, since that often means a deep hollow under the moss.
- **Do not cut the mat.** Crafters harvest blocks of living moss on a rotating cycle, and their cutting grounds are marked. Cutting elsewhere harms the regrowth and the people who depend on it. Ask before touching anything.
- **Do not kneel or lie down on the mat.** Something soft and numerous moves under the surface, and the mat will close over a body that stays still for long. If you need to rest, use a raised frame or a stone seat.

## Cold, Wet, and Breath

Everything here is cold and most things are wet. Your body will lose heat faster than you expect, and the damp will make it worse.

Dress in layers that keep warmth when wet. Wool is the obvious choice if you have it. Avoid cotton or any cloth that holds water against skin. Bring a waterproof outer layer that breathes, and a hat, because a large share of heat leaves through the head. Good gloves matter more than you might think, because cold hands lose the ability to tie knots, work a latch, or grip a lamp.

Keep a dry pair of socks in a sealed container and change them whenever your feet get wet, which will be often. Cold feet are the most common reason visitors turn back early.

Watch for the signs of the body losing heat: slowed speech, clumsiness, shivering that stops, and a sudden urge to sit down. Treat them as serious. Get out of the wet, into a warmed room, and drink something hot.

Your breath will hang in the air. This is normal. Do not confuse it with spores or smoke.

## Light

The basin is grey at midday and black at night. The fungal threads in the walls give a faint blue-green glow after dark, enough to tell wall from open air but not enough to read by or to walk the walkways safely.

Bring several independent light sources, and make sure at least one is sealed against damp. Fat-soaked wicks work and are what the town uses, but they smoke and drip, and they attract things. Bring a lantern with a shade. Keep it low when you walk, since the mat's crawlers scatter from light and you do not want to step among them in panic.

Be aware that the mat grows toward warmth and sound, and that light sources attract growth. Do not leave a lamp burning unattended for long. Where lamps have burned for years, the moss grows thick and pale in rings, and a guest who leaves a flame on a sleeping mat may find it has grown over their gear by morning.

## Sleep and Sound

The clicking of the bats is the loudest sound in the basin, besides the dripping. Most residents sleep through it. Visitors often do not. Bring earplugs made of wax, wool, or cloth, and expect that they will not fully block the sound. Sleep with a sound you can tolerate, such as a steady cloth wrap over the ears.

Do not sleep directly on the mat. Use a raised cot or a frame with a layer of woven stalk between you and the ground. The mat is warm where the body sleeps and will spread slowly toward warmth. Some visitors wake to find their blankets half-rooted.

The town may give you a bed on a platform. Accept it if you can. If you choose to sleep elsewhere, tell your host where you will be.

## Creatures

Nearly everything that moves here is small, and most of it is harmless if left alone. The mat is full of pale segmented millipedes, glassy-shelled beetles, and many-legged things that scatter from lamplight. You will step on some of them. Try not to. Crushing them makes the mat rot faster in that spot and disturbs the people who depend on the beetles' cycle.

Do not pick up anything from the mat, even something that looks interesting. Some of the crawlers are unpleasant to touch and a few may leave a residue that stings.

The blind-white crawlers thread through the peat channels and will climb onto boots and into gaps in clothing. Check your legs and sleeves each time you leave a walkway. Brush them off away from water, not into the pools.

The bats are not aggressive and do not seek contact with people. They catch insects in midair and will pass close to your head at dusk. Do not swat. Keep your hat on if the stream is thick.

The eyeless seep-fish are kept in woven traps and eaten by the town. Eat them only when offered, and do not catch them yourself without a host. The pale frogs do not call and are best left alone.

The grey burrower lives in the seep channels and surfaces at the thaw. If you visit at that time, stay on raised walkways and away from the channels. Do not wade. Do not attempt to observe it closely.

The town takes a share of the basin at its edges and does not try to take much. Respect that. Do not collect more than you are given.

## Air and Spores

The fungus on the walls and the cloud of insects in the canopy shadow both release fine particles. The sweet, fermenting smell is the most noticeable sign of this. Most people tolerate it, but some find it causes dizziness, headache, or a cough that lingers.

Bring a cloth to cover your nose and mouth in heavy air, especially near fruiting walls, during the thaw, or when the wind pushes damp spore clouds across the walkways. Breathe through it. If your chest tightens or your head swims, go to open air or a ventilated room.

Do not eat fungus raw. The preserved fungus the town stores is safe because it has been dried and rendered. Raw fruiting bodies from the walls may cause sickness, and some should not be eaten at all.

## Water and Food

Seep water is cold, mineral, and possibly unsafe. Boil it before drinking, and let it cool to a bearable temperature before you try. Bring your own clay or metal vessel for boiling, and keep it clean. Some visitors bring a sealed water bottle for the first days as a precaution.

The town eats fat, dried fungus, fish, and what the mat yields at its edges. Expect plain food. Eat what your host offers, and try a little of everything, since refusing food can read as insult in a place where food is scarce and shared.

Bring your own dry rations as a backup. Keep them sealed and off the mat. Damp and mold will get into anything left out.

## Manners and the Town

The town is sparse and inward-looking. Houses are spaced wide, with dark wet gaps between them that the mat and its creatures claim. Do not cut across these gaps or step into them without reason. They are not paths and not empty land.

Enter houses only when invited. Doorways are curtained with hanging moss, and the curtain is not a door to push through. Announce yourself at the curtain, wait, and lift it only when someone answers. Do not part the moss without warning. Living mat can tangle and bruise, and it is considered rude to disturb a home carelessly.

Greet residents courteously and wait for them to speak first. Keep your voice low at night and in the lower rooms, where sound carries through the moss.

Metal is rare here. Some residents wear small bright bits of it as ornament or charm. If you bring metal, be aware that it may be valued, may be seen as a trade good, or may be seen as a bad omen, depending on the household. Ask before showing or offering any. Do not give metal to children without asking an adult first.

Do not sing near the deep seep pools. This is a local custom, and the townspeople take it seriously, whatever you think of the stories. Do not sing loudly in the lattice-walkways or near curtains of moss that have grown heavy, since the town says the mat listens and leans toward voices. If you talk for long periods in one place, the curtains there may grow thicker over time. This is a minor thing, but the residents will notice it.

## Tools to Bring

You need less than you might think, but what you bring should be sturdy, dry, and easy to repair:

- A knife or small blade for cutting rope and cloth, not for cutting the mat
- A length of cord, long enough to tie back to the root stair
- A probe pole for testing ground
- Several dry-bag containers, sealed
- A bone or wooden needle and thread for repairs, since everything here wears out
- A clay or metal pot for boiling water
- A cloth for your face and nose
- Multiple lights with spare fuel, and a shaded lantern
- Earplugs and a sleep mat or raised cot
- A timepiece that works in cold and damp
- Dry socks, spare clothing, and a waterproof outer layer
- A small first-aid kit for cuts, scrapes, and chilblains

## Leaving

When you climb back to the root stair, the sky returns gradually. The light grows and the air loosens, and the cold that has settled into your joints will linger for some time. Do not hurry. Climb slowly and check your boots and clothes at the top.

Before you leave the stair entirely, brush off anything that clings: crawlers, bits of moss, spores in the fabric of your clothes. Carry out only what you came with, and leave no marks on the mat. Clean your boots away from any water, and dry your gear fully before you pack it, because mold will grow in anything left damp.

You will probably feel the place for days afterward. The drip will seem to follow you, and in quiet rooms you may hear the faint click of bats that are not there. This is normal, and it fades.

The Hollow is not a place you conquer or finish with. It is a place you visit, carefully, on its terms, and then leave.
