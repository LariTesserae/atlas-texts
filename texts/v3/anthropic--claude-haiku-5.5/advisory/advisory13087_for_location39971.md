# Preparing for the Shelf of Condensers

## Before you go

Plan around the altitude and the cold, which will wear you down more than any danger. The plateau sits near 2,000 meters, so give yourself a day or two of gentle movement before the first real climb. Headaches, breathlessness, and poor sleep are normal the first night and should pass. Keep an eye on them anyway. Nausea that persists, or confusion, means you descend.

The air is cool, around ten degrees by day and near freezing after dark, and it is always damp. Wet cold is much worse than dry cold. Pack for it in layers:

- **Base layer:** merino or synthetic. Cotton holds the condensation against your skin and will chill you within an hour.
- **Insulating layer:** fleece or light down that still insulates when damp. Down that gets soaked is useless.
- **Outer shell:** fully waterproof, with a hood that fits over a hat. The mist is not rain, so a shell that only handles showers will still let you get clammy and cold.
- **Hands and head:** two pairs of gloves, one waterproof, and a warm hat. Metal here stays cold, so anything you grip (rails, scarps, bucket handles) will pull heat from your bare hands.

Footwear matters more than anything else. The composite is slick when wet and glitters with a fine grit that gives almost no grip on smooth faces. Choose boots with stiff soles, aggressive tread, and ankle support, and break them in before you arrive. The scarps and fans of broken shards are sharp enough to cut through thin socks. Carry gaiters if you can.

## Light and eyes

The sun is close and the air is thin, so shadows are crisp and the glare comes up from the wet plates as well as down from the sky. Bring sunglasses with side shields and a wide-brimmed hat. Glare-induced headaches are common, and you will be squinting for hours without noticing.

Colours will look washed out in the day and suddenly rich at dusk. Do not judge the land by its colour at either time. If you need to record a route or a landmark, use photographs taken at midday with a bracketed exposure, and write down what you see in words as well. Your camera's colour may not match your memory.

## Water

There is water everywhere on the plateau, and almost none of it is safe to drink. The shallow trays, machine hollows, and copper-lined pits are all coated in the green mineral film or contaminated by corrosion. Carry all the water you need in sealed containers, plus a reserve. Expect to drink more than you think, because the damp air hides how much you are sweating.

The sweet smell of the film is pleasant, but it can make you drowsy and slow your reactions if you breathe it heavily for long periods. Take breaks away from the densest towers. Do not handle the film with bare skin.

Rain comes in short, fine bursts, and the channels can fill quickly once it starts. Never camp or stop in a runoff gully, and do not cross a channel while water is rising. The walls of these cuts are clean and steep, with no ledges to climb out on.

## Movement and hazards

**Gantries and conveyors.** Most of the conveyors move at the pace of a minute hand, but the chains can still pinch or drag a glove into the mechanism, and some are full of wet dust that will coat anything you touch. Do not reach into the buckets. Birds bathe in them, and they are not always empty. Do not climb a gantry without checking that the section you plan to use is stable.

**Scarps and fans.** Where a plate has failed, you can climb the stepped scarps with your hands, but the edges can break under your weight and the shards at the bottom are sharp. Test each hold, keep three points of contact, and do not stand on the lip of a scarp in the wet.

**Overhead pipes and drips.** The drip lines feed basins that overflow into troughs and then channels. Do not block, cap, or redirect a pipe. The whole ecosystem depends on the drip continuing, and changing its path could cause a flood somewhere you cannot see. Do not open valves or restart pumps. Machines here settle when their water stops, and restarting them may do things nobody has tested.

**The hum.** A deep, long note sometimes rises from beneath the plates. It is harmless as far as anyone has observed, but it carries through your feet and can make you feel unsteady. If you are camping on a plate, do not place your sleeping mat over a visible seam where the hum seems strongest.

**The things under the plates.** You will hear knocking, scraping, and a slow clicking from the cracks and cisterns. Do not put your hands or tools into any gap, however dark or inviting. Do not drop anything into a crack you cannot see the bottom of.

## Fauna

Most of the wildlife is patient and not interested in you, but some of it needs room.

- **Shorebirds** wade the trays and probe the sludge. They are harmless and should be left alone, especially at their nests in the gantry girders and fin hollows. Do not feed them.
- **Grazers** are six-legged, shelled, and move in grey herds along the troughs, leaving clean trails through the crust. Step around their trails rather than across them. They will flow around a standing person without fuss.
- **The large pale climber** is the one to watch for. It climbs the lattice towers at dusk and sleeps wrapped around the girders, its skin patterned with the same green as the film, so it is nearly invisible. Before you climb, grab, or rest against any tower, look up and along the girders. Keep your hands off anything that looks like a fold of crust. Give a sleeping one a wide berth and do not wake it.

## Navigation

This is the hardest part of the place to prepare for, and the one most likely to hurt you.

Sight alone is not reliable here. The land curves in a way the eye does not expect, so the horizon always looks slightly too close. Stairs may climb without the landing rising. Pipes may run downhill and empty into basins above them. A row of hangars may recede forever, and when you walk to the end you may find yourself back at the first door.

Some practical steps:

- **Travel in pairs at minimum.** A partner can confirm whether you are where you think you are, and can call out when you start to drift.
- **Keep sight of each other at all times.** The mist swallows distance and shouting carries poorly, so agree on signals in advance: a whistle, a light, or a flag. Do not rely on voices.
- **Keep a written log.** Note landmarks, counts of towers or gantries between points, and the time. Refer to the log when your memory says something different.
- **Use a physical tether** on the ground, such as a cord or a line of reflective markers, between you and your starting point. Expect the markers to move or be reused by the plateau's own geometry. Check them regularly.
- **Know the rule of the stairs.** If a climb continues longer than the landing count suggests, or you reach a landing no higher than the ground beneath it, stop and go back down. Do not try to push through.
- **Accept that you will not reach the far edge.** The plateau keeps extending. Pick a turnaround time in advance and keep to it, whatever you see ahead.

You may notice that the structures nearest you are slightly newer than those farther away, as if the builders worked outward from somewhere now behind you. This is consistent with the rest of the place. It is useful for orientation, but do not treat it as a reliable compass.

## Dusk and night

The cold arrives fast after sunset, and the light changes from white haze to saturated colour in a few minutes. Plan to be off the open plates before dusk, or to have a fixed camp ready with dry layers. Check your fingers and toes often. Shivering, slurred speech, or clumsiness in the cold means warm up at once. Hypothermia is the most likely serious injury here.

Camp on high, stable ground well away from drip lines, overflow troughs, and gullies. Do not sleep in the lee of a condenser tower if you have not checked for the pale climber. Keep your gear sealed, because the damp gets into everything.

## Leaving

Decide how you will leave before you arrive, and do not rely on the grate where the channels vanish. You can know the way in and still lose the way out. Set a return anchor, whether that means a fixed point you can reach from home, a pre-agreed means of extraction, or a companion who will wait for you at a known spot, and check it before you commit to walking.

If you feel the land has shifted under you, or you cannot account for the last hour, stop moving. Sit down near something solid, check your log, and wait for your partner to reach you. Patience is the right response here. Nothing on the plateau is in a hurry, and neither should you be.
