# Preparing to Visit the Drowned Terraces

Nothing about this place is hostile in a way that will chase you down, but it is unforgiving of carelessness, and it rewards a traveler who moves slowly, touches lightly, and asks for very little. Most of the preparation is about your body in cold, dark water, and most of the rest is about not disturbing a place that has been settling for a very long time.

## Breathing and Body

You will be breathing water or breathing from something you carry. Choose your method before you arrive, not after. A closed rebreather with a sealed mask is the most dependable option, since it keeps your air separate from the water and doesn't require you to trust an unfamiliar medium. If your home reality has no suitable equipment, go through a traveler who does, or spend time learning a method that suits your body. Test it in shallow, warm water first. Anything that fails here fails in a place with no surface to swim to.

The cold is the main physical threat. This water never warms, and it will pull heat from you steadily, so plan for it as you would for polar diving. Wear thick insulating layers under a sealed suit, and bring a heated core or a reserve of warmth you can access without fumbling. Cold numbs the hands first. Your fingers will lose dexterity well before you feel dangerously cold, so test your grip on a tether or a knot before you go deep, and carry a spare set of gloves inside your suit.

Expect to lose your sense of time. There is no day or night, and your body will not tell you how long you have been down. Bring a sealed timepiece you can read by touch or by a dim glow you can switch on briefly, and set a firm turnaround time before you enter.

## Light and Sight

There is no light here, and your own light will be the loudest thing in the basin. A lamp will throw hard shadows across the stone and make the mats look violently colored against the dark, which is not how the place is meant to be seen. Carry a light, but keep it off most of the time. Give your eyes at least an hour of darkness before you enter, and let them settle. You will then make out the pale edges of stone and the outlines of growth against deeper black, and that faint shape-sense is more useful than any beam. Use a light only to check a joint, read a worn mark, or find your tether, and cover it with your hand when you can.

## Movement and Buoyancy

The terraces are long, stepped, and smooth, and the mats cover almost everything. Set your buoyancy close to neutral before you touch anything. A traveler who drifts down onto the growth will crush filaments that have been holding the sediment in place for centuries. Move with deliberate, slow strokes and keep your hands and body above the mats where possible.

The lower tiers are silted with a fine grey mud that stirs into a cloud and hangs for a long time. Once that cloud rises, you will not see your hands, your tether, or the stone. Stay on the higher, firmer courses until you know your route, and never kick off the bottom. If you disturb a gas pocket, wait for the bubbles to clear before moving, and do not try to outswim a silt cloud.

Keep well clear of the sluices and weirs. Some gates are sealed, some are half open, and the water behind them may still be under pressure from ages ago. A gate that moves, even slightly, can release a sudden current that tumbles you into the frame or sweeps up the sediment. Do not touch the pivots or the stacked slabs, and do not try to open or close anything, however tempting the mechanism looks.

## Navigation and Tethers

The basin is wider than any view, and in total darkness you will lose direction within minutes unless you use a physical reference. Bring a tether, preferably a non-metal line with a reel that you can pay out and reel back, and fix its end at your entry point. Check it often. The kelp-ribbons and root-runners will snag it, so tie it off at intervals and move through gaps rather than pulling through the growth. Carry a cutting tool, but use it only to free yourself. Cutting the plants unnecessarily damages the mats that hold the place together.

The channels follow a plan, and once you understand a few of its patterns you can read the basin. Straight runs lead between tiers, bends mark junctions, and sluice frames mark the points where channels cross. Learn two or three of these before you go, and count your turns. Touch is your main map, so memorize the feel of the joints and the way the dowel-heads sit in the faces.

## Sound and Signals

Sound travels a long way in this water, and it carries very little. Your own voice will be muffled and strange, and most equipment noises will reach far beyond where you expect. Settle your gear before you move, and keep your speech short. Hand signals are your best option close to a partner. Tapping on stone carries farther than speech, though it is also a noise the place is not used to, so tap only when you need to be heard.

You will hear the basin's own sounds: the long groan of a stone settling, the whisper of grit sliding down a slope, the hiss of gas, the soft brush of kelp against stone. These are normal. A settling block can be startling in the dark, so do not flinch into the growth or the sediment when you hear one.

## Hazards in the Growth

The plants are not predators. They do not hunt or graze, and they will not reach for you. The fungal lobes and cushion colonies can, however, release fine spores or irritants when broken, so keep gloves on whenever you handle them, and do not press your face or skin into their folds. Do not eat anything you find, and do not let a mouthful of water carry plant matter into your breathing system. The filaments that bind the sediment will also cling to loose equipment, so keep straps and cords tucked in.

## What Not to Do

The builders are gone, and nothing in the basin has been repaired in ages. That is the state the place has been moving toward, and your presence should be as light on it as you can make it. Do not remove stones, dowels, clay, or any other fragment, however old or valuable it seems. The fossilized dowels in particular may tempt collectors, but a pulled joint or a moved block changes how the structure settles, and that change is permanent. Do not repair, reinforce, or re-seat anything, even a slab you can see is leaning badly. Do not leave anything behind either. Metal and synthetic debris will not break down the way the local materials do, and it will stand out in a place that has only ever been built from stone, wood, clay, and cord. If you bring metal fasteners or fittings, keep them on your person and bring them back out.

## Preparing for the Return

There is no surface to rise to. The basin does not end at a shoreline you can swim toward, and in total darkness the only reliable way back is the way you came. Mark your entry point before you descend, and establish a return anchor you can reach by touch, such as a particular slab, a sluice frame, or a fixed line. Fix the tether to it, and rehearse the route back in your head before you move away from it. Know your turnaround time, and honor it even if you feel you have only just arrived. Time moves differently here, and the water will not warn you that you have spent too long.

## Mind and Expectation

The Drowned Terraces will feel very large and very quiet. The water above you goes on without end, the terraces step down into a dark you cannot measure, and the silence is deep enough to make you hear your own pulse. Many travelers find the sense of scale calming and others find it oppressive. Either response is normal. Go in expecting stillness, not drama. Nothing will rush at you or change suddenly, except the occasional settling stone or the cloud of silt you have stirred yourself. The place is not waiting for you. It is simply continuing, slowly, toward the state it has been moving into for longer than anyone can count, and your job is to pass through that long process without adding to it.
