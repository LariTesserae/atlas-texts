# Preparing to Visit the Sump at Haldane Cut

The Sump is a young place, still falling into its shape. Its basin opened eleven years ago and is still settling, so the rules here change slowly and sometimes without warning. Prepare for the town as it is now, and expect it to differ next season.

## Think of the Town as a Bowl

Everything in the Sump collects at the bottom: heat, damp, dust, runoff, and the bloom. The upper terraces get sun and air, and the lower district gets the sediment. Plan your days around that. Work and rest on the higher terraces when you can, go down only for specific business, and climb back up before the afternoon heat sets in. When you're tired or overheated, go up. Almost every mistake a visitor makes comes from staying low too long.

## Timing Your Visit

**Check the storm calendar before anything else.** Ask at the radio shack or any lower-district ward for the current forecast. The storms form over the high plateau and drop their rain into the catchment within minutes, and the runoff can reach the gullies before you hear it coming. Treat every forecast as serious. Plan to be above the gullies and out of the culverts before any warning, and don't walk a gully bed to save time, even when it looks dry.

**Arrive between storms, not during the first hour after one.** The streets dry within an hour, but the walls stay wet, the rockfall risk peaks, and crews are still clearing debris. The first hours after a storm are the busiest and least safe.

**Avoid the late afternoon near the clay faces.** The sweat seeps from the walls then, and the rock is still loosened from the day's heat. Plan your walking for mornings and early evenings.

## Clothing and Gear

**Footwear** is the most important choice. Wet clay is slick, scab moss makes some surfaces treacherous, and the cut edges of the strata are sharp enough to slice rubber. Bring boots with aggressive, deep-cleated soles and ankle support, and wear them broken in, since you'll be on uneven stone for hours.

**Clothing** should be light, loose, and long-sleeved. The heat is thick and damp, so breathable fabric matters, but long sleeves keep the sweet distillation vapour off your skin and protect your arms from railings and wall dust. Quick-drying materials are better than cotton, which stays clammy for the whole visit.

**Eye protection** is worth carrying. The sun clears the rim for only a few hours but is sharp when it does, and the haze gives it a sickly sheen. Tinted goggles also help with the lamp-heavy lower district, where light is constant but dim.

**Gloves** should be thick enough to handle railings, which can be too hot to touch in the afternoon, and tough enough to keep crystalline sweat and bloom off your hands. Carry a second pair. They will get wet.

**Respiratory protection** matters more than most visitors expect. The distillation columns exhale an acrid vapour that settles on laundry and skin, and clay dust from rockfalls drifts across the lower terraces. A filter mask with acid-gas and particulate cartridges is best. Carry spares.

## Water, Food, and the Heat

Drink more than you think you need, and do it on a schedule rather than when you feel thirsty. The water here carries clay, iron, and salt, so don't drink from cisterns, channels, or open runoff without filtering it. Use the municipal supply, which comes through the filtration towers, or bring your own purification. Boil or treat everything, including water you use for washing your face.

Heat and sweat cost you salt, so bring electrolyte tablets and eat salty food. Pack light, dry, high-energy food, since the damp will spoil anything perishable within a day.

Watch for heat exhaustion. The air is thick enough that you may not feel how much you're sweating. Signs include headache, nausea, dizziness, and a flushed face that stops sweating. If you notice them, go up to the cooler terraces, sit in shade, and drink slowly.

## Light and Time

The sun clears the rim for only a few hours a day, and the lower streets are dim and brown for most of it. Lamps burn in the lower district from dawn to dusk, and the light tide passes through the town on a schedule. Don't rely on sunlight to tell you the time. Bring a watch that keeps reliable time without daylight, and keep a written log of your hours. Your body will lose track of day and night faster than you expect, so set a sleep routine and stick to it.

## Sound and Silence

The Sump is almost entirely silent of animal life. No insects, no birds, no rodents. The first visitors find this unsettling, and it's worth preparing for emotionally. The quiet is not restful. What you hear is mechanical and water-borne: valves knocking, condensation dripping in a steady percussion through the whole town, and steam hissing from vents in the lower streets. A dropped object rings off the walls for several seconds, so you may be startled by small sounds.

Earplugs help with sleep, because the drip and the valves continue all night. Carry a small notebook for the things you notice. Many visitors find the silence easier to tolerate with something to do with their hands.

## The Bloom

The crystalline mineral the workers call bloom grows on the cistern walls as thin white filaments. It reaches outward overnight and dissolves in water.

Don't touch it. Keep your hands and clothing away from the cistern walls, and never wet the filaments, since water dissolves them and you may not know what the dissolved material does. Pay attention to the ceilings of abandoned cisterns, because the bloom can reach them when the factories are idle. If you find a filament on your sleeve or boot, don't brush it off with a wet cloth. Dry it, then remove it with a dry brush and seal the brush in a container.

## The Sweat

Certain clay walls, especially the faces above the older workshops, exude a pale, warm moisture in the late afternoon. It crystallises into a fine white skin that flakes away when touched. The residents collect the sweat and use its water as medicine, though nobody has explained why it works.

Don't touch the walls in late afternoon, and don't collect sweat yourself unless a resident shows you how. The water is medicinal to them, but to a visitor it's an unknown compound. If you're offered sweat-water for medicine, ask questions first and take only what a local healer recommends. The sweat is heaviest after a storm, as if the rock were breathing out the rain.

## Scab Moss, Reeds, and Saltbush

Scab moss grows on drains and pump housings. It's slick, spreads a few centimetres a season, and gets scraped off every morning. Don't step on it without checking your footing, and don't touch it bare-handed. The reeds along the terrace seams are brittle and snap under hot wind, so don't lean on them for balance. The potted saltbush on the balconies is pruned and cared for. Admire it, but don't handle it unless someone invites you to.

## Rockfalls and Dust

Rockfalls happen when the clay soaks through and loosens. Each one sends a fan of pale dust across the lower terraces that clogs drains and pumps. Stay away from the faces after rain, and keep a few metres between yourself and any cut edge. If you see dust rising, move uphill and cover your mouth and nose. Don't run toward a fall to help; the crews know how to clear debris, and they will tell you where to stand.

## The Factories

The distillation columns, presses, and refineries line the lower terraces. They run in shifts and stop for the afternoon heat, so check the schedule before visiting. The vapour settles on skin and clothing, so wash thoroughly when you leave. Don't enter a working facility unless you've been invited and given a briefing, and follow the posted safety rules.

## Working With Residents

The town's life depends on reading the water, the walls, and the light. Locals will know when a flood is coming long before any warning. Listen to them, especially the crews. If someone tells you to move, move. If you're asked to stay clear of a gully or a wall, do it without argument.

Don't open sluices, drain tanks, or pull debris from gullies unless you're part of a crew. These are coordinated jobs, and an untrained hand can cause a flood or an injury. Learn the storm signals, and ask residents what each one means.

## Your Anchor and Your Return

This is the part most specific to multiverse travel. The Sump is still settling, which means the geometry you remember may not match the geometry you find.

**Choose your return anchor on the upper terraces,** where the ground is stable and the rock has had longer to settle. Avoid anchoring near the clay faces, the gullies, or the cistern tanks. A return point at the bottom of the basin may be buried, flooded, or moved by the next rockfall.

**Re-verify your anchor on every visit.** Rim-relative coordinates drift as the basin settles. Check your position against the upper terraces before each departure, and do it at least once per day if you're staying more than a few days.

**Protect your equipment from the environment.** Humidity, condensation, salt, and the factory vapour will corrode metal and short electronics. Seal instruments in airtight cases with desiccant packs, and check seals every morning. Expect the condensation to find any gap.

**Bring a dry, sealed container for samples.** The bloom dissolves in water and the sweat crystallises, so anything you collect may change within hours. Label everything with time and location, and seal it immediately.

**Plan for a reduced exit window.** Storms can close off the lower routes in minutes. Know two ways up to the rim or to the upper terraces, and know which of them runs clear of gullies. If a storm forms over the plateau, leave by the upper route at once.

## What to Expect Emotionally

The Sump is not a dying place, and it isn't growing either. It's young, with fresh edges and new wounds opening every season. Expect to feel a constant low tension: the heat, the drip, the sudden ringing sounds, the absence of animals, and the knowledge that the ground is still moving. Many visitors find it oppressive for the first day and fascinating by the third. Give yourself time to adjust, and go up to the light when you need to.

Prepare as if the town will test your attention, because it will. Read the water, watch the walls, keep track of the light, and listen to the people who live there. They have learned to survive a place that is still becoming itself, and that is the best guide you'll find.
