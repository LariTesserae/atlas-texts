# Preparing to Visit the Ember Shelves

Most travelers who go to the Ember Shelves are fine. Most of the ones who get hurt are hurt by overconfidence in something that seemed stable, like a path, a landmark, or a sheltered hollow. Prepare for a place that is always rearranging itself, and for a body that will be working harder than it expects.

## Before You Go: The Body

**The air is the first problem.** The upper terraces reach roughly ten kilometers. At that height each breath gives you much less oxygen than you are used to, and the effects come on slowly. You may feel fine while your judgment is already slipping: slow decisions, clumsy hands, a odd cheerfulness, or a sense that the cracks are not that dangerous. Treat any of these as a warning. Bring supplemental oxygen for every upper-shelf excursion, with more cylinder capacity than you think you need, and plan to use it earlier than you would like to. Carry a pulse oximeter if you can. If you cannot get an accurate reading, use the rule that you turn back when you feel mentally off, not only when you feel physically short of breath.

**Acclimatize in stages.** Spend time on the lower plateaus before climbing the terraces. Each stage should be a few days, and each should end with a rest day. People who try to rush this are usually the ones who have to be carried down.

**The sulfur haze is the second problem.** The air carries fine ash and mineral vapor that tastes of metal and burns the back of the throat. Your breathing apparatus needs to handle both thin oxygen and acid-gas or sulfur filtration. These are not always the same equipment, so check that your mask can do both. Use sealed goggles, not glasses. The ash gets into everything, and a gritty eye at ten kilometers is a serious problem.

**Heat comes up through the ground.** It is not hot enough to burn quickly, but it is constant, and it will wear down your feet and your patience. Wear insulated boots with thick, stiff soles that protect against both heat and sharp edges. Carry a sleeping pad with an insulating layer under it, and never lay your bedding directly on the rock. Expect your feet to swell, and bring a spare pair of socks and an alternative pair of boots if your weight allows.

## Footing and Movement

**The basalt is a hazard in two different ways.** Fresh fractures are sharp enough to cut through standard gloves and leave ragged gashes in your shins. Older, worn surfaces are smoother and safer, but they ring when you step on them, and that sound carries. Wear cut-resistant gloves, long gaiters, and ankle support. Watch each step. Looking up at the terraces is tempting, but most injuries happen to people who were looking at the view.

**The grey dust takes footprints easily.** That is useful for seeing where you have been and frustrating for anyone following you. It also means your tracks may be gone by morning, along with the path you planned. Do not count on retracing your steps tomorrow.

**Plates are the most dangerous ground.** The polygonal plates are a few meters across, and their edges glow faintly red after dark. Do not step on plate edges, and do not step onto a plate whose surface is moving. Learn to recognize the faint grinding and tilting that comes before a plate shifts. The gaps between plates breathe out vapor, so keep your distance from any gap that is steaming more heavily than usual. Salt crusts around the gaps can be caustic and will sting bare skin.

## Navigation

**Do not trust landmarks to stay put.** A cairn you marked may be tipped over, moved, or gone within a day. A ridge you used as a reference point may sink. Use landmarks as rough guides only, and check them against at least one independent reference.

**Bring multiple navigation methods and expect them to disagree.** Carry a paper map. Expect it to be outdated within days, but it still shows you the general shape of the terraces. Carry a compass and test it before each outing, because the iron-rich minerals may affect it. If you use satellite positioning, check it against your compass and your map, and keep a log of your bearings and times so you can reconstruct your route by hand. Do not trust any single system.

**Mark your route with materials that can be found again.** Flagging tape gets swallowed by new fissures. Wooden stakes are rare here and easy to lose. Consider a line of brightly colored markers placed at known intervals, and plan to check that they are still there each time you return. If you cannot find a marker, assume the route has changed and stop to reassess before moving on.

**Sunlight is unreliable.** The sun is a dim coin behind the haze, and its position is hard to read. Shadows lie on the ground rather than falling from objects, which makes them useless for judging direction. Stars can be seen on clear nights, but the sky does not hold still enough for them to be reliable references, and the pale band across the zenith may be a cloud of debris moving over the course of a night.

## Light and Sight

**The day is dim and brown, and it deceives the eye.** Distances are hard to judge. Shadows are soft and seem to hover. Use the sealed goggles with a tint that increases contrast, and keep a headlamp with both white and red settings. Red light is better for keeping your night vision and for seeing the glow of plate edges without washing them out.

**Dusk is treacherous.** The haze turns violet at the edges of the day, and then the dark arrives from the east and settles into the hollows first. Plan to be out of the hollows and on higher, older ground well before this. A hollow that is comfortable at noon can fill with vapor and cold air by early evening.

**Glass reflects and refracts.** The chemical gardens and the fused glass arches catch what little light there is and throw it around. This can make a nearby tower look farther away than it is, or make a collapsing one look less dangerous. Keep your sight line clear and assume the nearest tower is closer than it looks.

## The Chemical Gardens

The towers and branches grow from vents in a few hours and collapse without warning. Keep a distance from them at least equal to their height plus a safety margin, and more if the vapor is moving strongly through them. A collapse sends glassy shards in all directions, and the shards are sharp enough to cut through clothing. Wear eye protection near any active vent, and do not stand in a line between a tower and a tall nearby one that might fall onto it.

Do not take shards from the vents. They are pulled back in, and the gardens regrow in new spots, so removing material from one place does not solve anything and may increase the risk to you and to others who come later.

## The Dark Film

The thin mineral film on the older shelves gives when you press it and springs back. It is not known whether it is alive, or whether it is a slow chemical process that never finishes. Do not touch it with bare skin. If you need a sample, use a tool and seal the sample in a container. Do not leave any of it on your gear or clothing, and wash thoroughly after handling anything that has been near it.

## The Marks of Builders

The arches, cairns, slabs, and doorway may have been made, or may be the product of heat and wind working on stone. Either way, treat them as places you might still disturb.

**Photograph and record before you touch.** Note their position, their orientation, and their bearing. The arches face the lower shelves. The cairns line up along the ridges. Write this down. If you return and find them moved, that is useful information.

**Do not rearrange, climb, or disturb them.** The grooved slabs and the carved lintel may have meaning, or they may be stress marks. You do not need to know which to avoid causing damage. Moving a cairn may also change the local ground behavior in ways you cannot predict.

**Be careful about the doorway.** It stands alone, with nothing attached to it, and the ground is cracked into fresh plates on every side. Its lintel carries marks that might be letters or cracks. Do not pass through it. You do not know where it goes, whether it goes anywhere, or whether it is connected to the ground that keeps shifting around it. If you feel the urge to test it, that urge is the thing to be most careful about. Only approach it with a clear plan, a partner who will stay outside, and a predetermined time after which you will leave regardless.

**Do not read patterns into them too eagerly.** It is natural to look for meaning in the alignment of the cairns and the direction of the arches. Some of the pattern may be real. Much of it may be the human tendency to find purpose in things that have been shaped by heat, wind, and time. Keep a record of what you observe and separate it from what you conclude.

## Sound

The place is mostly quiet, but the silence is never complete. Plates groan, towers crack, glass ticks as it cools, and vapor hisses from new vents. Many of these sounds come from far away, and you cannot tell from the sound alone whether something is a few hundred meters or several kilometers off.

Develop the habit of stopping to listen before you move. If a cluster of sounds starts, pause and wait for it to finish before you continue. Sound is your early warning system here, and a traveler who ignores it because the quiet seems safe has missed one of the most useful tools available.

## Camp and Shelter

**Choose your ground carefully.** Older, worn basalt is generally more stable than fresh fractured stone, and it rings when stepped on, which means you will hear anything approaching. Avoid hollows, which fill with vapor and cold air. Avoid the edges of plates, the margins of fresh vents, and any ridge that shows signs of sinking or cracking.

**Plan for the ground to change while you sleep.** Crust forms overnight and breaks by morning. A plate can shift under a tent in a few hours. Sleep on a pad with insulation, keep your boots and gear packed and ready to grab, and set up your shelter so that you can leave quickly in any direction.

**Keep a clear exit.** Before you settle in, identify at least two ways out of your campsite, and check them each time you return. If you have to abandon your shelter, you should know where you are going without needing to think about it.

## Medical and Emergency

Carry a medical kit that handles burns from hot rock and vapor, eye irrigation for ash and salts, cuts from basalt and glass, and respiratory trouble from sulfur. Include treatment for hypoxia and altitude sickness, and know the signs of both. Bring a satellite communication device with a check-in schedule that someone outside the shelves is responsible for enforcing. If you miss a check-in, they should initiate a search after a set time without waiting for further word.

Carry more water and more food than you would normally think necessary. Thin, dry air pulls water out of your body quickly, and the physical work of moving over basalt and dust is harder than it looks.

## Mind and Company

Go with at least one other person. Do not go alone. Solo travelers here tend to lose their sense of time and scale, and they are the ones who most often follow an interesting shape over a ridge and never come back the way they came.

Keep a journal with times, bearings, observations, and anything that has changed since your last visit. Read it at the start of each day. It will tell you what the land has been doing and how your own judgment has been holding up.

Expect the place to feel unsettling. Its marks look intentional, and its silence seems to be listening. These feelings are normal. They are also not reliable evidence of anything except that you are in a strange place. Stay grounded in your observations, not in the impression that something is watching or waiting. Nothing here is final, including your plans, so build in margins, leave room to turn back, and treat every decision to go deeper as one you can revisit tomorrow.
