# Preparing to Visit the Sunken Hall at Low Glare

## What kind of place this is

The hall is a working machine that has been left running. Its builders are gone, but the inflow, the wheel, the outflow, and the sluices still keep the water level steady. Think of yourself as a visitor to a mill or a shipyard during a shift change, not a tourist at a ruin. Nothing here is decaying. Everything is new, sharp, and in use, so most of your preparation is about not being hurt by the machinery and not disturbing the process.

## Before you go: the air question

The description says the hall is full of water, but it also describes light entering the water through the crown opening and breaking into bars across the surfaces below. That suggests the water surface may sit below the oculus, possibly with an air gap under the vault, or possibly with the opening sealed by a surface film or lens. You cannot be sure, and the difference matters a great deal.

Plan for the worst case: **assume you will be fully submerged and must breathe through your own apparatus.** Bring a closed-circuit or long-duration rebreather if you have access to one, or at minimum a sealed breathing system with a reserve of at least two hours. If you find an air pocket under the vault, you can switch to open-air breathing. Do not count on it.

The warm water is pleasant and will not chill you, so you do not need thermal protection beyond light underlayers. The stone holds heat from the sun and gives it back, so the hall stays comfortably warm even if you stay for hours.

## Equipment

**Breathing and sealing.** As above. Check every seal before entry. The inflow roar is loud enough to mask a slow leak in your ears, so test your pressure and hearing before you go in, not after.

**Eye protection.** The light is close to unfiltered noon, and the water magnifies it through caustic focusing. Bring dark-tinted or polarized goggles. Your eyes will adjust to the bright zones, but the glare off the wheeling caustic lattice can cause a headache or temporary dazzle within the first hour. Carry a spare pair in case the first is fogged or cracked.

**Skin protection.** Sun intensity under magnified caustics can cause burns on exposed skin even in warm water. Wear a close-fitting, light-colored suit with long sleeves and gloves. Avoid bare hands on the stone during the brightest part of the day, since the blocks heat quickly.

**Lighting.** The hall is bright in the middle and dark at the vault edges. Bring a waterproof handlight with a low-output setting for the shadows, and a second light you can clip to yourself. The deep green and black zones swallow sound and light alike, so a lamp that points where you're looking will help you avoid stepping into a gap or onto an unfixed tile.

**Tethers and marking.** Bring a tether line long enough to reach from a fixed point to any part of the hall you intend to visit, and tie it to an anchor you can release quickly. Carry a small waterproof slate or writing tablet with a grease pencil. Sound will not carry reliably through the roar, so pre-agree on hand signals and write down any key instructions before entry.

**Gloves and tools.** Bring thick gloves for handling stone with sharp chisel facets, and a short folding blade for cutting yourself free of snagged cord or line. Do not bring tools you intend to use on the structure. Anything you drop may go into the wheel or the outflow.

**Sampling kit.** If you want to record things, bring a sealed case for notes and a waterproof camera or sketch paper. Do not bring containers for specimens. The filament mat and the rooted stalks are part of the system, and removing them could change the water chemistry or the growth pattern.

## Hazards by zone

**The inflow channel (east wall).** The stream is steady and strong. Near the channel mouth, the current will push you west and may pin you against the wall. Stay well clear of the opening and do not try to brace yourself against the flow with your hands. Move diagonally across it, never straight into it.

**The far wall fan.** Where the inflow strikes the west wall, the small waves slap back and create a churning zone with reverse eddies. Expect your balance to fail here. Keep your tether taut and move slowly.

**The outflow chamber and paddle wheel.** This is the most dangerous area in the hall. The wheel turns on its axle with a knock you can feel through the water, and the wooden pawls catch and release in a rhythm that can grab a limb or a line. The bailing buckets rise and fall on a chain. Never place your hands or any part of your body near the wheel, the chain, the pawls, or the buckets. Do not enter the outflow chamber at all unless you have been briefed by someone who has worked the machine. The sluice pressure plate, worn smooth at one spot, is the adjustment point for the outflow. Do not stand on it, touch it, or lean on the sluice housing.

**The tiled floor.** The square tiles are scored with maker's notches and may be slick with algae in places. The seams between tiles hold rooted stalks and occasional bubbling vents. Some tiles may not be fully seated. Test each step before committing your weight. Be especially careful near the pressure plate and any loose blocks.

**The half-dressed blocks.** Some stone faces are rough and have unfilled wedge-holes and cord-marks. These are sharp and snag gloves and lines. Do not move these blocks. Their placement may be part of a sequence the builders had planned, and loosening one could bring down a course.

**The spare tiles on the ledge.** Their glazed faces are cloudy with algae and may be slippery. Do not handle them unless you need to reach something behind them.

**The bubbling seams.** Gas rises from seams in the floor. You do not know what it is. Keep your face away from the vents and do not bring any flame or spark device into the hall. Note where the bubbles are strongest and avoid those spots.

**The dust.** Pale stone dust drifts through the light and resettles with every inflow surge. It will cloud your goggles and irritate your eyes. If you have a surface breathing source, make sure your intake is clear of dust and carries no suspended particles into your lungs.

**The walls and vault.** The walls are dressed stone in courses, and the vault is a ring of fitted wedge-stones. Do not climb the walls or pull on the vault stones. The wedges hold the ring together, and the crown opening is the only light source. Do not attempt to reach the oculus.

## The golden filament mat

The mat near the oculus is the most visually striking living thing in the hall. It pulses slowly and follows the sun's track, so the bright patch on the wall moves across the day. Its growth depends on how long it has been exposed to light.

Do not touch it. The filaments are fine and may be sensitive to contact or chemical change from skin oils. They may also be part of the water's oxygen or nutrient cycle, and disturbing them could alter the balance the machine depends on. Observe from a distance of at least one arm's length. If you want to record its growth, sketch its position against the sun's track and return at a different hour to compare.

## Living things and the rooted stalks

The only moving things are the water, the dust, the wheel, and the mat. There are no fish, insects, or crawling creatures, so you do not need to worry about bites or stings. The rooted stalks in the tile seams are sparse, bending in the current and then straightening. Do not pull them up. They are anchored in the seams, and their roots may hold the tiles or help with drainage. Step around them rather than over them when possible.

The green-brown film on the lower courses is algae. It is slippery and will stain gloves and suits. Avoid pressing into it.

## Behavior and the rules of the place

This hall was built by people who understood water as a tool. Its rules are simple: water flows in, water flows out, light comes from above, and the stone is shaped by hand. Treat those rules as the terms of your visit.

Do not adjust the sluices, the wheel, the chain, or the inflow. Do not finish, repair, or re-dress any block, even if it looks unfinished. The half-dressed stone is a work in progress and may not be yours to complete. Do not remove the template leaning against the wall; its arc matches the vault, and it may be needed for the next course.

Read the notches in the tiles if you want to understand the building sequence, but do not change them. Record what you see, leave what you find, and move on.

Expect the hall to feel busy. It is a workshop that has been left running, so do not stand in the path of the wheel or the buckets, and do not block the inflow channel with your body or equipment.

## Navigation and exit

The hall is small, but the roar, the glare, and the dark edges will disorient you quickly. Before you move away from your entry point, fix a landmark that you can see from anywhere in the hall. The oculus is the best one, since the light column is always visible from within the bright zone. In the dark edges, follow your tether back toward the light. Do not rely on sound to locate the inflow or the outflow; the roar comes from everywhere at once.

Plan your exit before you enter. The description does not name a door, so you must determine your route in advance. If there is no obvious exit, you may have to return the way you came. Confirm this with whoever sent you, and mark the route on your slate before you go in. Keep your tether attached to the entry anchor at all times.

## Timing

The light changes across the day. The bright patch moves with the sun, the mat extends or withdraws, and the caustic patterns shift. The hall is most intense near midday, when the light is closest to unfiltered noon and the stone is hottest. If you want a gentler visit, arrive in the early morning or late afternoon, when the light is weaker and the caustics are less dazzling.

Stay no longer than your breathing supply and your skin protection allow. The heat builds slowly in the stone, and it is easy to lose track of time when the light keeps moving.

## Psychological preparation

The hall is calm in a strange way. The warmth, the glow, and the steady sound are soothing, but the machinery never stops and the light never pauses. Visitors often feel watched by the machine, as if it is waiting for them to do something. That feeling is not a threat. It is the sense of being in a workplace that is still operating.

The darker edges can be unsettling. The water turns from green to near-black, and the inflow sounds farther away there, as though the room is larger than it looks. Keep your light on and your tether taut when you go there. If you feel disoriented, stop, face the light, and breathe slowly until your bearings return.

Be prepared for the sense that the builders have just left. The tools are in place, the materials are in process, and the notches suggest a sequence still underway. Resist the urge to finish their work. You are a visitor to a machine that is running without its makers, and your job is to understand it, not to take charge of it.

## Summary checklist

- Breathing apparatus rated for at least two hours, with a reserve.
- Tinted or polarized goggles, plus a spare pair.
- Light suit with long sleeves and thick gloves for the stone.
- Low-output handlight, a clip-on light, and a waterproof main lamp.
- Tether line and a secured anchor point.
- Waterproof slate, grease pencil, and pre-agreed hand signals.
- A folding blade for cutting free of snags.
- No flame, no spark, and no tools for the structure.
- Route to exit marked before entry.
- Visit during early or late light if you want a gentler experience.
- Stay clear of the wheel, chain, pawls, buckets, sluices, pressure plate, inflow mouth, and bubbling seams.
- Do not touch the filament mat, the rooted stalks, the half-dressed blocks, or the template.
