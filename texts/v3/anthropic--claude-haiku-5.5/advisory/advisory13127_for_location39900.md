# Preparing to Visit the Lowest Ward

## Before You Leave

**Know your exit.** The levee road is your last reliable dry ground. Note where it meets the paved road on the far side and fix that point in your mind before you descend. Once you're in the bowl, the streets look alike, the fig canopy hides landmarks, and the cooling towers are your only reliable compass. Pick a return route along the levee crest, not through the district, and walk it once in your head.

**Tell someone on the far side.** The pump operators live across the levee and keep a rough log of who goes down. Leave word with them, or with whoever is watching the crossing, of when you expect to come back. They are the only people who know this place well enough to notice when someone is overdue.

**Check your fitness honestly.** You will be walking on soft silt, over roots, through knee-deep standing water, and sometimes up a slope of wet concrete. The humidity is heavy enough that you will sweat through your clothes within minutes and stay damp all day. If you have heart or respiratory trouble, or a condition that is worsened by heat, this is a place to visit only with a companion who knows the route, and only briefly.

**Update your vaccinations** for tetanus and check that you're current on anything relevant to standing water and soil bacteria. Tell a physician you're going somewhere with heavy silt and stagnant water.

## What to Bring

**Footwear** is the most important choice. Waterproof boots with deep lugs and a firm sole, ideally with a steel or composite shank. The silt sucks at lighter shoes, and the old concrete has rebar and glass in it. Bring a second pair to change into.

**Clothing** should be lightweight, long-sleeved, and quick-drying. Synthetic fabrics outperform cotton here, which will hold water and chafe. Wear gloves: the vines are fibrous and rasp the skin, and the rust-beaded window frames and gutters have sharp edges. A wide-brim hat or a cap with a neck flap helps with both sun and the dripping canopy.

**Hearing protection** is not optional. The pumps produce a continuous low-frequency drumming, and the canal rush and freight trains on the trestle add to it. Foam earplugs or tight earmuffs will cut the worst of it. Without them, you'll have a headache by midday and may lose track of approaching sounds you need to hear, such as a pump cycling or a gantry moving.

**Water and food.** Bring more water than you think you need, sealed and clean, and do not drink anything from the bowl. Bring electrolyte packets. Pack food that won't spoil in the humidity.

**Light and signalling.** Carry a headlamp and a spare battery pack, since the canopy makes the district dim even at midday and the gantry's yellow blink is not enough to see by. A whistle is more useful than a shout, because voices don't carry over the machinery.

**Basic kit.** A first aid kit with antiseptic, bandages for cuts, antihistamine for insect and plant reactions, and a tick and leech removal tool. Waterproof bags for phones and papers. A simple map on paper, since moisture and the magnetic interference from the substation can make electronics unreliable.

**Do not bring** cuttings, seeds, or soil samples out of the bowl. The plants here have spent decades adapting to these specific conditions and could behave unpredictably elsewhere. Do not bring anything you can't leave behind if you need to move fast.

## What to Expect

The first thing you'll notice is the sound. The pumps come up through your boots before you hear them, and the canal and the wind in the fig canopy fill the rest. Plan to speak in short sentences and use gestures.

The second is the wet. Expect everything to drip. Your clothes, your pack, and your skin will stay damp. Plan breaks in shade, and rest on dry stone or the upper floors of buildings, not on the ground.

The third is the green. Plants grow over everything, and the paths you think are open may be roofed in vine. The reed beds stand chest-high and hide the sinkholes beneath them. Look at the ground before you step, and test footing with a stick or your boot before trusting your weight.

The fourth is the stillness of the animals. Herons and kingfishers are the only wildlife you'll likely see. The absence of nests, mammals, and large animals is not a sign that the place is safe. It is simply what this place is.

## Hazards

**Sinkholes.** These are the most dangerous feature of the bowl. Where a street has collapsed, the reed grass hides the drop, and the edges are undercut by roots and water. Never walk along the edge of a collapse, and don't stand on ground that has recently shifted. Keep a clear line back to solid ground.

**Structures.** Roots are cracking walls and undermining footings. Do not enter buildings whose floors sag, whose windows have fern hanging out of them, or whose outer walls show fresh cracks. Stay out of the substation vault entirely. The fig's roots have split the transformer housing, and the dead copper inside may still hold a charge.

**The gantry.** It still runs on its rail along the dead dock. Stay well clear of its path and its footings. It creaks forward about a meter every few minutes, but do not assume it will stop where you expect.

**Water.** Standing water in the low places may look shallow, but the silt underneath is soft and deep. Stagnant water carries bacteria, and the silt contains things you don't want in open cuts. Wash thoroughly after every contact and keep a close eye on any scrapes. Watch for leeches in the wetter sections.

**The pumps and discharge.** Stay away from the pump stations and their discharge pipes. The discharge can surge without warning when a cycle changes, and the canal weirs can knock a person off their feet. Do not climb on the pump housings or the weirs, and do not touch the engines, which will be hot and loud and are maintained by people who don't expect visitors.

**Freight trains.** When a train crosses the trestle, the windows hum, and you may feel the ground vibrate. Use the time to get inside or under cover if you can. Do not stand on the trestle approach.

**Heat and humidity.** Heat exhaustion comes on faster here than you'd expect, because sweat doesn't evaporate well in the moisture. Drink regularly, rest in shade, and watch your companions for signs of confusion or flushing.

## Conduct

The bowl belongs to its plants and machines, and you are a guest in an arrangement that has been working for a long time. Walk on existing paths where they exist. Don't cut vines or pull up plants. Don't carve or mark the buildings. Leave no litter, not even biodegradable scraps, since they feed the local ecology in ways you can't predict.

Don't interfere with the pumps or the gantry. Their schedules are kept by people you will never meet, and a stopped pump can flood a district in hours.

If you find someone else in the bowl, be courteous. Keep your distance. Anyone who has lived or worked here for long will know its dangers better than you do.

## On Turning the Pumps Off

Many visitors come with an opinion about whether the pumps should run, and some arrive intending to act on it. Don't. The pumps hold the water back, and the district's slow reassembly depends on that balance. Turning them off would not produce a forest; it would produce a lake, and the lake would reach the levee road within a season. Whatever you think of the place, the decision belongs to the people who live across the levee and bear its consequences. If you want to influence it, do that through them, not through the machinery.

## Time and Return

Expect the place to change slowly, and do not expect your visit to change it much. A root that cracked a wall last decade is still cracking it. A sinkhole will be a hand's breadth wider when you come back, if you come back. The silt will be a few millimeters higher. The canopy will have one more branch.

When you leave, walk back up the levee road without looking for a last view, and check your gear and your body on the crest before you descend the far side. Clean your boots and your skin before you sit down anywhere. Tell the people who logged your visit that you're out, and let your body dry out slowly over the next day, because the wet tends to stay with you longer than you expect.

The gantry will still be blinking when you go. It will creak forward a meter, stop, and carry nothing, the way it always has.
