# Preparing to Visit the Upland Lattice

## What to Expect

The Upland Lattice is not hostile in the ordinary sense. Nothing here is out to get you. But it is dense with systems that do not know you exist, and most of the danger comes from moving through them without noticing their rhythms. Plan for a cold, dry, loud, dim place where the most useful skill is patience. Rushing will get you hurt.

## Arrival and Anchoring

Arrive on a bare salt-stone terrace, not in the lattice. The open ground between frames is the safest place to set up, because the filaments can tangle anything that lands among them, and you want a clear sightline to the sky and the horizon.

Mark your arrival point before anything else. The distances here flatten and the landmarks look alike, so a cairn of salt-stone blocks, a bright strip of tape, or a reflective marker will be essential. Mark your return route at intervals of about fifty paces. In the amber light, you will otherwise lose your way within an hour.

Confirm your return anchor works before you go far, and check it again every few hours. The plateau is in a stable state, but stable is not the same as static. Don't assume the terrain you crossed in the morning is the terrain you'll recross at dusk.

## Cold, Air, and Breathing

The air is thin and cold, and the cold is dry. Acclimatize for at least a full day before any serious movement. Symptoms to watch for are breathlessness on stairs, headache, poor sleep, and clumsiness with fine tasks. Move slowly, rest often, and keep your breathing deliberate on uphill grades.

Dress in layers that cover everything. The hygroscopic hair on the lattice will cling to bare skin and fabric, and it irritates skin and eyes. Use long sleeves with tight cuffs, high collars, gloves you can seal at the wrist, and a hood or cap you can pull close. Treat any hair that gets on you as contamination and remove it outdoors, not in your shelter.

The fine salt dust in the open corridors is a respiratory hazard. Carry a filter mask and wear it in any dust corridor or whenever the wind is up. Wind here is frequent and brings dust with it.

## Water and Food

There is no running water on the plateau. The only pools are the mineral basins that fill with drip at dawn. The water is thin, cloudy, and rich with dissolved salts. Drinking it raw will dehydrate you faster and can upset your gut. Bring your own water and treat any basin water you intend to use, filtering out particulates and reducing salt content if you can. Do not drink from a basin that animals are drinking from without treating it first. Their tolerance is not your tolerance.

Bring enough food for the full trip plus a margin. Do not eat anything the lattice produces, including the filament tips the grazers crop. The plant tissue is structural and may be indigestible or toxic to you. Eat the grazers' tips only if you are certain they are not your tissue.

## Light and Vision

The sun is a dim copper coin behind permanent haze. Its light never sharpens into shadow, and distance and depth become unreliable. Estimate distances conservatively; a column that looks a few hundred meters away may be far more, and a stretch of lattice that looks thin may be dense enough to trap you.

Bring strong artificial lights, both white and red, and use them in the lattice. Carry a headlamp with a diffuse beam so you can see the filaments without throwing harsh glare. Bring a lightweight tripod or staff to test ground depth and vertical distance.

The lattice reflects light in green-gold sparks, and the flying fauna throw amber flashes. These can cause momentary visual confusion. Don't chase movement in your peripheral vision.

## Sound and the Pulse

The plateau is loud, and the loudness is part of the danger. The wind produces a continuous drone, and the posts emit a deep pulse you will feel in your chest. Between pulses there is a brief hush, and the whole plateau seems to hold its breath.

Wear hearing protection that still lets you hear voices and warning calls. Foam plugs with a low-profile earcup work well, or bring a directional headset. Set up a few signal calls with your companions, and make them distinct from the fauna's clicks and rasps. Agree on a visual signal too, because sound will be unreliable inside the lattice.

Learn the pulse rhythm before you move near a post. Time it by counting, and plan crossings for the quiet interval after a pulse, not during one. During a pulse, filaments near the post coil tight, and the grazers' attention is pulled toward the sound. That is exactly the window the predators use.

Pulses can also affect equipment. Keep electronics away from posts, and assume any radio, sensor, or screen may fail or misreport near a post. Carry mechanical backups: a compass, a paper map, a whistle, and a manual clock.

## The Lattice

Do not touch the filaments without reason. They are cable-thick, braided, and some are hollow, which means they knock against one another in the wind and can strike you. The hair on the filaments draws moisture and will cling to whatever it touches. Pulling or cutting filaments may harm you and will certainly harm the local balance.

Watch for coiling. Near posts, filaments will tighten during a pulse with enough force to trap a limb or tool. Do not stand within arm's reach of a coiling filament, and do not reach into the lattice to retrieve something that has fallen in.

Lattice-frames can span gaps and hang over paths. Duck and check overhead before entering any arch of growth, and never enter a frame you cannot see the far side of.

The lattice drips at night. Plan to be on dry ground, under cover, after dark. Keep your sleeping area out of the overhanging frames and clear of any spot where drip collects and runs.

## Structures and Causeways

The towers are grown and built, and both kinds of structure can fail. Stay out of heavily lattice-covered towers, where the growth may be holding up what is left of the masonry. Do not climb the ribbed or faceted surfaces. The apertures open and close with the sun, and they could trap or crush a limb.

The fused salt-glass causeways are cracked into hexagonal plates. Some plates may be loose. Step on the edges of plates, not their centers, and test each one before committing your weight. Keep to the causeway middle where the plates are most solid, and avoid the tall posts unless you are timing a pulse.

Mineral drip moves along the causeway channels, and in some places it flows uphill toward the towers at the pulse rhythm. Do not step into a channel. The flow can be strong, and the mineral content is high enough to irritate skin and damage gear.

## Fauna and Predators

The small segmented fauna are mostly harmless but numerous. Do not handle them; their chattering and clicking is communication, and the glassy-winged ones may carry something irritating. Keep food sealed, since they will investigate.

The armored grazers are slow but heavy, and herds move as a body. Do not get between a herd and its drinking basin, and do not approach a herd at dawn when they gather to drink. Keep at least thirty meters away, and never move directly through a herd.

The predators are the most serious hazard. They hunt in pairs along the causeways and move in step with the pulse rhythm, striking when the posts thrum and the grazers are distracted. Do not walk the causeways during or just after a pulse. If you see paired low-slung shapes on a causeway, retreat to bare ground at once and keep out of their line of approach. Do not run unless you are already committed; sudden movement draws them. Move steadily away and keep a structure or dense lattice between you and them.

## Navigation and the Convergence Point

Map the posts as you go. They form lines, and those lines converge on a point far off in the haze, where the lattice thickens into an opaque, dark green-grey mass. Light does not reach into it, and sound does not carry out of it. The fauna avoid it, and the drip does not flow toward it.

Do not go there. Nothing in the plateau's design is intended to keep you out, but the place is either very old or very new, and you have no way to judge which. Entering the opaque mass means losing your light, your sound, and probably your anchor. Treat its edge as a boundary and keep at least a kilometer from it. If you feel drawn toward it, stop and return to your anchor. Many visitors report a pull toward the mass that is hard to resist; take it as a warning.

Use the convergence point as your fixed reference for orientation. Even when the terrain confuses you, you can always find your bearing by where the lines lead.

## Biological Caution

Do not bring seeds, spores, soil, or organic material onto the plateau, and do not carry any out. The lattice is balanced, and introduced organisms may take hold. Clean boots, clothing, and equipment before arrival. Bag all waste and carry it out. Do not leave filament samples or grazer tips behind as bait.

Keep records of anything you disturb. If you cut, move, or contaminate something, log its location and the time, so that the next visitor and the plateau's own systems do not misread the change.

## Timing

The plateau runs on several clocks, and your schedule should respect all of them. The best time to move is mid-morning, after the drip has dried, the apertures have begun to track the sun, and the herds have finished drinking. Avoid dawn at the basins and dusk in the lattice, when the mist and drip make footing treacherous and the predators are most active.

Plan no more than four to six hours of movement per day. Rest in the open, on bare terraces, and keep watch in shifts. Do not sleep inside the lattice, and do not sleep within sight of a post.

Plan your exit before you go. Decide on a departure window and a turnaround time, and stick to it even if you feel you are close to something important.

## Equipment Checklist

- Layered cold-weather clothing with sealed wrists, collars, and hoods
- Gloves that cover wrists, plus spares
- Filter mask for dust corridors
- Hearing protection that allows conversation
- Headlamp with a diffuse beam and a red mode, plus a strong white handheld
- Tripod or staff for depth testing
- Compass, paper map, whistle, manual clock
- Water: at least three days' supply plus water treatment tablets or filters
- Food: sealed, with margin
- Marker tape, reflective strips, and salt-stone cairn materials
- Insulated bags for samples, which should be minimal
- Repair kit for boots and tears in fabric
- Emergency beacon that works without line of sight, with a mechanical backup
- A notebook for recording pulse timing, locations, and anything disturbed

## Psychological Preparation

The plateau is settled, but its settlement is a balance of many living processes, and visitors often feel it as a kind of pressure. The rock is smooth, the sky is low, the sound is continuous, and the regularity of the ridges and towers invites you to find patterns. It is easy to start reading meaning into the convergence of the posts and the spacing of the towers. Do not build a story around the plateau's design unless you can test it. The machine's purpose may be unknowable.

Expect to feel watched, even though nothing here is aware of you in any way you can measure. Expect the silence between pulses to feel like a held breath. Expect to be drawn toward the opaque mass. These are normal responses to a place this strange. Name them and continue.

Travel with at least one companion. Agree on a turnaround rule and honor it. If someone in your party becomes disoriented, withdrawn, or fixated on the convergence point, bring them back to bare ground and do not go on until they are steady.

## Final Note

The Upland Lattice rewards careful, patient visitors with a vivid and strange experience. It will not reward haste or curiosity that overrides caution. Respect the rhythms, keep your distance from the predators and the mass, touch nothing you do not need to, and leave the plateau as you found it. Whatever is at the center of the lattice has been arranging the land around itself for a very long time. You are a short visit in a long process, and the best preparation is to remember that.
