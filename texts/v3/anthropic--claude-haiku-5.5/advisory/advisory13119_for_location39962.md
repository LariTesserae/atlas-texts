# Preparation Guide: The Drowned Grid

## Arrival and First Hours

Expect to arrive wet. Most travelers appear either on the water or on a built surface near the waterline, so assume you will need to swim or wade within minutes. Keep your gear sealed in a dry bag, and wear a layer you can swim in under anything else you bring.

The water is cool, roughly cellar temperature. It feels comfortable for the first half hour and then begins pulling heat from you. Wear a full-length wetsuit, at least 3 to 5 millimeters thick, with a hood and gloves. Carry a thermal layer and a windproof shell for when you are out of the water, because the wind over open sea combines with the wet cold quickly.

Before you move, find a high point. The nearest built surface with a flat roof or a raised deck is the safest place to orient yourself. Establish your bearings there, note the storm direction, and only then begin moving.

## Water and the Drowned Grid

The sunlit grid below is beautiful but deep. The built plain lies forty to fifty meters down, which is well beyond casual snorkeling. Reaching the streets and footings requires trained technical diving with proper decompression planning, a reliable depth gauge, and a buddy. Do not attempt it as a tourist.

If you do dive, keep the following in mind:

- **Pipes and valves.** Pressure valves open and close on slow cycles and release bursts of compressed air or brine. Stay clear of outlets, and never place a hand or fin where a valve may close on it. Watch the bubble strings to learn each valve's rhythm before you move past it.
- **Brine drip.** Pipe joints weep flood-brine. Keep your mask sealed, and rinse your eyes and skin carefully after leaving the water.
- **Sharp edges.** Cast concrete and raw steel are sharp, and the biofilm hides them. Wear thick gloves and avoid kneeling on surfaces you have not inspected.
- **Biofilm.** The green-black felt on vertical surfaces makes everything slippery and conceals footing. Do not rely on handholds you cannot see.
- **Crustaceans.** Animals the size of dinner plates graze the pipes and crowd the crevices. They can pinch hard enough to cut or break skin. Do not reach into gaps, and move slowly around anything that is clicking.
- **Pale branching mat.** The lace-like colonies in rusted holes are unidentified. Do not touch them barehanded, and do not disturb them.

## Wildlife in the Water

The sea is busy, and most of its inhabitants are not interested in you, but some are large enough to matter.

The silver fish schools are harmless and will part around you. The long-necked, finned animals that surface to breathe make a rolling exhalation audible for kilometers. Keep a distance of at least thirty meters, and never swim into their path when they surface, since their wake is substantial. Do not approach them even if they seem curious.

The largest presence moves beneath the swell at irregular intervals, and the water lifts in a long pulse that rattles railings and can set small boats against pilings. You will learn to feel its approach before you see anything. When the pulse comes, hold a piling or a solid structure and wait. Do not swim out to investigate.

## Islands, Cranes, and Machinery

The islands are the places you will live, rest, and navigate from. Treat them with the same respect you would give a working industrial site, because they are one.

The pumping stations, cooling towers, and apartment blocks are structurally uncertain. Floors may be sound beside a collapsed wall, and a drowned window frame can hide a gap. Enter buildings only from the roof or upper floors, and always tell someone your route.

The eastern gantry is working. Its arm lowers, touches the water, and lifts something away, with no operator visible. Stay outside its swing radius and never assume a pause means it has stopped. Do not climb the rail, the arm, or the cranes standing on their reflections.

Do not touch, adjust, or interrupt valves, pumps, or turbines. The machines were built for work that has mostly ended, but they are still doing it, and interfering with them could release pressure or current where you do not expect it.

## Weather

The storms here are visible long before they arrive. Grey walls form on the horizon, and you can watch them approach over miles of open water. Once one is visible, you have time to reach high shelter, but you do not have time to dawdle.

Signs of an approaching system include a rising smell of ozone and cold iron, a shift in the cables' thrumming as wind builds, and the sea steaming faintly behind a passing front. The turbines on the cooling towers and the cables between towers become electrically active in storms, and lightning tends to strike the tallest structures. Shelter low and away from metal, and never shelter under a cable run or against a tower's base.

Between storms the sun is strong and the glare off bare concrete is intense. Wear polarized sunglasses, a broad hat, and high-factor sunscreen, and reapply after every swim.

## Sound

The place is never quiet. Waves knock against pilings, pumps drone, turbines whine, bells on buoys ring unpredictably, and every few minutes a valve releases with a long hiss that carries across the water. The noise can be disorienting and can hide approaching hazards.

Plan communication before you arrive. Hand signals work well, and a wrist device that vibrates is more reliable than audible alerts. If you have a partner, agree on a visual signal for danger and a rally point on a high structure.

## Open Water and Navigation

Between islands there may be kilometers of open sea with nothing to hold onto. Do not attempt to swim between them. Carry a life vest or a flotation device with a rated buoyancy well above your body weight, a locator beacon, a signal mirror, and flares.

Carry at least four liters of fresh water per person per day. Do not drink the sea, and do not assume rainwater is safe until it has been filtered. Carry food that does not need cooking.

Navigation by landmark is possible, but the drowned grid changes. The waterline shifts every week as the sea rises and the biofilm spreads, so a landmark that marks your route now may be underwater later. Use the highest built features, which change least, and check them against a compass.

## Health

The brine, the biofilm, and the rust all carry infection risk. Clean every cut immediately with fresh water and an antiseptic, and watch closely for redness, swelling, or heat over the next several days. Bring a broad-spectrum antibiotic prescribed for marine wounds, and make sure your tetanus vaccination is current.

Cold exposure is cumulative. Watch for shivering that stops, slurred speech, or unusual clumsiness, and get out of the water and warm up before you reach that point.

Sun exposure over open water is severe. Hydrate and rest in shade during midday.

## Ecology and Conduct

This is a young ecosystem that is still forming, and the biofilm and the algae mats are spreading quickly. Do not carry any plant, animal, or biological material from this location to another world, and do not bring anything in. Clean your gear thoroughly before leaving, paying particular attention to boots, wetsuits, dry bags, and any collected samples. Sealed containers and decontamination on exit are mandatory.

Do not collect samples of the pale mat, the crustaceans, or the biofilm unless you have a containment protocol. Photography is welcome. Recordings of the sound of the place are often more affecting than images.

## Exit Planning

Choose your return point before you leave your arrival site, and make sure it sits on a built surface well above the waterline. Do not rely on a point at sea level, because the water rises and your return may be underwater by the time you need it.

Plan for at least one storm during any stay. Plan for the sea to be rougher than it looked on arrival. Plan for a return window and a backup window, because the anchoring between worlds may depend on conditions here that you cannot control.

## Mental Preparation

The sparse presence of life against the enormous volume of water and sky can produce a deep loneliness. Between islands there is often nothing but the water, the sun, and the distant hum of machines that no one is listening for. Some travelers find this meditative. Others find it unsettling. Decide in advance how you will manage long stretches of solitude, and bring something to occupy your hands and mind.

Remember that you are a visitor to a world that is still settling, with machines still turning and a grid still filling with life. Move slowly, respect what is working, and leave the place as you found it.
