# Preparing to Visit the Drowned Shelf

The Drowned Shelf is not dangerous in the way a cliff or a fire is dangerous. It is unfinished, and everything in it moves. Most of your preparation should be about footing, sound, and not trusting anything to stay where it was. Plan for a stay of hours, not days, and treat the hollow as your base.

## Before You Go

**Decide your return point first.** You will not be able to find your way back by memory. The channels rearrange themselves, cracks open and close, and a route that was dry an hour ago may be a sheet of moving water now. Choose a fixed reference before you leave the hollow, such as the cliff face you came up past, or the bearing of the loudest cliff echo. Write it down, and write it again on your wrist or a card, because you will not remember it under the noise.

**Do not bring anything you need to stay dry.** The air is a suspended wet, fine droplets that drift on slow currents and get into everything. Electronics, paper maps, and unsealed food will fail within the first hour. Pack in roll-top dry bags or sealed tins. Bring oiled cloth or a spare tarp, though nothing here will stay dry long enough to need one.

**Bring a cloth for your face.** The mist carries a fine mineral grit that tastes of stone and irritates the lungs. A damp wrap over the nose and mouth helps. Change it when it stops breathing easily.

**Expect to be hit.** The heavy downpours arrive every few minutes and hammer hard enough to sting. A hood with a brim, a helmet, or a hat you can tie down is worth more than an extra layer. Keep your eyes covered during the bursts.

## Footwear and Movement

The basalt is glassy when wet, and the fresh edges are sharp. Wear boots with deep, aggressive treads and firm ankle support. Bring a spare pair of socks in a sealed bag, because your feet will be wet for the whole visit.

Carry a long pole or staff. Use it to test the depth of any sheet of water before you commit your weight. The depth changes from one moment to the next, and a channel a few centimeters deep can become a knee-deep torrent in the time it takes to take one step.

Bring a length of strong cord, at least twenty meters. Tie yourself to something that is not part of the shelf, if you can find something. Plates here slide with a long grinding sigh and can carry a person with them.

**Do not trust slopes.** Some water runs uphill over ridges. Your sense of which way is down will be wrong here. Do not assume that water is running the way it looks like it should.

## Hazards in the Stone

**Cracks.** Some are hair-thin. Others are wide enough to lose a hand or a leg, and the new ones open without warning. Test each crack with the pole before crossing. Do not kneel or lean across a crack. Note that a channel which suddenly drains is usually drinking into a new opening.

**Blister slabs.** Stones that stand half-lifted from the bed, with sand pouring from underneath, are being undermined. They can drop or shift without warning. Walk wide around them, and never stand under one or in its shadow.

**Falling plates.** The doors of rock that peel away along the cracks come off in long slides. Watch the cracks for movement, and listen for the grinding before you move near them. If you hear it, keep moving away.

## Sound and Speech

The roar of the channels drowns out footsteps and ordinary speech. Shouting helps only a little, because the cliff echoes return before your own words have finished. You will hear your voice answering itself from the far walls.

Agree on hand signals before you go. Choose a few simple ones: stop, come here, danger, return. Use bright cloth markers that can be seen through the mist.

Expect to be disoriented. You may respond to a question that has not yet reached you, or find that someone's answer arrives before they seem to have spoken. Speak slowly and deliberately, and pause after each sentence so the echo can clear.

The stones ring at different pitches when the rain hits them. This is beautiful and useful. A sudden change in pitch can warn you that the ground is shifting. Listen for it, but do not rely on it alone.

## Life Here

The pale, blind creatures in the deeper pools are fragile and quick to vanish. Leave them alone. Do not net them, touch them, or disturb the water they press against.

The lichen is thin and leathery, and it cracks when dry. Do not scrape it or tear it free. Some of it looks gnawed. Something else here eats it, and the damage may be the only sign of how the place feeds itself.

The grasses in the silt pockets are holding onto very little. Step around them.

## The Built Things

The cairn, the stone ring, the weir, and the chipped tools were made by someone, or by something, that intended to use them and then leave them for the water. Respect that intention.

Do not move, straighten, or use any of them. The weir is still working for someone. The cairn leans for reasons you cannot judge. The ring may be a tether, a marker, or something else entirely. If you want a record, sketch or photograph from a distance.

Do not take the tools. Do not add stones to the cairn. If you need to mark your route, use chalk or cloth on stones away from the built things, and expect the water to erase your marks within hours.

## The Hollow

The hollow is the best place to rest. Its walls cut the roar to a low grumble, and the water there gathers in a gentle basin rather than racing. Use it as your base.

It is not perfectly safe. Its ceiling drips and its lichen hangs in fringes that can tear loose. Check the ceiling for new cracks before you settle beneath it. Sleep near the back wall, away from the open side, and away from the stacked stones.

The stacked stones are dry, and someone arranged them to rest on. You may use them to sit, but do not take them or rearrange them.

Expect the hollow to hum when a slab shifts somewhere in the murk. This is normal. It does not mean the hollow is about to fail.

## Food and Water

The water is mineral and cold, and the channels carry grit. Filter everything before you drink it, or bring sealed water. Do not eat the pale creatures or the lichen. Keep your food in sealed containers, because the mist will ruin anything left open.

## Leaving

Set your departure time before you enter, and keep to it. The shelf will not finish building itself on your schedule, and it will not stop changing because you are tired or curious. The sense that the place is almost ready to settle is an illusion. Leave while you still can find your way back to your reference point.

## Mental Preparation

You will not understand this place. The water climbs hills and runs back down. Sound arrives before the sound that made it. The stone is being built in front of you, and you will feel like an intruder on a construction site that has no foreman.

Do not try to fix, stabilize, or explain anything. Do not stare at the uphill flow for long. The effect is vertigo, and it grows the longer you watch.

The Drowned Shelf is not made for visitors. It is making itself out of water and rock, and the best thing a traveler can do is take what they need, leave what they found, and go.
