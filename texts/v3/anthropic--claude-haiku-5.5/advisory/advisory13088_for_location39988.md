# Preparing to Visit the Ashen Shelf

## Arrival and Altitude

Most travelers arrive on the ridge road above Corvane, roughly two thousand meters up. The air is thin and clean, and it will make you slightly breathless for the first two or three days. Walk slowly, drink more water than feels necessary, and expect your sleep to be lighter than usual. If you have a heart or lung condition, consult a physician before you go. Rest on your first day instead of climbing the terraces.

The cold here is damp rather than bitter, but it settles into your joints. Carry a warm layer even in the afternoon.

## Clothing and Gear

Nothing dries out here, so plan for permanent dampness.

- **Outer layer:** A hooded waterproof shell that breathes. Fine rain falls most days, and the mist soaks through cotton within the hour.
- **Base layers:** Wool or synthetic only. Cotton stays cold and heavy.
- **Footwear:** The most important item you will bring. The basalt is sharp-edged where it has not yet weathered, and the moss over it is slick as soap. Bring boots with aggressive lugs and ankle support, and break them in before you arrive. Carry a second pair of socks in a sealed bag.
- **Gloves:** Thin, grippy, and waterproof. The stone cuts, and wet hands lose grip fast on rope and rock.
- **Headwear:** A brimmed hat keeps the drip off your neck and face. The forest canopy releases water in steady drops, and the drops are heavier than they look.

Pack everything in dry bags or sealed plastic. Store paper, maps, and medicines in double layers, and check them daily. Mold can appear in two days.

## Water, Food, and Health

Water is abundant, but treat all of it. The streams look clean, and they are full of fine silt from the volcanic soil and occasional animal runoff. Boil or filter everything, including water from the leaves.

Eat local food where you can. The tuberous crops and the silvery grain are filling and well-prepared by the terrace families. Introduce them gradually, since your gut may not agree with the mineral content of the soil at first.

The biggest health risks are:
- **Skin trouble:** Constant damp softens skin and invites fungal infection, especially between the toes and under the arms. Dry yourself thoroughly each evening, and apply a drying powder or antifungal cream.
- **Cuts:** Clean every scrape immediately. Basalt dust and wet moss make a poor combination for wounds.
- **Insect bites:** The forest hums with insects. Use repellent, and cover your ankles at dusk.
- **Respiratory strain:** Damp air can worsen asthma or chest colds. Keep your medications dry and accessible.

## Shelter and Fire

Shelter is available in the hamlets and in Corvane, where guest rooms are built of stacked stone with thatch or shingle roofs. Ask for a room on the upper floors. Lower rooms collect damp.

Fire is hard to start and keep going. Bring a waterproof fire kit with ferro rods, tinder, and a few dry sticks in a sealed tin. Locals have fire-hardened kindling and resin-sealed tinder, and they will often share.

## Money, Trade, and Goods

Metal is scarce and valued, so a few small copper items or fine steel tools are excellent currency. Salt is also highly prized. Coins from your home world will be curiosities at best. Ask about barter before you assume money works.

Avoid bringing machinery, batteries, or anything that will fail in damp conditions. Those items will not function properly here and may give you a false sense of security. Flashlights should be sealed and carried as backups only.

Good trade goods include:
- Fine steel needles, knives, and awls
- Salt in sealed containers
- Medicines (basic antibiotics, antifungals, pain relief)
- Thread and sturdy cord, which the locals value for their own weaving

## Customs and Manners

The people of Corvane are reserved but not unfriendly. Greet them with a raised palm, a gesture of openness that they return. Speak quietly. Sound carries far in this stillness, and loud voices draw attention.

**Respect the stones.** The families who lead the terraces re-lay the same stones after floods that never come. Do not move, reposition, or take stones from the walls or drainage channels, even if they look misplaced. Ask before you touch any carving on the columns. Many are maintained ritual records.

**Respect the cycle.** Harvests, plantings, and repairs follow fixed schedules. If someone suggests a different way to do something, the answer is almost always no, and they will not appreciate being asked why. Do not proselytize about improvement or change. People here regard it as a kind of rudeness.

**Respect the animals.** The grazing herds, the blue-black birds, and the basking reptiles are not hunted casually. Do not feed or chase them. The birds nest in the crevices of old buildings, and disturbing a roost is a serious breach of custom.

**Ask before entering a hamlet.** Paths between villages are public, but houses and terraces belong to families. Wait to be invited inside.

## The Stillness

This is the part that requires the most preparation, and it will not appear in any guidebook.

The Shelf does not change. Rain holds at the same steady intensity. Fog lifts at midday on schedule. The pools sit at the same level. Crops cycle in the same years. Visitors report that after a few days they feel calm and rested. After a few weeks, many stop noticing time passing. Some stop noticing that they are still here.

To protect yourself:

1. **Keep a daily written log.** Record the date, the weather, what you ate, whom you met, and one thing that surprised you. Date it by your own clock, not by the fog. Read it each week to confirm that time has passed.
2. **Carry an object that wears down.** A candle, a bar of soap, or a cord you knot daily will show you how much time has passed. Locals may find this odd, but it will not offend them.
3. **Set a departure date and keep it.** Tell someone in Corvane when you intend to leave, and ask them to remind you. Your own sense of "one more day" is the most dangerous thing here.
4. **Watch for sameness in others.** If the same people greet you with the same words, eat the same meal, and say the same things about the weather, begin your departure immediately.
5. **Note your exit route before you need it.** Find the ridge road and mark the first waystone on your map. The path out is easy to walk. The hard part is deciding to take it.

## Leaving

Walk back up the ridge road in the morning, before the fog lifts. Do not stop to say goodbye at every house, since the farewells will delay you. Keep your log open and write your departure time at the top of a fresh page.

The Ashen Shelf is beautiful, careful, and quiet, and it has settled into a single permanent shape. Visit it with respect for its patience, keep your own calendar moving, and bring your boots.
