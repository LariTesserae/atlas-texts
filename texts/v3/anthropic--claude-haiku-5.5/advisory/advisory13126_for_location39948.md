# Preparing to Visit the Stilled Plateau

## Understanding What You're Walking Into

The Stilled Plateau is not hostile in the way most dangerous places are. Nothing here is hunting you, and the machines have no interest in you. The danger is mostly physical and mostly predictable: cold water, sharp rock, salt, and the slow erosion of your sense of time. The place is loud, bright, and busy, but it is also very, very consistent. If you prepare for its regularity as carefully as for its hazards, you'll find it much easier to manage.

Plan for a stay of hours, not days. Many travelers who stay longer report that the stillness works on them in ways that are hard to notice until they've already gone too far.

## Physical Preparation

**Cold and wet.** The sea is cold enough to kill quickly. Even if you never enter the water, the spray, mist, and sleet will soak you within minutes of any exposed walk along a peak. Bring a layered system: a base layer that wicks moisture and keeps insulation when damp (wool or synthetic, never cotton), a middle insulating layer, and a fully waterproof outer shell with sealed seams and a hood that fits over a helmet. Gloves should be waterproof, with a thin liner beneath so your fingers stay usable when you work a latch or handle a line.

**Footing.** The peaks are raw basalt and granite, unweathered, with edges like broken glass. The salt crust makes every surface slick, and the iron-rich films in the shallows are worse. Use boots with aggressive lugs and stiff soles, ideally with ankle support, and carry a second pair of gloves in case you need to brace on rock. Bring a rope and harness if you plan to cross any sloped face. Do not rely on a single handhold.

**Eyes.** The light is cold, unfiltered, and reflects brightly off water. Glare alone can cause headaches and snow-blindness-like strain over a long day. Polarized sunglasses or tinted goggles are essential. Include a spare lens or pair, since spray will coat them quickly.

**Hearing.** This is the strangest preparation problem. The place is loud all the time, and the noise does not vary enough for the brain to tune it out. Over several hours it becomes fatiguing, then disorienting. Bring earplugs rated for moderate noise reduction, but do not rely on them alone. You need to hear ice fractures, which can be early warnings of wave surges, and the sudden changes in buoy tones that signal a shift in the swell. Some travelers carry a bone-conduction headset so they can hear their own voice and important alarms over the drone. Agree on signals with your party that don't depend on speech, such as a whistle pattern or a hand gesture visible across a peak.

## Navigation and Time

**Compass reliability.** The iron-rich rock may produce local magnetic deviation. Check your compass against the sun's position before relying on it, and carry a second method such as a map with landmarks noted in advance. The peaks look close because the air is so clear, but the distances are deceptive. Wading forms that appear a few hundred meters away can be kilometers distant.

**Keeping time.** This is the most important practical skill you'll need. The tide, the storm arrivals, and the pump cycles all repeat on fixed intervals. Use them. Before you descend to any peak, note the time of the next storm front and the next tide turn, and write them on your hand or on a card. A watch works, but a written log is better, since watches can drift in the cold and salt. Record one entry every hour: the time, your location, how you feel, and what you've eaten and drunk. The log serves two purposes: it keeps your sense of elapsed time honest, and it gives you something to compare with your memory later.

**Anchoring your return.** Mark your entry point clearly. Stake it, cairn it, or tie a bright marker to a fixed structure. The pumping stations are fixed reference points, though they are not always occupied, and their silhouettes can look alike from a distance. Learn the layout of your route from the entry point before you move away from it, and keep a line of sight back to it wherever possible.

## Dealing With the Machines

The pumping stations, intake pipes, and outflow channels are still running, and they follow their own rhythms. Treat them as you would any heavy industrial equipment, with the added caution that nobody has maintained them in a long time.

Do not climb onto turbine housings or enter any structure with exposed rotating parts. Their speed is fixed, which means you can predict their cycles. Observe a rotation for several minutes before you pass near one, and cross only during a clear gap. The collapsed towers are tempting shelters, but their roofs can shift in the wind, and the hanging pipes can swing with sudden force when the thermal expansion cycles pass through them. Shelter in the lee of a rock face instead, or in a shallow cut in the stone, and stay clear of anything with a hanging load.

Mooring platforms and boat skeletons are often the only dry flat ground near the water. Test every plank before you put your weight on it. Rope is brittle and may part under tension.

## The Creatures

The fauna is not aggressive, but it is large, heavy, and indifferent to your presence.

**Grazers.** The glassy, lattice-shelled animals scrape the iron films from the rocks with their bristled mouthparts. Their shells are sharp at the edges and can cut skin. Keep your hands and boots away from their feeding areas, and do not reach into the shallows to touch them. Their behavior at the outflow plumes is unexplained. Many gather there and stay for hours, apparently feeding on something the machines release. Do not approach the plumes, and do not sample the discharge. The brine is cold and heavily mineralized, and nothing about it is safe to drink or touch.

**Filter-feeders.** The large fan-shaped forms in the deeper water open and close slowly. They are not dangerous, but their sediment plumes can cloud the water quickly, hiding rock edges. Keep a margin of several meters from them.

**Gliders.** The long-winged birds ride the thermals off the peaks. Their calls are harsh, and some mimic the pump drone so closely that you may hear a pump where none exists. Do not assume a sound is mechanical until you have confirmed it visually. They are not typically aggressive, but they will investigate shiny objects and may steal small items left on exposed rock.

**Wading forms.** The frost-ridged animals move slowly through the shallows. They are heavy and deliberate. Give them wide berth, and never place yourself between one and open water. They do not seem to notice people, but they can knock a person down with a single shift of weight.

## Water

Do not enter the water. The cold will incapacitate you in minutes, and the swells can pull you off a ledge with little warning. If you fall in, the shock alone can stop your breathing. Keep your bag and gear well away from the shoreline, and tie anything that could slide down a rock face.

Drink only the water you carried in. The surface water does not freeze, and it steams faintly in the mornings, which is a curious sign of an energy source you do not understand. The steam itself is not harmful, but it's wise to avoid breathing in mist from the outflow plumes, which carry dissolved salts and metals.

## Sound and the Unknown Note

The buoy bells ring in long repeating patterns as the swells strike them. Most of the tones are predictable. But occasionally a low note sounds beneath the others, a tone no one has identified. It seems to hang in the air a few seconds longer than it should, like a held breath. Some travelers find it soothing. Others find it unsettling, and a few report that it becomes harder to stop thinking about once they have heard it.

Do not try to locate the source of this note while you are on a narrow ledge or close to the water. If you hear it, note the time in your log. Some visitors have tried to record it, but recordings tend to lose the tone or produce a false version of it. Accept that you may not understand it, and let it pass.

## The Stillness

This is the preparation most people neglect, and the one that causes the most trouble.

The place is in equilibrium. Nothing here is progressing. The tides, storms, pumps, and grazers repeat endlessly, and the eroding peaks are held in balance by deposition. Over time, the constancy can make your own sense of movement feel uncertain. Travelers who stay past their planned departure often describe a loss of urgency. They forget why they came, or stop noticing that they're cold. They begin to feel that leaving would mean moving into a less coherent world, and that feeling is hard to resist.

Set a departure time before you arrive, and tell someone outside the location, if you can, when you expect to be back. Build your log so that it asks you a question each hour: *What has changed?* Write down anything new, however small, even if it's just a different angle of light or a new crack in a peak. If you find you cannot name anything that has changed in several hours, that is a warning sign. Leave.

Pay attention to your own speech and thinking. If you begin to describe the place in the rhythms of its sounds, or find yourself counting pump cycles without meaning to, you are beginning to synchronize with the loop. Step away from the water, sit somewhere quiet if you can find it, and eat something with a strong flavor. Physical sensation tends to break the pattern.

## Equipment Checklist

- Waterproof layered clothing, with spare base layers
- Insulated waterproof gloves with liners
- Stiff-soled boots with aggressive tread
- Polarized eyewear and a spare pair
- Moderate earplugs and a bone-conduction headset
- Rope and harness, with a second anchor point
- Compass, map, and a backup navigation method
- Watch and a waterproof notebook with pencil (not pen, which can fail in cold)
- Carried water and high-calorie food
- Marking supplies: bright flagging, stakes, and a small cairn kit
- Emergency shelter and a heat source
- Whistle and a set of non-verbal signals agreed with your party
- Basic first-aid kit, with salt-water rinse and anti-inflammatory for cuts from rock and shell

## Final Advice

Go in with a clear purpose and a firm plan to leave. Log the hours, watch the storm fronts, and keep your distance from the machines and the animals. Listen closely to the sea, but do not let it become the only thing you hear. The Stilled Plateau does not want you, and it will not hurt you on purpose. Its danger is that it is easy to stay in, and very hard to notice that you have.
