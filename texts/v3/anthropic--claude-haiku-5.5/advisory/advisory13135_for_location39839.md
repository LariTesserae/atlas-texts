# Preparing to Visit the Stepped Basin

This is a place where the ground is always moving, the water is always running, and the people have been living with both for a very long time. Visitors who do well here tend to be the ones who slow down, watch the water, and ask before they touch anything. Below is what to prepare, roughly in order of importance.

## Before You Arrive

**Find your way back to the rim.** The plain is flat and pale, and the basin's edge appears with no warning. Before you descend, fix a landmark on the plain, a stone, a distant tree, the angle of the sun, and note it. In fog, after rain, or at dusk, the edge can be hard to find from below, and the terraces all look alike. Keep track of which direction the main stair climbs back out.

**Check the weather before you go down.** The rains come a few times a week, but they can arrive quickly. Look for a heavy grey band over the far plain, a sudden drop in temperature, and a thickening of the air. If you see these, don't start the descent. If you're already below when rain begins, go up. Don't wait it out at the bottom.

**Learn the basic rhythm.** Mornings bring condensation and damp. Midday is hot and close. Afternoons are when the water drains. Dusk brings the frogs and the bats. Most of the basin's work happens in the cooler hours, so plan to be on the terraces then, when people are around to talk to.

## What to Pack

**Footwear.** This matters more than anything else. Stone is always slick, moss grows on the lower courses, and the paving has settled unevenly, with cracks and step edges that have worn smooth. Bring boots or closed shoes with deep tread and good ankle support. Sandals will cost you a fall. Carry a spare pair of socks in a dry bag.

**Clothing.** Choose light, breathable layers that dry fast. The air is thick, so cotton holds damp and gets cold. A thin, packable rain shell is worth bringing, along with a wide-brimmed hat. Wear long sleeves and trousers against the sun on stone, the insects, and the lichen, which can irritate skin. Gloves are useful if you need to hold a railing or a wall.

**Water and purification.** The cisterns are cold and look clear, but they hold water the locals manage carefully, and you don't know what's in them. Bring your own water and a reliable purifier, and drink only what you carry or what you're offered. Ask before you draw from any cistern or channel.

**Dry storage.** Everything gets wet. Use dry bags for electronics, paper, medicine, and food. Silica packets help. Condensation will fog lenses and screens in the mornings.

**Insect and skin protection.** The hum never stops. Bring repellent that works in humid conditions, and an antihistamine in case you react to bites or to lichen and plaster dust. A small first-aid kit with antiseptic and bandages is essential, because cuts on damp, dirty stone go bad quickly.

**Gifts, carefully chosen.** The people here work with hand tools and make almost everything themselves. Iron is clearly precious to them, so don't display iron tools or fittings casually, and don't give them any as a casual gift. Salt, dried fruit, thread, needles, and good cloth are likely to be welcome. Offer gifts with open hands and let them decide what to do with them.

## Health Concerns

**Heat and humidity.** The air is wet enough to feel on the skin, and sweat doesn't evaporate well. Drink more than you think you need, and rest in shade at midday. Watch for dizziness, headache, and confusion, which signal heat exhaustion.

**The cold water.** Cistern water is much colder than the air a few centimetres below the surface. Don't dive in or plunge your arms in if you're overheated. The shock can be dangerous.

**Lichen, moss, and plaster.** The grey-green and orange lichen and the lime plaster can irritate skin and airways. Don't scrape them, and wash your hands before touching your face or eating.

**Mould and damp.** Rooms that were dry for a lifetime can suddenly seep. Don't sleep in or wait out rain in lower rooms. Choose quarters on the upper terraces, and if you notice a musty smell or a green bloom on a floor, leave the room.

**Tetanus and standard vaccinations.** Stone, rusted iron, and broken brick are common. Make sure your tetanus vaccination is current before you go.

## Navigating the Terraces

**Stay on the stairs and paving.** Use the main stairs for moving between levels. Don't cut across the channels or the aqueducts.

**Never stand in a channel during rain.** The channels fill within an hour of a downpour, and the current becomes fast and carries debris. Move to higher ground at the first sign of rain, and never cross a flooded aqueduct.

**Respect the sluice gates.** The deep bass drone when a gate opens is a warning. Step back from cistern edges and wait until the sound stops before you go near the water.

**Don't lean on walls or climb ruins.** The walls have settled, and cracks open after storms and close in dry seasons. Rubble slides happen. The ruined arch with the grey thorny shrub is especially unstable. Don't climb it or stand beneath it.

**Orient by the water.** Water runs downhill, and the low places hold the cisterns. Most of the basin slopes toward its centre. This is useful for orientation, but it also means the lowest parts are where things get dangerous.

## The Lowest Terrace

This is the most important thing to understand about the basin. The oldest cistern sits on the lowest terrace, and that terrace is warmer and damper than the rest, even in dry hours. Its shadows hold a faint pale glow, enough to read by in a cupped hand. The local people do not gather food there.

Keep away from it. Don't go down to it, don't sleep near it, and don't touch its moss or water. Don't eat anything grown or drawn from there. If you are offered food from that area, decline politely. If you want to know more about the glow or the warmth, ask the locals, who know the place better than anyone. They will probably tell you more than you expect, and they will take your questions seriously.

The basin is still settling. Something in its lowest parts is changing, and the locals have learned to live with the risk by keeping their distance. Follow their lead.

## Wildlife

**Lizards and bats.** Lizards bask on warm stone and are harmless. Bats leave the cisterns at dusk in thick spirals. Don't swat at them or try to catch them.

**Rodents and cats.** The small mottled rodents run along the aqueducts. The long-legged grey cat hunts them along the top terraces. Don't feed either animal, and don't leave food out where they can reach it.

**The blind fish.** In the deepest cisterns, pale blind fish rise to the surface when people drop scraps. Don't drop food into the water. It disrupts a system the locals depend on.

**The night caller.** At night a larger animal calls from the lower basin. The people answer with a rhythmic clatter of pots and stones. Join in if they invite you. Otherwise, stay in a dwelling on the upper terraces and don't go down to investigate.

**Goats and fowl.** These belong to the people. Don't pet, chase, or move them without asking.

**Insects and many-legged things.** Something with many legs moves through the damp moss. Check your boots and sleeping area each morning, and don't handle anything you can't identify.

## Dealing with the People

**Expect noise.** Someone is always hammering: stone being shaped, copper being beaten, a loom's shuttle knocking under a roof. Voices carry across the basin and bounce off the walls. Conversation from one terrace reaches the next as a murmur, and everything sounds bigger than it is. Speak more quietly than feels natural, and don't shout across terraces unless you need to call someone.

**Greet people.** Use gestures, eye contact, and a raised hand. Watch how they greet one another and copy it. Smile and wait for a response before going further.

**Watch and learn.** Notice how people carry water jars on yokes, how they sit and work, how they move on the stairs. The rhythm of the place is a language of its own. Joining in the work is often the best way to be welcomed, but ask first, and accept gracefully if they decline.

**Don't add to the walls.** The basin has been built on persistence, layer over layer. Don't dig, build, repair, or add anything to a wall or floor without permission, even with good intentions. What looks like damage may be a deliberate part of the structure.

**Respect the dwellings.** Rooms are cut into the stone and framed with carved lintels. Enter only when invited, and don't touch the lintels, roof tiles, or thatch.

**Photography and recording.** Ask before you photograph people, their work, or their dwellings. The sound of the basin is part of its character, so recording it is usually fine, but ask before you record conversations.

**Trade and exchange.** Bronze awls, bone needles, clay moulds, and cloth are the currency of daily life. If you trade, be fair and transparent. Don't offer iron as payment unless you understand what it means to them.

## Planning Your Stay

**Stay on the upper terraces.** Lodging is best in the dry, sturdy rooms near the rim. Avoid rooms with fresh cracks, seeps, or green bloom on the floor.

**Plan your days around the rain.** Do your descents and climbs in dry hours. Be on the stairs before the water rises, and expect to spend time indoors during downpours.

**Leave before dark.** The lower terraces are dangerous at night. The frogs and the bats make the basin loud, and the pale glow of the lowest terrace is more visible in the dark. If you must move after sunset, stay on the upper stairs with a light and a companion.

**Plan your exit.** Climb out by the main stair, not the channels. Watch for weather, and give yourself time to reach the rim before the afternoon drain or the evening frogs. Keep the landmark you chose on the plain in mind so you can find the way back up.

## Leaving

When you reach the rim and step back onto the flat plain, the change is abrupt. The ground stops being wet and moving and becomes dry and still, and the sound of the basin will seem to rise behind you and hang in the air above the edge. Your ears may keep the basin's rhythm for some time afterward. Give yourself a little while to readjust before you travel on.
