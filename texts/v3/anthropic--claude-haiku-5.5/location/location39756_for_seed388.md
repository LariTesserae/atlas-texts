# The Sunk Foundry Basin

The land here is a bowl, about four kilometers across, and it has been sinking for a long time. Its rim is a low ridge of slag and cracked concrete, and within it the ground never rises higher than a man's knee above the water. Everything in the basin has settled into the wet. Streets have softened into causeways, and the old rail spurs lie half-submerged like the spines of something that died in the mud.

The water is everywhere but rarely deep. It sits a hand or two below the surface of the pavements and wells up through every crack, so each step makes a small suck and sigh. Iron and copper have leached into the standing pools and stained them rust-orange, bronze, and a bruised green. Where the sunlight reaches the surface, a skin of iridescent film forms and breaks. The air is never dry. Droplets collect on everything and drip from every edge, and the drip is constant enough that the basin seems to be breathing water out through its pores.

The vegetation is lush, and it has taken over the industrial works. Vines as thick as cables climb the gantries and wrap the cooling towers in green ribs. Ferns the height of a tall man spread from the mouths of the furnaces, and horsetail stands in dense clumps along the drainage channels. Moss covers every horizontal iron surface. The plants are pale, nearly colorless in the dim, with leaves like wet paper and stems that are translucent and veined like old glass. Something climbs the sides of the overhead pipes in sheets, fleshy and white, with fruit that hangs in clusters and swells slowly toward the ground. Nothing in the basin is ripe. The fruit grows heavier each season and drops only when the water rises.

The light is dim and comes from above, filtered through a ceiling of cloud and steam that rises from the hot vents and condenses against the sky. The overcast never quite breaks. Late in the day a sallow glow comes through the haze, and the furnace stacks at the edges of the basin keep faint red coals going, so the lowlands are never fully dark. Shadows are soft and blurred. Nothing casts a sharp edge.

The place is loud, and almost all of the sound is mechanical. The great pumps at the basin's center still run. They were built to keep the water down, but they have been losing that fight for generations, and now they labor in a slow, heavy rhythm that you can feel in your chest before you hear it. Each stroke sends a shudder through the standing water, and the ripples spread across the pools in rings. Around them, smaller engines knock and hiss. Sluice gates grind open and shut on timers no one remembers setting. Iron groans as it expands in the heat and contracts in the cool, and every few minutes some distant structure lets go with a long metallic sigh and falls into the water with a crash that echoes off the ridge. Between these sounds is the endless dripping, a thousand small sources that never sync and never stop.

There are no animals here. No birds cross the haze, no insects drone, no frogs call from the reeds. The only living sounds are the plants, which rustle and creak as their vines tighten around the iron, and the water, which bubbles and settles. Without fauna, the basin runs on its own loop. The heat from the vents lifts the water off the pools, the vapor condenses on the underside of the lowering cloud, and it falls back as a fine, steady drizzle. The water cycles through the pumps, through the channels, through the plants, and back into the air. The system is closed and stable, and it has been stable long enough that it no longer seems to notice the outside world.

The strangeness is in the arrangement. The vines and ferns grow in lines that follow the old pipe runs and rail beds, as if the plants were still obeying a blueprint buried beneath them. The moss grows thickest on the sides of the machinery nearest the pump housings and thins toward the rim, so that the basin's dense center looks like a cross-section through the work of the old engineers. Where a pipe has burst and its water flows freely, the surrounding plants have grown toward it in arcs, reaching for the spray. Some of the iron itself is slowly green, with a coat that is half mineral, half living, and when touched it is warm.

Change here is glacial. Over years the water creeps a few centimeters higher, and the streets disappear one at a time under the spreading pools. Over decades the vines thicken, the fruit swells, a tower leans a little further and finally falls. Nothing happens quickly. A visitor who stood in the basin for a single day would see only the drip, the pump stroke, and the slow lift of the mist. Over a lifetime they might notice that the causeway they walked in is now a canal. Over a century the basin would finish becoming a lake, and the pumps would still be running.
