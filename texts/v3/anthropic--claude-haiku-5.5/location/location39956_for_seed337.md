The basin is a bowl of pale, freshly broken rock about forty meters deep, and the city fills all of it. Nothing here has finished settling. Three seasons ago a stretch of the old limestone shelf gave way along a fault, and the ground under the plateau dropped into a sinkhole that is still widening. The cut faces are raw: bands of grey marl, ochre seams, and white chalk that sheds fine dust when knocked. Scree fans spill down the walls in fresh tongues, and every heavy rain brings new slumps. Nobody calls it a sinkhole anymore. They call it the Pit, or just the Under.

The city climbs the walls in terraces, each one cut by hand, shovel, and pick. Lower terraces are packed tight with houses of rammed earth, rubble stone, and timber salvaged from the old surface town, which has its own roofs and chimneys sitting at the rim like spectators. The houses lean on each other. Alleys are less than an arm's width in places, and they run into stairs cut into the rock, ropes strung between dwellings, and bridges of planks thrown across gullies. Nothing is left open. Doorways, sills, and flat roofs are all occupied. Drying nets, goat pens, clay ovens, and cistern lids crowd every ledge. Above the highest tier, half-built terraces wait with their cut faces bare, and crews of masons work them in the mornings, hammering out small percussive knocks that echo off the walls and then soften.

The floor of the basin holds a braided river, shallow and fast, running over grey gravel between mudflats and reed beds. It enters from a cleft to the east, where a spring has opened in the stone, and leaves through a culvert the town has been trying to enlarge for years. Water seeps from almost every wall. Thin sheets trickle down the faces and collect in moss-lined channels, then drip from the eaves of the lower houses into barrels and clay jars. Condensation beads on everything. The air is damp and cool, and mornings begin in fog that pools in the bottom of the bowl and slowly burns off as the sun clears the rim. By afternoon the rock sweats again.

Light is ordinary. The sky is a wide, pale disc overhead, and sun reaches the floor for only a few hours a day, so the lower streets stay in a soft grey shade while the upper terraces take warm light. Shadows of the rim fall across the city in a slow line that the inhabitants track as a clock.

Vegetation is sparse and stubborn. Lichen in orange and grey crusts the stone. Moss fills the damp seams, and in a few sheltered crevices grow ferns and small succulents that store water in fat leaves. Reeds line the river's edges, cut back regularly for thatch and fuel. A few hardy shrubs have taken root on the scree and are being tended, since they hold the slope. Nothing grows inside the city except in pots and in one terrace garden, where people water beans and onions from the cisterns.

Fauna is rare but present. Swifts nest in cracks high on the cliffs and wheel through the fog at dusk. Bats hang in the old quarry tunnels that open along the south wall. Rats are everywhere, and the cats kept to hunt them are never quite enough. Frogs sing from the reed beds after rain. A few herons stand motionless in the shallows. Goats and a handful of chickens live in the lower pens.

The sound of the place is low. Dripping is constant, a patient percussion from a thousand eaves and channels. Under it run the river's murmur, the soft scrape of scree falling somewhere, and people's voices, pitched down because sound carries strangely in the basin and seems to come from everywhere at once. Hammers knock from the masons' terraces. Wind passes only over the rim, so the bottom of the bowl is almost still, and when a gust does drop down it makes the lanterns sway and the wash-lines snap.

The place is always changing. Rains bring floods that cut new channels and carry mud into lower houses, so people move their doors and rebuild each spring. Slumps bury whole alleys overnight, and crews dig out the debris and find new stone under it. Seasonal melt raises the river by a hand's width, and the water-watchers call out levels from the bridges. Sometimes a wall cracks with a sound like a shot, and everyone stops to look up. The city is arranged around the fact that the ground is still deciding what it will be, and the people who live in it have learned to build as if the floor might move again next year.
