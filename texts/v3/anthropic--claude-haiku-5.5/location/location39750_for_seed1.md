**The Low Grey Reach**

You come down from the last rise onto a plain so flat it seems to have been ironed. The land is low, barely above the level of the sea that must once have lain here, and it has been wearing down for longer than any name could reach. The hills that were once mountains are now rounded swells, smoothed past sharpness, like knuckles worn by a thousand thousand years of rain. Nothing here is young. Nothing here is rising.

**Ground and water**

The surface is a mosaic of pale mineral skins: crusted silt, glassy films, plates of something between stone and ceramic, all cracked into polygons the size of cart wheels. Water sits everywhere, just under the surface. Press your heel into any hollow and it fills, slowly, from below. The whole plain is a sponge that has forgotten how to drain. Sheets of standing water spread across it in wide, shallow lakes so still they hold the sky with perfect fidelity, and between them run braided channels that never seem to flow in the same direction twice.

Everything weeps. Rock faces bead and run. Stones sweat a thin oily sheen. The air itself is saturated, condensing on your sleeves, on your eyelashes, on the cold metal of your instruments, so that you carry the wet with you like a second skin. The cold is steady and damp, the kind that gets into bone and stays.

**No green**

There are no plants. Not a moss, not a lichen, not a single filament of living green. The absence is total and uncanny. Where a plain at this latitude should be dressed in sedge and cushion-plant, there are only the mineral crusts and, in the lower basins, a dull brown-black slick that may be a film of something simpler than life, or something that left life behind. When you touch it, it gives a faint warmth and then nothing. The world has been stripped to its skeleton and the skeleton is fully exposed.

**Machinery without buildings**

There is nothing built here. No walls, no roads, no shelters, no abandoned tools. But there is industry. Out across the basins, tall columns of rust-colored iron rise from the water at irregular intervals, some as high as a church spire, others squat and wide. They are not structures. They pump. Each one draws water up from below, pressing it through pipes of corroded banded metal that bend and fold into the ground, and at the top it exhales in rhythmic bursts, a mechanical sigh that carries for kilometers. Pistons of black stone rise and fall in the shallows. Gears of fused slag turn in the channels, grinding out a steady, patient labor. Who or what set them going, no one can say. They seem less like machines than like the land's own organs, doing the work of a body with no visible mind behind it.

The plain hums with it. Steam curls from vents in lines so straight they look drafted. Conveyor-like ridges of fused rubble carry sediment slowly across the flats, always in the same direction, always toward the same low point, where it sinks and the cycle repeats.

**Fauna, packed and loud**

And the animals. This is what fills the landscape. Not sparse, not hiding, but everywhere, layered at every height and depth. In the water, dense shoals of segmented, armored forms move in shimmering sheets that fold and reform like cloth in wind. On the mineral crusts, clusters of heavy, many-legged creatures with plated backs graze the slick films with a sound like tearing wet paper. Above them, swarms of winged things with glass-clear wings hang in columns that rise and fall with the pumps' rhythm. They do not seem to fear the machines. They seem to have adapted their timing to them. Where a pump exhales, the swarms part. Where a piston drops, the herds surge forward to drink.

Predators move through the gaps. You see them only in flashes: a long low body sliding beneath a lake surface, a jointed thing stalking the edge of a channel, the ripple of something large passing just beneath the crust. The ecosystem is saturated; there is no empty space between one presence and the next. Every inch of air and water and ground is occupied, contested, in motion.

**Light and sound**

The light is dim, a low diffuse grey-gold that seems to come from everywhere at once. The sun is a pale disc behind a permanent ceiling of cloud, and it casts almost no shadows. Distances are hard to judge. Objects shrink into haze at a few hundred meters, and the plain seems to fold back on itself at the edges, so that the far pumps appear sometimes nearer, sometimes farther, than they should.

The sound is loud, layered, and never quiet. The pumps sigh and thud. The fauna clicks, hisses, sings, chatters in a dense chorus that rises and falls with the swarms. Water drips and rushes. Gears grind somewhere under the earth. The sounds do not always arrive in order. A thud can come before the piston moves. A chorus can seem to continue after its creatures have gone silent.

**Reality breakdown**

This is where the place frays. The water in the lower lakes sometimes reflects a sky that is not the sky above it, showing a different light, a different cloud pattern, occasionally a different set of machines, as if the pools were windows onto a neighboring version of this same plain. Walking across a particular crust-plate, you may find that the cracks in the ground have rearranged since you last looked, the polygons forming a new pattern you can't quite recall from a moment before. Sound lingers in the air like a stuck record. Some pumps seem to take their rhythm from the animals rather than the reverse. Once, from the corner of your eye, a steady column of machine-smoke appears to bend around something that is not there.

No one who walks the Reach comes away certain of what they saw. The place does not hide its strangeness; it simply declines to hold still long enough to be fixed in memory. You remember the rhythm of the pumps, the wet cold, the packed and clamoring life, and the feeling that the whole plain is a single slow engine, grinding the ancient stone into finer and finer dust, while the world it serves watches from behind its low grey sky.
