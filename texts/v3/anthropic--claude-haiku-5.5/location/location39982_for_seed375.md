**The Drained Basin of Ossary**

The sea left four winters ago, or four hundred years ago. The ground can't say which. What it shows is a floor of pale salt, plated and fresh, with edges still sharp where the crust split as the water went down. The plates lift at their rims like the lips of shallow dishes, and the wind has not yet worn the corners down. Nothing here has had time to become soft.

The basin is low, a shallow bowl perhaps ten kilometers across, ringed by a rim of bare grey shale that rises only a few meters above the floor. Light arrives thin and sourceless, as if through a sheet of tracing paper. The sky is an even pewter, and the sun, when it shows, is a smear the colour of weak tea. Shadows are faint and blurred at their edges. Everything seems to be seen through a little haze, and the haze never fully lifts.

The air is cool and dry, with a bite in it, and it smells faintly of struck flint and something mineral and sweetish, like old copper coins. At night the temperature drops and the salt begins to drink from the air. Hygroscopic crusts pull moisture out of the cold, and by dawn every surface shines with a thin brine skin. The plates go slick and dark for a few hours, then the sun dries them back to chalk. This cycle repeats every day, and it is the closest thing this place has to a heartbeat.

**The Settlement**

Standing in the middle of the basin is a city. It is not ruined in the usual sense. It is layered, built in stacked tiers, and it looks as if it were built on purpose by hands or by something with hands' patience. Its walls are a pale composite of gypsum, halite, and fired clay, laid in courses that curve inward. The courses are not quite level, and the spacing between them follows a rhythm that is almost but not quite regular, the way a heartbeat is almost regular.

There are no roofs. Instead there are domed cisterns, many of them, their mouths open to the sky. They are dry, and their interiors are crusted with salt rosettes like frost. Streets run between the terraces, narrow and stepped, and they converge on a central mound that rises some twenty meters above the floor. The mound is hollow, and its interior is honeycombed with passages.

Nothing in the city is built to be entered in any ordinary way. Doorways are present but have no thresholds, and some open onto a blank wall of the next building. Windows are placed at odd heights, some at the level of a crouching body, others high up near the ceiling of a chamber that has no visible ceiling. Staircases end in mid-air at their tops and resume in unexpected places. This is not a labyrinth designed to confuse. It is more like a mind's attempt at a city, or a city drawn by someone who had heard about cities and never seen one.

The walls are also growing. Along the lower courses, fine crystalline fingers push outward from the mortar, slowly. They extend perhaps a centimetre a month, and at their tips they curl inward like tendrils. Where they touch the walls of another building, they fuse. Over the years, streets have narrowed and doorways have closed, and in a few places whole buildings have become solid blocks of interlocked crystal. The city is still making itself.

**Life**

Vegetation is sparse. It appears as low grey-blue mats clinging to the rims of the salt plates and in the cracks where the crust has split. These halophytes are succulent and hairless, and they are unusually patient. They open their leaves only during the night dampness and fold them tight by mid-morning, and in the brief wet hours a sheen of moisture runs along their edges and drips into the soil. A few upright stalks grow in the lee of the city walls, where the wind is weakest. Their tips are tipped with white spherical pods that rattle faintly when the dust moves.

Fauna is rare, and most of it is hard to see. There are pale burrowing things, blind and segmented, that push through the salt in long, shallow tunnels and leave raised ridges behind them. There is a species of small dark beetle that rides the dust devils, clinging to the surface film of the air currents as they cross the basin, and drops off at the edges. Something larger moves on the rim at dusk, low and quick, but it never stays in sight long enough to be described. Its tracks, a line of paired impressions in the crust, are the only evidence that it exists.

**Weather and Motion**

The basin is never still. Dust devils form in the early afternoon as the salt heats and rises, and they wander the floor for an hour or two before collapsing. Their paths are recorded in the crust as spiral scars, and the scars cross and overlap, so the floor is a palimpsest of old storms. The wind is almost always moving, carrying fine salt that scours the windward faces of the walls and deposits crystalline drifts in their lee.

The salt plates also move. They expand as they absorb moisture at night and contract as they dry, and this causes them to heave and crack. Some nights the floor sounds as if it is about to break open, though nothing audible happens. Some mornings a plate has lifted a hand's width higher than the day before. Over seasons, the whole basin is slowly tilting toward its eastern rim, and the city's central mound has begun to lean.

**Sound**

There is almost no sound. The wind makes a dry whisper across the salt. The crusts tick as they contract in the morning. Occasionally, the crystal fingers on the walls make a faint crackling as they grow. Otherwise the basin is silent in a way that feels deliberate, the silence of a space that has been emptied and not yet refilled.

**What It Means**

The place seems to be in the middle of a process that nobody started and nobody will finish. The sea is gone, the city is growing, the crust is rising, and the living things are small, quiet, and patient. Walking through it, one has the sense of arriving partway through a story whose earlier chapters are written in salt and whose ending is still being decided by a slow, cold, careful hand.
