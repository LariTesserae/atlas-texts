**The High Dry Lands of Ossuary Reach**

You arrive, or rather you find yourself already standing, on a plateau so high the sky has thinned to a deep, almost violet blue, with stars faintly visible at the zenith even under full daylight. The air is cold and clean. It carries no smell except a faint mineral sharpness, like struck flint or the inside of a cave. Each breath feels shallow, and your body asks for more than it gets.

**The ground**

The land was once mountains. Now it is a tableland worn so long and so patiently that the peaks are gone and only their roots remain, ground down to a surface that rolls in gentle, almost imperceptible undulations for hundreds of kilometers. The rock is pale grey and banded in rust and ochre, its layers exposed like the pages of a book read so often the edges have softened. Where the wind has scoured hardest, the stone is polished to a satin sheen. In the lower hollows, the ground is a salt pan, white and cracked into hexagonal plates that curl at their edges like drying leaves. Some plates are raised and hollow, singing faintly when the wind passes over them.

Nothing here is wet. There are no rivers, no lakes, no ice, only the dry memory of water in the form of evaporite crusts, mineral stains, and deeply carved channels so old they are rounded and smooth as bone.

**Vegetation and life**

Plant life clings to shelter. In the lee of boulders and inside the cracks of the salt plates grow low, grey-green mats, like lichen crossed with moss, that cling to the mineral crust and drink from the dew that forms in the cold nights. Some are woven into dense cushions only a hand's height, their surfaces dusted with crystal. Occasionally a taller form stands alone: a spindly, many-branched shrub with leaves like folded foil that turn slowly to track the sun. Their root systems reach far beneath the salt, tapping water that seeps through layers no one can see.

Fauna is rare and hard to find. Once, you catch sight of a creature the size of a hare, pale as the salt, that drops into a crack and vanishes. Higher up, on the stone ledges, there may be birds with brittle wings that circle on thermals and never seem to land. What lives here has learned to be small, quiet, and patient.

**The sky and the weather**

Here the weather is not a passing event but a resident power. Looking toward the horizon, you see a vast, slow wall of ochre dust and glittering crystal rising in the distance, forty kilometers away, its top spreading into an anvil that climbs past the tropopause. Lightning flickers inside it, but no thunder reaches you. It is not raining. Instead, the storm drops fine crystalline snow, and the snow sublimates before it touches the ground, so the sky itself seems to sparkle and dissolve.

Closer, smaller whirlwinds dance across the plain. Some are only a few meters across; others are hundreds, columns of salt and dust that wander, merge, and split apart. Their paths seem to follow lines in the ground that you cannot see, as though the terrain is writing instructions that only the wind can read.

Light is clean and strong, unfiltered by much air. Shadows are hard-edged and cobalt-tinted. At dusk the salt flats catch the low sun and glow amber, then rose, then a cold steel blue as the temperature drops fast.

**The built and the half-made**

Scattered across the plateau are structures, though calling them buildings may be generous. Small, low forms rise from the ground at intervals of several kilometers: squat domes, lattice towers no taller than a person, arches that span nothing. Their surfaces are a fine, ceramic-looking material, pale and faintly translucent, and they seem to be made of the same stone as the ground. Some have hairline cracks that have healed over; others appear partially built, with ribbed sections that end in rough, unfinished edges.

The strangest thing is that the structures change. Return to a dome after a few days, and its profile may have shifted, a new arch extended or a tower folded down into a low mound. Nothing here seems to be finished or abandoned. It looks as if something is constantly rebuilding and tearing down, the way a river cuts and deposits at once, or the way a coral reef grows and dies in the same tide. Walking among them, you sometimes hear a faint ticking, as if the material is cooling or settling, and occasionally a low hum rises from beneath the ground and fades.

Whatever engineered this land is not gone. It is still working, perhaps to shape the storms, perhaps to rebuild the mountains that wind and time keep taking away. Current physics has no clean name for the process. You can see the effects clearly: the way the dust settles in exact spirals around certain stones, the way the salt crystals orient toward invisible fields, the way the sky sometimes flickers with pale arcs that resemble auroras but move in directions auroras never do.

**Sound and silence**

The silence here is profound and almost physical. The thin air carries sound poorly, and nothing moves except the wind and the occasional shift of salt plates. You hear your own heartbeat. You hear the faint sizzle of sublimating crystals in the air, a sound like frying sugar too far away to see. You hear the ticking of the built forms, and once, from very far off, a deep, slow tremor that might be thunder from the storm, or might be something larger shifting its weight.

**The rules of the place**

- Water is absent, and so is almost everything that depends on it. Life survives by absorbing dew, deep roots, and the moisture of the storm's sublimating snow.
- Change is the only constant. Structures rise and fall, storms grow and collapse, and the ground itself slowly redraws its lines. Nothing stays the same for long, but nothing is ever truly destroyed. It is simply rearranged.
- Height is the defining fact. The air is thin, the cold is steady, and the land has been planed down by ages into a single enormous, ancient surface. Its age is written in every rounded edge and every faded layer.
- The land is alive in its own, mineral way. Its storms, salt, and built forms are a single system, and whoever once shaped it left the process running.

You walk on, and each step leaves a faint print in the salt that begins to soften and fill in as you watch.
