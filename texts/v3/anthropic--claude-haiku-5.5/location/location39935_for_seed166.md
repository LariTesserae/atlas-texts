**The Drowned Quarry**

The basin is roughly the size of a village square, walled on every side by pale, terraced stone. From the surface there is nothing to see but a flat plane of water, still as glass, with no shoreline you can name. Below it lies a room of water. The light comes down from above in a soft, even sheet, not bright, not dim, and it breaks into slow-moving bands that lie across the bottom like the reflections of a ceiling that isn't there.

**The Water**

It is cool, a clean grey-green that deepens to slate where the walls drop away. Visibility is good, perhaps twenty or thirty body-lengths, and the far stone terraces fade into a faint blue haze. The water is still, but not dead. A slow turnover moves through it, so the particulates drift in long, lazy curves instead of settling. Fine silt hangs in suspension and catches the light as it passes, so the whole volume seems faintly luminous, like the inside of a pearl.

**The Walls**

The quarry was cut into a pale limestone that has weathered into layered benches. Each bench is a single stratum, a shelf of fossil-speckled rock with a crisp, angular lip, and between them the faces are scalloped by long-gone blasting and then softened by centuries of water. Where seepage crosses the upper walls, mineral deposits have formed creamy travertine curtains that hang down in folds and have set hard. Along the lower terraces the rock has taken on a dark, glossy patina, a manganese skin that shines when the light crosses it.

**The Machinery**

This is the strangest part. Across the basin floor, half-buried in sediment, lie the remains of what was once an extraction plant: a long conveyor frame lying on its side, a row of ore-crushing jaws reduced to rust-brown bones, a squat tank whose seams have swollen closed, and a gantry arm that reaches out over the water like the neck of some enormous, patient heron. None of it is built anymore in any meaningful sense. It has been absorbed into the basin's geology. Sediment has packed around every joint, mineral crusts cover every flat plate, and in places the metal has fused with the stone into one continuous form, so you cannot quite say where the quarry ends and the machine begins.

Yet something still runs. From a squat housing on the far terrace, a pump draws a steady thrum through a pipe that rises from the floor, circulates across the basin, and returns. It never stops and it never changes its rhythm. Every few minutes a valve on the gantry arm opens and closes with a soft pressure-clack, releasing a short plume of fine bubbles that climb in a column and disperse at the surface. The cycle is identical each time. It has been identical for as long as anyone has measured it, and the water has long since stopped reacting to it.
