# The Stepped Highlands of Oruun

The land climbs in terraces, like a staircase begun by giants who never finished it. From the lowlands you see a wall of dark basalt draped in green, a cliff so tall its top vanishes into the white glare. Above it lies the plateau, several kilometers up, where the air should be cold and thin. It is hot. The sun comes down through rarefied air with nothing to soften it, and the rock gives back heat the way a kiln wall does after a day of firing. The heat pools in the basins and seeps from geothermal seams that breathe steam through the cracks.

## Terrain

The plateau is broken into blocks, each tilted a few degrees so that every surface drains toward a shallow sump. Erosion has worked here for an enormous span of time. Gullies are smoothed into rounded channels, mesa tops are worn like old knuckles, and nothing has an edge. Crusts of lichen in ochre and bruised violet cover the stone, and where the crust has flaked away the rock beneath is glassy and dark, polished by centuries of running water.

## Water

The air is saturated. Each dawn fog lifts from the sumps and hangs at mid-height, then condenses through the day onto leaves, which shed it in steady drips. The sound of this dripping is the base note of the place. Rock weeps too. Dark seams sheet down the cliff faces and collect in terraced pools whose surfaces grow mineral skins that shimmer with thin rainbows. At the bottoms of the blocks the water settles into marsh: reed beds, standing black pools, and floating mats of vegetation that rise and sink with the pulse of the steam vents.

## Vegetation

The growth is dense and tangled. Broad-leafed trees stand on root buttresses taller than a person. Ferns unfurl fronds the size of canoes. Vines hang like ship's cable from canopy to floor. Light reaches the forest floor as a green-gold murk, and the air smells of wet bark and something sweeter, like overripe fruit left in the sun. Flowers cluster in heavy pale heads that open only at dusk and release a scent that pulls moths in dense spirals.

Higher on the exposed ridges, plants have adapted to the altitude. Low cushion-forms hug the rock, and succulent giants store water in translucent columns that glow faintly amber when the sun passes behind them.

## Light and Sky

The light is ordinary and bright, though it has an unusual quality. Noon shadows are hard and black, and glare comes off every wet surface. Dawn lasts a long time because the fog scatters the sunlight into a pale gold haze that takes an hour to thin. Overhead the sky is an intense blue that fades to white at the horizon, and sometimes a second, fainter haze of cloud drifts at a lower altitude than the plateau, as if the highlands are breathing out.

## Sound

The place is loud. Insects hum in such numbers that the air carries a constant shimmering drone, pitched like a plucked wire held under tension. Waterfalls spill from the cliff edges in roaring veils. Birds call with sounds like shattered glass or meshing gears. At dusk the frogs begin, deep resonant chords rising from the reed beds in call-and-answer that carry for kilometers across the terraces.

## Fauna

Life is abundant. Flying things move in clouds, and at dusk they thicken into living columns over the marshes. Broad-backed grazers wade the shallows, their hides armored with overlapping plates that catch the light. Predators stalk with long patience, often standing motionless for hours in the reed shadows. Iridescent beetles cling in dense colonies to tree trunks, forming a living bark that ripples as they shift. In the deepest pools a long-necked filter-feeder moves slowly, dipping its head beneath the mineral skins and surfacing with its gills working.

## Built Remains and Technology

Scattered through the plateau are remains of something that does not fit the land's scale or timeline. There are slender columns of grey material that is neither stone nor metal. It stays cool to the touch even at noon, and its surfaces have no visible seams. Some columns stand in the marsh with their bases sunk below the waterline. Others lie cracked open like seed pods, hollow inside, with fibrous lattices grown through them by plants that have colonized the interiors. Certain columns hum at a pitch felt in the teeth rather than heard.

Near the steam vents are low domes whose surfaces gather moisture and channel it into grooves running downslope. The grooves are etched with patterns that repeat at scales smaller than a fingernail. These devices appear to pull water from the air, or to move heat, by means no one has mapped. Mist rises in vertical columns above some of them and drifts away from the sumps, which should be impossible under the prevailing currents. Some devices shimmer when viewed directly, as though the local rules bend slightly around them.

Across one gorge hangs a broken arch, a ribbon of the same grey material. It is half dissolved into the cliff face, with moss and lichen creeping over it at a glacial pace.

## Strangeness

Certain pools reflect a sky that does not match the one overhead. Their clouds move in the wrong direction, or hold shapes no weather would produce. The steam vents occasionally freeze in midair for a few seconds, then resume as though nothing happened. Some animals will not cross particular flat stones, and no one has found a reason. On a few west-facing slopes, shadows lag behind the sun by about a minute.

## Rules and Patterns

Heat rises from the ground more than the sun adds to it, so the plateau stays hot even when cloud covers the sky. At night, water seems to climb the grooved domes against gravity, drawn upward in thin silver threads. Plants cut from the forest regrow in the same shape within a season, and no fire will catch in the lower mists, however dry the wood appears.

## Dynamics

Change here is measured in long durations. The steam vents pulse on cycles of decades, and their rise and fall reshapes the marsh edges by a few meters per century. The lichen grows roughly an inch per hundred years. The grey structures decay so slowly that a crack visible one season will look almost identical after a human lifetime. Animal migrations follow pulses that take years to complete, and the forest itself seems to be in a slow, patient equilibrium, where each fallen tree is replaced by another exactly where it stood, on a timescale only the stones can measure.
