# The Drowned Shelf

You arrive at the shelf through cloud that is also water. There is no clear line where the air ends and the sea begins. The light is ordinary, a flat grey-white daylight that falls from everywhere at once, but everything it touches is wet, and the wet moves. The air hangs thick with suspended droplets, fine as flour, and they drift across your face in slow currents you can feel on your skin. Every breath tastes of stone and cold minerals.

The shelf is a plateau of raw rock stacked high on the flank of a mountain that is no longer visible. Its surface is young. The basalt is still settling into itself, a dark, glassy-grey stone with a faint sheen of fresh mineral crust. Cracks run through it in branching lines, some hair-thin, some wide enough to lose a hand in. Along the cracks, the rock is still shedding fragments. Plates the size of doors peel away and slide with a long grinding sigh, and the new edges are sharp and pale. In places the rock looks unfinished: slabs stand half-lifted from the bed, bulging like blisters, and fine sand pours from their undersides in thin, steady streams. Nothing here has had time to weather. The whole place still looks like something being made.

Water is everywhere, and it is not still. A sheet of it moves over the shelf at a depth of a few centimeters, never quite the same depth twice. It pours in braided channels that split, rejoin, and abandon old routes, carving new ones in the time it takes to count to thirty. Where two channels meet, they throw up fans of spray that hang in the air and fall in glittering curtains. The channels follow fault lines, and certain lines run uphill. Water climbs a slope for ten or fifteen meters, sheets over a ridge, and then slides back down the far side, as though some pull below the rock were reversing itself. No one has explained this to you, and you suspect the shelf would not explain it if asked.

Above all of this, water falls. It does not come in rain so much as in a permanent, heavy drift, a wall of droplets that thickens into sudden downpours and thins to mist again, roughly every few minutes. When the heavy rain comes, the sound changes from a roar to a hammering. Drops strike the basalt, the pools, the stacked stones, and each impact rings. The stones ring at different pitches, some high and bright, some low and wooden, so the downpour sounds like a played instrument. Between the rain bursts, the roar of the channels fills everything. You cannot hear your own footsteps or your voice unless you shout, and even then the words land a half-second late, because the echo from the far cliffs arrives before the sound of your own lips has finished. The cliffs that bound the shelf are invisible in the murk, but their echoes come back sharp and fast, like something answering from the wrong side of a wall.

The vegetation is sparse but stubborn. Grey-green lichen crusts the north faces of the stones in thin, leathery mats that crack when they dry in the rare lulls. Moss clings in the shelter of cracks, dark and springy. In the lee of the larger boulders, a few tufted grasses have found footholds in pockets of silt. They bend flat under the flow and rise again, combed and gleaming. Nothing here grows tall. Nothing here has room to spread.

Fauna is rare, and what there is is hard to see. In the deeper pools, pale, blind creatures the length of a finger drift just beneath the surface, pressing their bodies against the current. They move in slow spirals and vanish at the first ripple. Once, something larger passes below a sheet of water, a flat shape the color of old bone, and then the channel swallows it. Birds, if there are birds, do not show themselves. The only signs of life that persist are the pools' small trembling rings, and the way the lichens sometimes look as if they have been gnawed.

Then there are the marks of people, or of something that used tools. They are scattered and few, and you find them in the hollows where the water pools less violently. A cairn of flat stones stands waist-high. Its layers are carefully fitted, but it leans, and one side has slipped into a fresh crack. Nearby, a ring of stones lies half-buried in silt, its inner edge notched at regular intervals, as if someone once tied something here and then left. Further along, a weir of split wood and stone spans a narrow channel, funneling the current toward a basin. Some of its sticks have rotted; others are fresh and pale. Who built it, and when, you cannot tell. The water has eaten most of the edges. Here and there a chipped stone tool sits in the silt, its edge dulled, its shape deliberate. Nothing was made to last. Everything was made to be used briefly and then to be left for the water.

You find the hollow almost by accident. It is a bowl in the shelf, perhaps the width of a small room, walled on three sides by overhanging basalt and open to the cloud on the fourth. Inside, the water does not rush. It collects in a shallow, gently turning basin, and the sound of the channels fades to a low, distant grumble. The walls are sculpted into smooth, dripping curves, and the drips land in a rhythm that is almost regular. A few lichen mats hang from the ceiling like fringed cloth. A stack of flat stones sits against the back wall, dry for once, arranged by something that wanted a place to rest. You could stay here for a while. The sound is reduced, but not gone. The water still moves on the other side of the rock, still rearranges itself, still rises and falls, and every so often a fresh slab shifts somewhere out in the murk and the whole hollow hums with the vibration.

Beyond the hollow, the shelf keeps changing. A new crack opens and drinks a channel dry. A sheet of basalt tilts, slides, and sends a wave of silt across the bed. The rain comes again, heavy, bright, hammering every stone into song. The water climbs one ridge and runs back down another. Somewhere out in the cloud, the cliffs answer your footsteps before you have taken them, and the shelf goes on building itself out of water and rock, loud and unfinished, in a world that seems to be only just beginning to decide what it will be.
