# The Sunken Mat

You arrive by stepping down. The ground at the rim is firm, a pale crust of raw silt that cracks under your heel like dried glaze, and then it gives way to a long slope of green that runs down and out, a few metres below the level you came from. The land here has not finished deciding what it is. Everything is sinking a little, or being pressed down, and the sinking is so slow that nothing moves while you watch it.

The light is the first thing that fills you. It comes from a white sky without a visible sun, bright enough that the eyes water, and it lands on everything with equal force. Nothing casts a deep shadow. The leaves below you are pale, almost silver on their upper surfaces, and they reflect the sky back up so the whole basin glows from underneath, as if you are standing in the lit interior of a lantern.

The heat is thick and wet. It doesn't rise off the ground in shimmers. It settles, and it sits in the hollow like warm water in a bath. Your clothes would be soaked through within the hour, though nothing is raining. The moisture comes from everywhere at once: from the plants breathing, from the cool seep that rises in dark threads between the roots, from the condensation that beads on every surface and runs in slow lines toward the lowest point, which is always somewhere below your feet.

## The Mat

The vegetation is not growing on the land. It has replaced the land. What you are walking on is a single living mat, several layers deep, woven from stems that are thick as cables and fibrous as old rope, from rhizomes that crawl across one another in tight lattices, and from low fleshy cushions that give under pressure and then slowly rebound. Where the mat is thin, you can see the raw grey substrate below it: a fine-grained, unweathered stone, still crumbling at the edges, with sharp crystalline faces that have not yet been rounded by water or time. Where the mat is thick, you sink to the ankle and feel something cool and firm beneath.

The plants are of no single kind. Some are tall ribbed columns, hollow and perforated, that rise from the mat like the pipes of an organ, each one capped with a cluster of broad leaves that funnel water downward. Others are low rosettes packed so tightly they form a continuous skin. Between them grow climbing vines that have wrapped around the columns and braided into hanging curtains, and these curtains move very slightly, though there is no wind. Their movement is a slow, internal swaying, the way the surface of a dense liquid trembles when something heavy is shifting far beneath it.

The plants are also architecture. Where a column meets a horizontal rhizome, the join has thickened into a lattice of bowed arches, and the arches have grown into one another until they form low rooms, passages, and recesses that a person could crawl into. Some of these hollows are lined with a pale, waxy membrane that holds water in still pools the colour of weak tea. Others are dry and smooth as the inside of a shell.

## The Marks of Others

Scattered across the basin, at irregular intervals, are small built things. They are easy to miss because the mat has taken most of them. A low wall of fitted stone runs for three metres before disappearing beneath a surge of green. A flat slab sits at the centre of a ring of upright stones, and its surface is pitted with shallow channels that once, perhaps, carried water in a pattern. Near one of the columns is a niche, cut into the living wood, that holds a small cluster of smooth pebbles arranged by size, from largest to smallest, in a line that leads to nothing.

These marks are not recent. The stone has the same raw quality as the substrate, and the mat has grown through the joins and the cracks so completely that in places the wall and the plant are indistinguishable. Nothing here seems to have been built to last, and nothing seems to have been abandoned. The marks are simply part of the slow settling, the way sediment holds the impressions of creatures that passed over it and then were covered.

You find a doorway, or what might have been one: a rectangular gap between two rhizome-arches, its threshold worn smooth by feet, though whose feet you cannot guess. Inside it is cool and dim, and the pale membrane on the walls has been scratched, long ago, with a pattern of short parallel lines that repeats along the curve of the chamber.

## Sound

The basin is mostly quiet. What sound there is arrives in small, separated events. Water drips from the upper leaves into the pools below, each drop a clear, round note that you can count if you wait. The seed pods along the columns split with a dry click and release a fine dust that drifts and settles. Somewhere beneath the mat, something moves, a slow pressure and release, like a heavy breath taken under a blanket. There are no birds. There are almost no insects. The silence between the drips is deep enough that you begin to hear your own pulse, and then the blood in your ears, and then nothing at all.

## Fauna

Creatures are rare, and when they appear they seem almost accidental. A pale, segmented thing, no longer than a finger, crosses the surface of a pool and leaves a trail of rings that spread and fade. A small mass of translucent jelly clings to the underside of a leaf, pulsing very slowly, and does not respond when you approach. Once, in the far distance, something tall and grey stands at the edge of the mat for a long time without moving, and then is simply not there anymore, and you cannot say when it left.

## The Strangeness

What makes this place unusual is its refusal to change. The light has not shifted since you arrived. The drops fall at the same interval. The sway of the vines, the glow of the leaves, the thickness of the air all hold at a single level, held in an equilibrium so complete that you begin to feel it as a pressure, a kind of stillness that is not quite calm. The raw stone at the edges is still crumbling, but you cannot see it happen. The mat is still growing, but you cannot see it grow. Everything is in the middle of becoming, and has been for a long time, and will be for a long time more.

If you walk out toward the far rim, the basin continues, a single unbroken mat rolling to a horizon of low, broken ridges. The ridges are the same raw grey as the substrate. They, too, are sinking very slowly into green. You could walk for days and see only more of the same: the glow, the heat, the drip, the curtains of vine, the scattered walls, the pale hollows where something once knelt and scratched the walls.

You turn back. The rim is where you left it, and the path you made in the mat is already closing behind you, its stems springing upright, leaving no mark that the basin will remember.
