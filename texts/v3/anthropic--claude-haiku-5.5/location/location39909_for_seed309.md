**The Sunken Works at Harrow Deep**

You arrive by a stair cut into the lip of a basin that was once a street. The city above has slumped and grown over, and the descent takes you through a green throat of vines and hanging roots. Halfway down, the air changes. It turns warm and wet, and the sound hits you all at once.

The basin is roughly the size of a small town square, perhaps two hundred meters across, and about thirty meters deep. Its walls are poured concrete and riveted iron, stepped in terraces where galleries once ran between machine halls. Nearly all of it is now plant. Vines, aerial roots, and something like a bamboo-fern hybrid climb every surface and bridge the gaps. The vegetation is not decorating the ruin. It is the structure. Trunks have grown up through the old floor grates and lifted the iron into new arches. Bundles of stem fiber, some as thick as a man's torso, run along the old pipework and have hardened into ribs that brace the walls. Where a boiler once stood, a single tree has grown so large that its roots have taken the boiler's shape, and the bark has absorbed the rivets so that they look like scales.

The light is dim and green-brown, filtered through a canopy that closes over the basin's mouth about twelve meters up. Shafts of pale daylight come down in ragged columns, and they are full of drifting seed-fluff and insects. Below the canopy the air is thick and humid, and the walls sweat. Condensation gathers on the broad leaves and drips in a steady patter that never quite stops. In places it rains for real. The canopy holds water until it overflows, and a warm downpour falls for a few minutes, sends steam rising off the warm iron, and then stops as abruptly as it began.

The sound is the first thing you notice and the last you stop noticing. The machinery is still partly running. Somewhere in the lower halls a pump thuds on a cycle of roughly eleven seconds, a heavy piston pushing against sealed water. Steam valves hiss and shriek in irregular bursts. Old conveyor belts grind on rollers that have rusted into place, and every so often a gear train engages and a cluster of pipes shudders. Over all of this is the fauna, which is overwhelming. Cicada-like insects saw in the canopy, but they are not alone: there are chittering things in the wall-cracks, clicking beetles in the root mats, frogs calling from the cisterns in a rolling chorus that rises whenever the pumps stop. Birds roost in the iron rafters and wake in noisy waves at dusk. Something larger moves in the lower galleries, heavy and deliberate, and its calls carry up the shafts like the lowing of a buried animal.

The creatures have learned the machines. The pump's eleven-second beat has become a clock for the insects. Their chorus swells in the gap after each thud and falls silent just before the next, so the whole basin breathes in time with the engine. The birds have built their nests inside the pressure-release valves, where the steam warms the eggs. A species of ground-dwelling mammal uses the conveyor tracks as a highway and times its crossings to the belt's rhythm. Predators have adapted too. Long-legged hunters wait beside the steam vents and strike at anything that flees the hiss.

Ecologically the basin runs on a loop. Plants draw water from the cisterns and from the condensation they make themselves, and they turn the old industrial heat into growth. Fungi thread through the iron and feed on rust. Insects pollinate the flowers that hang from the pipes, and the flowers' sap ferments in the warm pools into a thick sweet liquor that the larger animals drink and then stagger drunkenly along the gallery edges. Predators eat the insects, the birds eat the predators' young, and the carrion goes back into the roots. It is an enclosed ecosystem with no clear exit, and it has been running long enough that its edges have softened into one another.

The strangeness is in the details. There are no clean lines anywhere. Pipes curve and then disappear into trunks, and old warning signs are overgrown until the lettering reads like a single living word. In the deepest gallery a row of gauges is still functioning, their needles trembling in response to the vibrations of the roots beneath them. Someone, long ago, fitted a copper mesh over one of the cisterns, and the plants have grown through it in a dense lattice that now flowers in patterns that repeat in the mesh. The air smells of warm iron, wet bark, and something sweet and rotten, and it tastes faintly metallic.

Standing still in the middle of the basin, you can feel the place working around you. The pump thuds through the floor. The steam valves sigh. The insects pulse in their chorus, and a bird lands in the rafters above with a rattle of wings. Water beads on your sleeves. Somewhere overhead the canopy shifts and a warm drop falls onto the back of your neck. The machine and the forest are still in the middle of an argument, and neither has won.
