The basin lies at the top of a high plateau, though nothing in it looks like height. It is a bowl of still, warm water, roofed by a dark that is not quite night. A little light seeps down from somewhere far above, maybe through a crack or a gap in the rim, and it fades within a few body-lengths. Everything visible is lit faintly from within the water itself.

The water is warm, close to bathwater, and very clear where it is not murky. Stand at the margin, where the shore becomes a stair, and you can feel a faint upward current that smells of wet clay and something sweetly mineral, like struck flint. Nothing grows here. There is no reed, no moss, no algae. The surfaces are bare, and where anything has settled it is a crust, not a living mat.

Everything is made. The terraces that step down into the basin are cut and laid stone, fitted without mortar and worn smooth by time. Their edges have rounded like old soap. Beneath them run channels, troughs, and cisterns, and a labyrinth of small chambers, all shaped by hand with simple tools: chisels, rubbing stones, fired clay pressed into molds. The tool marks are still visible at the lowest levels, parallel scratches that have not been softened. The makers are gone. Their work has settled into its final form, and the erosion that follows is now slow and nearly flat. Ridges that were once sharp have become gentle curves, and the carved reliefs on the upper walls have blurred into suggestions. Some panels show repeating figures, a row of open hands or a spiral that might be a count or a pattern for weaving. Their meaning has dissolved along with their edges.

The fauna is abundant and dense. The water is full of small, patient creatures that have colonized the constructions. Pale shelled things cling to the stone in layers, so thickly that the surface looks quilted. Thin, translucent bodies drift between the terraces, some the length of a finger, some longer and ribboned, that pulse very slowly and sometimes gather into loose clouds that turn in unison and then disperse. Along the channel floors, small grazers move across the crust, leaving pale furrows that fill in again within a season. Many creatures seem to feed on the thin films of mineral and organic residue that coat the built surfaces. They do not need plants. The stone itself is their pasture. Some have carapaces patterned with the same spirals and hands as the carvings, which might be coincidence, or might be a long, unhurried mimicry.

The sound is almost nothing. The water absorbs most noise. There is a faint tick and tap of shells on stone, a soft hush of moving water through the channels, and sometimes a low note, a hum, when a cistern gate shifts or a shelf of old stone settles another fraction. Speech would die in a few steps. Your own breathing sounds loud.

The weirdness is quiet but persistent. The stone is faintly warm to the touch and seems to hold the light, so that its glow lingers after the source has gone. Some of the clustered fauna pulse in step with each other across the whole basin, a rhythm so slow it takes minutes to notice. Water levels rise and fall by a few finger-widths over whole days, as if the entire structure breathes. Nothing here moves fast. Change happens over generations of creatures and the long weathering of stone, and the only visible events are the drift of a cloud of pale bodies, the slow crumbling of a rim, and the gradual filling of old channels with fine silt.

Along the upper terraces there are dry niches, hollows that never flood, filled with fine sediment and lined with fired tiles. They might once have held offerings, tools, or stores of food. Now they hold only the sediment and the faint marks of small creatures that have come up to shelter and then gone back down. The deepest point of the basin is a square well, very dark, with a circular opening at its center. The water there is still, and no light reaches the bottom. Its rim is the most worn part of the whole structure, polished by ages of hands that no longer come.

This is a place where making has outlasted the makers, where the water keeps everything warm and slow, and where the abundant small lives treat the made world as their only land.
