**The Drowned Shelf of Hesk**

You are a long way down, and the water is the whole world. It is not a sea you float on or a lake you see across. It is a medium that presses on everything from every side, and it is never still. Above, far off and faintly lit, there might be a surface, but you can't see it and the place doesn't care whether it exists.

The light is almost nothing. A dim, grey-blue wash reaches down from very far above and dies within a few body-lengths. Most of what you see, you see because it glows. Thin filaments of cold light crawl along the surfaces of rock, pulsing slowly like breath. Specks drift in the water and flare briefly as they pass through the current, then go out. Below, to the lower edge of the visible dark, a single faint orange smear pulses where a vent breathes heat into the cold. The orange is the only warm color here, and it is far away.

The ground is a shelf, an enormous plateau that has been worn down for longer than anything has been alive on it. Its surface was once jagged. Now it is rounded, smoothed by ages of current and sediment, so that the old volcanic peaks have become long, low swells like the backs of buried animals. The rock is dark, nearly black, and furred with a pale silt that lifts in soft clouds at every touch. Where the silt has been scoured away, you can see banding in the stone, layer on layer, each one a record of some older sea. Cracks run across the shelf in patterns that look almost deliberate, polygonal and vast, and in them the cold collects in pools of heavier water that never mix.

Nothing is built here, and nothing has been for a very long time. There are no walls, no roads, no marks of making, except for a few flint-grey nodules scattered across the silt. Most are simply broken stone. A few have a sharp edge that is too regular to be an accident of pressure, flaked on one side, the way a hand might shape a tool. Some are half-buried in sediment. Whatever shaped them is gone, and the water has learned to move around them. Their edges have begun to soften.

The density of the place is what strikes you first and keeps striking you. Every cubic arm's length holds something: suspended grains of silt, drifting fragments of dead matter that rain down in a slow, endless snow, flecks of mineral that catch the glow and glint, threads of mucus-like film, and the particles of living things too small to see clearly. The water is thick with presence, like a crowded room where nobody is looking at you but everyone is touching you. You do not have a clear line of sight so much as a field of near-things, each one shifting.

And the current is never still. It comes from several directions at once. A long, cold drift flows steadily along the shelf, and it breaks against the swells and folds back into eddies that spin off into the dark. Where the drift meets the warmer rising water from the vent, the two collide in violent shears and boils. Silt plumes erupt upward in great slow columns, twist, tear apart, and settle again somewhere else. The shelf's whole surface seems to be re-arranging itself at a slow, relentless pace. Ridges appear overnight where a sediment bank has been pushed into a new shape. Pools open and close. The rock is old and stable in its bones, but the sediment on top of it is in constant upheaval, and that is the only kind of change this place knows.

Life is sparse, but it is present. Pale mats of bacteria cling to the rock in the lee of the swells, thick and quivering, feeding on the chemistry seeping up from below. Where the mats are thickest, they form low cushions, honeycombed with tiny channels, that pulse with the light they emit. Stalks of something like sea-glass rise from a few cracks, nearly transparent, bent by the current into long curves, each one tipped with a faint bloom. They do not grow quickly. Nothing here grows quickly. Their roots are spread so thinly across the rock that a single stalk may be the only one for dozens of body-lengths.

The animals are even fewer, and they are slow. Something drifts past, a pale translucent shape the size of a hand, trailing a cloud of glowing particles that it pulls from the water with a fan of filaments. It does not look at you. A longer, darker shape moves along the edge of the shelf far off, too distant to resolve, leaving a faint wake of disturbed silt. Most of the time there is nothing moving at all except the water and the drifting snow, and then something surfaces from the silt, pauses, and sinks back down as if it had never been there.

The sounds are few, and they are quiet, almost entirely below the threshold of hearing. A low, constant hum runs through the water, the pressure of the whole ocean settling on the shelf. Stone ticks and groans as it cools and contracts. Now and then a deep, distant knock comes from somewhere far below, perhaps a boulder shifting on the slope, perhaps the vent pulsing against its chimney. Sediment whispers as it slides down a flank. Between these sounds is a silence that feels thick, not empty, as if the water were holding its breath.

It is cold, with the dense, unmoving cold of water a very long way below anything warm. The cold does not bite. It settles, and keeps settling, and the only heat anywhere is that orange pulse at the edge of sight, and it is far away, and it is not for you.

Nothing here knows you are present, and nothing here will notice your leaving. The shelf has been carved into its final shape by ages it does not remember. The stone is old, the silt is restless, the light is faint and pulsing, and the whole packed volume of the water keeps moving, keeps touching, keeps drifting, and keeps becoming something slightly different each time you look back at it.
