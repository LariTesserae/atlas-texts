**The Sweating Plain**

The light comes from everywhere and nowhere. A sun sits behind a permanent haze the color of weak tea, and it gives a dim, even brown-orange glow that flattens every distance. Shadows are soft and brief. Nothing casts a clear edge, so the eye keeps guessing at depth and getting it wrong. The air is hot and heavy, and it's wet in a way that's hard to name. Surfaces are always faintly beaded. Your skin feels as if it's been breathed on.

The land is low, a plain that might be a basin or a seabed that never finished draining. It is also not quite ground. The surface is raw, unconsolidated, a grey-brown silt that has the texture of drying clay and gives underfoot like wet bread. Where it's been disturbed it stays disturbed. Footprints fill slowly with seeping water and then harden into shallow bowls. Everywhere the substrate is young. Rills scratch new channels that wander a few meters and quit. Gullies form in an afternoon and collapse by evening. The geology is still deciding what it wants to be.

Almost all of it is made. Walls run across the plain in long, low courses, rammed earth and fired brick, some as high as a knee, some as high as a house. They don't meet at corners so much as approach them and stop. There are terraces stepping down toward shallow pans, each one edged with a lip of baked clay. Tall narrow chimneys, or perhaps cisterns, stand in clusters without any visible reason for their spacing. Their makers used hand tools: you can see the thumbprints in the plaster, the scoring of coarse adzes, the uneven courses of stones set by eye and not by measure. The technology is stuck at the level of simple fabrication, pottery, kilns, rope, wooden frames, and it has been pushed to its limits in endless quantity. Nothing here is industrial. Everything was made by hands, and most of it is now wet, sagging, or leaning.

Water is everywhere, but seldom as a river. It seeps. Brine and fresh water rise through the silt and collect in shallow pans whose surfaces show a constant trembling. The walls weep, beading on their faces and running down in thin dark streaks that leave pale mineral crusts behind. Pale white bloom spreads over old brickwork like frost that never melts. Condensation drips from the undersides of overhangs at a steady tick, and the ground under every wall is soft. Mist forms in the low places at every hour, thick enough to hide the next wall, then lifts into the haze without a breeze to move it.

The living presences are sparse and strange. There are mats of dark, slick growth that might be fungal or might be something with no name, spread in rings across the damper ground and turning a sickly violet where the light hits them at an angle. Bulbous glassy knobs rise from cracks in old masonry, translucent, faintly humming when touched, filled with something that moves slowly inside. None of it is green. None of it is clearly alive, though it responds, slowly, to water and to heat. Between these clusters there are long stretches of nothing at all. The plain holds a few things, widely scattered, and leaves the rest empty.

The sound is mostly the sound of water. Drips strike pans with a delicate, unhurried rhythm. Walls settle and tick as they drain. Silt slumps with a soft wet sigh. Every few minutes something far off gives a long low groan, a wall or a bank giving way, and then the quiet closes over again. There is no wind to speak of. Sounds carry strangely, flattened by the haze, so that a voice a hundred meters away can seem to come from underfoot.

The place is in constant storm-state. Nothing holds its shape for long. A wall you passed at dawn may be a mound of rubble by noon, its bricks carried off into a new silt bank, and at dusk a fresh wall may stand in its place, built from the same bricks by no one you can see. Pans appear overnight and drain in a week. Channels reverse. The terraces seem to rise and sink in a slow, unsettling breath. Sometimes a whole district tilts a few degrees and the walls shear along lines no one planned. Yet it isn't simple chaos. Wherever you look, something is being built, unbuilt, or half-finished, as if the plain is an argument between making and unmaking that never settles.

Reality bends at the edges of this. Corridors between walls are a little longer each time you walk them, or a little shorter, depending on which direction you take. A line of cisterns seen from one side is a different number of cisterns from the other. Doors open onto the wrong side of the wall. Sometimes the haze thickens into a shape that holds steady for a few moments and then lets go. Water runs uphill in certain places, and some pans reflect a sky that isn't the sky above. The old makers left marks: handprints in the plaster, carved signs that repeat at intervals too regular to be chance. No one reads them. No one remains to explain whatever they were making.

Walking through it leaves you with a particular kind of exhaustion, the sort that comes from never being sure where you are. The heat presses, the damp gathers in your clothes, the silt pulls at your feet. The walls rise and fall at the edges of your sight. Somewhere a drip keeps time. The plain is vast, and it doesn't care whether you cross it. It keeps building itself and tearing itself down, slowly, wetly, endlessly, in the dim light of a sun that never quite reaches the ground.
