**The Shelf of Condensers**

You arrive on a plateau that was never a mountain. It is a stepped tableland, roughly two thousand meters up, and every surface on it was poured, cast, rolled, or bolted into place by something that wanted a flat floor for a purpose. The floor is still there. Under it, in the cracks, you can see older layers, the ground that was here before, buried a few meters down and grey as ash.

**The ground**

The substrate is a poured composite, sand and slag and something that glitters when wet, laid in plates the size of city blocks. The plates are young. Their edges are still sharp, and the seams between them have not yet softened. Rain has been cutting channels into them for only a few centuries, so the runoff gullies are narrow and deep and clean-walled, like cuts in fresh cake. Where a plate has failed, it has failed cleanly, leaving stepped scarps you could climb with your hands. Shards of the composite lie in fans at the bottom, each one still angular.

Water is everywhere but not in lakes. It sits in shallow trays, in the hollows of old machine bases, in pits lined with corroded copper that have filled and crusted and filled again. Some of these pools are perfectly still and reflect the sky so cleanly they seem to be holes. Others drip constantly from overhead pipes into basins that overflow into troughs, which overflow into channels, which run for kilometers across the tableland and vanish into a grate.

**Structures**

Industry is the only thing that grows here. Condenser towers stand in ranks, each one a lattice of ribbed fins that catch fog and wring it into gutters. There are thousands of them, stretching to a horizon that is always slightly too close, because the land curves up in a way the eye does not expect. The towers have been running, at a glacial pace, for longer than anyone counted. Their fins are furred with a pale green crust that flexes when touched, a living mineral film that drinks the drip and leaves behind a faint sweetness in the air.

Between the towers run conveyors on raised gantries. Some still move, at the speed of a minute hand, carrying nothing but wet dust. Others have stopped mid-cycle, their buckets hanging open and full of rainwater, and birds bathe in them.

Some things are wrong here, in ways that accumulate rather than announce themselves. A staircase climbs three flights and then continues, unbroken, into the cloud, and its landing, when you reach it, is no higher than the ground you came from. A pipe runs downhill in a seam of light and empties into a basin that is somehow above it. A row of identical hangars recedes in perspective, and when you walk its length you find you have never left the first door. The weirdness is not violent. It is the feeling that the builders' geometry has loosened, that the plateau keeps a slightly different map of itself than the one you are standing on.

**Light**

The sun is close and hard. At this altitude the air is thin enough that shadows have edges like knives, and the wet composite throws the glare back up so that everything is lit from below as well as above. The mist catches the light and turns it into a bright, even haze that has no source. You squint constantly. Colours look washed to white and then, at dusk, abruptly saturated, as if someone had turned up a dial. The towers cast long lattice shadows across the plates that do not move until you stare at them for a while.

**Air and weather**

It is cool, around ten degrees by day and near freezing after dark. Rain comes in brief, fine bursts, and the rest of the time condensation does the work: the air is always half-water, beading on every surface, running down the fins, dripping from every wire. Your clothes stay faintly damp. Metal stays cold to the touch. The air smells of wet stone, ozone, and the sweetish film on the towers.

**Sound**

Little carries. The mist swallows distance, and the constant dripping is so pervasive that the ear stops registering it until it changes. The loudest sounds are small ones: a bucket tipping, the tick of cooling metal, the soft rasp of conveyor chains turning once an hour. Now and then a deep hum rises from somewhere under the plates, a long sustained note that fades so slowly you cannot tell when it ended. Birds are nearly silent. They communicate with clicks and low chirps, mostly from the gantries.

**Fauna**

Life here is dense and patient. Birds nest in the gantry girders and the hollows of condenser fins, shorebirds with long legs that wade through the trays and probe the sludge for something invisible. Small grazing creatures, six-legged and shelled like beetles, move in grey herds along the troughs, feeding on the green film and leaving clean trails across the crust. Something larger, pale and slow, climbs the lattice towers at dusk and sleeps wrapped around the girders, its skin patterned with the same mineral green so that it is nearly invisible. There are things under the plates too, in the cracks and cisterns, and you hear them more than you see them: knocking, scraping, a slow patient clicking like a clock someone forgot to wind.

**Rules of the place**

Water runs downhill, mostly. Machines keep running as long as there is water to feed them, and when the water stops they do not die so much as settle. Fauna depends on the film, the film depends on the drip, and the drip depends on towers that have been dripping for centuries. Nothing here is hurried. The plateau changes in decades and centuries: a plate slips, a tower leans a degree further, a new channel cuts through a seam, a family of birds moves from one gantry to another.

Walk long enough and you begin to notice that the far edge of the tableland is never reached, and that the structures nearest you are slightly newer than the ones farther away, as if the builders had been working outward from somewhere that is now behind you, and never quite finished the job.
