The basin runs out past the horizon in every direction, low enough that the far rims seem to hang in heat shimmer. It is young ground. The crust is pale, layered, and still settling, and the rock underneath is less stone than compacted sediment, sometimes soft enough that a foot leaves a print that slowly softens and closes. Ridges of fresh deposit rise and crumble at their edges. Where the last flash flood cut a channel, the walls are still raw, banded in grey, ochre, and white, and the gravel at the bottom hasn't yet been rounded. Nothing here has finished being geology.

Water is the scarcity that organizes everything. There are no rivers. The only standing water is a brine pan in the deepest hollow, a flat mirror rimmed with white salt crust that the animals approach carefully. Most of the moisture arrives as dew, which lies heavy on the broad leaves each dawn and drips in the long hours after sunrise. The land's real water is held in its life. Vines, succulents, and swollen-trunked trees store it, and the whole basin breathes it back into the air, so the humidity is thick and warm, the sky slightly hazy, and the afternoons bring towering cumulus that build over the canopy and rarely break.

The vegetation is almost absurdly lush. It has overgrown a land that has barely been allowed to exist. Roots thread through cracks in the young crust and bind the loose sediment, so where the plants thrive, the ground is held and the gullies stop. Where the roots fail, the erosion returns. The green is layered: ground mats of fleshy, dew-catching leaves; shrubs as tall as a person; trees with buttressed trunks and wide pale canopies that trap heat and rain; and above them, a few giant emergent species whose crowns catch the wind and sway like sails. Vines drape everything. Lichen-like mats climb the bare faces of the newest banks, which are the first colonists of rock that is still forming.

Built is the strangest feature, and the most crowded. Across the lowlands stand the mound-cities, built not by any tool-using people but by a colonial builder that works in a mixture of clay, salt, and secreted resin. Its towers rise in tiers, each generation of walls a new terrace on the old one, so the oldest sections have sunk and fused into dense, weathered plateaus, while the newest spires are still pale and damp. Galleries thread between them, corridors and ramps and vents that pull air through in slow cycles. The mounds are packed so tightly that their edges touch, and between them run lanes worn by generations of traffic into smooth, shining troughs. Inside the lowest levels, the walls are warm and humid and crowded with moving bodies. The city's roofs are the nurseries, open to the sun, carpeted in seedlings that the builders have planted and tended for their own gardens.

Fauna is dense in the way a crowded market is dense. Grazing animals move in long, shifting herds through the understory, their bodies brushing against each other, their heads down. Flocks of small winged creatures rise from the canopy in clouds and settle again, and insects form a constant haze that catches the light, rising off the warm brine pan and thickening over the mounds. Burrowers push soil into fresh cones at the edges of the lanes. Larger predators range along the herd edges, patient and low-slung, with the herds keeping close to the mound-cities for protection, which means the predators have learned to move along the gallery walls and through the vents. Everything is moving, and the movement never stops. Weather never stops either. Dust devils walk across the open flats. Wind pushes through the canopy in long, rolling waves, and the sky changes color and cloud by the hour.

Sound is low, muffled by the thick vegetation and the damp air. There is no thunder of large engines, no voice pitched above murmur. What carries is the soft tick of drops falling from leaves, the rasp of dry grass in wind, the crackling of growing vines, the hum of insects, the distant percussion of wingbeats, and a low, constant rustle from the packed bodies in the herds and galleries. Every sound is close and half-hidden. The silence of the place is a kind of thick, continuous, unintentional murmuring.

Light is ordinary and warm, filtered through humidity into a soft gold that lies long across the flats in the morning and evening. At noon the glare off the salt crust is hard enough to make the eyes water, and the shadows under the canopy are deep and green. Everything is saturated: colors bright in the leaves, the banded cliffs, the wet mound surfaces, the red-brown animals moving through the grass.

Nothing here is finished. The ground is still rising and crumbling, the mounds are still climbing, the vines still spreading into new cracks, and the herds still wearing new paths into the basin. The place is in the middle of a long, packed, noisy, slow-motion becoming, where living systems and young rock are building each other and tearing each other down at the same time.
