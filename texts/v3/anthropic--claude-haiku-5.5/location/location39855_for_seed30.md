**The Weeping Shelf**

The land rises here as a broad, tilted table, perhaps four kilometers across, its edges broken off into cliffs that look freshly cut. Nothing about the ground has finished deciding what it will be. The stone is young. In places it is still dark and glassy, like cooled slag, and in others it has the grainy, bruised look of wet clay that has started to harden. Hairline fractures run across everything, and water moves through them constantly. The rock weeps. Beads gather on its faces, slide together into threads, and drip off the lips of the ledges into pools that did not exist an hour ago.

The air is warm but heavy, and the light is dim, a bronze-gray haze that never quite becomes sunlight. The sun is a pale disc behind thick cloud, or a dull coin behind a veil of spray from the falls below the cliffs. Everything is faintly lit from no particular direction. Shadows are soft and brief. Moisture hangs in the air, and in the mornings a fog rises out of the cracks in the stone like breath from a sleeping animal, then burns off by midday, only to return at dusk.

**Ground and water**

The pools are the most active features of the shelf. They are shallow, clear at the edges, and cloudy at the centers, where fine silt rises in slow spirals. Their floors are a pale mineral crust that is still hardening. Walking across it, you hear a faint crackling, like frost breaking, and the crust gives slightly under weight before setting again. Some pools have grown a skin of thin gray film that wrinkles when the wind passes over it, and this film is not inert. When disturbed, it flows toward the disturbance and knits itself back together, leaving behind a smoother, harder surface. In a few places it has built up into low, ribbed ridges that look like the gills of a fish, and these ridges are slowly lengthening.

Water runs in new channels that did not exist yesterday and will be gone by next season. The land is rearranging itself in small, constant ways. A rivulet diverts when a fracture seals and opens another two meters to the east. A ledge slumps into a basin overnight. Rain comes in pulses, not steady showers but sudden heavy downpours that last ten or twenty minutes and then stop, and between them the shelf drains with a rushing, bubbling noise that rises through the stone itself.

**Vegetation**

Plant life is sparse, scattered across the shelf like punctuation marks. The commonest form is a low, pale mat, springy and tough, which spreads in rings around the edges of the pools and slowly drifts toward the water as the ground shifts. Its surface is studded with small, translucent bulbs that fill and empty in rhythm with the rains. A few taller forms rise on thin, reedlike stalks, leaning toward the moisture like sunflowers toward a light. Each has a single bladelike leaf at the top, and the leaves rattle softly in the wind, a sound like dry paper. Nowhere is there dense growth. Between the plants is open stone, and the open stone is where most of the life happens.

**Fauna**

The shelf is loud with animals, and the abundance is startling against the sparseness of the plants. The pools are full of small crustacean-like creatures with many jointed legs and bodies the color of wet slate. They click constantly, a dry, rapid ticking that rises and falls across the water like rain on a tin roof. When they are startled, the whole pool goes silent for a moment and then erupts in a rattling wave.

Above the pools, flocks of gliding creatures ride the warm updrafts from the cliffs. They have wide, membranous wings, veined and iridescent, and they call to one another in long, descending whistles that echo off the rock faces. At dusk the calls merge into a chorus that is almost unbearable in its volume, a layered, rhythmic din that seems to come from the stone itself. There is a deeper sound beneath it, a booming pulse from somewhere under the shelf, perhaps from creatures that live in the fractures and feed on the water moving through them. The booming arrives every few minutes and shakes loose pebbles from the ledges.

Predators are present but hard to see. Stone-colored hunters move low through the mats, tracking the crustaceans, and when one strikes, the pool's ticking breaks off abruptly. Carcasses are rare because the shelf absorbs them quickly: the mineral film wraps around dead matter and draws it into the stone within days, so the ground is littered with faint impressions of things that were once alive and are now becoming part of the rock.

**Built traces**

Something was here before, and something still works here, though nothing that exists now seems to have been built by the creatures on the shelf. Scattered across the plateau are small structures: squat plinths of dark metal, half-sunk into the ground, their surfaces etched with fine geometric patterns that shift when you look at them directly. Some are only the height of a knee. Others rise to the shoulder, and a few have collapsed into ring-shaped ruins whose interiors are filled with the same gray film that covers the pools.

The most striking of these are the condensers, lattice spires rising eight or nine meters out of the stone. They are open frameworks, delicate as wire sculpture, and their lattices are dense with fine filaments that catch the fog. Water collects on them in beads, gathers, and runs down the spires into collecting troughs at their bases, where it feeds the pools. They appear to be working. Some have fallen silent, their filaments dull and brittle, and the stone around them has grown over their bases in slow, lumpy swellings. Nobody seems to maintain them. The film does it instead, rebuilding broken filaments in patient increments, and the spires tilt a little more each season as the ground shifts beneath them.

Stranger still are the marks that are not structures at all: patterns of concentric grooves cut into the rock, too regular to be natural, too weathered to be recent. They radiate from the centers of the larger pools, and each one seems to be slowly widening.

**Rules of the place**

A few things seem to hold here. Stone grows where water runs, and it grows fast enough to be watched. Anything that dies on the shelf is absorbed into the rock within days, so the ground is slowly accumulating a record of everything that has lived on it. The film responds to disturbance, which means that the shelf is, in a loose sense, aware of being touched. The rains come on their own schedule, not the seasons', and they seem to be the pulse that sets the rhythm for everything else: the pools fill, the crustaceans multiply, the predators hunt, the flocks rise, the stone shifts, the spires drink, and then the water drains and the cycle begins again.

Silence never arrives here. Even on the calmest afternoon, something is clicking, dripping, whistling, or booming. The place is always becoming, and its noise is the sound of that becoming, too loud and too busy to be mistaken for emptiness, yet sparse enough that each presence stands alone against the open stone, weeping, listening, and slowly turning into a landscape that has not yet decided what it is.
