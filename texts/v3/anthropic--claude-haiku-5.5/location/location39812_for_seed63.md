**The Cinder Bowl**

You arrive at the lip of a shallow bowl, perhaps four hundred paces across, sunk into a high plateau. The air is thin and very hot, the way the air over a kiln is hot, and it smells of struck flint and something sweetish underneath, like overripe fruit left in a sealed room. The light comes from nowhere in particular. A grey overcast hangs low, thick with fine ash, and beneath it the basin holds a dim, brownish dusk that never brightens and never fully fades. Your eyes take a long time to adjust, and when they do, the floor starts to move.

**The ground**

There is no soil. The bowl is floored with a crust of black and ochre mineral: fused basalt in some places, pale sinter in others, and everywhere a fine glassy grit that crunches like sugar. Ridges of this material have been worn into long, even terraces, the marks of a very old and very patient water that has since gone. Those terraces are the only sign that anything here ever flowed. Now the bowl is dry. Its floor is hard and hot through the soles of your boots, and the rock ticks faintly as it cools in the thin air.

Across the basin, pale vents breathe from cracks in the crust. Each one exhales a slow column of white vapor that condenses at the rim and drips back as a thin, mineral-tasting film. That film is the only moisture in the place. It collects in shallow glazed hollows, brackish and warm, and the creatures crowd around these hollows like people around a well.

**The fauna**

This is the part you cannot prepare for. The bowl is alive, and it is packed.

The floor is not visible in any ordinary sense. It is covered by animals, layered and interlocked, so that the ground appears to be made of shifting bodies. Most are small, between the size of a fingernail and a fist. They have hard, banded shells, glossy as wet slate, in colors of rust, bruised violet, and bone. Some are flat and disc-shaped and grip the rock like lichen. Others stand on jointed legs in thick ranks, their bodies tilted into the wind of the vents. They do not seem to graze, because there is nothing to graze. They seem to scrape. Their mouthparts rasp at the mineral crust, and the grit they lift settles into fine dust on the backs of the creatures beside them.

The density changes the basin's whole texture. Nowhere is there bare rock. Animals sit on animals, and the shells have grown together into low, ridged mounds that rise to knee height in the warmest hollows. These mounds are not built. They are the accumulated shed skins and cemented bodies of generations, fused by heat and the sulfurous residue of the vents. Walking across them means stepping on a crust that gives under your weight with a soft, dry crackle.

Above the mounds, the air is busy. Winged things that look like folded paper, pale and translucent, drift in slow spirals above the vents and settle back down in clusters. Fat, segmented forms the colour of old brass move beneath the crust, and where they tunnel, the ground heaves and collapses in small, sudden craters.

**The built and the broken**

The bowl was shaped by something other than water, and it was also shaped by something that could build, though not for long and not for much. Near the western wall, six standing stones form a broken ring. They are smooth, worn almost to roundness, and inscribed with shallow grooves that run in parallel bands rather than letters. The grooves are filled with the same brown dust the creatures shed. Two of the stones have toppled. A third has split down the middle and the two halves lean toward each other like people about to speak.

Further in, at the centre of the basin, there is a low archway of dark stone, half-buried in a mound of shells. Its keystone is missing. Through the opening you can see a passage going down, and from that passage comes a faint, rhythmic tapping, as though something inside keeps time against the stone.

There are no tools here. Nothing in the bowl is made of metal or wire or fired clay. Whatever built the ring and the arch was made of the same stuff as the land, and the land has been reclaiming it for a long time. The grooves on the stones have started to fill in with the bodies of the creatures themselves, as if the animals have been slowly absorbing the structures, or the structures have been slowly becoming animals.

**Sound and silence**

The basin is mostly quiet, in the way that a room full of sleeping people is quiet. Under the faint hiss of the vents there is a steady, low noise: thousands of tiny mouthparts working the rock, a whisper that never stops and never rises. Occasionally a sharp click cuts through, one shell striking another, and the sound carries like a dropped pin. The tapping from the archway continues, slow and regular. Every so often a heavy, wet shudder runs through the mounds as a large animal shifts beneath them, and the entire basin seems to sigh.

**Rhythm and motion**

The place is not still. Its processes run on a cycle you can feel within a few minutes of standing there. The vents swell, then ease, pushing the vapor higher; as they peak, the creatures on the windward side begin to move, edging around the hot cracks, and the ones on the cooler flank retreat into the shadow of the mounds. When a vent falters, a wave of animals rolls across the floor toward whichever hollow still holds moisture, and the crowds at the water's edge thicken until the surface is hidden under a seething mass. Then the wave recedes, leaving bare, scraped rock and a scatter of cast-off shells.

Predation runs beneath all of this. The disc-shaped creatures wait motionless on the crust, and when a small walker passes too close, they lift their edges and fold over it, and the crust under them hisses and darkens. The fat brass forms burrow up beneath the mounds to take what falls through the cracks. Dead things are gone within hours, pulled down into the rock by a dozen small mouths. Nothing rots in the bowl. Everything is eaten, and the eaters are eaten in turn, and the cycle keeps the mounds growing.

**Leaving**

As you climb back toward the rim, the vapor from the vents rises past you, cool at the top and warm at the bottom. Looking down, the basin seems to breathe as one body. The ring of standing stones, the fallen arch, and the tapping in the dark passage are all sunk under the moving skin of the place, and it is hard to say where the creatures end and the architecture begins. The grey light doesn't change. The heat follows you up the slope for some distance before it lets go, and then you are on the high, cold plateau again, looking back into a bowl that is still very much awake.
