The hollow sits at the lip of a delta that is still deciding what it will be. Nothing here has been settled for long. A sheet of pale silt lies across the low ground, glossy where the water has just left it and cracked into plates where it has begun to dry. Every few hours, the river upstream lays down another film, and the land rises a hair's width. You can watch the edges of the flats creep outward if you stay still long enough, though most who come here don't stay still.

The light is always dim. A low lid of cloud sits on the canopy, and what sun gets through is green-gold and diffuse, the color of light through old bottle glass. The canopy itself is young and broad-leaved, plants with leaves the size of umbrellas, their undersides beaded with condensation that falls in slow, fat drops. The air is warm and thick, and it smells of wet clay, rot, and something sweet and resinous that comes from the seed-pods hanging low on the vines. Fog gathers in the troughs between the mounds each morning and burns off by midday, only to return as the light fades.

The mounds are the strangest thing here. They rise out of the silt in clusters, pale and fluted, some as tall as a person, some no taller than a knee. They are built, unmistakably. Their walls are fine-grained and striated in concentric rings, glazed on the outside where the damp keeps the surface sealed. Galleries run through them in spirals and branching chambers, and their openings are shaped like keyholes and slot-windows, angled to catch the moisture-bearing wind. No hand made them. Something secreted them, layer by layer, mixing river clay with a cement of root-fiber and saliva-like resin, and the colony keeps building as the silt rises. Some mounds are abandoned, their tops crumbling back into sediment. Others have fresh, wet seams where the builders were working the night before. Several are interconnected by low bridges of cured clay that arch between them like the shoulders of sleeping animals.

Life is abundant but spread thin. The space between presences is the first thing you notice: a fallen log, a frog the color of wet bark, then a long gap of silt before the next. Pale, long-legged insects walk the flats in dotted lines, following trails the colony has laid down with scent. Beetles with iridescent shells the blue-black of oil on water turn over the mud at the edges of the shallows. Things with too many joints move along the galleries' walls, and occasionally one stops, lifts two front limbs in a gesture that could be greeting or threat, and goes on.

The predators are patient. A long-necked, flat-headed thing lies half-sunk in a pool beneath the broad leaves, its eyes just above the surface, still for an hour at a time. When a trail-walker wanders too near the water's edge, the pool erupts in a splash so quick it is over before the ripples reach the shore. Above, small winged hunters dart in the canopy gaps, and the drops from the leaves sometimes carry the sound of something being carried away.

The sounds are few and soft. Water dripping from leaves onto leaves. The tick of silt settling as it dries. A scrape of claws on clay. Now and then a low, bubbling call from deep in the pool, and then nothing. The quiet is not empty; it's the quiet of a place where everything is listening and very little needs to be said.

Nothing here is finished. The river will keep laying silt, the colony will keep raising walls, the vines will keep sending roots into the new ground, and the predators will keep waiting in the pools. In a season, this hollow may be a forest. In a decade, it may be a flat, glassy plain with no trace of the mounds. For now it is an unfinished city in dim green light, quietly being made.
