The plateau rises about two thousand meters above the lowland haze, a broad table of dark, vesicular basalt cut by ancient rivers into terraces and gorges. The top is nearly flat for hundreds of kilometers, then breaks into stepped cliffs that drop in tiers toward a distant, invisible sea. Erosion here is old and finished. The gorges have long since reached their working profiles. Their walls are fluted and pitted, honeycombed by weather, and their talus slopes are stable under a crust of orange and grey lichen-like mineral film. Nothing green grows. A few rust-colored crusts cling to the sheltered faces, and the ground is bare stone, gravel, and the pale dust of old tuff.

Water is everywhere, though it rarely pools in the open. The plateau is wet the way a cellar is wet. Warm air rises off the lowlands, meets the cool stone of the heights, and condenses into a fine, persistent mist that beads on every surface. In the early morning the whole table is wrapped in cloud, and the cliffs weep. Seepage comes out of the rock in thin sheets and runs down the faces in threads that deposit gypsum and calcite, so every cliff is glazed with slow, wet mineral lace. Where the seepage collects on the flats, there are marshes: shallow, reed-less pans of brown water, coated with a skin of iron bloom, where the ground is spongy and the light turns copper at dusk. The marshes are never quite still. Something is always moving beneath the surface.

The city is built into this ground. It is not a single city but a stacked sequence of them, grown over centuries. The upper level is a grid of low, heavy blocks with flat roofs, built from the same basalt they stand on, their edges softened by weather. Below them, the terraces step down the cliffs, and the lower terraces are older and more layered: aqueducts on arches, cisterns cut into the rock, tiers of factories hung along the escarpment where the wet air makes the ground stable enough to build on. Cooling towers rise out of the gorge mouths in long, slow plumes that merge with the mist. Furnaces burn somewhere below, and the pale heat shimmers over the roofs. Cables run from pylon to pylon across the gorges, sagging under the weight of their own dampness. Everything is maintained, and nothing is new. Infrastructure has been patched and repatched until it has the look of geology.

The engines never stop. Pumps lift water up from the cisterns in a constant rhythm, and the sound carries across the plateau: a deep, low chugging that comes up through the stone and into the chest. Above it, the factories grind and hiss, and the cooling towers exhale with a sound like surf. The city's noise is loud enough that people speak close together, and the animals adapt around it.

The fauna is overwhelming. Birds fill the air at every altitude, and most of them are swifts or swallows that nest in the cooling towers and in the cracks of the cliffs, mud-and-slag nests packed so densely that the towers look furred. At dusk the whole sky above the gorges flickers with them, and their calls merge into a single shrill texture. Beneath them, the marshes teem with amphibians, grey-brown frogs whose chorus rises at night until it sounds like a second machine. Beetles, many of them large and iridescent, crawl through the cisterns and along the aqueducts, feeding on the mineral films and on each other. In the gorges there are colonies of things that might be called lizards or might be something else entirely: low, many-legged, pale-skinned creatures that move along the seepage lines and feed on the gypsum lace, leaving trails of their own calcified mucus. They have built small domed shelters from slag and sediment, and their settlements are dense enough to be mistaken for ruins from a distance.

The light is ordinary, a pale, diffuse daylight softened by the mist, with sharp contrast at midday when the cloud breaks. The air is warm and wet, always faintly metallic, and it carries the smell of hot iron, wet stone, and standing water.

The weather here is its own. Each afternoon, the heat from the lowlands builds a cumulus deck over the plateau, and by evening it releases a steady, lukewarm drizzle that lasts until morning. In winter the cloud deck thickens and the mist turns to fog so dense that the towers vanish. Nothing about the system changes quickly. The cliffs are still dissolving at the pace of centuries. The gypsum lace thickens by millimeters each season. The city grows by replacing one block at a time, and the fauna shifts across generations. The plateau moves through its cycles the way a tide moves, slowly and without urgency, and everything on it has learned to wait.
