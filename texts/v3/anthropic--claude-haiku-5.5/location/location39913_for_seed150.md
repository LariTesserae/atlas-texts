The basin sits high, perhaps three thousand meters up, where the air is thin and cold enough to hold a permanent low cloud. The rock here is young. Its shapes are still sharp: fractured ridges of dark, glassy stone rising in broken tiers, and gullies cut so recently that their walls have not yet softened. Water seeps from every fracture. Meltwater and fog-drip collect in the cracks, trickle down the faces in thin sheets, and pool in the flat places where the stone has not yet decided where it will drain. Nothing here is dry for long.

Almost nothing is bare. Plant growth covers the stone and then builds upward from it, and the result is less a landscape of plants than a town made of them. Streets run between walls of woven stem and bark. Some are wide enough to walk two abreast, and they bend around old outcrops in the way roads once bent around hills. The walls are not planted in rows. They grew, and the growth was guided by something, perhaps by water, perhaps by light, perhaps by the slow pressure of one species crowding another. Vines thicken into pillars. Pillars fork and lean into arches. Arches meet overhead, and the streets run under them in green half-light.

The structures are layered, like a city that has been rebuilt many times over. The lowest level is mats of moss and a thick dark liverwort that holds water like a sponge. Above it, ground-hugging shrubs with leathery, grey-green leaves form the foundations of the walls. Above them are the woody climbers, which form the framework: heavy trunks and braided stems that carry the weight of the upper levels. At the top, the canopy is a continuous roof of broad leaves and epiphytes, so thick that very little light reaches the street. Lichens cover every surface, and moss grows on the lichens. Ferns and small flowering things have taken root in the crotches of the trunks, so that the walls themselves are gardens.

Light is dim and comes mostly from above, filtered through cloud and leaf. The town is lit like the bottom of a lake. Pale rounded fungal bodies on the trunks give off a faint, uneven glow in the deepest shade, and at dusk they brighten enough to mark the paths between buildings.

The place is quiet. Water is the dominant sound: a constant trickle along the gutters, drops falling from leaf to leaf, the wet slap of meltwater finding a lower channel. When the wind moves through the canopy, the whole structure creaks and settles, with a low wooden groan from the thickest stems and a soft hiss from the leaves. Animals are rare. Once in a while a small, dark creature moves along a wall, or a bird calls once from somewhere in the fog and then falls silent. Their absence is noticeable. The town is not empty, but it is not crowded with life that moves.

Change is slow but never stops. Sap rises and falls with the temperature. Vines extend their tips toward new light, and the old ones dry and fall. Some passages close as the walls thicken, and others open where a trunk has split or a rockfall has exposed fresh ground. After rain, the gutters fill and the channels reroute, and the whole network of water flows differently for a few days before the plants adjust. Seasonal cycles are visible in the pattern of growth: new green in the warm months, rigid grey stems through the cold, and across it all a steady, unhurried rebuilding, as if the town were always in the middle of being finished and never intended to be done.

Odd things accumulate here. Some of the arches are too regular to be accidental, with thicknesses and spacings that repeat across the basin. The stone ridges lean toward one another in a way that suggests the gullies were shaped to channel water toward the town center. Nothing in the place has built anything in the sense of tools or hands. But the arrangement reads as deliberate, and a visitor who stays long enough begins to feel that the streets are moving slightly, the way a crowd shifts its weight, and that the town is growing toward some arrangement that it has not yet reached.
