**The Drowned Stair**

The land here is older than almost anything that lives on it. A range of basalt mountains, worn down over unimaginable spans until their summits are blunt and rounded, climbs out of a sea that has no clear floor. The peaks still rise to extreme height, some over five kilometers, their upper ridges bare and grey where the cloud line cuts across them like a seam. Below that line the slopes are steep, terraced by ancient fault blocks into a staircase of ledges, and the lower steps have been swallowed by water. The sea is not quite an ocean. It is a flood-sea, brown-green and cold, that fills the valleys and lowlands to a level that rises and falls.

Nothing here is still. The water breathes. Sluice gates and dam remnants at the basin's mouth open and close on a cycle no one has mapped, and each release sends a surge up the valleys that tears at the shallows, then drains back and leaves the ground glistening and full of thrashing life. Whirlpools form over the sunken river channels and spin for hours before tearing apart. Foam lines chase each other across the open water in long, pale arcs. The surface is never the same from one hour to the next.

The air is cool and wet, carrying the mineral smell of wet stone and the sweet rot of drowned vegetation. Mist rises off the water in columns that lean with the wind and merge into a low ceiling of cloud. Sunlight comes through it flat and even, without shadows. The light is ordinary, neither harsh nor dim, and it turns the water a pale jade where it is shallow and near-black where it is deep.

The vegetation is lush beyond reason. Ferns the height of trees grow from the stepped ledges, their fronds dripping onto the water below. Vines hang in curtains from the cliffs and loop between the rock pillars that stand up out of the flood like the teeth of some enormous jaw. Mangrove-like trees with arched roots wade in the shallows and interlock into thickets that trap silt and create new islands, which the next surge then erodes. Moss covers every exposed face. Blooms, white and violet, float on the still backwaters in masses so dense that the water is hidden beneath them.

Fauna is abundant and loud. Flocks of long-necked waders stalk the shallows in great numbers, and their calls carry across the water like pipe organs. Fish of many kinds ripple the surface, and where a surge has just passed, the water boils with them. Large amphibious grazers, heavy and slow, move through the reed beds with their heads up, grazing the floating mats. Insects hang in clouds over the lagoons, and the air hums and clicks with their wings and calls. Something enormous moves beneath the deepest pools, and its passing lifts a long swell that rolls the lilies.

Built things are scattered and mostly broken. On the lower ledges stand the skeletons of an old hydroelectric works: a row of intake towers, rust-red and streaked white with mineral crust, their gates hanging half open. Some of the turbines still turn when the surge passes through them, and their deep, grinding drone mixes with the bird cries and the rush of water. Pylons march up the lower slopes, half-sunk, their cables trailing into the flood, humming faintly with current. A dredge-barge lies tilted against a rock pillar, its hull colonized by barnacles and vines. Higher up, a few stone shrines or waystations cling to the upper terraces, mostly reclaimed by roots. No one seems to be living here, though fresh footprints sometimes appear on the mudbanks after a surge, and then vanish with the next tide.

The sound never stops. The rushing of the channels, the grinding of the turbines, the calls of birds and beasts, and the slap of water against stone rise and fall in a constant, layered roar. In the quieter hours, the sluice-gates groan and the deep swell moves under everything, and the whole valley seems to shudder softly like a sleeper.

This is a landscape that has settled into its final shape and is still arguing with it. The mountains are patient and ancient. The water and everything living in it are restless and loud. The old machines keep working for reasons no one remembers, opening and closing the gates that decide when the land will drown and when it will rise again.
