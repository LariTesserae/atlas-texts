**The Unfinished Plateau**

You stand on a high tableau, perhaps three thousand meters up, though nothing here gives you a number. The air is thin and warm, the sun is ordinary and bright, and the sky is a clean, unbroken blue that deepens toward a dry, pale horizon. Nothing here has ever held water. The ground is dark, glassy, and fractured, the color of old iron and cooled slag, and it rises and falls in long, shallow swells as if the land were still deciding its shape.

**The Ground**

The rock is young. It is raw geology: fresh fault scarps with edges so sharp they have never been weathered, lava-smooth ridges that were never broken, and wide, flat pans of crumbly mineral crust where salts and sulfur have crystallized into pale yellow and gray rosettes. Some of these crusts are thin enough to crack under a boot and reveal hollow, honeycombed layers beneath, like the inside of a half-formed bone. Scattered across the plain are boulders that look as though they were recently extruded from below and then stopped, halfway through becoming stone. Some are still faintly translucent at their edges, veined with mineral filaments that catch the light.

Nothing erodes here in any way you can see. The fractures have stayed sharp. The crusts have held their shape. The ridges have not been rounded by wind or rain, because there has been no rain and the wind, when it comes, only moves dust that settles back exactly where it was.

**The Vegetation**

The lushness is startling against the mineral ground. Dense, low, and deep green-gray mats cover the lower folds of the land, and taller forms rise out of the swales like frozen fountains. These plants are not green in the way of chlorophyll-rich leaves. Their tissues are waxy and translucent, filled with a viscous amber fluid that is clearly not water. Where a stem is cut or split, it beads and clings like cold honey. It smells faintly of ammonia and warm stone, a sharp clean smell that stings the back of the throat.

The plants grow from the crust and from the boulders. Their roots are pale, thick, and branching, and they seem to feed on the mineral pans themselves, drawing up the sulfur and salt compounds and converting them into their own tissue. Their growth is dense and layered, with fleshy leaves stacked like shingles and softer, membranous fronds that open slowly toward the sun and close at dusk. Some have seed-pods that look like faceted quartz, hard and clear, which rattle faintly when the wind passes through them.

Where the plants meet the fractured ground, they form soft ridges of new growth. These have an uncanny stillness, as if they were paused halfway through their own development. You never see them move. You only notice, after a while, that a patch you passed an hour ago has spread a finger's width toward a crack.

**The Machines**

There is industry here, but none of it sits still. Far off across the plateau, low, rounded engine-forms crawl slowly across the mineral pans on wide, segmented tracks. They are the size of small hills and dark in color, with exhaust vents that puff out faint clouds of pale vapor. They do not build anything. There are no roads, no pipes, no towers, no foundations. Each machine moves in a long, patient loop, passing over the same few kilometers of ground again and again, and where it passes, the crust is scraped thin and the lush plants are sheared back in clean, geometric strips.

Up close, one of these machines is less a building than a creature of gears and piston-housings. Its underside is open to the air, revealing cylinders and rotating drums that draw up mineral slurry, separate it, and feed the pale residue back out behind it in a fine dust trail. The trail settles, and the lush plants grow back over it within a few days, always to the same height, always in the same pattern.

The machines are not idle. They work steadily, endlessly. Yet nothing they do changes the land. The loops repeat, the cuts regrow, the trails settle into the same dusting. Whatever they are for, they have been doing it long enough that the doing has become the landscape's permanent state.

**The Atmosphere**

The air carries a faint, constant haze. It is not smoke or cloud but a thin suspension of pale mineral particles, lifted from the pans by the machines and held in the still, warm air. It softens the far horizon, so distant ridges blur into a uniform bluish ochre and the sky seems to press down close and bright. The light is clean and hard at midday and turns honey-gold by late afternoon, catching the amber sap of the plants and making the whole plateau glow faintly, like a field of old glass.

There is no wind to speak of, only the occasional slow drift of warm air coming up from the fractures. It stirs the dust, rattles the quartz seed-pods, and dies away again. Temperature is warm and even. Nothing feels cold or damp, and nothing is ever dry in the sense of having once been wet, because it never was.

**The Sounds**

The quiet is nearly complete. You hear your own breathing and the faint scrape of your footsteps on the crust. Now and then a seed-pod knocks against another, a sharp, glassy click, and then silence returns. Far away, the machines make a low, steady thrum, more felt in the chest than heard, and it never seems to get louder or quieter. Occasionally, something ticks or settles in the rock, a small sound like cooling metal, and then nothing again.

There are no birds, no insects, no rustling of animal life. The plants make no sound when they grow, because they grow too slowly to hear, and the fractures make none when they open, because they never open further than they already have.

**The Rules of the Place**

Everything here is in a kind of suspended becoming. The rock is forming and never finishes. The plants grow and never change their overall shape. The machines work and never alter the land they cross. The haze hangs in the air but never settles into anything denser. If you were to return in a year, or a century, you would find the same ridges, the same crust, the same clicking pods, the same thrum on the horizon. The plateau has reached a balance it will never leave, a stillness built out of endless, faithful motion.

And there is something about the way it holds together that is unsettling. The plants are lush, almost overwhelmingly so, yet they have no animals to eat them, no water to nourish them, no seasons to turn them. They are abundant the way a thing is abundant when it has only itself to answer to. The machines are industrious the way something is industrious when it has forgotten what it was meant to produce. Nothing is wrong. Nothing is finished. Nothing is ever going to be.

You can walk for hours across the plateau and see no one, no sign of anyone, only the slow loops of the machines, the amber-beaded plants, the pale crust, and the clean blue sky. Somewhere past the far ridge, the thrum continues. Somewhere beneath the crust, the rock is still deciding what it will be, and has been deciding for as long as anyone could say.
