**The Weeping Shelf**

You arrive on a high plateau, perhaps two thousand meters up, though nothing about the air announces altitude. It is warm, the kind of warmth that sits on the skin like a damp cloth. The light is ordinary daylight, unfiltered and white-gold, and it lands on everything with a faint shine, because everything is wet.

The ground is basalt, dark and columnar where it breaks, and it is never quite dry. Water comes up through it. You see it first as darkness spreading across stone, then as a sheen, then as threads running down the joints between the hexagonal columns. The rock weeps. Mineral crusts have built up along the seams in pale orange and green bands, like the tide lines of some slow sea that drained away long ago and left its signature behind. Where the seepage gathers, shallow pools lie in the hollows, still and reflective, with a skin of iridescence on top.

Vegetation is sparse but stubborn. Low, grey-leaved shrubs cling to the cracks. Pale lichen spreads in rings across the exposed columns. Some reeds grow in the wettest channels, bent flat by the constant flow, their tips always pointing downhill. Nothing grows in the open. The plateau has been stripped and re-stripped, and what remains has learned to hug edges.

The city begins where the basalt gives way to terraces. It is not a single city but a sequence of them, stepping down the plateau's flank in tiers, each one built on the ruins of the last. The earliest layers are weathered almost to smoothness: rounded concrete, pitted iron, walls whose original faces have eroded into soft ridges that follow the old formwork lines. These have been long in balance with the weather. Moss-like crust covers their north faces. Their windows have become simple holes that rain drips through in regular rhythm. People no longer quite remember what the lower tiers were for, though they still use the stairways cut into them.

Above those, the newer construction is dense and dark. Stacked housing blocks, galleries of corrugated panels, catwalks slung between towers. Pipes run everywhere: gunmetal conduits along walls, painted bands marking flow directions, valves wheeled open and shut by hands you cannot see. The buildings are spaced far apart, though. Between them lie wide courtyards of standing water and gravel, long empty avenues, and whole districts where only a few lights burn after dark. The city is open, sparse, and extending over a great distance. Each block feels like a single outpost on a shelf of rock.

The industry is everywhere and it is loud. Pumping stations sit at every level, huge squat cylinders thrumming against their foundations. Turbines spin in sluice channels where the seepage is collected and forced downward. Gravity-fed penstocks drop between the terraces with a hollow roar. Cooling towers exhale plumes that merge with the natural mist, and ventilation stacks breathe in slow, resonant bellows. The deafening sound is a single enormous fact of the place: a continuous low hydraulic thunder that you stop hearing after an hour and then hear again whenever you move to a different level. Conversations happen at a shout, or through written slates, or simply don't happen. People have learned to read faces and gestures. Children sleep through it. Visitors feel it in their teeth.

Fauna is rare. You see a few birds, grey-bodied and long-legged, nesting in the upper gantries of the pump halls where the noise keeps predators away. Insects hover in the damp air in small swarms. Something moves in the reed channels at dusk, scaled or furred, too quick to name. At night, the pools reflect the city's lights and small pale creatures surface and sink in them.

The strangeness of the place lies in the water's behavior. The weeping has been increasing very slowly, over generations. The lower terraces, once dry, are now permanently wet. The pumps have been running for so long that the city's founders are beyond memory, and the seepage line, the boundary where the basalt meets saturated ground, has crept upslope by perhaps a few meters in the time anyone has kept records. Old maps show the pools as small dots. Newer ones show lakes. The engineers measure this shift with brass instruments and argue quietly about whether to pump harder or let the water take a higher place. No one has decided. The pumps keep running, and the thunder keeps coming, and every year the rock sweats a little more.

Dust does not form here. Nothing dries completely. Iron gratings rust in fine orange bloom, concrete grows green at its seams, and the whole plateau holds a faint sheen of moisture even when the sun is hottest. Leave a cup of water out and it will be half-full of pale mineral grit by morning. Leave a tool on a rail and it will be furred with rust by evening.

The city's rhythms are slow. Seasons barely change the place; the rain comes in long, uniform sheets, and the temperature holds near warm all year. Things change across decades: a terrace collapses, a pool doubles, a new pump is bolted onto an old foundation, a set of stairs is abandoned as the water rises past it. The people live inside these slow cycles, adjusting their routes and their homes as the rock slowly decides where the water should go.

At the far edge of the shelf, beyond the last terrace, the basalt drops away in a sheer wall. Mist rises from the cliff face constantly, where water seeps out of the stone and falls into the air below. From the lip, you can hear the pumps behind you and the water falling ahead, and between them a long pale plain stretches to the horizon, flat and reflective, already half-drowned.
