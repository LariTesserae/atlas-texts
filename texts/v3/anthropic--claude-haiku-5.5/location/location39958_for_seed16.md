# The Sunken Terraces of Halvane

The basin lies low, a shallow bowl of white salt crust that has cracked into hexagonal plates the size of doorways. Nothing here has ever held standing water. The crust is hard enough to ring underfoot and pale enough that the copper sun, filtered through a permanent haze of fine dust, leaves almost no shadows. Light arrives from everywhere at once and dims as it falls, so the lower terraces are always in a kind of brown dusk.

The city was built into the basin's northern slope in tiers, each one a walled garden-court cut into the salt and faced with pale fired brick and gypsum plaster. The builders worked with hand tools: chisels, adzes, wooden molds, sledges. Their work is careful and modest. Stairs are shallow and wide, drains are channeled and lined, and the walls have hand-set niches where lamps once sat. The settlement is perhaps two thousand years abandoned, though nobody here counts years. The buildings have not so much fallen as been slowly taken apart and reassembled by something living.

## The Vegetation

It is lush, and that is the strangeness. The plants here are thick, fleshy, and dense. They grow in tiers that match the terraces, climbing the walls and spilling over the edges. There are three main forms.

**Ribbed columns** stand eight to fifteen meters tall, with pale green, segmented trunks that look like stacked bone-white drums. Their interiors are hollow and porous, and the wind passing through them produces a deep, rising hum that the city's old builders may have used as a signal or a clock.

**Leaf-sponges** are broad, overlapping sheets, each the size of a tent, with a surface crusted in tiny salt crystals. They are hygroscopic. At night the air cools, the salts pull moisture out of the cold dew-haze, and the leaves swell and then drip a thick, faintly sweet brine into the grooved troughs that run along each terrace. The brine is not water. It is dense and mineral, and the plants depend on it entirely. No creature is ever seen drinking from the troughs, because nothing here drinks.

**Pod-vines** trail down the walls like tangled rope. Their seed-pods are dry, papery husks packed with dense, rattling seeds. The pods split with sharp cracks in the afternoon heat as they expand, and the sound carries across the basin like gunfire spreading from one terrace to the next.

The plants have no leaf-litter, no fallen wood, and no fungal mats. Decay happens very slowly. Dead stalks crack, dry, and collapse into pale dust that the wind carries back into the air. The ecosystem is a closed loop of salt, dew, growth, and dispersal, with no visible consumers.

## Sound

The basin is loud, but nothing in it is alive in the usual way. The sounds come from process rather than animals:

- **Morning:** the thermal creak of brick and stone as the night cold releases its grip. Walls tick, plaster flakes, and loose stones settle with dull knocks.
- **Midday:** the pods split. Crack after crack rolls through the terraces, and seeds rattle against brick like hail.
- **Afternoon:** the wind rises. Dust devils form on the salt flats and spiral up the slope. The hollow columns sing as the air forces through them, a low, resonant drone that rises and falls with the gusts.
- **Evening:** the leaves swell in the cooling air with a faint, wet creaking, and the brine begins to drip into the troughs, a constant patter of tiny sounds across every terrace.

## Dynamics

Nothing here is still. Roots push up through paving and lift flagstones by a finger's width every season. Walls lean slightly further each year as the vines pull at their mortar. Stairs have collapsed into ramps of rubble and salt, and the plants have claimed the ramps. The growth you can see from one day to the next is slow, but over a few years the entire settlement changes shape.

The basin's weather runs in predictable cycles. Night dew-haze, dawn cracking, midday heat and pod-bursts, afternoon dust-devils, then cold evenings that pull moisture back into the leaves. The cycle is almost liturgical. Anyone who had lived here would have built their days around it.

## Scale and Enclosure

Although the basin is large, the living world is intimate. Each terrace is a self-contained room: walls on three sides, the slope rising behind, the open salt below. Moving from one court to the next means passing through a gap in the wall and into a new cluster of columns and sponges, each one a little different. There is no horizon from inside, only the nearest stair, the nearest trough, and the copper haze overhead.

## Unusual Rules

- **No drinking, no shelter-seeking, no mobile life.** Nothing moves except wind, dust, falling seeds, and slow mineral drip.
- **Moisture comes from the air through salt.** The plants are hygroscopic engines. Where the brine collects, the troughs stay wet for only a few hours before the salt crusts over again.
- **The city is the ecosystem's skeleton.** Walls and troughs and stairs channel the brine and shelter the leaves. The plants have, in effect, adopted the architecture as their own structure.
- **Silence is a missing sense.** There are no insect hums, no birds, no rustling animals. The loudness is entirely mechanical and vegetal, which makes it feel mechanical and purposeful, like a machine that has forgotten who built it.

Standing in the lowest court at dusk, the brine dripping around you and the hollow columns humming in the last gusts, you can feel the place working. It is a closed, patient engine. The city's builders are gone, but the system they unknowingly built around, the walls, the troughs, the terraced slope, has become the thing that keeps the basin alive.
