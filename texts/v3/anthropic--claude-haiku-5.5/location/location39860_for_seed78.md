**Thinly Living Highlands**

You arrive in the dark, but not total dark. A faint, bruised glow hangs low in the sky, the light of a sun that has sunk behind a lid of cloud and never quite finished setting. Everything is lit from a direction you can't see, so shadows have no source and seem to pool up out of the ground. The air is cold enough to sting the nose and carries a fine mist that beads on your sleeves and never quite falls as rain.

The land is young. It was lifted recently and has not yet been worn into comfort. Basalt ridges stand up in broken, angular teeth, their edges still sharp enough to cut a glove. Between them lie fields of scree and fresh gravel, unsorted and loose, that shift and tick under your weight. Some slopes are still settling. Cracks run through the bedrock in long, clean lines, and in places a plate of stone has slid a body length downhill and stopped, leaving a step where there was none before. Water finds these fractures first. Thin streams thread through them, some running clear over pale stone, others dark with peat, all of them cold enough to ache.

Life covers everything, yet almost none of it stands near anything else.

The ground is not ground so much as a skin. Lichen in tight grey rosettes overlaps with a dense, velvet moss the color of old bruises. Beneath the moss, a pale fungal mat threads through the gravel, binding it, so the scree holds its shape where it should slide. Where the lichen thins, you can see the rock is warm beneath it, a faint thermal life that the cold air can't reach. The whole surface breathes, a slow exhalation of spores that rise in the damp air and catch the faint light like dust in a cellar beam.

Above this skin, the plants rise up in their own solitude. These are not forests. They are individuals, colossal and far apart, so that you may walk a full kilometer between one and the next and feel you have crossed a country. They look like tall reeds from a distance: pale, jointed stalks thicker than a house, ringed with knuckles, climbing toward the cloud. Up close the stalks are hollow and porous, pocked with breathing holes. Near the top they split into a crown of thin, dark, needle-leaved fronds that droop with condensation and drip constantly. The drip forms small puddles in the moss beneath, and the puddles feed the streams.

Between these giants is the emptiness, and the emptiness is the most striking thing about the place. It is not barren. It is simply vacant, a cold volume of air and mist that stretches far enough to have its own weather. You can watch it happen. A grey front builds in the east over a ridge, a column of cloud curling down into a sudden squall that scours the gravel and sweeps mist across the plateau in a visible wall. The storm moves across the land like something alive, drawing its own rain out of the thinning air, and then it passes, leaving the stalks streaming and the moss sodden. Weather here runs on a continental clock. A single storm can take a day to cross a valley. You learn to read the sky the way sailors read water.

The sound is constant. The hollow stalks act as organ pipes, and the wind across their openings produces a low, shifting hum that rises and falls with the gusts, a chord that never resolves. Over that comes the fauna. Something with long, membranous wings glides low over the moss in flocks, calling in sharp, overlapping chirps that echo off the basalt walls and come back doubled. Burrowers thump the ground. Things move in the dark between the stalks, rustling, clicking, sometimes a wet cough or a long, descending whistle. The predators here hunt by sound, and the prey answer with their own calls, so the whole landscape is a kind of argument carried on in the dark. Rockfall adds its percussion from the slopes, and every so often a crack of ice or stone splits the air like a rifle shot.

Life moves fast here. The biology keeps a biological pace: spores bloom and collapse within a week, the moss regrows over fresh gravel in a season, the stalks shed their crowns and grow new ones in the cold months. The fauna migrate along the ridge lines, following the weather. Predator and prey cycles run through the valleys like tides. Nothing stays still, but nothing crowds, so the motion is spread thin across a vast quiet.

People have been here, though not many, and not for long. Their marks are small and scattered. A cairn of flat stones stands on a ridge, its top stone carved with a spiral that has weathered to a suggestion. A low shelter of stacked stone and stretched hide sits in the lee of a stalk, half collapsed, its doorway pointing downhill as if whoever built it expected the wind to come from above. Inside, you find a hearth of blackened rock, a bundle of tough fiber cord, and a set of bone hooks, each one carved to a different angle. Nearby, a row of hollow stalk segments has been cut and set upright in the moss. Some still hum faintly when the wind catches them. Whoever made them tuned them, or at least meant to. Their meaning is lost, or perhaps they never had one beyond the music.

The tools are simple and hand-made: chipped stone, bone, sinew, hardened wood. There is no metal anywhere. The makers seem to have worked with what the land gave them and left the rest alone. Nothing is abandoned in a hurry. Everything is patient, slow, and waiting.

Strangeness gathers at the edges of the place. The fronds drip in a rhythm that seems to follow the wind, not the gravity, and sometimes the drops hang in the air for a moment before falling. The spore clouds, when they catch the faint light, form slow spirals that hold their shape far longer than they should. And on certain nights, when the cloud breaks for an hour, a faint curtain of pale green light unfolds high over the ridges and ripples like cloth in a current. The fauna goes silent for that hour, and the stalks stop humming, and the whole vast highland seems to hold its breath until the cloud closes again.

You will leave the way you came, through the long cold miles between the stalks, and you will remember the silence more than the sounds. Not an absence of life, but the space life leaves for itself, wide and dark and cold, humming faintly, waiting for the next squall to come over the ridge.
