# The Cradle of Raw Stone

The basin sits high on a ridge that is still rising. You can feel it underfoot as a faint tremor when the rockfalls come, and you can see it in the cliffs, which are pale, sharp-edged, and unweathered. Nothing has had time to round them. The rock is the color of fresh bread crust and breaks with a clean crack, and the scree at their feet is angular, bright, and mostly unstained. This is geology that has not finished deciding what it will be.

The basin is small, perhaps a few kilometers across, enclosed by a horseshoe of cliffs with one gap to the west where a river escapes in a long white fall. The air is cool and thin, and clouds settle into the bowl in the late afternoon and linger until the morning sun burns them off the rim. Everything is damp. Stone surfaces shine. The breath of the place is a fine mist that beads on wool, on hair, on the edges of the wooden shutters.

## Water

Water is everywhere, and it arrives from many directions. Fractures in the young rock weep constantly. Springs break out along the foot of every cliff, some as a trickle through moss, some as a sudden jet from a crack no wider than a finger. The people have learned to read the fractures. Where the seep is thick and faintly rust-colored, they dig cisterns. Where it runs clear and sweet, they lay channels of split log and clay pipe. Where it runs cold and tastes faintly of iron and something close to sugar, they leave it alone and mark the spot with a cairn of white stones.

Condensation is a second source. Mist gathers on the leaves of the broad ferns, drips from the eaves, and pools in shallow stone troughs set at the corners of buildings. The whole town hums with the sound of falling water, a soft ticking on leaves and a steady rattle of drips into barrels.

The river is the loud heart of it. Where it pours over the western lip, it drops in a double cascade into a plunge pool that churns white day and night. The roar carries across the basin and is the sound everyone grows up with. It is so constant that the people speak over it without raising their voices much, and they say a quiet night means something is wrong.

## Vegetation

The growth is lush in a way that feels almost aggressive. The soil is young but the water is abundant, and the plants have moved in fast. Moss covers everything that holds still for more than a season: boulders, roof shingles, the sides of wells, the lower courses of walls. The moss is thick and springy, deep green going to gold where it dries at the edges, and the people walk on it in the lanes without thinking.

Ferns grow taller than a child, their fronds dripping at every hour. Giant horsetail stands in the wet ground near the springs, hollow-stemmed and ridged, and the children break the stalks to make whistles. Broad-leaved plants with pale undersides spread across the terraces, their leaves so large that a single one shelters a sitting adult from the drizzle. Lichen spreads across the fresh cliff faces in rings of orange, grey, and a blue-green so vivid it looks painted. The rock itself is the slowest thing to grow, but even it is being slowly colonized.

Trees are few and stunted near the rim, but in the lower basin there is a stand of tall, straight conifers with thin bark, planted deliberately by the first settlers and now self-seeding into the lanes. Their resin is used for waterproofing, and the smell of it hangs in the damp air around the sawpits.

## Built Environment

The settlement is layered. It rises up the basin's inner slopes in terraces, each one supported by a wall of stacked slate and hand-cut blocks. The walls are battered inward and capped with flat stones, and between them run narrow lanes just wide enough for a handcart. Stairs are cut directly into the bedrock where it is firm enough, and rope rails are strung along the steepest ones.

Houses are timber frames set on stone footings, with steep roofs of split shingle, the shingles already furred with moss. Each house has a cistern at its corner and a trough beneath the eaves. Doors are oak planks pegged together and swollen so that they must be lifted to close. Windows are small and shuttered. Chimneys are rare, since smoke trapped by the cloud is unpleasant, and most cooking is done in open courtyards under lean-to roofs.

The oldest structures are at the bottom, built of the same pale stone as the cliffs, and they are dense and heavy. Higher up, the stone gives way to timber and mud brick, and the newest things are at the top, which is where the builders are working on the terraces right now. The layering tells a story. People have been building here for generations, adapting each generation's work to the one before it, and no one has ever torn anything down completely.

There are bridges, too. Rope-and-plank bridges span the gorge where the river cuts the basin's floor, and a stone causeway crosses the wetter ground near the springs. The causeway is slowly sinking, and the people keep raising it with new fill, so its surface is a stack of different eras, each one a different shade of stone.

## Tools and Craft

Nothing here is made with metal beyond a few precious iron wedges and chisels, traded or carried in long ago and carefully kept. Most work is done with wood, stone, bone, and fiber. Stone is split by driving wooden wedges into cracks and soaking them with water until they swell and burst the rock. The people are very good at this, and they can take a block from the cliff face in an afternoon. They dress the blocks with hammerstones and abrasive sand, and they fit them without mortar, using gravity and careful shimming.

Timber is shaped with stone adzes and pegged with split oak. Rope is made from fibrous bark and horsetail stalks twisted tight. Pots are coiled from clay dug near the springs, where the clay is grey and very fine. Cloth is made from wool of a short-haired mountain animal the people keep in walled pens on the upper terraces.

The craft is patient and repetitive. Nobody rushes, because nothing here needs to be fast. A house takes a generation to finish, and that is considered normal.

## Fauna

Animal life is sparse. Birds are the most common visitors: small grey species that nest in the cliff cracks and call with a rising whistle, and a larger dark bird that circles the rim in the thermals in the late morning. Insects are present but thin, pale-bodied things that gather near the springs in clusters and do not seem to bite. A few small rodents live in the scree, and the people keep a single breed of short-legged dog that is hardy, woolly, and oddly silent.

Larger animals are almost absent. The basin seems too young and too wet for a rich food chain. The people say that the first settlers found no large fauna at all, and that the mountain animals they keep were brought up from the valley below, where the grass is better. Nothing here is hunted, and nothing hunts them.

## Weirdness

Several things here do not behave as expected, and the people have learned to live with them.

The most noticeable is the stone. The fresh cliffs ring faintly when the air cools at dusk, a low tone that sounds like a struck bell held under water. The people call it "the evening hum," and they tune their evening songs to it. When the hum is loud, the scree slides more often in the night.

Another is the growth of the walls. Every few years the builders find that a course of stone has shifted a finger's width, or that a doorframe has tilted, and they re-level the work. They say the ground is still rising, and that the buildings must rise with it or be left behind. There is a tradition that the walls must be checked with a plumb line every spring, and that a family which neglects this will find its house split by the following winter. The people treat this as ordinary maintenance, though it is strange to see.

Finally, the springs. The iron-sweet water from the cairned springs does not freeze, even on the coldest nights, and it stays warm to the touch. No one drinks it in quantity. Children are sometimes allowed to bathe in it on feast days, and they come out flushed and quiet, with a faint taste of sugar still in their mouths. Nobody is entirely sure what it does, and nobody has ever wanted to find out too quickly.

## Light and Atmosphere

The light is ordinary daylight, pale and even through the cloud, with the sun appearing in brief bright bands that send shafts through the mist and turn the wet moss to gold. At midday the basin can be almost luminous. In the evening the cloud thickens and the light goes grey-blue, and the cliffs take on a faint violet cast before they darken.

The atmosphere is heavy with moisture and the smell of wet stone, crushed fern, resin, and the faint mineral tang of the springs. Sound carries strangely in the damp air. Voices from one terrace can be heard clearly two levels down, but a shout from across the basin dissolves into the roar of the falls.

## Dynamics

Nothing here changes quickly. The cliffs rise by a few centimeters a decade, and the rockfalls come in long intervals. The moss spreads slowly over the walls. The springs shift their courses over generations, and the people follow them with new channels and cisterns. The settlement grows terrace by terrace, house by house, and each new course of stone is laid on the bones of the old.

The rhythm of life is patient, cyclical, and deeply tied to the water and the stone. Seasons come and go, the mist returns each afternoon, the evening hum rises and fades, and the walls are checked each spring. The place is becoming itself very slowly, and the people are becoming themselves alongside it.
