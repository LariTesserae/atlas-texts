**The Ringing Flats**

You come down from a glassy ridge into a basin where the ground has not finished cooling. The rock is young, black and grey-green, with edges still sharp enough to cut. Nothing has worn these surfaces smooth. Rain and seep have only begun to work on them, so the whole basin looks freshly broken, as if the world were cast here last season and the mould has not yet been cleared away.

The light is dim and even, the kind that comes through a thick overcast or from a sky that is always just before dusk. It has no obvious source. Shadows are soft and pooled. Far off, the basin's rim is a low, bruised line, and beyond it the sky is the colour of wet slate.

**Water**

Water is everywhere, and it is never still. The basin floor is a marsh that has no clear border with the land. Shallow sheets of water lie across the rock, a finger deep or less, and they reflect the dim sky so faithfully that you cannot always tell where the solid ground stops. Every fracture in the basalt holds a thin film. Seepage beads on the vertical faces and runs in slow, glittering threads toward the low places, where it gathers into pools the colour of weak tea.

The air is saturated. Condensation forms on every surface and on your skin, and it collects on your eyelashes and in your collar. Fog lifts off the pools in the early hours and hangs in the hollows until the cool afternoon burns it off, though the basin never really dries. Even the stones feel cold and damp to the touch, and they keep the water in their pores.

The water is faintly mineral. It tastes of iron and something sharper, like struck flint. It is clear and safe enough for a thirsty traveller, but it leaves a fine pale film on the tongue.

**The Standing Stones**

The most striking feature is the density of the upright forms. Across the marsh rise thousands of slender spires of pale stone, each one a few body-lengths tall, some thinner than a wrist and some as broad as a doorway. They grow close together, rarely more than an arm's span apart, so that walking through them is like wading through a reed-bed that has turned to rock. Their surfaces are ribbed and fluted, banded with rust and milk-white, and they are hollow near the base.

They are not plants, though they have the look of plants. They grow from the rock by accretion, a skin of mineral deposited from the seep-water layer by layer, and they lean gently with the prevailing wind. You can see the growth rings if you look closely, and you can tell the young spires from the old: the old are pitted and lichenous with flaked crust, while the young are glossy and wet and still ringing faintly as they harden.

Some of the spires are tall enough to meet overhead, forming arched galleries you can walk through for a hundred paces before the passage closes. In those galleries the sound changes completely.

**Sound**

This is a loud place, and the noise is constant. Water is the main source. It drips and trickles from ten thousand points, ticking on stone, splashing into pools, and running in thin unseen channels beneath the rock. Where the wind passes across the hollow spires it sets them singing, a low, breathy hum that rises and falls with the gusts. In the galleries the hum becomes a resonance, and footsteps come back to you doubled and hollow, as if someone were walking beside you a moment behind.

Every so often a spire sheds a flake of its crust and it rings like a struck bell for a long moment. The sound carries across the flats, and the marsh answers, one note after another, as if the whole basin were tuning itself. The noises never stop, and after a time you stop hearing them as separate sounds and begin to feel them as pressure on the chest.

**Built Remains**

Scattered through the marsh are fragments that might once have been made. They are not tools, and they are not dwellings in any sense you would recognise. There are rings of worked stone half-sunk into the mud, each about the size of a cart, with grooves cut in a regular spiral. There are arches that stand alone, with no walls attached, their keystones cracked and their faces smoothed by water. Some of the spirals are still clean and sharp, and they look newer than the arches. There are no inscriptions and no paths. The marks are too faint to read and too few to suggest a plan, and you cannot tell whether they were built to last or simply left behind when someone was done with them.

**Fauna**

Animal life is rare, and what there is keeps mostly to the water. In the deeper pools you may see slow, grey-shelled things that move like stones until they tap against the rock with a hard, deliberate click. They are blind, or at least they show no eyes, and they seem to navigate by the vibration of the spires. Once in a long while something larger moves through the fog at the basin's edge, a shape that stays too far off to name. It is gone before you can decide whether you saw it.

**Strangeness**

Some things in the basin resist explanation. The fine films of water on the rock sometimes run uphill, slowly, toward a crack that is warmer than the rest, and then stop when the crack cools. Pools that you left in the evening can be found in the morning standing on a different slope, their margins moved a few body-lengths. The standing stones are never all the same height on two visits, though you cannot catch them growing. When you press your palm to a young spire it is warm for a moment and then cold again, and the warmth seems to move from the stone into your hand.

**Time**

Nothing here hurries. The marsh spreads by a few centimetres a year and the spires add a film of mineral each season. The pools deepen and shallow over decades. The basin's rim is slowly eroding into the flats, though no single day shows it. You feel that the place is patient, that it has been working toward some state for a very long time and will keep working long after you have gone. The shape you see is only the one it happens to hold at the moment you arrive.
