**The Sunken Dry**

You arrive at the lip of a basin, and the first thing you notice is the sound. It does not come from one direction. It fills the air like pressure, a layered roar of ten thousand stridulating things, rattling seed-husks, the wet tick of sap under stress, and beneath all of it a low continuous hum like a struck bell that never quite stops ringing. Your ears adjust after a few minutes, but the sound never becomes quiet. It only becomes something you stop hearing, and then you hear it again.

The basin is roughly the width of a valley, sunk perhaps two hundred meters below the surrounding plain. Its floor is not bare rock. It is covered, edge to edge, with vegetation so dense that the ground can't be seen. Stalks as thick as a person's waist press against stalks as thin as wire. Broad, fleshy leaves overlap like roof tiles. Everything is pale grey-green, almost silver where the dust has settled on it, and everything is moving.

**The water that isn't**

There is no standing water here, no stream bed, no damp hollow. The air is warm and dry, and it tastes of salt and iron. The plants hold their moisture inside themselves: their stalks are swollen, hollow, and rattle faintly when shaken, and when one is cut it does not bleed sap but releases a thin, cool vapor that condenses on your skin. The local people, if you find them, drink from the stalks by piercing them with a bone and waiting for the dry mist to gather into drops. It takes most of an afternoon to fill a gourd.

The plants have no roots as we know them. They anchor into a crust of pale salt and crumbling mineral that covers the basin floor, and they feed on the crust itself, breaking it down, drawing dissolved minerals up through networks of fibrous filaments that run like veins through the soft rock. Where the plant mass is thickest, the rock is no longer rock. It is spongy, pale, and warm to the touch, a half-mineral, half-living substrate you can press a thumb into.

**Emerging ground**

This is the strangest thing about the place: the geology is still forming. The basin walls are not weathered cliffs but raw slopes of fresh stone, layered in pale bands that look poured rather than deposited. In places, new ridges are visibly rising, pushed up from below by pressure you cannot see, and the living mass races to colonize them as they appear. Cracks open and close over days. A depression you crossed yesterday may be a low hill by evening, its surface already threaded with new stalks.

The plants and the stone are co-constructing one another. Where a stalk dies, its mineral heart hardens into a column that other plants climb. Where a mat of leaves decays, it crusts over into a plate that bears new growth. The basin is building itself out of its own living death, and its shape changes faster than you can map it.

**Fauna**

The animals are everywhere, and they are overwhelming in number rather than in size. Most are small: six-legged burrowers with translucent shells that click against each other like dice, flocks of long-winged things that rise from the canopy in clouds and settle again in seconds, hopping creatures that stridulate by rubbing hind legs against ridged plates on their backs. There are larger forms too, slow grazers shaped like barrels that move through the stalks and leave trampled channels behind them, and predatory things that stalk the channels in the early morning, their bodies barely visible against the grey.

Nothing sits still. The ground ripples with movement underneath, and the air crackles with the wing-hum of thousands of insects. The seed-husks burst with a sharp snap when they ripen, showering the basin with spores that drift like dust. Animals eat the spores, excrete the seeds, and the cycle runs faster than seasons, so that a patch of ground can go from bare crust to choking thicket and back again in a week.

**Marks of others**

People have been here, but they are few and they leave little. Scattered across the basin you find:

- Lean-tos made from woven stalks, bound with cords of stripped fiber, abandoned and half-swallowed by growth.
- Cairns of pale stone, each one stacked with care, marking water-vapor collection points or perhaps old boundaries. Some have been knocked over. Others are being slowly pushed apart by the stalks growing through them.
- Knapped flint and chert flakes, the only tools in evidence, sharp and grey, scattered where someone once butchered a grazer or cut stalks.
- Fire rings of blackened stone, their centers choked with new sprouts.
- A single carved marker, a tall post with a spiral cut into the bark, bleached and cracked, leaning as the ground shifts beneath it.

The makers are nowhere in view. Whether they retreated to the rim, or moved underground, or were absorbed into the living mass, is unclear. Their work is provisional, fragile, scattered across the basin like punctuation in a sentence no one finished.

**Light and sky**

The light is ordinary daylight, filtered through a haze of dust and spore-cloud. Above the basin the sky is pale and hazy, and the sun crosses it in a wide arc. In the basin itself the light comes down in shafts through gaps in the canopy, which is dense enough to form a loose roof in places, bridged across the rim by drooping leaves. Walking the floor is like walking through the understory of a forest that grows out of stone, with light moving in patches across the ground as the leaves and stalks sway and the wind pushes the spore-clouds around.

**The strangeness**

Some things here do not add up. The sound has a rhythm that seems almost deliberate, rising and falling in long cycles that coincide with the opening and closing of the seed-husks. The mineral filaments, when cut, pulse faintly, as though something is still circulating through them. Animals seem to avoid certain patches of ground where the crust is unusually smooth and pale, and nobody can say why. When you stand very still in the middle of the basin and listen, the roar seems to organize itself into tones, a chord that changes as the plates shift, as if the whole place were tuning itself.

The basin is never finished. It is always becoming something else, and the people who came here, whatever they expected, were also changed by its restlessness. You feel it in your own breathing after a day here: a quickening, an impatience, the sense that if you stop moving for too long, the ground will rise up and bury you in the next season's growth.
