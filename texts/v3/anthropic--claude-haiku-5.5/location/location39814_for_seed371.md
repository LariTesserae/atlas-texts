**The Stamping Basin**

The place is a bowl of raw stone about two kilometers across, walled on every side by young mountains that seem to have been thrown up overnight and have not yet been worn down. Their ridgelines are knife-edged, their faces still fractured in plates the size of houses. Some spires stand with their points unbroken, and a few lean at angles that look too precarious to hold. The rock is dark basalt and a grey-green schist, glassy where it has split recently and rough where it has weathered a few seasons. Above the rim the peaks climb into the clouds, and the summits are never visible.

The basin floor is at an extreme altitude, and the air is thin, cold, and wet. It is nearly always dusk here, with a ceiling of low cloud that seems to press down on the highest trees. Light comes only as a grey seep through the cloud and a faint glow from the vegetation itself. There is no sun to speak of, only a diffuse pallor that makes shadows hard to find and distances hard to judge.

**The vegetation**

Life crowds the basin so densely that the ground is almost invisible. The lowest layer is a carpet of moss and pale liverwort, thick enough to soak through boots and sponge up underfoot. Above it rise ferns the height of a person, with fronds the width of a door, and between them grow fleshy fungal shelves that climb the stone in tiers, some as wide as a dining table. Many of the fungi carry a dim phosphorescence, a cold blue-green light that brightens after rain and fades as the wet dries. It is not enough to see by, but it marks the trunks and tangles so the basin reads as a cave of faint lanterns.

Vines hang in curtains from the taller trunks, and the trunks themselves are fibrous and pale, bark peeling in long strips that drip sap. Lichens coat everything they can reach, in orange, rust, and a grey so pale it looks like frost. Where the ground drops toward the streams, tangled root mats form arches and tunnels, and water runs through them in the dark.

Nothing here eats or flies. There are no animals, no insects, no birds, not even the small scuttling things that usually mark a healthy forest. The silence of animal life is absolute, and the plants fill the space the animals would have taken, growing into one another so tightly that the basin feels like the inside of a single organism.

**Water**

Water is the basin's main constant and its main agent. Rain falls several times a day, usually without warning, a sudden roar on the canopy that lasts for minutes and then stops. Between storms the air holds a heavy condensation, and every surface beads and drips. Fog pools in the lower hollows and rises through the trees in slow columns.

Streams cut down from the rim in white torrents and braid across the floor into a shallow mere at the center. The mere is dark and still, with a skin of foam and a sulfur smell. Several springs bubble up through fissures in the rock, warm enough to steam in the cold air. Where these springs break the surface they leave pale mineral crusts, tiered like shelves, which grow and collapse as the water changes course.

**Sound**

The basin is loud, and the sound is constant. The roar of the streams is always present, a deep chord of falling water that rises when the rains come and drops to a steady hiss in dry hours. Beneath it, sharp cracks run through the stone, the sound of rock splitting along old fractures. Rockfalls come every few minutes, somewhere on the slopes: a rumble, a long clatter of stone against stone, and then a dust-cloud rising over the trees. The sound carries through the cloud and seems to come from every direction.

There is also a rhythm to the basin. Vents in the eastern wall release gusts of steam with a hiss that builds, peaks, and stops, and these come roughly in a cycle, like the exhaust of a great engine. Some of the springs pulse, pushing water up through narrow throats with a thump. Together they make the basin feel like a working machine that never stops.

**The engine of the basin**

Nothing here is built, yet the place works like a factory. The mountains are grinding themselves down, and the springs and vents are their exhaust. Water runs into fissures, heats against deep rock, and is forced back up through the vents under pressure. The rock fractures in response, and each fracture drains a little more water into the system. The grinding throws up dust and gravel, the streams carry them into the mere, and the mere deposits silt that the forest feeds on. The whole basin is a closed process that consumes stone and water and turns them into plants, minerals, and noise.

The plants respond quickly. A fungal shelf can double its size in a few days after rain and collapse just as fast when the substrate shifts. Whole stands of ferns can be buried by a rockslide and reappear within a season, growing up through the debris in fresh green spikes. The forest does not rest, and its growth is as violent as the geology: a rush of new life followed by sudden loss.

**Change**

Nothing in the basin holds still. The mere shifts its shoreline weekly, the springs move their vents, and the streams carve new channels in a single night. Mineral shelves crack and tumble into the water. Trees lean and fall, their roots pulling up slabs of moss. A new spire can rise in the night where a pillar of rock has split away from the cliff face, and the sound of its breaking will carry for kilometers. Maps of the basin go stale in days.

Things are born here and die here at a pace that makes the land seem alive, though nothing in it has a mind or intent. It is only motion, rock and water and green growth, pushing and collapsing and pushing again.

**Summary**

A dark, wet, high-altitude basin of young and violent stone, carpeted in dense, glowing, animal-free forest. Its noise never ceases: streams, rockfalls, steam-vents pulsing like pistons. It is built by no one and maintained by nothing, but it runs with the rhythm of a machine, grinding its own mountains into soil and turning water and stone into a forest that is always being torn down and grown again.
