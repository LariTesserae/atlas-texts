**The Sunken Terraces of Hadrun Mere**

You arrive by climbing, though the climb is less a climb than a slow surrender to water. The air at this height is not air so much as cloud, thick and cold, and it has been raining here for longer than anything has been counted. Rain does not fall so much as hang. Every surface is wet to the point of dissolving: stone, leaf, bone, skin. You breathe it like a fish that has forgotten it was ever a fish.

The mountain is not a mountain in the ordinary sense. Its summit was planed down by an age of weather until only the bones remain, broad and rounded, stepping away in tiers that run for kilometers. Each tier is a terrace, and each terrace is held level by a lattice of roots thick as cathedral pillars, grown together over ages into walls, channels, and cisterns. Nothing here was quarried or cut. It was grown, or grown into a shape that serves as architecture, by root and fungus and the slow secretions of animals long dead and long since fused into the stone. The result is built through and through: aqueducts of bark, reservoirs lined with pale calcareous moss, stairways where the treads are the knuckled crowns of ancient trees, and arches formed by two trunks that grew toward each other across a gorge and never quite touched.

The terraces are flooded to varying depths. Water sits in the lowest steps like black glass, perfectly still, reflecting nothing, because the light is too poor to reflect. Above them, water moves through everything. It seeps from moss-curtains, trickles through root-channels, drips from the undersides of leaves that hang in dense sheets sixty meters overhead. The vegetation is total. There is no bare ground, no bare rock. Every crevice holds a fern, a liverwort mat, a hanging vine heavy with pale tubers. Canopy trees with trunks like cliff faces form the far walls, and their epiphytes form their own small forests, which in turn hold their own small pools.

Light is scarce. The cloud ceiling is low and continuous, and what reaches the ground passes through several layers of leaf and water before it arrives, so it is a dim, greenish, unvarying gloom. Nothing casts a sharp shadow. Distance is hard to judge, and the far tiers dissolve into a grey-green haze after a few hundred meters. In the deepest hollows it is nearly night, and things are known by sound and touch before sight.

And the sound is enormous. Water is everywhere and always speaking: the roar of falls spilling between terraces, the hiss of rain on a million leaves, the gurgle of channels, the drumming of drips into pools. Over this layer sits the calling of fauna, which is abundant. Frogs with vocal sacs like lanterns fill the lower pools with a deep, rhythmic chorus. Birds whose bodies are mostly feather and water call in long, descending phrases from the canopy. Insects drone and click in the wet air. Something large moves through the middle distance, and its footfalls thud through the root-floors like a slow heartbeat. Everything is loud, and the noise has the quality of something that has been going on without interruption for a very long time.

Life here is dense in places but sparse overall. Between the terraces lie wide stretches of open air and empty water, where the only presence is cloud and the sound of rain. A traveler might walk a full day without seeing an animal, then round a bend and find a hundred of them, all motionless in a shallow cistern, heads just above the surface, watching nothing. They are not hunting. They are not moving toward anything. They have been there, or others like them, since before the terraces were finished, and they will be there when the next storm arrives.

That is the strangest quality of the place: nothing changes. The rain falls at the same angle it always has. The water stands at the same level in every cistern. The calls of the frogs repeat in the same intervals. The light holds its same grey-green tone. Erosion has finished its long work, and the mountain has settled into a final form that the weather now merely maintains, forever, without gradient or trend. Time passes here the way water passes through a sponge: it fills the space, moves through it, and leaves everything exactly as it found it.
