**The Salt Gallery, Level Nine**

You stand at the lip of a shaft that opens into a cavern large enough to hold weather. The air is cool and dry, with no smell of water at all, only a faint mineral tang, like a struck match held near a clean stone. The floor falls away in terraced white plains of halite, cracked into polygons like dried mud on some older world. Nothing here has been wet for a very long time.

Above, the roof is lost in a haze of suspended dust and a slow drift of pale salt flakes that catch the light and fall without hurry. Light comes from a grid of sodium lamps strung along the ceiling girders, still running on a generator somewhere deeper, their orange cast softened by the dust into something closer to amber. The light is ordinary and steady. It reaches every surface, which makes the place feel more exposed than it should.

Vegetation covers almost everything, but not as gardens. It has taken the architecture. Pale, fibrous vines as thick as cable bundles climb the steel frames of abandoned ore hoppers and conveyor gantries, wrapping them so completely that the original structure reads only as a skeleton inside a living lattice. The plants are grey-green and sheathed in a crust of salt they have excreted to protect themselves from the brine-dry air, so every stem glitters faintly. Where roots have split concrete footings, they have lifted slabs into tilted shelves that now hold moss-like mats the color of old bone. Some of these mats are soft and velvety; others are rigid, branching like coral, and ring faintly when tapped.

The industrial past is everywhere, partly swallowed. A row of boiler stacks rises out of a bank of vegetation, their rivets crusted white. A rail line runs across the cavern floor, its ties long rotted but its rails still gleaming where the plants have not reached. A dead crane arm angles over a pit now filled with a dense, low canopy of silver-stemmed growth, its cable hanging into the green like a plumb line. Machines with no obvious operators sit in clearings, their dials furred with lichen, a few still ticking as slow expansions in the metal release heat.

The geology is young here, or rather it is becoming young again. In the far reaches, where the old workings have caved, new crystals are forming in the dust: long, translucent halite needles growing from the floor, and tiny cubic clusters climbing the walls like frost. The rock surfaces are raw and unweathered, veined with fresh salt seams that have not yet been stained by time. The cavern is still settling, and every so often a distant groan rolls through the ground as a shelf of salt shifts and sends a small rain of flakes down onto the terraces.

Life is abundant but mostly small, and mostly hidden until you look. Pale beetles with glassy wing-cases move in slow columns along the vines, tapping the stems with antennae and drinking the sap that beads at the wounds. Long-legged crawlers, pale as the salt, hunt among the mats, and a few larger creatures with segmented bodies and fanned, papery appendages move between the girders, pausing to feed on the fallen flakes. Where a beetle column meets a crawler, the movement is fast and brief, then the line closes again and continues. The whole cavern seems to pulse with this quiet traffic: feeding, sheltering, climbing, dying, the plants absorbing what falls and the animals turning the plants into more of themselves.

Sound is sparse. A dry rustle of stems in an unseen breeze, the faint hum of the generator, the ticking of cooling metal, the occasional drip of brine from a higher seam, and then long silences in which you can hear your own breath and the small, persistent hiss of salt crystals settling. The quiet is not empty. It is the sound of a place doing its slow work.

The strangeness is in the balance. A factory was built here to extract a resource from the dry earth, and the earth has turned that factory into a host. The machines now serve the plants, the plants serve the animals, and the animals are slowly remaking the salt into soil that has never existed here before. You are standing in the first generation of a world that will grow something new out of what humans left behind, and it is not yet clear what that will be.
