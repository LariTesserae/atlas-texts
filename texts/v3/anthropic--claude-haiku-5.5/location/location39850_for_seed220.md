**The Undercourt of Varr**

You come down into it by a stair that never quite ends. The steps are worn to shallow bowls in the middle, and the walls on either side are cut from a pale, layered stone, each stratum a different ochre or grey. Warmth rises to meet you as you descend, and then the damp, a breath on the skin that beads on your collar and runs slowly down the back of your neck. The air tastes of iron and wet brick and something sweeter underneath, like cut fruit left too long.

At the bottom the land opens into a basin perhaps a kilometer across, sunk some forty meters below the surface. Its roof is not sky. It is a vault of strata and old foundations, dim with the grey of buried concrete, and from it hang the ends of structures that once belonged to the city above: the blunt stumps of towers, the undersides of bridges, a cluster of spheres like bruised fruit still held in their cradles. Nothing up there touches light. Nothing down here has seen daylight in a long time.

The city below is layered. The lowest level is a sump, a flat floor of black silt laced with channels where warm water moves in slow, oily braids. Above that rise terraces of stacked construction: pumping houses, cisterns with curved walls scored by centuries of condensation, arches that carry catwalks across the gaps. The architecture has the look of many generations building on one another without ever tearing down, so the newest machinery is bolted to the bones of the oldest. Some of it is clearly tooling and infrastructure. Some of it is harder to read. There are lattices of thin conduit that catch the faint glow of mineral deposits and seem to hum, and a few towers whose surfaces sweat not water but a fine luminous fog that thins as it rises and vanishes a few meters above the highest catwalk. Some of the engines here run on heat drawn from the rock itself and do not seem to obey the proportions of their own gears. A flywheel the height of a house turns one slow revolution every few minutes, and the air shivers around it in a visible ripple that is not heat haze.

The sound is the first thing you learn to live with. Water is everywhere, and it is never quiet. Drips strike the cisterns in rapid, irregular rhythms, each one ringing off a different wall so that the basin sounds like a tuned instrument played badly. Somewhere a weir pours continuously, a roar that rises and falls with the pressure of the pumps. Pumps knock and cough. Valves slam shut with the sharp crack of water hammer, and the sound travels the whole basin and comes back doubled. Under it all is a low mechanical thrum, a bass note you feel in your teeth more than hear, which seems to come from the foundations themselves. Speech carries but dies quickly in the damp, so people here, if there are people, shout short phrases or use hand signals.

Vegetation is sparse and specialized. Along the walls, where condensation runs, grow pale mats of fungus that open and close in slow pulses, releasing thin plumes of spores that drift and catch in the lantern-light. In the sump's shallows there are reeds, but they are stiff and grey, their stalks streaked with the same mineral glow as the conduits, and they bend toward the warmest vents rather than the water. Lichen-like crusts cover the stone in patches of rust and bruise-purple. There are no trees. The nearest thing to a canopy is a tangle of hanging root-filaments from the vault, which may be roots, or cables, or both.

Fauna is rare and watchful. Small pale creatures move along the catwalks, long-legged and blind, that retreat into cracks when the pumps cycle. Larger shapes sometimes glide through the sump's black water with barely a ripple, following the warm currents. You see them most clearly by the glow of the mineral deposits, and then they are gone. They seem to avoid the machines. Their tracks lead in circles around the pump-houses and stop at the edges of the luminous fog.

The light is low and comes from several sources that do not agree. Lanterns, if any remain, are sparse. The mineral deposits give off a faint greenish phosphorescence, brighter in the wet places and dimmer where the water has stopped. The tower-fog holds a diffuse silver glow that never quite reaches the floor. The overall effect is of walking through a memory of light. Shadows are deep and soft-edged, and the eye keeps adjusting without ever settling.

Everything here moves, slowly or quickly, and nothing is at rest. Water seeps from the vault and collects into rivulets that cut new channels into the silt each season. Cisterns fill and overflow. Fungal mats bloom and collapse on a rough biological clock. The machines cycle through their long rhythms, and every few hours the pressure shifts and the whole basin seems to exhale: the weir surges, the drips speed up, the fog rises and thickens, then the pumps cut out and the silence hangs for a minute before the next cycle begins. Predators and prey circulate too, in small, quiet ways: a blind creature hunts fungal spores in the damp, something larger hunts the blind creatures in the sump, and the machines, whatever they are, sometimes hunt the small things that come too close to their lines.

It is a place that has been running for a very long time, and it does not seem to know it has been abandoned, or that it was ever meant to be otherwise. The stair you came down is still wet from the last time it rained somewhere above you. Somewhere a valve opens, and the basin shudders, and the roar of the weir rises to meet you.
