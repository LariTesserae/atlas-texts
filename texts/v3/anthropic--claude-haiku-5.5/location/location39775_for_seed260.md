**The Thinning Shelf**

You stand on a plateau that rises out of the clouds at a height the air does not quite support. The ground is young rock. It has not finished becoming stone. Its surface is pale, glassy in places and crumbly in others, and it still holds the shapes of the processes that made it: ripple-marks frozen in tilted slabs, bubble-pits like pumice, long fissures where the crust is still parting by fractions of a finger each year. Nothing here has been weathered for very long. The cliffs are sharp-edged. The gullies have no settled floors. Whole ridges look as if they were pressed up yesterday and have not yet decided what shape to keep.

**Water and the absence of it**

There are no rivers, lakes, or puddles. Where a stream should be, there is a dry bed of fine grey grit, ridged as though something flowed through it once and stopped mid-thought. The lush growth around you does not rely on rain. Instead, a heavy, cold vapor hangs in the lower hollows, a dense fog of some condensing non-water fluid that beads on leaves as a thick, faintly sweet oil and drips slowly into the cracks. The plants drink it. When you touch a frond, it leaves a slick residue that smells of cold metal and crushed mint. The fog is not wet on the skin. It is simply heavy, pressing you down a little.

**Growth**

The vegetation is extravagant. Broad-leafed ferns the height of houses lean over the fissures, their fronds translucent and veined with pale green. Between them, mats of spongy moss cover the raw stone, and tall, spiral-stemmed reeds stand in clusters, each one tipped with a pod that opens slowly like a flower at the speed of a clock hand. Nothing eats these plants, because nothing here eats anything. The growth simply accumulates, layer on layer, over the young rock, as though the land were trying to cover its own unfinished face. Vines trail down the sheer cliffs and anchor themselves in cracks that widen a little each season, so that the greenery is slowly prying the mountain open.

**Cold and dim light**

The air is cool and still, with a bite that comes from altitude as much as temperature. The sun, if it is one, sits behind a permanent veil of violet-grey haze and gives a light like late evening in a deep forest. Shadows are soft and do not quite meet their objects. Colors are muted except where the growth catches a faint glow from within, a bioluminescent pale gold in the pods and mosses that brightens slightly when the fog moves across them.

**Sound**

This is the most overwhelming aspect of the place. The silence you expect is absent. Instead, the air is full of constant roaring and grinding. Part of it is wind funneling through the fissures, which produces a deep, continuous note like a vast organ with no end. Part is the geology itself: the crust creaks and ticks as it shifts, cracks ring like struck bells, and falling flakes of stone hiss down the slopes in long cascades. The reeds rustle with a sound like surf, and the pods release a faint, high chime when they open. Under everything there is a low, rhythmic thrum that you feel in your ribs more than hear, as if the mountain has a heartbeat it is still learning.

**Traces of others**

Scattered across the shelf are small, rough structures: stacked-stone walls no higher than a knee, cairns of flat slabs, a circle of standing stones with notches chipped into their tops. They are hand-made, crude but deliberate, and most are half-swallowed by growth. Vines pull at the joints. Moss hides the carved marks. Some have fallen in, and their stones have become part of the young ground. Tools lie in a few sheltered hollows: sharpened flakes of the glassy rock, a bone-handled scraper, a hollow reed filled with a dry yellow powder. Nobody is here now. The traces suggest a people who lived lightly and moved often, and who left almost nothing behind except the marks their hands made against the slow rise of the land.

**Where the world begins to fray**

This is the place where the rules loosen. The horizon does not stay put. Look toward the far edge of the plateau and the cliffs seem to fold back on themselves, like paper creased and opened again, so that a ridge you saw a moment ago now lies on the opposite side of you. Your shadow sometimes falls a half-beat behind your movements. Sounds arrive slightly out of order: a crack from the cliffs may reach you before the stone visibly breaks, and once in a while you hear your own footsteps from a few seconds in the past, faint and echoing off stone that is not there. In certain hollows the fog thickens into a pale column that seems to hold a figure for a moment before dissolving. Stones sometimes appear to have been moved, though no one has touched them. The land is not quite consistent with itself, and the inconsistencies are not dangerous so much as unsettling, as if the place has not yet agreed on what it is allowed to be.

**Slow change**

Nothing here moves quickly. The fissures widen by a fraction each year. The vines work their way into cracks with the patience of a tide. The pods open over days. The young rock erodes so slowly that the cliffs look nearly the same from one visit to the next, though the ripple-marks in the slabs are, over long spans, flattening and softening. The cycle of growth and decay runs on a scale that outlasts any person who might walk the shelf. Everything is becoming, and becoming takes its time.
