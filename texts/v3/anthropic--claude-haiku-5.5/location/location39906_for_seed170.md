**The Plateau of the Slow Engines**

You arrive at the edge of the plateau without climbing to it. The ground simply stops being a slope and becomes a level, as if the land had decided to stop rising. The air is thin and hot, pressing on the skin like a palm. The sky is a pale bleached blue, nearly white at the horizon, and the sun sits there without moving much, a disc that looks heavier than it should.

**The ground**

Nothing here is wet and nothing has been wet for a very long time. The surface is a crust of pale salt, cracked into polygons that curl at the edges like old paper. Where the crust has broken, the material beneath is dark and fine, a grey-brown dust that clings to boots and smells faintly of struck metal and scorched stone. In places the salt has built into low ridges that run in parallel lines across miles, all oriented the same way, as though the wind here blew from a single direction for ten thousand years and never changed its mind.

The erosion is old and settled. Stone pillars stand in groups, rounded and pitted, each one worn into the shape its resistance allowed. Their faces are glassy in patches where the surface has partially fused under heat. Some pillars have been hollowed into arches, and the arches have been hollowed again, so that the oldest forms look like lace. Nothing falls quickly here. Rock comes down a grain at a time.

**The engines**

The tech on this plateau is not built. There are no roads, no walls, no stacked housings or signposts. Instead, scattered across the flat at intervals of several kilometers, there are machines grown into the ground, or perhaps the ground grown around them. Each one is a mechanism the size of a cathedral, half-buried, its visible parts dark metal and banded mineral, joined by pistons, gears, and great slow shafts. They appear to be drilling. Their heads are sunk into the crust, and their bodies rise and fall over periods you can only measure by coming back.

They are loud, but the sound is deep and continuous, a pulse felt in the sternum more than heard. Put your hand on a pillar near one and it hums. The machines turn by a few degrees per year. You can see the difference if you photograph them, but not if you watch. Some have stopped, their gears locked with a sheen of mineral crust, and those seem to be slowly sinking into the plain. A few have shifted their angle so far that they now lean across their neighbors, and the shafts that once pointed at the sky now point at the horizon.

Nobody maintains them. There is no fuel line, no cable, no visible power source. Energy seems to come from the heat itself, drawn up through the crust, or from the slow gradient of the planet's inside. The machines do not seem to know they are machines. They are simply doing the thing they do.

**The fauna**

The overwhelming presence here is animal, but it is rarely seen. The plateau is enormous, and the creatures that live on it are large, widely separated, and very patient. Their bodies are plated and banded, dull ochre and bone-white, shaped somewhat like tortoises but with long jointed legs that fold beneath them when they rest. They move in migrations that take seasons. Once a year, or perhaps once in a human lifetime, a line of them crosses the flat, dozens of kilometers long, and the ground trembles under their steps.

Their voices are the loudest thing on the plateau. Each creature calls from a resonant chamber in its chest, and the calls carry for miles, low and rising, overlapping into a chorus that never quite stops. In quiet hours, at dusk, the entire plain seems to sing. The calls do not sound like warnings or mating. They sound like the machines, as though the animals and the engines have been tuned to the same slow frequency over a very long time.

Between the creatures there is nothing. You can walk for days without seeing a living thing, and then turn a corner of rock and find one standing motionless, watching, or find only its tracks, deep grooves in the salt, filled with a faint blue-black residue that does not evaporate.

**The lack of plants**

There is no vegetation in the usual sense. Mineral crusts are colored in layers that look almost like growth, blooming into rosettes and branching threads, but when you touch them they are cold and brittle, and they do not regrow when broken. A few fungal-looking mats cling to the shaded south faces of the pillars, grey and papery, but they are sparse and seem to be dying slowly, releasing faint puffs of dust when disturbed. Otherwise the plateau is bare.

**The weirdness**

This is where the place begins to loosen. Light behaves normally in the first hours after you arrive, but then you begin to notice things. A shadow falls a few seconds before the object casting it comes into view around a pillar. Sound returns from directions that do not match the sources; a call from the east echoes back from the west, a half-second late, as though the air holds a memory of where it was spoken. The horizon sometimes repeats: a line of machines seen to the north appears again in the south, identical, a few degrees shifted, and when you walk toward the second set they are gone and the first set has moved.

Distance is unreliable. Walking toward a pillar that looks two kilometers away, you reach it in twenty minutes, then find yourself still two kilometers from the next one, which had been hidden behind it. Maps made here disagree with themselves. Stone strata in one cliff face run upward in sequence and then, a few meters on, run downward, the same layers in reverse order, as if the mountain has folded in on itself and forgotten which way it was facing.

Some of the engines seem to be shifting not only their angle but their position, slowly, so that a path you marked last week no longer leads where you expected. The machines themselves may be a kind of clock, or the plateau may be keeping its own time, one that does not match the sun's.

**The rules, as best they can be guessed**

Nothing here moves fast. Change happens over seasons, decades, centuries. The engines turn, the stone wears, the animals migrate, but none of it is urgent. A traveler who stays long enough begins to feel the pace in their own body, the heartbeat slowing to match the pulse in the ground.

Water does not exist, and everything that lives here has adapted to its absence, and to the heat, and to the sound. The animals drink from nothing visible; perhaps they draw moisture from the salt, or from the machines' heat exchange, or from something not yet understood. Do not expect to find a spring.

At the far edges of the plateau, where the flat finally ends, there is a drop-off into darkness and a faint cool wind rising from below. The engines do not extend that far. The animals do not go there. Whatever the plateau is part of, it seems to end at that line, and nothing crosses back.

**Closing**

If you stand at the center of the flat at dusk, the engines and the animals are all calling at once, and the light goes from white to amber to a deep violet that seems to come from below rather than above. The shadows lean in the wrong direction. You may hear a voice that sounds like your own, a few seconds behind you. The machines continue turning. The salt continues to crack and reform. The plateau does not notice you, and it does not need to. It has been doing this for longer than the idea of a place has existed, and it will keep doing it after the idea is gone.
