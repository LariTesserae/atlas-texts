The land stands above the clouds, or near enough. Long ago this was a mountain range, a jumble of peaks thrust up by slow violence, and then the ages wore it down. The summits went first, then the ridges, until what remains is a high tableland a few thousand meters up, flat as a cooling slab. Its edges fall away in broken escarpments where the old folds show through like the grain in driftwood. Below them, a white sea of cloud lies on the lowlands, and the plateau rises out of it into a warm, wet air that never quite clears.

Warmth is the surprise. At this height the air should bite, but the latitude is kind and the cloud acts like a blanket. The temperature sits at blood heat in the shade and climbs in the thin sun. Everything here is damp. Moisture collects on stone in beads that run together into threads. Rain comes in squalls that sweep across the tableland in grey curtains, drum for twenty minutes, and then drift on, leaving the air steaming and the rock shining. Between squalls, condensation hangs in the hollows, and a fine mist threads the lower vegetation, rising and falling with the slow breathing of the plateau.

Almost all the surface is alive. There is little bare rock, and where there is, it is slick with a green-black film of cyanobacteria and crusted lichen. Beyond that, plant growth covers everything in layers. Moss carpets the flat ground in thick cushions that squelch underfoot and hold water like sponges. Above the moss stand low shrubs with waxy leaves, and above those rise tree-ferns with trunks as thick as a man's body, their fronds spread wide enough to make a roof. Vines run between them in ropes and curtains. Epiphytes crowd every branch and fork: bromeliad rosettes that hold pools of water in their cups, orchids clinging like barnacles, mats of fern on fern. Where a fallen trunk lies, it is already a garden, its bark furred with liverworts and its hollow filled with seedlings. The plants are not scattered across the land so much as they are the land. There is no soil visible, only a deep brown loam heaped under the living layers, rich with the rot of previous generations.

Life thrums through this green. The fauna is overwhelming, and the air is never quiet. Insects rise in clouds from every disturbed leaf: beetles with iridescent carapaces, moths the size of a palm, midges that swarm in columns over the pools. Frogs call from the bromeliad cups in a rolling chorus that sounds like wet fingers on a drum. Birds with harsh and musical voices work the canopy. Something larger, a kind of cricket or cicada, saws continuously from the fern crowns, a sound like steel rasping on steel that rises and falls in waves. Monkeys or something like them howl from the far side of the plateau, and the howls roll across the flatness and return from the escarpments. Predators move in the layers below the canopy: a striped cat that stalks the moss, snakes that hang from the vines, and great birds that drop out of the cloud with a sudden rush of wings. The whole place is busy with eating and being eaten, growing and rotting, the constant crackle of things moving through leaves.

Yet for all this noise, the place is strangely empty. The life clusters in patches. Between one knot of ferns and the next there may be a hundred paces of open moss where nothing stirs but drifting mist. The plateau is vast, and its inhabitants are few compared to its extent. Walk for an hour and you may pass only a handful of living groves. The silence between them is almost as striking as the noise within. Then you round a bend, step into a valley of tree-ferns, and the sound hits you like weather.

People have been here, though not many, and not for long. Their marks are faint and mostly swallowed. A low wall of dressed stone runs along the lip of an escarpment, its blocks pried apart by roots and now half buried in moss. Inside a ring of fallen trunks, a few upright stones stand in a rough circle, their faces carved with spirals worn almost to nothing by rain. Near a stream bed, a scatter of flaked black glass lies among the leaf litter, the sharp chips of tools made by hands that knew the stone well. There is a stepped platform cut into a rock shelf, its treads green with algae, perhaps once a place for gathering or for offerings. Some of the stonework has been taken over entirely by the plants, so that a wall is now a hedge with a stone heart, and a doorway is a tangle of vine roots framing a dark gap. The people who made these things used hand tools and simple fire. They knew how to split stone, how to lash poles, how to build with what lay around them. Their craft is in the fitted joints and the careful spacing of the old stones, and in the fact that they chose this high grey table to mark.

The ground is always moving. Water is constantly on the way somewhere, pooling in hollows, seeping out of moss, spilling over the escarpment edges in threads that drop hundreds of meters into the cloud. Streams cut shallow channels through the moss and vanish into sinkholes that lead down into the rock. The old stone is slowly dissolving, and the sinks widen over the seasons. Trees fall, and their trunks are colonized within weeks. Rain washes soil into the pools, which turn brown and then green as algae blooms. The weather cycles run through every day: morning mist, midday sun that draws steam from the leaves, afternoon squalls, evening calm, night rain. Above it all, the cloud sea shifts, sometimes rising to swallow the plateau entirely so that the world shrinks to a few meters of visibility and the sound of dripping, then falling away again to reveal the lowlands far below, green and vast, the peaks of older mountains standing as islands in the white.

It is a place of ancient settling, of things worn into their final shape and then covered in life. The mountains are gone, and what is left is a table of patient stone, carpeted in breathing green, loud with creatures that do not stop, and marked only faintly by the hands that passed through and left their small, careful traces.
