**The Stair-Terraces of Hallowmere**

You stand on a plateau that should not be this high. The air is thin enough to notice in the lungs, yet it is hot and wet, thick as a bathhouse. Sweat beads and does not run; it hangs on the skin in a fine mist that never quite falls. Above you the sky is a clean, ordinary blue under normal light, but the light does not agree with itself. Shadows fall toward the north from some trees and toward the south from others, and the stone steps throw shadows that stop a hand's width above the ground, as if the sun were a few feet away.

**The city**

Hallowmere is a city cut into the flank of a caldera. Its builders carved terraces from the volcanic rock in tiers that descend in long, shallow arcs, each one a street, each street a wall of basalt blocks fitted without mortar. The stonework is immense and patient. Doorways are sized for something taller than a person, and the lintels bear carvings worn nearly flat: spirals, branching lines, rows of hollow circles that might be windows or might be eyes. Nothing here was made by machines, and nothing here moves. The city is built for permanence, and it has achieved it.

Stairs connect the terraces, hundreds of them, in flights that double back and cross one another at different heights. Some stairs rise to a landing and then continue upward into the face of the next terrace, as if the stone had been pierced by a staircase that always meant to go through it. Walking them, you lose a sense of which level you are on. The rock wall beside you seems to lean outward slightly, so that from the bottom the upper tiers look like they overhang, and from the top the lower ones seem to be pressing up toward you.

**The vegetation**

Everything grows here. Ferns the height of trees, their fronds unfurled like the ribs of enormous umbrellas, crowd the lower terraces and push through the paving in slabs. Moss lies in thick green pelts over every horizontal surface and climbs the vertical ones, so the city reads as a sculpture of living velvet with stone showing only in the sharpest edges. Vines as thick as a man's arm have wrapped the columns of old gateways and split them, and in some places the stone has opened like a fruit around a tangle of roots that wrapped it from within.

Flowers appear in unlikely places: from cracks in the lintels, in the hollows of carved eyes, along the rims of dry fountains. They are large and waxy, colored deep crimson and bruised violet, and they hang heavy with water. There are no bees, no butterflies, no birds, no insects of any kind. The flowers open and close on their own slow schedule and are never pollinated, never visited. They simply exist in the air, dripping.

The fungi are the strangest growths. Pale shelves of them spread across old walls in tiers that echo the architecture, as though the mycelium had learned the terraces and followed them. Some of them glow faintly at dusk, though the light never actually changes, so the glow appears always to be just beginning or just ending.

**Water**

Water is everywhere, and it is the reason the place sounds the way it does. Springs rise from fissures in the terrace floors and run in channels cut long ago, feeding a network of basins and aqueducts that carries them downhill through the city. Some channels run uphill, which should be impossible, but the water climbs the sloped gutters with the steady patience of a river that has forgotten gravity or been persuaded out of it, and it spills over the upper rims into basins that feed the next channel up. The loop is closed. The water has been circulating through Hallowmere for as long as the city has existed, and it has never once slowed, risen, or fallen.

Condensation drips from every surface. The stone sweats constantly. Water runs down the faces of the terraces in thin sheets that catch and bead and fall in a fine persistent rain that is not raining, because the sky above is clear. The cloud sits at the level of the rooftops, low and white, and does not drift.

At the center of the city a great cataract pours over the rim of the caldera and falls a thousand feet into the mist below. It does not vary. Its width, its volume, its roar, are identical at every moment. A leaf dropped into its upper stream will be found, after any length of time, exactly where it was meant to be found.

**Sound**

The deafening sound comes first from the cataract, a continuous thunder that fills the caldera and reflects off the walls until it has no direction. It is so loud that conversation would be impossible, and so constant that the mind stops registering it after a few minutes, though the body never quite stops feeling it: a pressure in the chest, a faint tremor in the teeth.

Beneath the roar, other sounds layer in. Springs hiss as they rise through fissures. Channels gurgle and knock against their stone sides. Wind moves through the open doorways and the carved eyes of the lintels, and each carving sings a single low tone, so the whole city hums in a chord that shifts with your position but never with time. Where the vines have split the stone, the cracks whistle. Where the moss is thickest, the sound is muffled to a soft breathing.

Sometimes, faintly, from deep within the terraces, comes a knocking, rhythmic and slow, like a hammer on stone. It never comes closer. When you locate its direction, it has moved, and when you turn back, it is coming from where it was before.

**Weirdness and the rules of the place**

Reality here does not hold its seams. Distances fold. A stair you have climbed for an hour places you at the same arch you passed at the beginning, though you did not turn around. A doorway opens onto a terrace that lies a level below the one you entered from, and then, if you step back through it, you find yourself where you began. The city has the logic of a dream that has been written down and cannot be revised.

Scale wavers too. From some angles the lintels are the size of a cathedral's windows; from others they are no larger than a hand. The flowers are sometimes as big as a doorway and sometimes as small as a thumbnail, though they hang on the same vines. The statues that stand at intervals along the stairs turn their heads to face the traveler, slowly, when no one is watching, and always face the same way when you look directly at them.

Time itself is frozen in place. Leaves on the ground are caught mid-fall in the air, though they settle to the moss without ever landing. The water in the basins holds the same ripples, the same reflections of the same low clouds. Light falls at the same angle, the same temperature, the same brightness, forever. There is no dawn here and no dusk. There is only the present, held still, with the cataract thundering through it.

**The atmosphere**

The heat is heavy and damp, and it pulls at you. Every breath tastes of stone, moss, and standing water. The air carries a faint mineral sweetness, as if something beneath the city were slowly cooking. Your clothes never dry. Your skin never quite cools. The sense of being inside something alive, something that has been breathing for longer than the stone has been cut, is hard to shake.

You feel watched, but you cannot see the watcher. The carved eyes on the lintels are dark hollows filled with moss, and yet you are sure, at certain angles, that they have followed you across the terrace.

**Leaving**

There is no clear way out. The stairs lead downward toward the cataract and upward toward the caldera rim, and both routes curve back on themselves. Somewhere beyond the walls, the plateau drops away into a sea of cloud, and the cloud is warm and white and utterly still. If you climb high enough, you may see the rim of the crater, but you will find that it is always just a little farther than it appears, and the stair you are on is always just one turn from the one that leads back down.

The city holds its shape. It keeps its water. It keeps its silence beneath the roar. And it keeps you, for a while, in the long, unhurried present where nothing ever arrives and nothing ever leaves.
