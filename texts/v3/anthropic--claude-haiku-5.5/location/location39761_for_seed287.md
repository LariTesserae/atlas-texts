You descend into it, or you have already descended. The land drops away in terraced steps of cracked concrete and bedrock, and the air thickens with each one until it has the weight and warmth of breath. This is a basin roughly the size of a village square, walled on all sides by sheer, weathered stone. The rim above is hidden by a canopy of broad, glossy leaves, so you never see the sky, only a pale, diffuse dimness that seems to come up through the vegetation itself.

**Terrain and materials**

The floor is a settled sediment of clay, ash, and crushed masonry, packed hard by long ages and softened at the margins by standing water. Where the ground dips, pools hold a warm, brownish water that steams faintly in the mornings of whatever passes for morning here. The walls are a dark, vesicular stone, eroded into fluted columns and deep horizontal bands, as if the rock had been slowly pressed and combed by water over thousands of years. Nothing here is raw. Every surface has been rounded, stained, and lichened into a settled equilibrium.

Scattered across the floor and climbing the walls are the machines. They are large, iron-colored, and mostly ruined in the way that a thing becomes ruined when it has stopped mattering to anyone: cylindrical boilers lying on their sides, a cluster of riveted tanks, the ribbed housing of a turbine half-sunk in silt, conveyor frames bridging nothing, a great wheel the size of a house rusted to the color of dried blood. Pipes run in every direction, some still joined to one another, some broken into sections that sit at angles like the bones of something that once walked upright. Gauges remain on several panels, their glass long clouded and their needles frozen against stops.

**Ecology**

The vegetation is dense and nearly total. Vines spiral up the columns and wrap the pipes. Broad-leafed plants the color of dark jade fill every gap, and something like a fern with stiff, metallic-looking fronds grows in tight rings around the boilers. Moss lies across everything in thick velvet. Fungi form shelves on the rusted metal, pale and wide, glowing faintly where the dim light touches them.

Odd things grow where the machines are. Roots have penetrated the cracked tanks and reached in, fat and white, to drink from the condensation inside. Stems wind through the gaps of the turbine housing and emerge from the other side bearing small, waxy fruit. Some vines follow the old copper conduit lines exactly, as if the plant has learned the shape of the wiring, and along those lines the leaves are a shade brighter. You will notice this only after a while.

There is no animal life. No insect hums, no birds, no scuttling in the leaf litter. The stillness of living things is absolute, and it makes the rest of the place feel more deliberate.

**Atmosphere and light**

The heat is constant and close, the kind that soaks into clothing and sits in the lungs. Humidity is high. Water beads on every surface, drips from the upper canopy in slow, irregular plinks, and condenses on the cold iron in a fine silver mist that hangs in the air at knee height. In the lower basin the mist thickens into a warm fog that clings to the pools and the machines.

Light arrives from no single source. A dim, greenish-gold glow seeps down through the canopy and seems to come from the vegetation as much as from above. In places near the fungal shelves the glow is stronger, a soft cool luminescence like moonlight on water. Shadows are deep and soft-edged. Long stretches of the basin stay in a permanent twilight, the colors muted to ochre, olive, and rust.

**Sound**

This is the loudest part of the place, though the loudness is hard to pin to any single source. Beneath everything is a deep, low resonance, a throb felt more in the chest than heard in the ears. It comes from somewhere deep in the basin floor, perhaps from pipes or chambers that still channel heat or water from the depths below. Over it rise the sounds of the wet world: the splash and drip of water into pools, the gurgle of hot springs breaking the surface, the hiss of steam escaping from fissures in the rock. Every so often a tank groans as the metal expands in the heat, a long wail that rises and falls and slowly dies away. Something inside one of the turbines knocks, a slow metallic clank that repeats roughly every ninety seconds, as if an old mechanism is still turning a long way down.

**Rules of the place**

Some things here seem to be governed by principles that do not quite match the world outside.

The machines are not dead. They are not running either. They persist in a state of very slow activity. Pressure gauges shift by a hair over the course of weeks. Valves that were closed have drifted open a degree. Vines that wrap a machine tighten their grip over seasons, and the metal bends a little in response.

Growth follows metal. The plants are not simply colonizing ruins. They appear to lean toward iron, copper, and the warmth of the old machinery, and the density of life is highest where the infrastructure once carried heat or current.

Water seeks the lowest point and always finds it. Everything that falls into the basin drains toward the central pool, and the central pool is slowly rising, so that each year the lower terraces are a little more submerged and the vegetation there grows thicker to meet the water.

Nothing decays quickly. Leaves that fall into the pools persist for a long time. Dead vines remain on the pipes like fossil imprints. The basin keeps its things, holding onto them in the damp and heat, and it does not give them back quickly.

**Slow change**

Over the span of human lives this place would look almost unchanged. Across generations you would see the pools deepen and creep up the terraces. The vines would thicken and darken, their roots working deeper into the iron. A boiler might split along a seam and collapse into the silt, and a new pool would open where it had stood. The loud resonance from below would rise a little, or fall, following some cycle of heat and pressure that no one here has ever measured. The leaves would turn over, season by season, and the mist would thicken or thin, but the basin would stay the same basin, a sunken chamber of warm water and old iron, slowly becoming a garden that remembers being a factory.
