**The Hollow of Anvel**

You come down into it rather than across it. The land tilts toward a basin so wide that its far rim seems to sit on the horizon, and the ground under you has been worn so long that nothing about it is sharp. The plateau that once stood here has been planed by ages of water and wind into long, low swells of pale grey bedrock, polished like the back of a sleeping animal. Gullies run through it in parallel, all pointing toward the center, like the grain in old wood.

**Light**

The sky is high, bleached, and nearly white at the zenith, with a warm gold smear where the sun hangs over the eastern rim. The light is strong without being harsh, because the ground reflects almost as much as the sky gives. Shadows are thin and blue. Everything is visible at great distance, which is why the scattered structures of the basin look closer than they are. The bright mineral surfaces hold their glare long after dusk, so the nights here are never fully dark. The bedrock shines faintly pale-green for an hour or two after sunset, as if it had been storing the day.

**Ground and Water**

The basin floor is a mosaic of pale silt, crusted salt-white in the dry hollows and dark and slick where the water has been. Wherever the bedrock dips, shallow pools lie in it, clear as glass and cold to the touch. The water here is never still for long. Every few days a grey wall of rain comes over the eastern rim and falls for hours, filling the gullies and sending brown sheets down the slopes. The basin then drains slowly through a series of cut channels toward a sink at its center, where the water disappears into fissures no one has ever followed to the bottom. Fog gathers at dawn in the lowest parts and lifts by midday. The stone sweats constantly, so every surface carries a fine film of moisture that beads and runs in the grooves of the rock, sometimes uphill, following the faint pull of old fractures.

**The Built Landscape**

The settlements of Anvel are stone, and they are very old. Cities here have not been founded so much as accumulated. Each generation has built over the ruins of the last, and the foundations of the first, cut from the bedrock with stone chisels, lie a dozen courses beneath the present streets. Walls are of dry-laid basalt blocks, fitted by hand and set at the angles the flood water takes. Houses rise in stepped tiers up the sides of the gullies, linked by stairs and narrow causeways over the channels. Cisterns are carved into the rock, lined with fired clay, and fed by runnels that catch every shower.

Nothing is built flat. Streets climb. Roofs are shallow and drain toward the uphill side, so the rain runs off in a controlled sheet. Wide, low embankments of rammed clay line the lower quarters, grey and pitted, and each one is marked with flood lines painted in rust-red, one above another, like the rings of a tree.

Tools are of stone, bone, and fired clay, with occasional fittings of hammered copper, worn smooth and carefully kept. Kilns smoke at the edges of the lower town. Pottery, net-weights, and grinding stones are common. There is no evidence of writing beyond tally marks and symbols scratched into lintels.

**Fauna**

This place is teeming, though nothing in it is green. Grazers move across the silt in herds, low and armored, with shells like flattened domes and many short legs that click against the stone. They feed on the dark bacterial mats that form in the shallow pools, which bloom and fade in cycles with the rain. Filter-feeders hang in the clear pools, extending fan-shaped plumes of translucent tissue, and small, quick things with glassy carapaces skim the surfaces in loose, shifting clusters. Larger predators are rare but present: long, pale, flat-bodied hunters that lie along the rocks in the sun and move only when the light angles just so.

Everything here seems to have adapted to the mineral world and the constant moisture. The animals have no eyes that look like eyes. Some have clusters of sensing pits along the margin of the shell; others read the vibration of the bedrock. In the stone itself, pale strands of something like fungus or mineral filament run through the cracks, though they are not plants and do not spread like them.

**Sound**

The Hollow is almost silent. Rain makes a steady patter on the rock and a rushing hiss in the channels. Wind rarely reaches the floor, and when it does it passes over with a low, hollow breath. The grazers make soft clicking sounds, and the shells of the filter-feeders chime faintly when they close. The people speak quietly, as if the stone carried sound too far. Bells and drums are not used. Even at market, the voices stay low.

**Dynamics and Change**

The Hollow is never still. The water rises and falls on its own timetable, reshaping the lower quarters each season. A flood can take a street in an afternoon and leave a new channel where the road had been. Mineral deposits grow overnight in the pools, forming fins and lattices that the next storm breaks apart. The pale predators shift their hunting grounds with the sun. Even the bedrock flakes and slides after heavy rain, so that the map of the basin is redrawn by every generation.

The people live with this by building in layers and by moving. Families keep two or three houses at different heights and shift their hearths when the water reaches the lower floors. Markets are temporary, set up on whatever terrace is dry that week. The city's oldest stone, the deepest and most ruined foundations, is said to have been laid by people who stayed through the largest flood of all, when the sink at the center rose and the whole basin filled to the rim for a season.

**Rules of the Place**

- Water always returns. What the flood takes, it also leaves behind in new forms.
- Nothing grows upward. Things here spread across the stone or sink into it.
- The light lingers. Night is a pale, glowing dusk, never fully dark.
- Silence is a habit of the place, not an absence. Sound travels oddly here; a whisper can be heard across a terrace, and a shout can vanish in the rain.
- The center sink is never explored past its lip. The people say it breathes, and that when it does, the rain comes early.
