# The Weathered Table

The land is a high plateau that was once mountains. Whatever raised it has long since stopped, and the work of wind, water, and frost has taken the peaks down to a broad, undulating expanse at roughly the height where the air thins and the clouds sit low. The rock is granite and gneiss, rounded into domes and tors, their edges softened to a tactile smoothness, like river stones that forgot the river. Nothing here is sharp. Even the deepest gorges have slumped into long, graded V-shapes, their walls weathered into terraces. The eye can find no fresh break anywhere. This is a landscape that has reached its final shape, and it lies under a sky that seems to have settled into the same patience.

## Air and Water

The air is warm, close, and always faintly wet. The altitude keeps the heat from becoming oppressive, but the latitude gives it a soft, steady warmth, the kind that makes wool unbearable and bare skin bead quickly. Mist rolls across the plateau most mornings and lingers in the hollows until late afternoon. It does not quite fall as rain. It settles, beads, drips. Rain does come, but rarely in storms; it arrives as long grey drizzles that last days, and then the sky clears for a week and everything steams.

Water is everywhere and nowhere. There are no streams worth the name, only seeps that issue from cracks in the bedrock and run a few meters before sinking back into the sand-choked fractures. Pools form in the shallow basins worn into the domes, some no wider than a hand, some the size of a village pond, all of them clouded with silt and pollen. Between these, the stone itself holds moisture. Every surface that faces the sky carries a film of condensation in the cool hours, and the creatures of the plateau have learned to drink from it.

The water cycle here is slow and fine-grained. Nothing floods, nothing is swept away. The land has been wet and dry in alternation for so long that everything is adapted to both, and the dampness has become a permanent condition rather than an event.

## Vegetation

Plants are sparse. They are not absent, but they are distributed the way a few coins might lie on a wide floor. Lichens are the dominant life on the bare stone: grey-green crusts, orange rosettes, black rings that spread at a fraction of a millimeter per year. Their colors give the domes a mottled, camouflaged look, as though the rock has been slowly painted by something with infinite time.

Mosses fill the seep channels and the cracks between flagstones. Where a seep sinks into sand, a ring of low, dense cushion plants grows in a brilliant band of color, then thins out into bare ground. Stunted shrubs with thick, waxy leaves cling to the leeward sides of the larger rocks, their roots wrapped around boulders like fingers. A few trees exist, but they are twisted, dwarfed, and very old, some of them likely hundreds of years old at the height of a human's arm. Their trunks are sculpted by wind and fog into strange, bulging shapes, and their canopies are thin, mostly dead wood with a few clusters of greenery at the tips.

There are no forests, no meadows, no grassy sweeps. The plateau is open, and the open ground is the rule.

## Fauna

This is where the land becomes overwhelming. The plateau is teeming. Not with large animals, which are rare and mostly seen at a distance, but with an immense density of smaller life that covers every available surface and lives in every crevice.

The ground is alive. Where the soil has accumulated in sheltered places, it is so full of burrowing invertebrates that the earth crumbles under the fingers in a way that feels like pressing into a sponge. Shelled creatures, some the size of a lentil and others as large as a fist, crawl over every stone in slow, continuous migrations. Their trails are visible in the morning mist as silvery streaks, and when the sun comes through, the ground glints.

Insects are everywhere. They hover in clouds above the pools and rest in dense mats on the lichen. There are beetles with iridescent carapaces that cluster on the seep-moss in shifting, mosaic patterns. There are flying things that move in slow spirals above the domes, thousands at a time. The air hums with them, though this is a low, unbroken sound more than a noise.

Among the invertebrates are larger creatures that move with deliberate slowness: long-legged walkers with segmented bodies, something like armored millipedes that curl into spirals when disturbed, and broad, flat grazers that move across the lichen fields like living carpets, scraping the rock clean in their wake. Their scraping is one of the few sounds on the plateau, a faint, continuous rasp that rises and falls with the wind.

Vertebrates are fewer but present. Small, quick, mottled things dart among the rocks. Slow, heavy animals with thick hides and short legs graze the lichen, and their droppings, feeding the soil, are part of the whole chain. Birds nest in the crevices of the tors, and their calls are sparse and far apart. Nothing dominates. Everything is packed in, and nothing is loud.

## Built Traces

Only faint marks of anything that might be called building remain. Stone rings, once used for purposes no one now remembers, lie half-buried in the moss, their outlines recognizable only from above or in the low light of evening. A low wall, knee-high and mostly collapsed, runs for some distance along a ridge, its stones fused into the lichen so that it reads more as a natural ridge than an artifact.

In a few places, flat slabs have been stacked into cairns beside the seeps, perhaps to mark the water, perhaps to honor it. These are very old. Their stones are worn smooth, and lichen has grown over most of them, but a few still stand upright, leaning slightly toward one another like old people who have been talking for a long time.

There are no tools, no pottery, no metal. Whoever made these things left no inventions behind, only shapes. Their purpose is absorbed into the landscape and no longer recoverable. The walls and cairns might be the work of a people, or the work of the land itself, shaped by the same patient forces that wore down the mountains. The plateau does not say which.

## Strangeness

The land is strange in its settledness. Everything seems to have arrived at its final form, and yet everything is still moving, slowly, in cycles too long for a single life to notice. The lichen grows across the domes so gradually that one can only tell by comparing against old maps, if such maps existed. The beetles migrate between pools in patterns that repeat every few decades, and the seeps shift their courses so slowly that the cairns, once beside them, now stand on dry stone a dozen meters away.

There is a quality of attention here. The fauna's density gives the impression of a land being watched, or of many small minds, each occupying a cell in a vast mosaic, all acting without hurry. The stones are dense with life, but the life has no edge. It flows into the stone and out again, and the boundary between living and unliving feels provisional.

Shadows move slowly over the plateau in the long afternoons. Light falls normally, without drama. The sky is high and pale, often veiled by mist, and the sun appears as a diffuse brightness rather than a hard disk. Colors are soft: greens, greys, orange-lichen rust, the silver of crawling trails, the dark blue of the pools at evening.

## Sound and Time

The plateau is quiet, but not silent. The loudest things are the rasp of grazers, the hum of insects, the drip of condensation from the rock edges, and occasionally the call of a bird. Long stretches pass without any sound that rises above the ambient soft rustle of countless small bodies moving through lichen and moss.

Time here is measured in erosion. A dome loses a few grains per decade. A seep moves a few meters per century. A cairn settles a few millimeters per generation. The landscape does not change in any way a person would witness, and yet it is always changing, the way a body is always renewing its cells. Nothing here is urgent. Everything here has already happened, and everything here is still happening, very slowly, under the warm, damp sky.
