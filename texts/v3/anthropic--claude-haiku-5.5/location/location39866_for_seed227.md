**The Throat of Hollin Dell**

You find it by listening before you see it. Somewhere past the alders and the black-trunked birches, a sound rises through the trees, a continuous roar with no edges, the kind of noise you feel in your sternum before your ears sort it out. The ground dips. The light, already thin under the canopy, thins further, and then the land simply stops.

The place is a sinkhole, a doline about forty paces across, where the valley floor has given way over some span of centuries. Its rim is a ring of wet limestone, grey-brown and furred with moss so thick it swallows a boot to the ankle. Ferns hang from every crack: hart's-tongue, shield fern, a pale lacework of something that may be a liverwort grown to the size of a hand. Lush does not begin to describe it. Everything here is layered, dripping, and slowly reaching toward the thin light, and nothing seems in a hurry.

The light is the first strangeness. The canopy overhead is dense enough that even noon arrives as a dim, greenish dusk, and inside the sinkhole the walls rise so steeply that the sky is a grey coin far above. You see by a kind of gloaming that seems to come up from the ground itself. Moss holds water, water holds the faint light, and the whole bowl feels like the inside of a closed hand.

The cold lives in the stone. Even in summer the air carries the bite of a deep spring, and the limestone sweats a fine mist that beads on your sleeves. Breath hangs. The water that feeds the sinkhole comes from a spring somewhere up the slope, and it arrives already chilled, having traveled a long way through the dark bedrock. It seeps down the walls in a hundred threads, collects in the moss, and finally falls.

It falls into the throat. At the center of the basin the floor tilts and opens into a fissure perhaps three body-widths across, black and wet-edged, and a stream of water drops into it from a lip of rock in a single white column. The sound of that drop fills the whole bowl. It is not merely loud; it is total. It comes off the walls, rebounds from the fissure, rolls up the slope and back again, so that there is no direction to it and no quiet to retreat into. Speech is impossible at a distance of two paces. Thought itself feels crowded. You understand, standing there, why the people who once knew this place probably came only in certain seasons, or spoke only with their hands.

Around the rim, if you look closely, the marks of human hands are everywhere, and all of them are old. Fitted stones form a rough curb along the north edge, worn smooth and rounded, now half-buried in moss. A few of them have shallow grooves cut across their tops, perhaps for lashing or for setting timber, though the timber has long since rotted away. Near the spring-lip someone set a row of upright slabs, three or four of them, leaning slightly outward from the wall like teeth. Their faces bear pecked marks, dots and short strokes in a pattern you cannot quite read, and lichen has crept into the grooves so that the marks are as much green as grey. There is a stone trough, big enough to hold a few gallons of water, its lip cracked and mended with clay that has itself become stone. Nothing is intact in the sense of a building. Everything is a remnant, a sentence that has lost its verbs.

Tools survive, mostly in the wet margins where the mud has kept them. A flint blade, its edge still sharp under a crust of silt. A cracked antler handle with a hafting groove. Wooden stakes gone black and soft as bread, still driven into the banks at angles that suggest a footpath once ran down the western slope, though the path has long since slumped into the sinkhole's gradual embrace. Someone once made a crude ladder of lashed poles, and parts of it still hang from a root in the eastern wall, green with growth.

Fauna is sparse, which seems right for a place this enclosed and this loud. A few things move. A dipper bobs on a stone at the edge of the spray, bobbing and bobbing, unbothered by the roar. Something small and pale, a salamander or a blind cave-adapted newt, hides beneath the moss near the fissure. Once, high on the wall, you glimpse a larger animal, a shape shifting between ferns, but when you look again it is gone. The insects are few and slow in the cold. Spiders hang their webs in the mist, beaded and sagging. Otherwise the bowl belongs to water, stone, and green things.

The whole place changes only at the pace of geology and growth. The spring deposits its minerals in pale crusts on the rock, building up a little each season. The moss creeps outward. The fissure widens by a hair's breadth every decade as water works at the limestone, and every few centuries a slab might fall from the rim, making a new gap, a new fern-garden, a new lip for the water to fall from. The stone curbs are slowly sinking into the sodden ground. The painted marks grow greener. Nothing here is violent. The sinkhole is patient, and its deafening voice is simply the sound of a very old process still in motion.

If you stay long enough, and can bear the roar, you begin to feel the place as a kind of room that has been furnished slowly by water and by a few human hands, and then left alone to become itself. Nothing here expects you. You are a single damp visitor in a vast, deep green silence that only seems silent because it is so loud.
