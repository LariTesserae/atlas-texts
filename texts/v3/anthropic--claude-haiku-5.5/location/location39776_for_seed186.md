The first thing you notice is the height. The land rises in stepped terraces, each one a little higher and a little thinner in air, until the highest ridges disappear into a permanent cloud deck the color of wet slate. Below the cloud, the valleys are full of something that looks like weather held in place: fog that doesn't drift so much as settle, pooling in the hollows and rising slowly between the ridges.

**The ground**

The bedrock is dark, fractured basalt, split into plates and stacked like broken crockery. Between the plates, soil has built up over a very long time: black, spongy peat that gives underfoot and releases a smell of iron and cold rot. Water seeps everywhere. Small rivulets braid across the slopes and disappear into moss, then reappear a few meters down, cloudy with silt. Where the water collects, it forms tarns, flat black pools so still they hold the cloud's reflection, and sometimes a second, inverted cloud beneath them.

The erosion here is old and settled. The ridgelines have been rounded by millennia of rain and freeze-thaw, and the gullies have reached a balance where they carry exactly what the slopes shed. Nothing is collapsing dramatically now. Scree fans sit at the foot of the cliffs, mossed over, with boulders gone green and soft-edged.

**The vegetation**

The lushness is unexpected at this altitude and cold. Everything grows low and thick and close:

- Mosses in great cushions, some the size of sheep, bright green on top and rust-brown underneath, holding water like sponges. Pressing them releases a cold stream.
- Lichens in pale grey and ochre sheets over the rock, sometimes lifting into frilled, ruffled plates that catch the fog.
- Dwarf shrubs with waxy, dark leaves and small red berries that stay on the branch long after they should have dropped, shriveled and sweet.
- Tall, pale stalks of a grass-like plant that grows in dense stands up to waist height, each blade sheathed in a film of condensation, so the whole stand shimmers when the wind moves through it.
- Fungi everywhere. Some form shelflike brackets on the sides of the larger plants. Others rise as clusters of pale, tall stalks with caps that open into thin, translucent membranes, and their spores drift out in faint plumes when disturbed.

Lower down, where the cloud thins, there are trees: stunted, twisted, and heavily draped in hanging mosses, their trunks crusted with lichen and their branches thick with epiphytes. Their roots grip the basalt plates like fingers.

**The machinery**

This is the strangest part. There is no built structure anywhere, no walls, no roads, no shaped stone. But the place runs on mechanisms. Across the slopes are vents and pressure-systems that behave like industrial equipment without ever having been made. Geysers of steam thump out of cracks in the ground on a rhythm, each one pushing a column of heated fog into the air, and the fog condenses into rain that falls back over the same ground within minutes.

Some of the fauna is equally engine-like. Large, slow, armored creatures move along the valley floors pulling water up from the soil through siphoning tubes along their backs, and as they move, their tubes whine faintly, like a turning gear. Their paths are grooved and regular, like the tread of machinery worn into the peat. Smaller things crawl along the mossy walls in coordinated lines, carrying and sorting material with the precision of an assembly line, though no one is directing them.

**Light and atmosphere**

The sky is almost never fully bright. The cloud deck is thick and continuous at altitude, and light passes through it as a dim, even grey, like a room with the shutters closed. At the edges of the day, the cloud glows faintly from beneath, not with sunlight but with something bioluminescent in the mists, a pale green or blue that pulses slowly and dims at dawn. The deepest hollows are nearly dark, lit only by the slow glow of fungi and phosphorescent moss.

The air is damp and very cold. Breath clouds at once. Everything is slicked with condensation, and the cold bites through layers.

**Sound**

It is quiet, but not silent. The loudest sounds are water: dripping from the leaves, trickling under the moss, the soft slap of tarns against their banks. Then the vents, a deep, rhythmic thump every few minutes that you feel more than hear. Beneath it, the whine of the siphoning creatures when they move, and the rustle of the stalked grasses when wind passes through them, a sound like breath through a sieve. Occasionally a high, thin call from something in the cloud above, and then nothing for a long time.

**Scale and emptiness**

The land stretches for many kilometers. From any high point, you can see several ridges, each a little paler than the last, fading into the cloud. Between them lie long, empty valleys dotted with the occasional cluster of life: a patch of bright moss, a clump of fungi, a pair of creatures grazing a slope, a steam vent throwing up its column. The space between these presences is vast. You can walk for hours without seeing anything move, and then pass a stand of waving grass or a tarn full of drifting spores and realize how much life there is in the spaces that feel empty.

**The rhythm of the place**

Everything here is moving on its own timing. The vents cycle. The fog rises and falls with the temperature. The siphoning creatures follow their grooved paths in patterns that repeat over days. The fungi release their spores at dusk. The berries ripen and drop in slow waves across the slopes. Predators stalk the grazing creatures along the valley edges, and the grazing creatures move in tight herds that thin and reform as the cloud shifts. Nothing stays still for long, but everything moves at its own pace, so the landscape feels alive with many overlapping clocks.

It is a cold, dim, wet, and very old place, built by water and heat and time, and now running on its own strange machinery. It has no need for people, and it would be unsettling to find one there, but it is not hostile. It is simply busy, quiet, and full of its own purposes.
