**The Shelf of Tallow Rock**

The land rises here in a long stair of grey stone, and the town is built on the third step. From below you see it as a single mass: roofs of split slate and turf, stacked one above another like shelves in a cupboard, held to the slope by retaining walls that have been rebuilt so many times that no one remembers which stones are original. The highest houses nearly touch the sky. The lowest are rooted in the scree at the edge of the shelf, where the path comes up in switchbacks.

The altitude makes its own weather. Cloud sits on the upper slopes most mornings and comes down in threads, so the town is often wrapped in a fine damp that beads on everything: the stone walls go dark and glossy, the thatch sags with water, and the wooden doors swell so that they must be lifted to close. Rain is not frequent but it is patient. When it comes it falls for a day or two in steady grey curtains, then stops, and the air afterward smells of wet lichen and cold iron. Condensation gathers on the inside of the windows at dawn and runs down in small channels that have worn grooves into the sills over generations.

Water is the town's real wealth. Every roof is pitched to feed a gutter of hollowed stone, and the gutters drain into a network of cisterns cut into the bedrock: round basins, square tanks, long narrow troughs with lids of flat slate. Some hold water year round. Others are dry pits lined with moss, waiting. A central cistern, the Mother Well, sits beneath the square and is reached by a spiral stair. Its surface is almost always still, reflecting a disc of sky through the grate above. Children are told not to lean over it, and mostly they don't.

Vegetation is sparse and hardy. Grey-green lichen covers the stones in plates and rings. Moss fills every shaded seam. Between the walls, stunted junipers lean away from the prevailing wind, their trunks twisted and silvered. Tough grasses grow in the cracks of paving, and in summer a few low cushions of pale flowers open among the scree, though they are small and seldom noticed. There are no orchards. Terraces that were once cultivated are now mostly rock-and-moss, the soil washed down the slope long ago. A few hardy plots of kale, barley, and a grey-leafed root are tended in sheltered corners, behind walls built against the wind.

Light is ordinary and kind. Mornings often bring thin sun through breaks in the cloud, which lights the wet roofs into a sheen of pale gold, and the afternoons settle into soft even grey. Shadows are short and clean. At night the sky is clear more often than not, and the stars are very sharp at this height, though the town keeps few lamps: a handful of oil lanterns hang at the crossroads and over the cistern stair, and the windows show a dim, warm yellow through their paper-stretched shutters.

The people build in stone and timber and the rhythm of their work is slow. Tools are of bone, flint, bronze-edged scrapers (rare and prized), and iron-tipped hand implements that are reforged rather than replaced. Stone is cut with wedges and patience. Timber comes up from the lower forest on the backs of pack animals and is seasoned under the eaves. Pottery is fired in pits. Cloth is wool, dyed in madder and walnut. There is no sign of wheeled haulage beyond a few handcarts, and the switchback path is the main artery of trade, travelled on foot and by the slow, sure-footed hill goats, which are kept for milk and wool and are rarely seen in the open anymore.

Fauna is scarce. Crows and a pale, grey-winged bird that nests in the eaves are the commonest visible animals. Occasionally a fox crosses the scree at dusk, or a pair of high hawks circles over the rim. Insects are few in the cold, though in late summer there are flies in the sun-warm corners of the walls. The town keeps its dogs, mostly shaggy and quiet, and its goats, and that is the full extent of the living company.

Sound is muted. The loudest things are the wind moving through the junipers, the rattle of loose stone on the slope, and the dripping. Water is always dripping somewhere: from a gutter lip, from a roof edge, from a sodden tree, into a cistern that never stops filling and never quite overflows. Voices carry a little in the cold, but people rarely shout. A bell hangs in the square and is rung for weddings, deaths, and the turn of the year, and its note rolls down the terraces and dies out over the empty drop below.

Nothing here moves fast, and nothing has changed in living memory. The same doorways stand in the same leaning walls. The same slate is patched with the same grey mortar. The rain falls in the same seasons, the cisterns rise and fall with the same rhythm, and the same families trade the same wool for the same salt each autumn on the same packed-earth ledge at the head of the path. Visitors sometimes remark on this, and the townsfolk tend to answer that stone is patient, and so they must be too.
