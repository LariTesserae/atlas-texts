# The Weeping Table

The land rises in long, stepped shelves of dark basalt, a plateau so wide that its far edge dissolves into cloud. The rock is old. Ten million years of rain have cut it into a country of gorges, stacked strata, and standing towers called tors, which look like the pillars of a collapsed cathedral. Nothing here is young. Every surface has been worn to a patient smoothness, and the erosion has reached an equilibrium where the rock still falls, slowly, in the same places it always has.

The air is warm, which surprises anyone who expects high country to be cold. The plateau sits near the top of a humid atmosphere, so the heat is thick and wet, and the sun, when it appears at all, is a bruised ember behind a permanent lid of cloud. Light comes in amber for a few hours in the afternoon, then drains into a long grey dusk and a darkness that is nearly total. Stars show only on the rare clear nights, and even then the sky has a murky depth, as though viewed through smoked glass.

## Water

Water is the dominant process here, though it rarely arrives as a storm. Drizzle hangs in the air for days, and the basalt is always beaded with condensation. Each column and ledge carries a film of moisture that gathers into droplets and then runs down in threads. The tors weep constantly. Their faces are streaked with mineral trails in pale ochre and green, and the streaks branch and reconnect like the veins of a leaf. In the gorges, small streams slide over the lip of each shelf in thin, unbroken sheets, forming hanging gardens of moss and fern that catch the mist.

Rain comes in pulses, usually at dusk, when the cooling air above the plateau can no longer hold its moisture. Every few days a larger system forms over the high center, building into a towering anvil that drags the clouds around it into a slow rotation. These storms drop heavy rain for an hour or two, then clear, leaving the rock slick and the gorges roaring. The flood pools collect in shallow basins across the upper shelves and turn into mirrors that reflect the cloud layers. There are pools here that have been reflecting the same sky for centuries.

## Vegetation

Plants are sparse but persistent. The soil is thin, leached, and mostly exposed rock, so growth clings to cracks and wet hollows. Low, dense shrubs grow in tight cushions, with leaves coated in a waxy grey film that sheds water quickly. Lichens cover nearly every stone in mats of rust, ochre, and dull violet, and they grow over the older tors in thick, layered crusts that look like old leather. Mosses fill the seep lines and the gorge walls, and where the mist is heaviest, broad fungal shelves spread from fallen logs in pale fans.

Some of the fungi glow faintly. A soft green light emanates from their gills at night, which gives the gorges a dim, underwater luminescence. Bioluminescent lichens on the tors do the same, so the landscape is never fully dark. The glow is too weak to read by, but enough to show outlines: the curve of a ledge, the shape of a grazing animal, the trail a predator has left behind.

## Fauna

The life here is abundant, and it moves. The plateau supports vast herds of heavy, slow grazers that are built like sheared-off boulders, with plates of armored hide and wide, flat feet for walking on wet rock. They travel in long columns, following the seep lines from shelf to shelf, and their hooves sound like distant hammering on the stone. Smaller burrowing animals honeycomb the soft banks of the gorges, and their tunnels breathe in and out with the pressure of the weather.

The air is busy as well. Flocks of winged animals with long, rigid wings ride the updrafts above the tors, circling for hours without a beat. Their calls are high and thin, the only bright sound in an otherwise muffled landscape. Below them, ground predators hunt the herds: lean, low-slung creatures that move in silent pairs through the mist and wait on ledges above the crossings. Their presence shapes everything. The herds bunch tightly at night, the burrowers seal their tunnels at dusk, and the grazers have learned to walk the seep lines at the edges of gorges, where the footing is firm and the predators have trouble approaching from above.

The cycle of predator and prey runs on the weather. When the storms come, the herds shift to higher ground and the predators follow the flood-channels down, where the prey is caught in the narrows. After a storm, the carrion draws the flyers, and the fungal shelves bloom fast on the decaying flesh. Nothing here is still for long.

## Sound

The plateau is mostly quiet, but the quiet has texture. The drip of water is constant, a thousand small clicks from the tors and seeps that never fully sync. Under that, the wind moves through the gorges with a low, hollow moan that rises and falls with the pressure. The herds add their slow hoofbeats, and the flyers add their thin cries, but these are scattered and distant, as if the landscape is too large to fill with sound.

Then there is the other sound, the one that is not from here. On certain nights, when the wind comes from the southeast, a faint, steady throb reaches the high shelves. It is so low it is felt more than heard, a rhythm like a machine running very far away. It never stops, though it sometimes swells and falls, and no one who lives here has seen what makes it.

## The Distant Furnace

Far beyond the lowest shelf, past the cloud-line, a basin of industrial glow lies under the cloud deck. From the high plateau it appears as a dull orange stain on the underside of the clouds, brightening at night and dimming in the day. Its source is not visible: no towers, no chimneys, just the reflected light and the throbbing sound. The heat rising from that distant basin drives the warm, moist air up the escarpments, and the plateau's rains are its byproduct. The land here owes its weather to a source it will never reach, and the cycle that feeds the herds, the flyers, and the mist has its roots in machinery no creature on the table has ever touched.

## The Rule of the Weeping Stone

The strangest feature of the place is its stone. The tors and columns do not merely collect water; they shape it. Condensation on the basalt flows in spirals, following fine grooves that the rock has carved over time, so that rain-runoff on certain faces winds upward against gravity along the updrafts of wind eddies. Seasonal streams reverse their courses when the great storms come. The herds have learned to read these reversals, and the predators have too. Those who know the stone can predict where the water will run, and so where the grazers will walk and where the hunters will lie in wait.

The Weeping Table holds a balance that is fragile and perpetually in motion: cloud, stone, and blood, moving over old rock in the dim warm air, fed by a furnace no one here has seen.
