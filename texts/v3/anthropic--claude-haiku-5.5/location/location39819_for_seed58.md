**The Sump at Haldane Cut**

The town sits at the bottom of a hole. Eleven years ago the ground under the old salt flat gave way in a single night, and the basin that opened is still settling. The walls are raw: vertical strata of pale clay, red iron seam, and grey shale, all cut so cleanly that the edges look machined. Nothing has had time to soften them. Rockfalls still happen. Each one sends a fan of pale dust across the lower terraces, and the dust settles into the drains and clogs the pumps.

The light never fully reaches the floor. The rim is perhaps four hundred metres up, and the sun clears it only for a few hours at midday, so the streets stay in a brown dusk even on the brightest days. Lamps burn in the lower district from morning. The upper terraces get more sun, and the people who can afford it live there. Smoke from the furnaces and refineries stays trapped under the rim and drifts in slow layers, so the sky is a lid of ochre haze with a sickly sheen where the sun is.

**Heat and water**

The heat collects in the basin like water in a bowl. By afternoon the air is thick enough to feel with the hand, and the metal of the railings is too hot to touch. Everything is damp anyway, because the water comes in pulses. Once or twice a week a storm forms over the high plateau and dumps rain into the catchment in minutes. The runoff pours over the rim in brown sheets, carves new gullies down the terraces, and roars through the culverts for an hour before it drains into the cistern tanks at the bottom. Between storms the rain evaporates so quickly that the streets are dry in an hour, but the walls keep the damp. Condensation beads on everything that is cooler than the air: the undersides of ducts, the glass of observation ports, the bellies of the cistern tanks. It drips continuously, a slow percussion through the whole town.

The water is never clean. It carries clay, iron, and a faint sweetness from the salts. The engineers have built filtration towers along the lower channel, great stacks of gravel and charcoal that wheeze and clank as the flow rises and falls. The whole economy is organised around the storm calendar. When the forecast crackles on the radio shack's receiver, the town opens its sluices, drains its tanks, and sends crews down into the gullies with hooks and carts to pull out debris before the next flood.

**Sparse life**

Vegetation exists only in the cracks. Along the drains grow flat grey mats of a lichen-like organism that the workers call scab moss. It feeds on damp and mineral runoff and spreads outward a few centimetres a season, so crews scrape it off the pump housings every morning. Thin reed-like stalks poke from the terrace seams, pale and brittle, and they bend and snap under the hot wind. Some of the older residents keep potted saltbush on their balconies, pruned into hard little domes. Nothing larger survives the heat.

There are no animals. No insects hum, no birds cross the rim, no rodents scurry in the walls. The silence of life is the first thing visitors notice, and the second is that the town's quiet is not restful. The sounds that do exist are mechanical and water-borne: the dull knock of valves, the drip of condensation, the long hiss of steam releasing from vents in the lower streets. The quiet is so complete that a dropped spanner rings off the walls for several seconds.

**Industry and weirdness**

The factories line the lower terraces. Distillation columns rise above the roofs, tall and corroded, and they breathe a sweet acrid vapour that settles on laundry and skin. The presses and refineries process salt, iron, and something the workers call "bloom," a crystalline mineral that grows on the cistern walls. The bloom forms in thin white filaments overnight, reaching outward like frost fingers, and it dissolves in water. No one fully understands why it grows faster in the wet season or why, when the factories shut down for a week, the filaments in the abandoned cistern reached the ceiling.

The stranger effect is the sweat. Certain walls, especially the clay faces above the older workshops, exude a pale, slightly warm moisture in the late afternoon, when the basin is hottest. The moisture collects, crystallises into a fine white skin, and by morning the wall has a new thin layer that flakes away when touched. The residents have built channels to collect the sweat, and the water it yields is used for medicine, though no one has successfully explained why it works. The sweat is most copious after a storm, as if the rock were exhaling the rain it had swallowed.

**Rhythm**

Everything in the Sump moves in cycles. The storms come and go on a schedule set by the plateau's weather. The factories run in shifts and stop for the afternoon heat. The bloom grows by night and dissolves by day. The rockfalls come when the rains have soaked the clay enough to loosen it, and the crews clear the debris within hours before the next surge. Even the lamps have a rhythm: the lower streets are switched on at dawn and off at dusk, a long tide of light that passes through the town and leaves the terraces in shadow again.

The place is not dying and not growing. It is young, still falling into its own shape, with fresh edges and new wounds opening every season. Anyone who lives there learns to read the water, the walls, and the light, because the town's whole life depends on knowing which way the next flood will run.
