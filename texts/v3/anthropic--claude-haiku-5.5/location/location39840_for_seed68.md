**The Sluice Flats of Harrow Reach**

The land here is new. Forty years ago, or perhaps four hundred, depending on whom you ask and what they count, this was the floor of a shallow sea. The water withdrew and left behind a plain of grey silt, and the earth beneath it is still rising. The rebound is slow, a few centimeters a decade, but the land is young enough that its bones show through everywhere. Shelves of pale, sharp-edged shale break the surface like the teeth of something buried. Their edges have not yet been softened by weather, and they cut the hands of anyone who climbs them carelessly. Between the shelves lie basins of black peat and grey mud, and the water that fills them is never quite still.

Rain comes in long, grey, unhurried sheets, and when it is not raining the air is still heavy with what has fallen: a fine mist that beads on every surface and runs in thin tracks down the iron roofs. Condensation gathers on the inside of windows by morning. Moss grows on the north faces of everything, on fence posts and tram rails and the cast-iron legs of the pumping station. The air smells of wet stone, alder sap, diesel, and the sweetish rot of reed beds at the margins of the lagoons.

**The town**

Harrow Reach is small, a few thousand people spread across a grid of streets that were laid out in a confident hurry and then partly abandoned to the wet. The original plan was ambitious: a port, a canning works, a grid of brick terraces with slate roofs. Only parts of it were built. The terraces run along the old sea wall, now a raised causeway of crushed shell and concrete, and behind them the streets dissolve into lots where the vegetation has taken over. Houses sit a hundred meters apart with alder thickets and fern drifts between them. Some are lived in, with washing lines and kitchen gardens and bicycles chained to railings. Others have had their roofs taken for scrap and their windows left open to the rain, and inside them willow saplings push through the floorboards.

The industrial core is the sluice works, a sprawl of iron gates, brick culverts, and steel pump houses on the town's eastern edge. It is the loudest place in the Reach by a wide margin. The great diesel pumps run day and night, hauling water out of the lower basins and throwing it into the canals that feed the lagoons beyond. Their rhythm is a thudding, grinding heartbeat that can be heard for kilometers, and it sets the tempo of daily life. People sleep to it, argue over it, and measure time by it. When a pump fails, the silence is almost frightening, and within an hour people are out in the streets, looking up, uneasy, as though something important has stopped breathing.

The works also houses a foundry and a small electrical plant. Power lines hang in long catenaries between iron pylons, and their hum adds a higher register to the pump-thud, a sustained tone that merges with the rain. Workshops along the canal front make pump parts, cable, enamel pans, and tinned fish, and the clang of hammers and the whine of grinders float out over the water all day.

**The living things**

The lushness of the Reach is almost aggressive. Reed and sedge fill the lagoons in dense, humming stands. Alder and willow crowd the margins, and birch and rowan colonize the drier shelves. Ferns grow waist-high in the lots. Brambles arch over the causeway and tangle through old fences, and along the canals grows a thick green skirt of marsh plants that bends in the wind like the fur of an animal. Lichen patches the shale in orange and grey. Mosses carpet everything else.

The fauna is abundant, and loud. Frogs begin at dusk in choruses that rise and fall in waves across the basins, and some nights the sound is so thick that conversation requires raised voices. Waterbirds crowd the lagoons, herons and coots and a dozen kinds of duck, and gulls wheel over the sluice works, stealing fish from the outflow channels. Crows and magpies work the town's rubbish. Larger birds nest on the pylons. Insects hang in clouds over the standing water in summer, and in the damp evenings bats hunt them above the canals. Eels move through the culverts in great numbers, and people fish them from the causeway with hand lines baited on the old tins.

Mammals are less visible but present. Foxes come out of the lots at dusk, and deer move through the alder woods at the edges of town. Otters have colonized the canals, and locals sometimes find their slides worn into the mud banks. Rats are everywhere, and the cats are fat from them.

**Atmosphere and sound**

The sound of Harrow Reach is layered: the pump-thud at the base, the electrical hum above it, the rain on iron roofs and water in the drains, the frogs, the birds, the grind and clang from the workshops, and under everything the soft susurrus of wind moving through reed and alder. Visitors are startled at first by how little true quiet there is. Residents describe the silence of the outer marshes as the strange thing, and go there deliberately to feel it.

The light is ordinary, a soft, overcast daylight that rarely turns harsh. Clear days come in spring and early autumn, and on those days the shelves of pale shale glow and the lagoons turn a startling blue. Evenings are long in summer, and the fog that rises off the basins after sundown lingers until mid-morning.

**Rules of the place**

Water is the governing law. The sluice works are not a choice but a necessity: without them, the lower basins would flood the town within a season. The pumps must run, the gates must be tended, and the sediment must be cleared from the culverts or the canals silt up and the water backs into the streets. The town's rhythms derive from this. Work shifts follow the tides of the pumps, not the sun. Children learn early which culverts are dangerous and which gates to stay away from.

The rebound changes the map slowly. Surveyors come every few years to re-level the benchmarks, and sometimes a street that was dry at one measurement is found to be a little lower, or a shelf that was an island is now joined to the causeway. The town adjusts constantly and without drama. Houses are moved, drains are re-routed, and old shorelines are quietly absorbed into gardens. Nothing happens quickly, but nothing stays put.

Over decades, the vegetation advances. The lots that were cleared a generation ago are now alder woods, and the woods are slowly thickening toward the causeway. The town is being absorbed, slowly, into the wet green, and the people of Harrow Reach keep pushing back: cutting, draining, rebuilding, and then, in their quieter moments, wondering which of them will be the last to hold the line.
