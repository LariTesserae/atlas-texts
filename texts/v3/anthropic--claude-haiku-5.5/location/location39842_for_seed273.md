# The Stepped Massif

The mountains here were built, and the building is still settling.

From the lowland plain the range looks like an ordinary escarpment, a long blue-grey wall that climbs out of the haze. Closer, it resolves into terraces. Thousands of them, each a poured shelf of engineered earth and concrete, stacked on one another in ragged tiers that rise perhaps three kilometers. No bare rock shows anywhere. The cliffs are faced with cast panels, the slopes are fill, and the peaks are cored with ferroconcrete and buried machinery. Whatever stone once stood here was ground down, carried, and reset generations ago, and no one remembers the first course laid.

The terraces are wide enough to hold villages, orchards, and waterworks, and narrow enough that the drop from one edge to the next can be a few meters or a few hundred. Between them run ramps, cable lines, and conveyor spines, some still moving, some rusted into the slope.

## Water

Water is everywhere in motion and never in a hurry. Rain comes in long afternoons, usually from the west, where warm air lifts off the plain and drops its load against the stepped faces. Each terrace has a catchment lip, a drainage channel, and a settling tank, and the whole system passes water down the mountain like a slow stair. Cisterns hold it. Sluice gates open and close on timers that have been running so long nobody questions them.

Where the channels overflow, they fall in cataracts that thunder through the mid-levels. Mist hangs in the gullies, and condensation beads on everything: on panel seams, on cable sheathing, on the undersides of leaves. The air at the middle elevations smells of wet concrete, iron, and green.

Erosion has done its quiet work on the built slopes. Gullies have cut the old fill into rills and ravines that follow the grain of the original pour. Sinkholes open where buried conduits have collapsed. Silt has built fans at the foot of each step, and those fans are now farmland, fertile from centuries of runoff carrying topsoil that the mountain itself is slowly reconstituting. The mountain is still becoming a mountain, and the rain is the one process that never stopped.

## Vegetation

The plants are the most convincing part of the place, and the most abundant. Terrace edges are planted with dense bands of broadleaf and bamboo-like growth, some of it cultivated and some of it escaped and gone wild. Creepers climb the cast panels and bridge the seams. Mosses coat every north-facing surface, and ferns root in the cracks where the panels have settled. Where soil has built up over decades, trees have taken hold: thick-trunked, low-branched things whose roots are slowly lifting the old shelves.

The cultivated terraces are laid out in patterns that follow the contour: rice-like paddies stepping down the hillside in mirror-bright sheets, rows of leafy crops under mesh canopies, orchards of dark-fruited trees planted in trenches dug into the fill. Irrigation hoses, rusted pipe, and copper runners wind among them. Some plants grow through old machinery, and in places the two have become indistinguishable. A pump housing has a fig tree growing from its intake. A monorail pylon is wrapped in vine so thick its shape has changed.

The lushness is partly the result of deliberate care and partly the result of neglect. Things grow where no one has pulled them. The mountain's ecology is a product of the built world's ruins, and it is thriving.

## Fauna

Animal life fills the vertical space. Birds nest in the cliff faces, in the gaps between panels, in the rafters of abandoned hangars on the upper terraces. Their calls carry across the valleys in layered chorus, and the loudest sounds of the place are often theirs, rising and falling with the light. Insects are thick in the warm damp: beetles, moths, and flying things that cluster around the lamps the people have left burning on the lower walkways.

Bats roost in the service tunnels that run through the core of the massif. Small reptiles bask on the sun-warmed concrete at the terrace edges. Fish live in the cisterns and the settling tanks, and frogs in the paddy channels, and in the early evening the frog calls rise from every level at once. Larger animals move through the lower forests, and goats and similar grazers keep the orchard grasses trimmed.

Nothing here is wild in the old sense. Every animal lives inside something made, and most of them depend on the made things for shelter, water, or the insects that gather around them. The ecology has adapted to the architecture.

## Machinery and Sound

The tech level is a working industrial one, unhurried. Pumps thrum in their housings on every level. Sluice motors click and grind. Cable cars run on a few lines, pulled by counterweights and old electric drives, and their wheels sing on the rails. Smelting sheds sit near the base, where salvage is broken down and reused. Turbines catch the falling water in the cataracts and send a steady hum down the power lines to the plain. The noise is constant, layered, and never quite loud enough to drown out the birds, which is why the place feels loud without ever feeling chaotic.

Light is bright and warm. The sun comes up over the eastern plain and strikes the terraces in sequence, each shelf catching the light before the one below, so that the whole mountain seems to brighten from the top down. The panels hold heat, and the air at the base is warm and heavy. In the late afternoon the rain comes, and the light turns gold and silver through the mist.

## Rhythm

Nothing here changes quickly. A terrace takes decades to settle. A gully takes centuries to cut. A tree takes a generation to lift a shelf by a finger's width. The sluices run on timers set long ago. The people who live here measure time in cycles of rain and planting and the slow creep of the slopes. Events happen, but they are absorbed by the larger rhythm, the way a single raindrop disappears into a stream.

The mountain is always in the middle of something very slow, and the most interesting thing about it is how much life has learned to fit inside that slowness.
