**The Drowned Grid**

The first thing you notice is the water, and then that there is nothing under it but more made things. The sea covers everything, a flat grey-green expanse that runs to a horizon where the sky and the water meet without a seam. It is cool, around the temperature of a cellar, and the air carries the wet mineral smell of fresh concrete, machine oil, and open brine. Nothing here is bedrock. Every shape you can see, above or below the surface, was poured, cast, bolted, or welded by someone.

The sea is shallow over the built plain, perhaps forty or fifty meters, so the light reaches down easily. Sunlight shatters on the swell into moving coins that dance across the foundations of the drowned city. Looking down through the water you can make out the grid: long straight avenues of cracked pavement, square footings for buildings that no longer rise above the surface, pipe runs tracing the streets like the veins of a leaf. Some of the pipes still hum. Pressure valves open and shut in slow rhythms, and their exhalations rise as silver strings of bubbles.

Above the surface, the built world rises in scattered islands. Most are squat: flat-roofed pumping stations, cooling towers half-collapsed into the sea, the tops of apartment blocks with their windows drowned to the sills. Cranes stand on their own reflections, their booms still turning. A gantry on the eastern horizon moves slowly along a rail that disappears into the water. Its arm lowers, touches the surface, lifts, and carries something dripping away. No one is visible operating it.

The vegetation is sparse but stubborn. Green-black biofilm coats every vertical surface below the waterline, a dense felt of algae that turns the concrete piers into soft, furred columns. Where steel has rusted through, rafts of a pale, branching mat have colonized the holes, forming ragged lace in the gaps. Along the high rooflines, a few wind-bent shrubs have rooted in the gutters, rustling dryly and clinging to their soil-filled troughs. The plants never cover much area, and between them are stretches of bare, reflective concrete scoured clean by the tides.

Fauna is abundant, and it is loud. Schools of silver fish pour through the drowned avenues in shifting clouds, flashing when they turn. Crustaceans the size of dinner plates crawl over the pipes and graze the biofilm with a constant clicking. Long-necked animals with fins instead of limbs glide between the towers, surfacing to breathe with a rolling exhalation that carries for kilometers. Gulls and terns wheel overhead, screaming. Something very large moves beneath the swell at intervals, and the water lifts in a long low pulse that rattles the loose railings on the nearest island.

The sound of the place is layered and never quiet. Waves slap against hulls and pilings with a wet knocking. Pumps grind somewhere in the distance with a low, steady drone. Turbines on the cooling towers whine as the wind turns them, and the cables stretched between towers thrum in the gusts. Bells on buoys ring as the swell shifts them. Every few minutes a valve releases pressure with a hiss like a giant exhaling, and the sound rolls across the water for a long time.

The weather here has its own agenda. Vast grey systems form on the horizon and roll inland in slow columns. Rain falls in walls you can watch approaching from miles off, the surface turning white and hissing under them. Behind each wall the sea steams faintly, and the air smells of ozone and cold iron. Between storms the sky is clear and bright, and the sun lays long gold lanes across the water, which the fish flash through.

Nothing here is empty in the sense of dead. But the presences are sparse against the enormous volume of water and sky. Between one island and the next there may be kilometers of open sea, shifting and bright, with only a passing fin or a drifting raft of algae to mark that anything lives there. The great structures are far apart, and the hum of their machinery reaches across that space like a signal no one is quite listening for.

It is a young world, still settling. The concrete has sharp edges, the steel is raw and bright where the sea has not yet taken it, and the pipes still drip the flood-brine out of their joints. The biofilm is new and spreading fast. Every week the line where water meets built surface shifts a little, and the grid below grows more crowded with life, and the machines keep turning, still doing the work they were built for in a world that has mostly stopped needing them.
