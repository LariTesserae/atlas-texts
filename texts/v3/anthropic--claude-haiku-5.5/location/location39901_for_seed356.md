The first thing you notice is that there is no ground. What looks like soil is a thick, black, spongy mat of root and rot, perhaps forty meters deep, and it floats on water. Lift a hand into the air and you'll find the air is already wet. It isn't raining. The atmosphere is saturated to the point where it has weight, and every surface carries a film of condensate that never quite beads or runs off. The whole landscape sweats.

**The plants**

Everything is vegetation, and nothing stands apart from it. Trunks rise out of the mat and fuse overhead into a continuous canopy, so there is no sky, only a roof of leaves. Between trunks, vines, stolons, and runners braid into a lattice that fills the volume of the air, so dense that a body would have to push through it like kelp. There are no bare patches and no clearings. Where a tree dies, its neighbors grow into the gap within a season, and the dead wood becomes the lattice's new strut.

The plants are not simply living. They are working.

Each trunk is hollow and ribbed like a pipe organ, and its bark is a graded membrane of pores that draws water up from the mat against enormous negative pressure. Listening at the base of one, you can hear a slow, continuous hydraulic hum, the sound of a column of liquid being hauled upward and never allowed to slacken. The upper leaves are broad, glossy, and tilted at precise angles. They do not photosynthesize so much as condense. Their undersides collect the saturated air into droplets, which run along grooves and drain into the trunk's pressure system. Where many leaves are angled together, they work as a single vast heat exchanger, shedding the day's warmth upward into the canopy.

The root mat is an electrical network. Fine filaments thread through the peat and bind to one another in dense, conductive bundles. Potentials build across the mat and discharge in slow, branching arcs that cross the surface like the veins of a sleeping animal. In places where the mat has been compressed by standing water, the discharges concentrate into pale, flickering seams.

**The industry**

Because nothing here is made, the tech is entirely grown, and it is industrial in scale. The vines that run between trunks act as conveyors, carrying nutrient slurries and mineral salts from the mat to the crown. At several junctions the stems swell into pressure chambers, thick-walled bulbs that accumulate sap until a membrane gives way with a dull concussive pop. The released fluid is routed through a cascade of sieving tissues that strip out minerals, which crystallize in rows of white plates along the undersides of branches. These crystals are harvested by nothing, and they simply accumulate, forming shelves and sheets that the plants grow over and use as load-bearing structure.

Some of the larger trees have produced whole organs for fermentation. Inside their hollow crowns, sealed vats of sugary sap stand for months, giving off heat and a sour gas. The gas is channeled through pipes of woody tissue to a second chamber where it is converted, slowly, into a dense fibrous mat that the plants use to patch their own wounds. The patch is thick and shows a faint internal structure, as if something were laid out in courses. Nothing lays it out. It arises from the chemistry.

**Light and darkness**

Very little light reaches the floor, and what does arrive is filtered through many layers of wet leaf. The canopy is nearly black at midday. Occasionally a faint phosphorescence leaks from the seams of the pressure chambers, a cold blue-green that lights the nearest few trunks and then fades. The plants seem to use it as a signal: when one chamber flares, the nearby ones answer in sequence, and the glow travels outward through the forest in a slow wave and dies. Then the dark comes back.

There is no sunrise and no sunset. The light level is almost constant. It is simply dim, and it has been dim for so long that it no longer feels like an absence.

**Sound**

The forest is almost silent. The dominant sound is the hydraulic hum from the trunks, low and continuous, felt more in the chest than heard. Beneath it you can pick out the faint hiss of condensate running down grooves in the leaves, the tick of crystals settling on shelves, and the occasional soft pop of a pressure membrane releasing. Water drips, but seldom. The air absorbs most of what is made. Sound dies quickly here. A shout would carry perhaps ten meters before the wet air swallowed it.

**Weather**

The forest has its own weather, but it is not the weather of an open sky. Fog is generated inside the canopy, rising from the mat where the root filaments heat up during their electrical discharges, and it drifts horizontally between trunks in slow banks. Occasionally the canopy condenses enough water to release a brief, heavy fall through the leaves. It lasts a few minutes, and the forest drinks it in, and then it is quiet again.

**Stillness**

Everything here seems to be in balance. The hydraulic pressures hold steady. The electrical discharges repeat their branching patterns. The crystal shelves thicken at an imperceptible rate, and the fermentation vats cycle through the same seasons of heat and sourness, and the glow waves travel outward and fade in the same order each time. No part of the forest is growing toward anything. The landscape has reached an equilibrium that it keeps renewing, like a machine that runs forever at idle, and you get the sense that if you returned in a thousand years you would find the same arrangement of pumps and vats, the same wet dark, and the same sound of water being carried up through the trees.
