# The Weeping Terraces

You climb into the place through cloud that is not quite fog. It hangs at knee height and beads on your sleeves, and when you look up the sky is a hard, cloudless blue. The sun is strong enough to hurt. Up here the air is thin and cold, and the light is brighter than it has any right to be, bouncing off a thousand wet surfaces at once.

The ground is a shelf of pale grey stone, weathered for so long that its edges have rounded into soft, pitted lobes, like old bread. Water comes out of it everywhere. The rock sweats. Thin films slide across the surface in sheets a finger deep, pooling in hollows, dropping over lips, and gathering into channels that have been cut so smoothly they look poured. Nothing here is still for long, but nothing is ever dry, either. Where the sun touches a wet slab, a faint steam rises. Where the cold reaches it at dusk, a skin of ice forms, glassy and fragile, which breaks at the first footstep and makes a sound like a plate set down very carefully.

## Terrain

The land falls in a series of terraces, each a few metres lower than the last, stepping down into a bowl far below that you can only partly see through the cloud. The terraces are not natural in their regularity. Someone has been working them for generations: walls of dry-laid stone curve along their outer edges, and the lower ones have been dug into channels that carry the seep water down in controlled runs. Some of these runs have failed. The walls lean where the ground beneath them has shifted, and small slumps of gravel fan out below the breaches like spilled sugar.

The stone itself is layered. In the cut faces you can see bands of darker rock and bands of pale, and in places the layers have been bent into folds by pressure long ago. Where the seep is heaviest, the surface has softened into a kind of mudstone that holds footprints for an hour and then slowly smooths them away.

## Materials and Building

Everything is built from what the place gives. Stone is abundant but heavy, and it is shaped with hammerstones and wedges, the faces roughly knocked flat and fitted by eye. Timber is scarce. The few trees are low, twisted things that grow in the lee of the larger boulders, and their wood is hard and grey. Where it can be found, it is precious, used for lintels, door frames, and the long poles that hold up the drying racks.

Houses are squat and round-backed, built into the slope so that their uphill walls are half buried. Their roofs are layered slabs with gaps between them, because the rain never really stops and the people here have learned that a sealed roof floods. Every building has a gutter cut into its upper edge, feeding a stone trough. Water is never wasted. It is carried through clay pipes, drained into cisterns, or ladled across fields of grey moss that grow in shallow trays.

Fibre is made from a tall grass that grows in the wettest hollows, and it is woven into mats, nets, and coarse cloth. Leather is rare and prized. Bone and antler are used for needles, hooks, and handles. Metal is nearly absent. The people here work with stone, wood, bone, and fibre, and their tools show an enormous amount of patience.

## Vegetation

Plant life is sparse, but what grows is tough and stubborn. Grey-green lichen covers the sun-facing rock in crusts like dried paint. A low, cushion-forming plant with waxy leaves grows in tight mats between the terraces, bright green where the seep runs and bleached white where it does not. Tufts of pale grass cling to the wall gaps. The scrub is thorny and dense in places, which is why people go around it instead of through.

Some of the plants are odd. Certain mats of moss have a faint lacy structure when you look closely, as if they were drawn in fine lines. Some of the lichens seem to thicken after rain, and in the morning a few of them are noticeably taller than they were the night before.

## Fauna

For a land this sparse, the animals are remarkably thick. Hundreds of small, pale creatures live in the seep channels. They are something between newts and fish, with translucent bodies and feathery external gills. They cluster in the shallows in dense knots and scatter when shadows pass over them. The ground is loud with their shifting, but the sound is so soft that you mostly feel it through your boots.

Above the terraces, flocks of small grey birds wheel constantly. They do not sing. They make a thin, dry clicking with their wings and beaks, which seems to be how they communicate. The flocks are restless and form new shapes every few minutes, sometimes splitting into two clouds that drift away over the bowl and sometimes merging into a single dark mass that drops onto a terrace and lifts again a moment later, leaving a scattering of feathers and nothing else.

Larger animals graze the lower terraces: stocky, long-haired grazers with thick, matted coats that shed water like thatch. They move slowly and are herded in small groups. Children tend them with slings and a great deal of shouting, though the shouting is almost the only loud noise in the whole place.

## Sound

The dominant sound is water: a constant, low trickle and hiss, layered from a dozen directions, so that it seems to come from the rock itself. Above it you hear the clicking of the birds, the soft shifting of the amphibian crowds, the occasional knock of a stone set in place, and the crack of ice as it breaks in the morning. Voices carry strangely. They don't echo. The wet air swallows them, and people here speak low and close, so that a conversation across a terrace is nearly inaudible. It is quiet enough that you can hear your own breath.

## Atmosphere and Light

The light is harsh and clean. The sun is high and nearly white, and because the rock is wet almost everywhere it reflects in sharp flashes that make the eyes water. Halos form around the sun in the thin, cold air, and in the early morning you can often see small rainbows caught in the spray of the lower channels. The shadows are deep and blue-tinged. At dusk the light turns amber and then almost violet, and the wet stone glows faintly for a while after the sun is gone.

Cold settles hard at night. The seep slows, the amphibians sink into mud, the birds roost in the cracks of the high walls, and frost forms on the stone in feathery patterns that melt by mid-morning.

## Dynamics and Change

This is the strangest part of the place, and the people who live here have built their whole way of life around it. The land does not stay put. The seep channels shift, sometimes overnight. A stream that ran steadily down the third terrace may cut a new course by the next morning, undermining a row of houses and opening a fresh run across the field below. The ground softens and settles. Stone walls lean and fall. Terraces collapse into new shapes, and the people rebuild them, not always in the same places.

Rebuilding is constant. Every family has a rhythm of repair: clearing silt from troughs, reseating wall stones, relaying the gutters, moving a house a few paces when the ground beneath it begins to sink. The settlement is never finished. Its shape is a record of the water's last few seasons of decisions.

Some of the change is slower and stranger. The lacy moss spreads across the terraces in a few weeks and then vanishes. The lichens grow after rain and shrink in dry spells. Certain channels seem to run uphill for a short time at dawn, a thin silver thread climbing a step of stone before it sinks back into the rock, and no one has ever fully explained why. The old people say the water is remembering where it used to go.

## Rules of the Place

- Water is always present, and it always moves. Nothing built here stays dry for long.
- The land changes faster than people can settle into it. Permanence is something you maintain, not something you inherit.
- Sound dies quickly in the wet air. Voices must be close to be heard, and silence is the default.
- Life is dense but not lush. Many small creatures crowd into the spaces the water leaves, and the sparse plants hold on in the cracks.
- The strange movements of water and growth follow rhythms that are only partly understood. The wise pay attention to them, and the careless are caught by them.

You leave the terraces as the light begins to slant. Behind you the flocks are settling into the high walls, and the water keeps running down through its channels, carrying the day's small changes into the dark.
