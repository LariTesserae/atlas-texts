**The Condensary at Hesk Ardal**

The valley is not a valley. It is a bowl hewn into the flank of a mountain at roughly four thousand metres, a cirque whose lip was cut square and whose floor was flattened into nine stepped terraces. Nothing here is a natural rock face. The walls are retaining walls, faced in grey poured stone and bolted with plates the size of doors, and where the stone has weathered it has weathered the way old architecture does, slumping at the seams and sprouting a skin of moss. From the rim you can see the whole shape at once: a stepped amphitheatre, perhaps two kilometres across, with the sky a flat white lid above it.

The lid is cloud. It sits on the cirque like a tray. Inside, the air is warm and wet, almost tropical, which is strange at this height and stranger still once you notice where the heat comes from. Pipes as thick as a man's body run down the terraces in bundles, their flanks sweating, and somewhere beneath the lowest step a buried plant burns and hisses. Its waste heat rises through vents in the floor and feeds the cloud, and the cloud, pressed against the walls, condenses and falls back in. The whole bowl is a closed loop of weather. It was built to be one.

**The vegetation** arrived after the machines, and it has taken them over with patient thoroughness. Ferns grow from the cracks in the terraces, broad-leaved things like elephant ears with leaves a metre across, and vines thick as hawsers climb the gantries and pull the cranes out of true. Moss furs every gauge face and every dial, so that the readouts are only faint shapes under green. In places, roots have pried the paving into plates and lifted whole conveyor sections into shallow arches. Some of the larger trees have grown straight through the rusted frames of old rolling mills, so that their trunks carry the ghosts of gearwheels, and the wood has swallowed the iron in rings.

**The machinery** dominates everything, and it is the sound that dominates the machinery. The condensers are huge: each terrace holds a row of vertical cylinders, and each cylinder is fitted with a piston as tall as a house. The pistons do not run fast. Their cycle is measured in long intervals, one stroke every several minutes, and when one drops, the sound is a concussion you feel in the teeth and in the sternum before you hear it. It travels across the bowl and bounces off the far wall, and the echo comes back a half-second late, so each blow arrives twice, the second weaker and rounder. Then the valves shriek, steam hisses out of brass stacks, and water pours through chutes with a roar like surf. Then silence for a little while, and then the next piston. The sequence never varies. The works has been running this loop for longer than anyone has counted.

Nobody is here, or almost nobody. The density of the place is extraordinary: vast gulfs of warm wet air between each machine, each gantry, each drifting stand of ferns. A walk across the lowest terrace might pass a dozen structures and see no living person at all. There are tracks worn into the pavement by boots long gone, and a few rooms with cots and cold stoves, but the people who kept the works have been gone for generations, and the works kept going without them. It maintains itself in a slow way. Valves that stick are freed by corrosion that eats the seized parts, and then the rust is flushed by the next condensate surge. A cracked pipe weeps, and the weeping moss grows into a seal.

**The fauna** have adjusted to the rhythm. Birds nest in the high gantry lattices, and their colonies are huge, thousands of them packed into the girders, but between the colonies there are long stretches of empty air where the swifts wheel. The birds time their flights to the pistons: they rise after each blow, when the air is still ringing, and drop back in the quiet that follows the valves. Lizards bask on the warm pipes between cycles. Beetles the colour of wet slate cluster around the condensate drains. Something larger moves in the fern-shade of the lower terraces, a grazer that browses the broad leaves and wanders the bowl along a route that loops every nine cycles. The fauna is abundant, but the abundance is layered and spaced, a crowd in each pocket of the structure and wide open quiet between pockets.

**The strange part** is what the works is for. It does not, in any obvious sense, produce anything. It draws vapour from the warm air through a battery of intakes on the uppermost terrace, compresses it, and drops it through the cylinders into a set of cooling tanks. The tanks overflow in a sequence of sluices and cascades that feed a long descending channel, and the channel runs out over the lip of the cirque as a thin, steady fall that thins into mist before it reaches the valley floor below. The water that comes out of the bowl is cold, pure, and tastes faintly of iron. Downhill, in the forests and fields of the valley beyond, something grows on that water that grows nowhere else in the region: a pale, ghost-white reed, tall and silent, that flowers only when the pistons fall in the same hour of the day.

Nobody has ever learned whether the works is meant to make rain for the lowland, or whether the lowland was meant to be fed by the cloud, or whether it was only ever a machine that needed to keep running and the rain was simply what it did. The question is old and the works does not answer it. Every few minutes the piston falls, the echo returns, the valves shriek, and a fine warm mist lifts off the lowest terrace and drifts slowly toward the rim, where it spills over the edge and is gone.
