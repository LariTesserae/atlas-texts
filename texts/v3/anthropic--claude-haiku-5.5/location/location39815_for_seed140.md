**The Sunken Cistern of Ahl**

You stand on a plateau at roughly the height where clouds sit under you. The air is warm and thick, and it tastes of iron and green rot. There is no light at all. Not moonlight, not starlight, not a glow from the ground. The darkness is complete enough that your hand disappears at arm's length, and you learn the place by touch and sound.

The plateau is a broad, shallow bowl about two kilometers across, ringed by low escarpments that have been worn almost to nothing. Their edges have rounded into soft humps, like knuckles of an old hand. The rock is a dark, fine-grained stone, pitted and polished smooth by millennia of rain. Where water runs over it, it leaves glossy black streaks. Everything here has been erased, and what remains has settled into a final shape. Nothing sharp survives.

The floor of the bowl is mostly water held in shallow, interconnected basins, some only a finger deep, others deep enough to hide a body. The water is warm and still, with a skin of fine silt that clings to your boots. Mist rises constantly from it and condenses on every surface, so the air never fully dries. Water drips from fern fronds the size of canoes, from the undersides of ledges, from the stone itself. The ground weeps. You hear it everywhere: a fine, continuous patter that never stops and never quite syncs with itself.

The vegetation is lush but sparse. Enormous organisms stand far apart, each with its own territory of bare, wet soil. Their trunks are pale, fibrous columns wrapped in mats of something like moss but thicker, and they rise into a canopy you cannot see. Their fronds fold and unfold slowly, with a faint wet creak. Between them grow thick, low fungal mats that glow faintly when touched. They are not bioluminescent in any light-producing sense, but they give off a dull warmth you can feel through your palm, and when you press them they exude a clear, sweet sap. The ground between the giants is a rich carpet of soft, spongy growth that yields underfoot and smells of cut stems.

Fauna is abundant, and you hear it before anything else. The chorus rises from every direction at once: deep, resonant calls from the basins, clicking and tapping from the trunks, something like a rasping bellows from far off on the escarpment. Some creatures sound like they are the size of houses. Others are so small their individual voices blur into a crackling hiss. You catch movement only as displacement: a ripple across still water, a shift in the mist, a pressure in the air when something large passes close. Once, a warm mass brushes your leg and moves on without hurry. Things here are numerous but scattered, and the sound fills the space the way water fills a basin.

Scattered through the bowl are the remains of something built, though nothing about them announces a builder. At the center of the largest basin, a dome of smooth grey material breaks the surface, perhaps thirty meters across. Its curve is too regular to be natural and too weathered to be new. Its surface is seamless except for a fine, branching pattern of hairline grooves that catch the dripping water and channel it in slow, deliberate rivulets toward a single low aperture. Something inside hums, a sub-audible vibration you feel in your teeth before you hear it. Around the dome's edge, half-sunk into the silt, lie arched frames of the same material, now cracked and furred with growth. Their inner faces carry raised markings that could be script or could be the accidental pattern of wear. Stairs descend into one basin and vanish under the water.

The technology here is not something you can explain with ordinary physics. Water seems to move toward the dome even on sloped ground. The mist thickens in patterns that repeat on cycles too slow to see but evident over hours. Growth nearest the dome is denser and taller, and the fauna gather there in larger numbers, though they never enter the water's deepest parts. Something is maintaining a balance of heat, moisture, and life, and it has been doing so for so long that the plateau has come to depend on it.

Change happens slowly. Trunks thicken by a finger's width over a season. A basin deepens or shrinks over years. An escarpment loses a few grains a century. The dome's hum rises and falls on a cycle you cannot measure in a single visit. Each moment feels unchanging, but the place is always becoming something slightly different, and it has been doing so for longer than anything you could name.

Standing at the edge of the largest basin in total darkness, with the drip of water on every side and the hum of the dome through your bones, you understand that this is not a ruin. It is a machine that has become a landscape, and the landscape has become its final form.
