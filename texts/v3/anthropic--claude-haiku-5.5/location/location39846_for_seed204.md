**The Cistern Cirque**

You stand on a shelf of raw stone at about four thousand meters, in a bowl of cliffs that seems to have split open only recently. The rock is pale grey and angular. Nothing has had time to round it. Edges stay sharp, and where a slab has fallen, the fracture faces still glint, unweathered. The bowl is perhaps six hundred meters across. Its walls rise straight up into a sky that is clear and cold and very blue, with the thin light of high altitude.

The water is everywhere but never falls as rain. The rock weeps. Dark seams run down the cliff faces, and from them beads of water gather, swell, and slide in slow threads toward the floor. Where they meet, they pool in shallow basins cut into the stone, each one clear enough to show the sharp bottom. Mist hangs in the hollows and drifts up the walls in the early morning, then burns off, then returns in the evening as the cold settles. The air is always damp against the skin. Metal grows a fine bloom of moisture. Stone is always darker where it is wet, and the wet is always changing.

The city fills the floor of the bowl, though "city" is generous. There are perhaps forty structures spaced far apart, with wide stretches of bare rock between them. They are built of a pale, faintly translucent material, something between ceramic and glass, grown or cast into lattices and fluted towers. Some are tall and narrow, branching at the top into fine tines that catch the mist. Others are low and wide, shaped like shallow dishes tilted toward the sky, their surfaces beaded with condensation that runs down grooves into channels that feed the pools. A few are sealed, their surfaces dull and opaque, and these seem inert, though you cannot tell whether they are dead or merely resting.

Nothing here grows. There is no moss on the stone, no lichen, no smear of green. The ground is mineral all the way down: grit, scree, polished pebbles, and occasional smooth lenses of something glassy that rings faintly when stepped on. The absence is not a barren emptiness. It feels deliberate, as if the surface has been scoured clean on purpose, or as if life has never tried.

Fauna is rare, but not absent. Occasionally something small moves at the edge of sight: a jointed, many-legged thing that skitters along the channels and drinks from the pools, pausing when you move. Once, high on a tower, a larger shape unfolds from a ledge, stretches wings made of translucent membrane, and drifts out over the cliff and away. Its shadow passes over the basins and is gone. These creatures seem to be grazers of water or condensate, not of plants. They leave faint tracks in the wet grit that fill in slowly.

The sound is minimal. Water ticks and trickles, and the drip from the weeping walls makes a soft, irregular percussion. The mist hushes everything. Wind is faint, sliding over the lip of the bowl and rarely reaching the floor. Now and then a tower gives a low, resonant tone, like a wet finger circling the rim of a glass, which rises and dies in under a minute. The structures seem to hum when the condensate moves through them, and the pitch shifts with the flow.

Light is ordinary and bright above, but the cirque is deep. Sun strikes the upper walls for a few hours, and the shadowed floor stays cool and dim. Where light falls on the wet surfaces, the towers flash and refract, throwing small rainbows across the stone.

The place is active. Condensation builds in the early hours, peaks, and drains over the course of the day. Pools rise and fall. Channels clear and clog with silt. Some towers angle slowly toward the light, turning on joints too faint to see until the change is already complete. Overnight, the mist thickens, and the lattices collect so much water that the channels run loud. In the morning the pools overflow and the ground darkens. The whole bowl seems to breathe: fog in, water out, fog in again.

Nothing here is ruined, and nothing is fully alive. It is a machine for pulling water from air and stone, running on the cold and the height, shaped by a process that is still going on. The cliffs keep splitting, the seams keep weeping, and the city keeps catching what falls.
