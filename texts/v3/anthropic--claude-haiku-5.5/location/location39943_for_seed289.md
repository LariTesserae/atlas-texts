**The Undercroft of Unfinished Stone**

You arrive through a crack in a limestone shelf, a fissure no wider than a shoulder, and the air changes at once. It is cool and wet, and it smells of iron, cold mud, and something sweet underneath, like crushed green stems left in a jar. Above you the crack narrows to a throat of raw rock. Below you the floor drops into a pocket of a chamber, perhaps forty paces across, sealed on every side except the one you came through and a low seam at the far end where water seeps in and does not leave.

The rock here is young. Nothing has finished becoming. The walls are scored with fresh fracture planes that still hold their sharp edges, and the chamber floor is a jumble of angular blocks that have not yet been rounded by water or time. Some of the ceiling hangs in plates that look ready to fall but have been poised that way for longer than anything you could measure. Pale mineral skins coat the surfaces in thin, glassy sheets, still forming, layers too thin to count. Where a drip has hung from the roof, the stalactite stands half-grown, a blunt pale finger that will never reach the floor, because the chamber will never change.

The light is the strangest thing. It should not be here. Under the ground, with no sky, the chamber nevertheless glows, a steady, unflickering brightness like noon through thin cloth. It comes from the mineral skins themselves: a cold blue-white bloom that seeps from the rock and pools in the hollows, and in the seams there are veins of amber-green that pulse with no pulse at all, simply lit, bright and constant. Shadows are short and hard. Nothing moves to change them.

Sparse vegetation grows in the cracks where the light reaches. It is not plant as you know it. Pale threadlike growths, the color of bleached hair, fan out from crevices and gather in low mats. Each strand is tipped with a bulb of translucent gel, and the bulbs catch the mineral glow and throw it back in small hard points. Between the threads grows a lichen that forms flat rosettes, rust and ochre, each rosette perfectly circular and perfectly spaced from its neighbors, as though laid out by hand. There are few of them. Each one is precious, and everything else in the chamber seems to know it.

Then the sound reaches you, and it is loud, and it is everywhere at once.

Water drips from a hundred points, each drop striking a different pitch on a different surface, so that the chamber rings like a struck instrument. Beneath the drips, a continuous chittering rises from the walls, fine and rapid, a thousand small voices in a language of clicks and scrapes. Something beats wings in the high dark above you, and the echo makes a single bird of them, though you see only the ceiling. Further off, a deep bubbling pulses from a pool you cannot yet see, rhythmic and heavy, like a slow heart beneath the stone.

The fauna is crowded here, packed into every available space. The walls are alive with small grey crawling things that look like crustaceans but move like insects, shifting and jostling over the mineral skins, leaving faint wet tracks that glisten in the glow. Blind pale fish hang in the shallow standing pools, dozens to a single pool, their bodies translucent enough that you can see the bright glow through them. Long-limbed spiders with too many joints climb the stalactites, their legs drumming on the stone. And the ceiling is a mass of clustered bodies, wings and claws folded tight, hanging so densely that the rock itself seems to be breathing. Nothing has room to be alone.

Scattered among all this are the marks of something that was here before, or that tried to be here. They are not buildings, exactly. They are arrangements. A ring of flat stones, set on edge, forms a circle a little wider than a body, the stones worn smooth on their inner faces, as though rubbed by hands or by other bodies pressing close. Inside the ring the floor is bare. A cairn of small rounded pebbles stands against one wall, stacked with a deliberate-looking regularity, but the top stones have fallen and lie scattered, and nothing has come to restack them. Scratched into a fracture plane near the entrance are a dozen parallel lines, neatly spaced, unfinished, the last line stopping halfway across the rock. Whatever made them stopped, or was stopped, and the chamber has held the moment ever since.

You notice that none of the marks are old. The edges are sharp. The ring's stones bear no lichen, the cairn's pebbles no crust. It is as if the chamber has been waiting for a builder who arrived and then did not stay, and the fauna crowding its walls have taken over the rest of the work.

The rules here are plain, and they feel older than the rock is. Nothing grows toward the light, because the light does not move. Nothing dies in a way that changes the room; the crustaceans that fall from the ceiling are taken within minutes by the pale fish and the spiders, and the chamber's population stays exactly as dense as it was when you came. The drips fall at the same interval forever. The bloom in the stone pulses at the same brightness. If you wait, nothing will decay. If you wait long enough, the crowd of living things will simply go on being crowded, and the unfinished stones will stay unfinished, and the sound will go on filling the space because there is nowhere else for it to go.

The far seam, where the water enters, is the only place that seems to be going somewhere. The water runs in, pools briefly over the lowest blocks, and then drains away into deeper cracks you cannot follow. Its sound is the lowest in the chamber, a constant hush beneath everything else, and the fauna gather at its edge in a dense, shivering band, as though waiting for something that has been about to happen for a very long time.

You could stay. The chamber would not mind you, and it would not change to make room for you. Every surface is already full. Every sound is already the only sound. The light will go on being bright, the stones will go on being new, and somewhere in the throat of the crack above, the day is still going on without you, as if you had never gone down at all.
