**The Sweating Heights**

The land rises in stepped shelves, each one younger and sharper than the last. Up here the mountains are still breaking open. The ridgelines are knife-edged, and the rock is pale grey granite and dark fractured basalt, with clean faces where the last fall of stone has not yet softened. Nothing has had time to round the corners. Water has only begun to cut the valleys, so the gorges are narrow and steep-walled, and their floors hold pools as clear as glass.

The air is cool and thin, and the light is hard and bright. The sun comes through a high, thin veil of cloud and turns everything silver-white. Wet stone throws the glare back up, so the valleys seem lit from below. Shadows are deep blue and sharp-edged. Even at midday the far ridges look close enough to touch, and the distance is hazed with a pale sheen of mist.

The vegetation is extravagantly lush and crowds every surface that holds moisture. Cloud-forest trees with broad, leathery leaves grow in tiers down the slopes, their trunks hung with long grey beards of lichen and ferns the size of canoes. Moss covers every horizontal face. Vines climb the cliffs in dense green curtains, and where the cliffs are sheer, hanging gardens drip with orchids and fleshy succulents. Nothing is bare. Even the fresh scree is beginning to green at its margins. The growth is so thick that the forest seems to have layers of its own: a canopy, an understory of shrubs with waxy red flowers, a floor of fern and fungus, and a sponge of living moss between them, all of it saturated and dripping.

Moisture is the governing fact of the place. Fog rolls up the valleys each afternoon and settles into the trees. Light rain falls for an hour or two most days, and between rains the air is thick with condensation, which beads on every leaf and runs in threads down the rock. Streams run everywhere, through rock channels and over ledges in thin white sheets, and they gather into pools that sit in the hollows like dark mirrors. The ground is spongy underfoot.

Scattered through this green world are the remains of a great industry. It is not easy to read at first. A row of rusted iron lattices climbs a ridge, each frame as tall as a cathedral and draped in moss. On closer look they are mesh towers, built to catch fog, and their fine wire netting still hangs in sagging tiers, beaded with water that drips into copper gutters. The gutters run down the slope into a system of pipes, some of them split and overgrown with ferns, others still intact and gleaming where rain has washed them clean. Concrete pump houses sit at the valley mouths, their doors rusted open. Inside, the machinery is furred with green and bright with orange rust. Pistons, valves, and great flywheels are slowly being reclaimed by the forest.

Almost none of it is active, but a few things still move. In one valley a huge wheel turns once every several hours, lifting water from a pool into a wooden trough that feeds a cistern on the hillside. The wheel groans, but the sound barely carries. In a gully to the north, the fog nets have been mended in places by something, and the patches are neat and new. Nobody is visible tending them. The machines seem to run on a long, patient cycle, the way the mountains rise and the rivers cut.

Animal life is sparse. A few small frogs with translucent skin call from the pools, but the sound is faint and soft, like wet fingers on glass. Occasionally a bird crosses the gorge, a dark silhouette against the bright sky, and then it is gone. Insects are almost nonexistent. The forest is full of stillness, and the quiet is the thickest thing in the air: no wind in the leaves, no voices, only dripping water, the faint tick of cooling metal, and the slow turning of the wheel far off.

The strangest thing is how the forest grows. Along the old pipe routes, moss and fern grow in narrow green lines that follow the metal exactly, as if the plants have learned the shape of the machines. Some of the trees have grown around the iron lattices so completely that their trunks have swallowed the frames, and the wire netting now runs through the living wood like veins. In the oldest sections, the mesh has become a kind of second skin, and water collects on it and feeds the trees that hold it up. The industry seems to have started a process it no longer controls, one in which rock, metal, and forest slowly merge into a single wet system. It does not look like a ruin. It looks like a place still deciding what it will become.
