# The Sunken Grid

The first thing you notice is the water, and you notice it through your skin before your eyes adjust. The air is thick and warm, close as breath in a sealed room, and everything is slick with condensation. Walls sweat. Stone weeps in long dark streaks. A fine mist hangs at knee height and never quite rises or falls. The ground is saturated, not flooded. Water sits just beneath the surface everywhere, so each footstep leaves a print that fills slowly from the bottom.

This was a city. It is still a city in its bones. It spreads across the lowland in a grid so regular it seems drawn with a ruler, dozens of blocks wide, and it runs out of sight in every direction. The streets are paved with fitted basalt, dark and glossy. Curbs, drains, and gutters still run true, though most of the drains now carry standing water rather than moving it. Buildings are low and heavy, built of a pale fired brick that has gone the color of old bone. Their facades have weathered into soft, rounded shapes, and their window openings are mostly empty, though a few keep the dull gleam of old glass.

Nothing here is new. Erosion has been at work for a very long time, and it has reached a kind of settled balance. The stone sheds its edges slowly, grain by grain, and the sand collects in drifts against doorways and in the corners of courtyards. Lime and mineral crusts have formed over everything, white and orange and green, building up in tiers like coral. Some walls have grown thick mats of pale algae that drape downward in soft ropes. Where roofs have fallen in, the rubble has been cemented together by the same mineral skin and has become a kind of tabletop terrain that carries small pools on its back.

The vegetation is sparse but stubborn. Thin grasses sprout from cracks in the paving and from the rooftops, pushing up through sand that has collected in the gutters. There are patches of dense low moss in the shadows, and in a few courtyards something like reed or sedge has rooted in standing water and grown tall and gray. The plants are pale and slightly translucent, as if they had been left in the dark too long. They don't rustle. There's no wind to move them, and the air is too still and heavy.

The light is the strangest part. There is no sun. The sky is a low, unbroken ceiling of dark cloud, and the illumination is faint and general, the kind of dimness you get in a cellar a long way from the stairs. It seems to come from everywhere at once and from nowhere in particular. In the flooded streets, though, the water holds a pale sheen, and on certain nights the surface of the standing pools seems to glow faintly from within, as though something beneath them were slowly breathing.

The silence is nearly total. There are no insects, no birds, no movement in the undergrowth, nothing scuttling across stone. The only sounds are the ones the world makes by itself: water dripping from eaves in a slow, uneven rhythm, the soft hush of mist settling, sand sliding down a rooftop, and the occasional deep settling groan of a wall giving up another few grains of its substance. Your own footsteps sound too loud.

Then there is the weirdness, which is hard to pin down and easy to notice.

The grid is not consistent. Streets you walked down a moment ago may be longer when you look back, or they may end at a wall that wasn't there before. Some intersections have four corners where there should be three, and the buildings on one side of a block do not quite match the buildings on the other. The city's measurements seem to change with attention. If you pace off a block, the count comes out differently the second time.

The water behaves oddly, too. Reflections do not always match what stands above them. A pool in a courtyard may show a tower with three stories, while the only standing structure is a single wall. A puddle may show the sky shifted to the wrong depth, or show a street running in the opposite direction. Rain sometimes falls upward for a few seconds, beading off the ground and drifting toward the ceiling of cloud, then returns to normal. The water table rises and falls on a cycle that does not match any tide or season, slowly submerging the lower floors of buildings and then exposing them again. Doorways that were dry in the morning may be half underwater by evening, and the mineral crusts seem to regrow overnight in patterns that remember where they were before the flood.

Walking through it, you get the sense that the city is still working. Not in the way of people or machines, but in the way of a process that has been running so long it has become the landscape itself. The stone is dissolving and reforming. The water is rising and falling. The crusts are growing toward a shape that might be a pattern, or might be a map of something, or might be nothing but the ordinary slow chemistry of warm wet stone. The grid keeps reasserting itself, wherever the flood and the erosion and the strange shifts of space leave it room, and the city keeps almost, but not quite, being the same city it was.

You might walk for a long time without seeing the far edge. The mist thickens as you go, and the sheen on the water grows brighter. Somewhere ahead, a pale line of wall rises above the flood, its windows dark and its surface glistening. It is hard to tell how far away it is. Each time you look at it, it seems to have moved a little closer, or a little farther, or to have shifted slightly to the left, as if the whole city were quietly rearranging itself around your footsteps and waiting to see which way you would go.
