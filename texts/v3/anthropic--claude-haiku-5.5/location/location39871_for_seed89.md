**The Sunken Hearth**

You stand near the lip of a high volcanic bowl, perhaps three thousand meters above where any sensible river would start. The sky is low and grey-brown, a lid of cloud pressed down onto the rim, and the light that gets through is thin, the color of weak tea. Your eyes take a long time to adjust. When they do, you see that the bowl is not empty.

**Terrain**

The basin is roughly a kilometer across, its walls stepped in old lava terraces, each band a different age and shade. The floor is broken ground: plates of cooled basalt tilted at angles, polished smooth where water has run over them for millennia, and cracked into polygons where it has dried. Between the plates run gullies, some still wet, some choked with fine grey silt. Erosion here is old and settled. The edges of every boulder have been softened into something almost round, and the cliffs carry long vertical grooves from centuries of seepage. Nothing is sharp except the new fractures near the vents, and even those are already crusting over.

**Heat and water**

The warmth is the strangest thing. At this altitude the air should bite, but the floor is warm underfoot, and the air hangs thick and close, around the temperature of a greenhouse in early summer. The heat comes up through fissures at the center of the bowl, where vents breathe out steam in slow, pulsing plumes. Where the steam meets the cooler upper air, it condenses, and the bowl is perpetually damp. Mist beads on every surface. It collects on the undersides of rock overhangs and falls in slow, heavy drips. Every few hours a warm rain forms from the vent clouds and drifts through the basin for a quarter of an hour, then passes, leaving the stones glistening. Pools form in the hollows of the basalt, warm as bathwater and faintly milky with dissolved minerals. Some are stained yellow at the edges, some rust-red, and a few are an unnerving clear green.

**Air and atmosphere**

It smells of sulfur, but beneath that is something rich and mineral, like wet iron and crushed leaves. The mist softens distance. Anything more than a few hundred meters away becomes a grey suggestion, and the far wall of the bowl seems to float. Light comes mostly from the cloud itself, which glows faintly, as if lit from somewhere behind. Shadows are weak and diffuse. Colors are muted except where they are not: the lichen mats are startling orange and violet, and the pools throw back sudden flashes of color when a gust moves the mist.

**Vegetation**

Plant life is sparse but tenacious. Mats of crusted lichen cover the sunniest basalt, slate-grey and rust-orange, rising in ridged, coral-like whorls where the humidity is highest. Low cushions of moss, dark green and almost black, fill the cracks and the lee sides of boulders. Near the warmest vents grow a few stunted, fleshy shrubs with thick grey-green leaves that sweat constantly and drip onto the rock beneath them. They never grow taller than a knee. Nothing here is lush, but there is enough that the stone never looks entirely bare. Plants grow slowly, cling close, and hold water.

**Fauna**

The fauna is overwhelming, and you feel it before you see it. The sound comes first: a dense, shifting drone that seems to come from everywhere at once, layered from the insect hum in the moss to a bass thrumming that may be the vents themselves or the bodies of things moving under the rock. Insects are everywhere. Pale, many-legged things crawl over every surface, and clouds of small winged creatures rise from the pools and settle again in waves, so the air is never quite still. Larger animals keep to the shadows of the terraces: squat, wet-skinned amphibians with ridged backs and throats that inflate into loud, bubbling calls, which answer one another across the pools in ragged chorus. Above, shapes move in the cloud, long-winged and silent, gliding in slow circles on the thermals from the vents. Something ground-dwelling and heavy shifts under the moss now and then, and the ground answers with a dull knock.

The fauna is densely packed. You rarely have more than a few meters of clear space. Movement is constant and close, and the margins of every pool are churned by life.

**Traces of tool-use**

There are no structures here, no walls or cairns, nothing made in any deliberate shape. But some of the stones are wrong. Chips of dark volcanic glass lie in clusters near the warmest pools, flaked to edges so regular that they cannot be natural. Some of them are worn smooth from use. Scattered among them are split bones and shells, some cracked open with precise blows, the marrow or meat removed. The tool-users are not visible. They may be among the amphibians, the insects, or the heavy things under the moss. The chipped stones are simply there, reclaimed by lichen and mud, as if the bowl itself were slowly absorbing the labor of whatever first learned to knap here.

**Weirdness**

Certain things here don't follow ordinary rules. When the vent-wind blows across the basalt terraces, the stepped rock gives off a low, sustained tone, like a hand drawn across the rim of a glass, and the tone rises and falls as the wind shifts. The lichen mats change color through the day, moving from grey to violet in the mist and back, as if responding to something other than light. And the pools seem to know when a rain is coming. Their surfaces stir an hour before the vent clouds gather, and the frogs go quiet, then all start calling together the moment the first drops land.

**Rhythm and time**

Nothing here changes quickly, but everything changes. The cycle of the vents swells and fades over weeks. A new fissure may open and seal within a season. The pools slowly deepen or drain, and their mineral skins build up into terraces over the years, the way the old lava terraces once did. The erosion creeps at a pace you can barely see. The rain comes and goes on a rhythm of hours, the lichens shift on a rhythm of days, and the whole bowl breathes, slowly and steadily, like something very old that has long since stopped needing to hurry.

Standing there in the warm wet grey, with the drone pressing in and the pools steaming around your feet, you have the sense that the place has been running this way for a very long time, and that it will keep running long after anything that tried to change it has been ground back into the stone.
