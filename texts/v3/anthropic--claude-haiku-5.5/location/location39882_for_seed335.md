The first thing you notice is the sound. Before the shapes resolve out of the fog, there is a steady, mechanical knocking, irregular enough to be alive, rhythmic enough to be machinery. It comes from somewhere below and to the left, and it doesn't stop.

**The basin**

You are in a pocket, a shallow bowl perhaps two hundred meters across, sunk between ridges of raw, dark stone. The rock is young. Its edges are still sharp enough to cut a hand, and the fractures are clean and pale where fresh breaks show through the old weathered crust. Nothing here has had time to soften. The ridges look as though they were shouldered up out of the ground only a few seasons ago and have not yet decided what they will become. Their faces are scored with parallel slabs that slid loose and never settled, leaving shelves, overhangs, and narrow ledges where water collects.

The floor of the bowl is low and wet. A marsh lies over it, but it isn't the soft, reedy marsh you might imagine. The ground is a mat of dark, spongy peat laced with grey silt, and it gives underfoot with a sucking sound. Standing water shines in patches, black and still, and between them run threads of cold, clear channels that feed a larger, slow stream winding toward a gap in the eastern rim.

**The mechanism**

The knocking comes from the machinery.

Near the center of the basin, where the stream cuts through a narrow throat of bedrock, something has formed that works like an engine. Water pours from a fissure high on the western wall, falls into a bowl-shaped hollow of hard, ringing stone, and is channeled through a series of stepped basins. Each basin is lined with a grey, glassy mineral that the water has polished into smooth lips and sluice gates. When a basin fills past a threshold, a slab of rock, balanced on a pivot of fused gravel, tips over and releases the water into the next. The slab slams down with a percussive crack, and the impact sends a tremor through the floor that you can feel in your shins.

It is an engine made of geology. Nothing built it. It is simply a sequence of pressure, weight, and water that happens to behave like a pump, a set of trip hammers, and a governor. Over the basin's life, the mineral deposits have become more regular. The gates close more cleanly than they did last year. The rhythm has tightened. Someone standing here long enough might start counting the strokes and feel that they are being measured.

Around the mechanism, the air carries the smell of cold iron and wet stone, the faint sulfur of a spring that bubbles up through the peat nearby, and something green and rank from the plants.

**The vegetation**

The plants are dense and tall where they grow at all, but growth is clustered, not continuous. Thick mats of a cold-loving sedge crowd the channel banks, their blades stiff and blue-green and slightly glossy with moisture. Clumps of broad-leaved herbs with pale, fleshy stems rise out of the peat, each one a little island of shade. Between these clusters are wide gaps of bare silt and bare stone, sometimes twenty or thirty meters across, where nothing grows because the ground is too young or too often flooded. The effect is lush without being thick. Plenty of green, but very little green per square meter of ground.

On the drier shelves of the ridges, lichens spread in orange and grey crusts. Moss hangs in dripping curtains from every overhang that catches the spray from the falls. Where the water runs fastest, mats of a low, matted plant lie flat against the stone like wet hair, and tiny white flowers open in the mornings and close in the afternoons. The flowers are the only color in the basin besides the grey-green of the sedges and the rust of exposed iron in the stone.

**Fauna**

For how empty the space feels, there is a great deal of life in it, and most of it is busy.

Insects drift in the mist above the water, pale moths and midges that cluster around the falls and make a high, dry whine beneath the knocking. Beetles with metallic backs scuttle across the peat in quick, jerky bursts. Long-legged insects walk the surface of the still pools, leaving trembling rings behind them.

Larger animals are heavier and slower. Squat amphibians with broad, pleated throats sit half-submerged along the stream banks, inflating their sacs in a slow, deep chorus that rolls through the basin in waves. Their calls are low and resonant, and when several start at once, they blend into a sound you feel in your chest. Between them, smaller frogs make bright, staccato clicks that sound almost like the mechanism's tiny echoes.

In the deeper channels, pale fish with translucent fins hang in the current and dart away from shadows. Crayfish-like animals with enlarged claws work the silt under the flat stones, and shelled creatures with ridged backs graze the film of algae on the sluice gates.

Predators keep to the edges. A long, sinuous animal with a dark banded body moves through the sedges, and where the cover is thin, it strikes at the amphibians with fast, precise snaps. Birds with narrow, hooked beaks hunt the falls from the overhangs, dropping into the spray and rising with fish or insects pinned in their beaks. Nothing here is abundant in the sense of being everywhere; the life clusters around water, edges, and the gaps between plant islands, and between each cluster, the ground is bare and quiet except for the mechanism.

**Light and weather**

The light is dim. The sky overhead is a low, uniform grey, and the sun, when it's visible at all, is a pale smear behind high cloud. Shadows are soft and seem to come from everywhere. The ridges rise on three sides and block most of the horizon, so the sky appears as a narrow lid stretched across the top of the basin.

Mist is almost constant. It gathers in the hollows, rolls down from the ridge faces, and hangs over the water in drifting layers. Droplets condense on every surface: on leaves, on stone, on the hair of animals, on the lenses of anything you might carry. The air feels heavy and cold against the skin, and the wet makes sound travel strangely, so the knocking of the mechanism seems to come from several directions at once.

Rain falls in short, heavy bursts, then stops. After each burst, the falls grow louder, the channels rise, and the basin seems to exhale. The mechanism speeds up during these pulses, its strokes coming faster and heavier, and then slowly settles back into its usual rhythm as the water drains off.

**Rules of the place**

The landscape follows a few consistent patterns. Water is the organizing force: it shapes the stone, drives the engine, feeds the plants, sustains the animals, and determines where anything can live. Where the water is fast and regular, the mechanism runs and the stone stays clean and sharp. Where the water is slow and stagnant, the peat builds up, the sedges spread, and the amphibians gather. Where the water has not yet reached, the ground stays bare, and nothing is willing to make a home.

Growth and decay happen at the same pace. The ridges are still breaking, the mechanism is still refining its shape, and the plants and animals are still filling in their gaps. The whole basin seems to be in the middle of an argument with itself, and the outcome is undecided. There is no sense that anything here is finished.

You get the sense that the place has been running for a long time without ever becoming anything other than what it is: a small, wet, cold bowl where the land is learning how to work. The knocking continues. The mist thickens. Somewhere in the sedges, an amphibian begins to call, and another answers, and the rhythm of the mechanism takes up the pulse, and for a while the whole basin is one loud, steady machine made of stone, water, and animal breath.
