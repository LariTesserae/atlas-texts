The land rises in long shelves, each one a step in a stair that no hand cut. Climbing into the highlands, you pass the last of the open grassland, where the ground is pale and fired-looking, and then the forest closes over you. The air thickens and cools as you gain height, but it never gets dry. Everything here holds water: leaves, walls, the bark of trees, the backs of the animals.

**The ground**

There is no bare soil. What looks like earth is a sediment of old construction, pressed and cemented over many lifetimes. Where you scrape the surface with a fingertip, it gives a little, then reveals a layer of fine grey-brown material packed with fibres, chitin flecks, seed husks, and the tiny spiral shells of something that once lived in it. Below that the texture changes to a dense, almost ceramic crust that rings faintly when struck. The lowest courses are harder still, glassy in places where some secretion has cured over centuries into a stone-like resin.

The shelves are walled. Each is held back at its edge by a curved bank, a meter or several meters high, built from pellets of worked mud bonded with saliva-cement and wound through with root and silk. The banks are weathered now into smooth rounded forms, the way old limestone is smoothed, with ferns and mosses growing in their pockmarks. Lichens the colour of rust and lime cover the north faces. The walls are not ruins. They are maintained, slowly, by the same populations that built them: a pellet pushed into a crack here, a rebuilt buttress there, a whole section redirected over a season when the water shifts course.

**Water**

Water moves through the terraces in a network of channels cut by the builders themselves. They are not straight. They wind between mounds and under lattice bridges, and they pool in basins where the flow slows. Each basin is lined with a dark, fine sediment that smells of iron and rot and sweetness. Water seeps out of the banks in dozens of places, so the whole slope weeps. Mist gathers in the hollows every morning and does not fully lift until midday, if it lifts at all. Rain comes most afternoons, heavy and brief, and then the channels run brown and fast for an hour before settling back.

Where the water runs off the highest shelf, it falls in threadlike curtains over ledges of the same cemented material, and the falling water has built a travertine-like fringe below each fall: grey-white tiers that stick out over the pool like shelves of wet paper.

**Structures**

The architecture is layered in function as well as in form. The lowest and oldest structures are the great mounds, rounded hills of mud and organic matter standing taller than a person, pocked with galleries and vents. Their surfaces are a living skin of moss, fungus, and small burrowing things. Thin cracks breathe out warm damp air. Put your ear to one and you hear it working: a faint continuous scraping, a rustle of many small bodies moving at once, a sound like rain on a roof that is not raining.

Between the mounds are the bridges. These are woven: fibres of silk and root spun and knotted into lattice spans, anchored to pillars of cemented stone. Some are wide enough for a dozen animals to cross together. The oldest have thickened into near-solid ribs, their silk fused to a mineral crust, so that they look like the spines of extinct fish.

Higher up, the structures become more delicate. Tall spires, thin as reeds, rise from the canopy floor, hollow and perforated, their walls honeycombed with cells. They sway in wind but do not fall. Some are topped with pale hoods that drip constantly, and the water pools in a lip around each spire's base, feeding the moss that climbs it.

The overall effect is of a city that grew from the ground up without any plan visible from within, and yet has a logic at every scale: the channels feed the basins, the basins feed the moss, the moss holds the soil, and the soil is the building material for the walls that hold the channels in place.

**Canopy and light**

Above it all is the canopy, a dense roof of broad leaves, many of them lobed and waxy, layered in tiers three or four deep. Hanging epiphytes drape from the branches in long curtains. Light comes down through this roof as a dim green-brown wash, even in the middle of the day. Shadows are soft and seem to sink into the ground. Colour is muted except where a bloom or a fruit or a slow-moving body catches what little light there is and flares briefly.

Nights are close to total dark. A few fungal growths along the lower walls give off a faint blue-green glow, enough to find the edge of a channel but not enough to read by. Otherwise you move by touch and sound.

**Sound**

This is the most overwhelming thing about the place. The noise is constant, layered, and close. Insects fill the air in dense swarms that you hear as a single hum at first, then slowly separate into a dozen distinct textures: clicking, buzzing, shrill, low. Frogs or things like frogs call from every pool, in ranks that answer one another across the slopes. Birds, or birdlike creatures, call from the canopy in sharp repeated phrases. Water rushes, drips, splashes, and gurgles from a hundred sources at different pitches. Rain on the leaves produces a roar that drowns out everything for minutes at a time.

Beneath all this is the sound of the builders: a constant scraping, grinding, rustling, pattering, the sound of enormous numbers of small things working. Sometimes a deep knock comes from inside the mounds, like a hollow drum struck once. Sometimes a long wet tearing as a bridge is pulled tight. The noise has no silence in it. Even at what seems like a lull, there is always something moving close by.

**Fauna**

The fauna is packed to saturation. The ground and walls are alive with small things: beetles, mites, springtails, worms, grubs, and crawling many-legged animals that move in trails along the banks. Above them, in the canopy and on the walls, larger animals move constantly: tree-dwellers that climb the spires, grazers that browse the moss on the mounds, flying forms that swarm around the basins at dusk. Everywhere, at every height, something is eating, building, or being eaten.

The builders themselves are not one species but a community. Some pellet and bind; some spin; some excavate; some tend the fungal gardens that the others depend on. Each seems to have its own role, and each has its own shapes of cooperation with the others, so that the whole structure is less a monument than a continuous negotiation between many kinds of worker.

**Time**

Nothing here changes quickly. The shelves shift a few centimetres in a generation. A bridge is rebuilt over several seasons. A mound may take centuries to rise a metre. The erosion is slow and settled, the weathering smooth and complete in places, but the process never stops: the rain keeps wearing, the builders keep repairing, the water keeps finding new paths. Every feature you see is the current stage of something that has been going on far longer than anything you could witness, and will go on far longer than you could stay.

The place does not feel like a wilderness that has been left alone, nor like a settlement that has been abandoned. It feels like a process that has become a landscape: a long, patient, wet, dark, deafening work, still under way, with no beginning you can point to and no end in sight.
