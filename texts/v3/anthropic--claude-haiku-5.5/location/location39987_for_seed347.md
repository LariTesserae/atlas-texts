**The Sunk Terraces of Hallow Mere**

You come down into it rather than across it. The land tilts gently at first, then the ground gives way to a long bowl, perhaps nine kilometers across, whose rim is a ragged wall of raw grey stone. The wall is young. Its faces are still sharp-edged, unweathered, with fresh fracture planes glinting where a slab sheared off last season and slid into the basin. Pale scree fans lie at its foot like spilled bone. Above the rim the sky is a low, even pewter, and the cold comes off the water in a constant slow breath that beads on your skin and collects in your collar.

The basin floor is water, but not open water. It is a marsh so thoroughly grown over that the mere shows only in glimpses: black pools shining between mats of sphagnum, moss so deep and spongy the ground gives under your weight like wet bread, and rivulets threading through everything in a silver net. Every step squelches. Every hollow fills with seep. Rock weeps here. Basalt outcrops bead with drops that run down in lines, and the mosses on them are emerald and stippled. The air is saturated, so that the far terraces soften into blue haze a few hundred meters off and the whole scene feels as though it is being drawn through wet glass.

Then there are the buildings, and they are the strangest part, because they were made for a world that is no longer here, or was never quite here. The settlement climbs the inner slopes of the bowl in stepped terraces, dozens of tiers, each held up by dry-laid stone walls with no mortar, fitted so tightly that the joints are hair-thin. The stone is the same grey as the rim, so the city looks grown out of the mountain rather than set on it. Timber galleries cross between tiers on posts sunk into the mire. Many of the posts are black with age and furred with lichen, and some have gone soft and bent. Causeways of split log and flat stone link the terraces across the swamp, and the causeways sag and dip, sometimes submerged, sometimes rebuilt on top of older ones, so that the walkways show their layers the way a cut bank shows sediment.

Nothing here has a machine, a wire, a lamp, or a metal fitting that you would call a device. There are no roads in the sense of paved paths, no rails, no sign of kilns that burn coal. The builders worked by hand and by biology: stone chipped with patient repetition, timber lashed with fibre, pulleys of vine and wood. Some of the upper walls have carvings of stacked shapes and spirals, weathered almost smooth, and there are nested hollows in the stone where something seems to have been ground or pressed for a very long time.

The vegetation is lush and cold-adapted, which gives the whole place a dense, dark-green, almost velvet character. Dwarf birch and alder crowd the terrace edges, their trunks wrapped in moss and their leaves already turning rust at the tips. Willow scrub fills the wetter ground. Cold-hardy ferns, huge fronds unfurling in tight coils, grow out of the cracks in the stone walls, and giant horsetails stand in the shallows like green bundled rods. Lichens of several colours (orange, grey-green, a bright chalky white) cover every exposed surface that is not wet. Above the basin wall a dark fringe of conifers climbs the slope and thins toward the rim, their crowns stunted and wind-combed.

Fauna is abundant and crowded. Everything you see is occupied. In the shallows, long-legged amphibians the colour of wet slate sit shoulder to shoulder on the logs, and their throat sacs pulse in unison in slow waves. Small, dense flocks of grey and white birds work the mud, probing and calling in short, sharp bursts, while larger wading birds stand motionless at the channel edges. Bats come out early, even at the grey midday, and hang in dark clusters under the galleries. In the tangled underbrush, things rustle and shift constantly. Heavy, furred, short-legged mammals with dark eyes move along the causeways in loose herds, grazing the moss and watching you with a patience that feels like judgment. Predators move through the same spaces: a sleek, low-bodied hunter threads through the reeds, and where it has taken a kill the water is churned and the feeding cries carry for a long way.

The sound is almost nothing. Drip, seep, the faint suck of boots on moss, a distant wing-beat, the soft settle of wet leaves. Voices are muffled by the damp air. The loudest sound is the water itself, trickling down a thousand rock faces at once, a fine ceaseless hiss beneath everything. You find yourself lowering your voice without meaning to.

Light is ordinary and diffuse, filtered through cloud and wet air, with no harsh shadows. The sky is never fully bright here. Fog comes and goes in drifts that lift off the mere in the morning, climb the terraces, and dissolve by afternoon, only to pool again at dusk. Rain falls in short, sharp showers, then stops, and the whole basin steams and steams again as the sun breaks briefly through.

Everything here is moving. The scree is still settling from the last slump. Water is cutting new channels through the peat. Plants are colonising the bare patches left by the last rain, and the lichen is spreading across the new-cut faces. Animals shift between terraces with the seasons of food and water, and the predators follow them. The city itself is in motion too, in the slow way of anything that is continuously repaired: a gallery sags and is shored up, a wall bulges and is re-laid, a causeway is rebuilt over the last one. The mere is always rising and falling, and the terraces are always being reclaimed and rebuilt, so the place seems to be a single process of growing, falling down, and being built up again, with nothing ever quite finished and nothing quite gone.
