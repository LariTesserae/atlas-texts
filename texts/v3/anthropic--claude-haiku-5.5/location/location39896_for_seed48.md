The low ground runs flat to the horizon in every direction, and the air is wet and warm, thick enough that the far edges of the land soften into haze. Water sits just under the surface everywhere. Where the crust has cracked, black seep fills the seams. Where it hasn't, a skin of brown water lies over the ground and reflects the sky, which is a shade of pewter that never quite brightens. Light comes from no single source. It seems to leak through cloud from all directions at once, so the shadows are faint and the colors look wrung out.

Nothing grows here. The ground is mineral: packed grey silt, glazed clinker, the pale crust that forms where brine has dried and risen and dried again. Plant life left long ago, and what remains are the outlines of its absence, pale traces in the silt where roots once held and then dissolved. Everything that stands upright was built.

The city is large, and it's old. Its foundations spread outward across the flats in grids that the water has softened at the edges. Towers rise in clusters, their upper floors gone or stripped to rebar, and the lower stories are sheathed in a slow mineral bloom: white, rust, and a green-black crust that is not a plant but a film of something that grows on concrete and metal. Roads run between the blocks, though most are broken into causeways that stand a meter or two above the standing water. Pipes come up out of the ground at angles, some still leaking. Gantries stand over empty lots like the frames of enormous unfinished animals. Cooling stacks lean in pairs, and from several of them a thin vapor still rises and is slowly bent sideways by the wind.

The machinery still runs, or runs enough. In the centre of the city a large plant hums under the water, and the deep vibration reaches your feet before you hear anything. Pumps knock somewhere in the distance. A conveyor, long dead at one end, shivers when the water rises through its housing. Turbines in a row on a concrete quay turn very slowly, turned by a current nobody has bothered to stop. The mechanical sound is persistent and low and it is the closest thing this landscape has to weather.

The quiet is the most noticeable thing. The humidity swallows distant sounds, and the wind rarely moves faster than a breath. Footsteps on wet concrete make a dull slap. Voices don't carry. Even the animals are muted, though there are a great many of them.

They are everywhere. Their presence has a density that the emptiness of the landscape seems to hide until you look closely. Across the flats, individual creatures are rare, but where they gather they gather in enormous numbers. Crabs the size of dinner plates cover the lower causeways in shifting carpets, their shells clicking softly. Amphibians with pale, flattened bodies sit motionless in drainage channels by the thousands, and their throats pulse in unison when the water pressure changes. Rust-coloured birds with long legs stalk the shallows in flocks that lift all at once and settle again a hundred metres away, leaving the sky spotted for a few seconds before it empties. Under the waterline, something larger moves in slow arcs, its back breaking the surface now and then in a dull grey ridge before it sinks again. The fauna is not spread evenly. Where the old buildings have collapsed into deep, cool basements, the animals pack in tight, and the sound of their gathering is a constant soft shifting. Elsewhere, for kilometres, there is nothing but water and haze.

The dynamics here are slow but continuous. The tide, if that is what it is, pulses through the drainage channels every few hours, lifting the crabs and birds and pushing them to higher ground until the water recedes. Brine rises through the crust in pale blooms and dries into new white plates that crack and curl. The cooling stacks breathe on a cycle. Storms come up from the south in long grey banks, and when they arrive the rain runs down the glazed walls in sheets and fills the gutters within minutes, and the fauna drops into the water and waits. After the storm passes, the city is clean and dripping, and the animals come back out to the causeways in a rush of movement.

Taken as a whole, this is a settled place. The built world and the water have reached an accommodation. The city does not rebuild, and it does not fall, though its parts continue to fail and be replaced by new forms, most of them animal. The machines still turn. The creatures still gather. The vast, hot, dim air holds all of it in a low, damp stillness, and the only sounds are the slow knock of pumps, the tick of crabs on concrete, and the water moving through the empty streets.
