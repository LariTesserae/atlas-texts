**The Sinkwell**

You arrive at the rim and look down into a bowl perhaps four hundred paces across, cut into the plain like a thumbprint pressed into clay. The land around you is dry, pale, cracked into hexagonal plates. The bowl is not. Its floor is wet, glossy, and restless, and you can hear it from here.

The sound comes up the walls in layers. Nearest, there is a continuous hiss, like a kettle held just below boiling, and under that a deeper percussion, water striking stone in thousands of places at once. Every few seconds a louder report cracks across the basin, a sheet of something giving way. Somewhere on the far side, a metallic note rings, long and falling, then another answers it from a different direction. The bowl is loud the way a foundry is loud, and it does not stop.

**The walls** are the oldest thing here. They are terraced in broad, uneven steps, each step a different color: ochre at the top, then rust, then a bruised violet near the waterline, then black. The terraces are weathered smooth, rounded like river stones the size of houses, and their faces weep. Water seeps from hairline seams and runs down in thin glassy threads that catch the light. Where the seeps gather they cut shallow gullies, and those gullies have been cutting for so long that the walls look grooved by patient hands. The rock is soft enough to crumble in your fingers at the top and harden to glassy basalt-like grain near the bottom, as if the bowl had been slowly tempered from the outside in.

**The light** is fierce. The sun sits high and almost directly overhead, and because the bowl is sunk below the plain, it pours into the basin like liquid into a cup. Shadows are short and hard. Every wet surface throws the light back up, so the air over the floor shimmers and the walls seem to glow from within. You will need to squint. Within an hour you will notice that the glare has a faint green tint near the water, and that the reflections in the pools do not quite match the things they should reflect.

**The water** is everywhere and nowhere in particular. There are no lakes. Instead the floor is a skin of standing water, a few finger-widths deep in most places, over a bed of pale sediment that rises and falls in long slow swells. Here and there the skin breaks. Columns of mist stand up out of the breaks and spread out at about the height of a tall man, then drift toward the walls and condense back into rain on the terraces. The basin rains on itself. The water cycle here runs in a tight, hot loop, and the air is thick enough that your clothes will feel damp within minutes, though no drop has touched you.

Near the center the skin thickens into a dark circular pool, perhaps forty paces across, and this is where the turbulence is strongest. The surface does not ripple. It *erupts* in short, precise pulses, a ring of spray rising, collapsing, and rising again, on a rhythm that almost sounds like breathing. Around its edge, the sediment has been pushed up into low, rounded ridges, like the tracks of something very large moving slowly underneath.

**The built things** are few and strange. Partway down the eastern wall, a row of stubby pillars stands in the terraces, each about the height of a man, each capped with a cracked, opalescent dome. The domes are fogged on the inside. When the light hits them at an angle you can see faint filaments in the glass, like the veins of a leaf, slowly pulsing amber and then fading. Some of the pillars have collapsed, and their pieces lie half-submerged, still humming faintly through the water. Nothing here seems to have been built to last, yet everything has lasted. The machines appear to be repairing themselves slowly, or else they are simply unfinished, still in the middle of some long assembly.

A little further along the rim, you can make out a cluster of lattice frames rising out of the water like the ribs of a sunken ship. They are made of a pale metal that bends where it should snap and stays where it is bent. Threads of condensation run along the lattice in a pattern that looks deliberate, funneling water toward the center of the bowl. Whatever they once did, they still do: they gather the mist and carry it inward. It is hard to say whether they serve the basin or the basin serves them.

**The living things** are sparse. Lichen-like mats cling to the higher terraces, grey-green and wrinkled, spreading in patient rings from the seeps. Lower down, where the damp reaches, the mats thicken into mounds the color of wet bread, and they twitch when the spray touches them. A few stunted trees grow in the cracks near the rim. Their bark is thin and peeling, like wet paper, and their leaves are broad and translucent, so that the sun passes through them and paints the rock below in tiny dappled blurs. You will see no more than a handful of animals in the whole basin. A pale, long-legged skimmer moves across the shallows in a fast zigzag and never seems to touch the water. Once, high on the wall, something with too many joints clings motionless to a seep, feeding, and then is gone when you look again.

**The dynamic**, if you stay long enough to feel it, is what defines the place. Nothing here holds its shape for long. The terraces shed flakes that fall into the pools and dissolve. The ridges in the sediment migrate a few inches each hour. Some pools vanish overnight and reappear a hundred paces away. Pillars seem to tilt, straighten, and tilt again. Sounds come and go in bursts: a sudden chorus of metallic ringing, then silence, then the hiss returning all at once like a door opening. The whole basin behaves like a storm that has learned to stand still for a moment and then remembers it is supposed to move.

You feel a pressure in your chest that is not quite fear. The air is warm, bright, and loud, and you are standing at the edge of something that has been unmaking and remaking itself since before the plain above was dry. The bowl does not look at you. It seems to be doing something, slowly and enormously, and you have simply happened to arrive in the middle of it.
