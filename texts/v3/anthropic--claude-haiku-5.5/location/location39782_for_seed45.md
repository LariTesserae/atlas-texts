# The Ember Shelves

The land rises here in terraces, each one a shelf of black and rust-colored stone stacked against the next like the steps of a dry amphitheater. The highest shelves are near ten kilometers up, where the air is thin enough that breathing would come in short pulls. Below them the plateaus spread out for hundreds of kilometers, and beyond those the mountains go on, too tall to look at for long without the eye losing its footing.

The ground is warm. Not hot enough to burn, but warm through the soles, the way a stone is warm after a long afternoon. Heat comes up from below. In places the crust has split into polygonal plates, each a few meters across, and their edges glow a faint dull red after dark. Where the plates have pulled apart, the gaps breathe out thin, mineral-tasting vapor that condenses on the nearby rock as a crust of pale salts. The salts are white, yellow, and a green that looks wrong against the black.

## Light

The sky never fully opens. A permanent haze of sulfur dust and fine ash hangs above the plateau, and the sun, if it is visible at all, is a bruised coin behind it, giving off a light the color of cooling iron. Shadows are soft and brownish and seem to lie on the ground rather than fall from anything. At midday the world looks like an old photograph left in a window. At the edges of the day, the haze turns faintly violet, and then the dark comes in from the east and settles into the hollows first.

Some nights the haze thins, and then the sky shows stars that have no names. There is also a faint pale band across the zenith, like a scar of mist, which is probably a ring or a cloud of debris, though it moves slowly enough that it might be something else.

## Ground and Material

Most of the surface is basalt, glassy and fractured, with a sheen that catches the dim light. Where it is fresh, it is so sharp that walking on it means watching every step. Where it is older, wind and grit have worn it smooth and rounded, and it rings faintly when kicked, like a bell with a crack in it.

Between the basalt plates, the ground is fine grey dust that takes footprints the way wet clay does. The dust is always moving in small ways. Tiny columns of it rise from the warm cracks and fall apart. Crusts form overnight and break apart by morning. The surface is never the same from one day to the next, and this is what makes the place feel alive: it changes faster than geology should.

## The Growing Things

Nothing here is alive in any sense you would recognize, but some things grow. The vents at the lower edges of the terraces have built chemical gardens: tubes and towers of silica and iron-rich mineral, some as thin as a reed and some taller than a person, rising from the cracks in a few hours and spreading in pale branching patterns. They are lovely in a fragile way, glassy and translucent, with a faint shimmer when the vapor moves through them. Then, without warning, a tower will lean, crack along its length, and collapse into a heap of glittering shards. The shards get pulled back into the vent, and new towers rise a few meters away.

On the older shelves, a thin crust of dark mineral film covers the rock in patches. It is not really a lichen, though it seems to behave like one in that it spreads slowly over bare stone and pulls color from the surrounding salts. When you press a finger to it, it gives a little and then springs back. No one who has studied it has been able to say whether it is a fixed structure or something closer to a slow reaction that never finishes.

## The Marks of Builders

Scattered across the terraces are things that look as though someone once made them. Most are small: a waist-high arch of fused glass with no room behind it; a cairn of blackened blocks stacked so neatly that it seems deliberate; a circle of standing slabs, each with a row of shallow grooves cut into its face, the grooves eroded almost flat. There is a stone doorway on one shelf, its lintel carved with rows of marks that could be letters or cracks, standing alone with nothing attached and the ground around it cracked into fresh plates on every side.

The strangest thing is that no one can say for certain they were built. The arches and cairns may be natural formations, sharpened by the heat and the wind and the glass-forming vapor until they took on shapes that look like intent. The grooves may be stress marks. The doorway may have been moved by the ground. But the placement is hard to explain by chance. The cairns line up along the ridges. The arches face the same direction, toward the lower shelves. Each one seems to be pointing somewhere.

There are no inhabitants and no sign of recent use. The marks are old, but the ground around them is young, so they may have been made, or formed, in an earlier arrangement of the land that has since been unmade.

## Sound

The place is mostly quiet. Wind moves over the plateaus, but it is thin and dry, more a hiss than a roar. The loudest sounds are the ground's: plates grinding together with a low, drawn-out groan; the sharp crack of a tower collapsing somewhere out of sight; the ticking of warm glass cooling after dark; the soft rush of vapor from a fresh vent. Some of these sounds come in clusters and then stop for hours. Some come from far off, distant enough that you cannot tell whether they are a few hundred meters away or several kilometers.

Silence here is never fully silent. It is always holding something back.

## Change

Everything shifts. Plates crack and reform. Gardens rise and fall. Crust forms in one season and breaks in the next. Ridges sink, and new ridges push up out of the dust. A path you walked yesterday may be gone today. A cairn may have tipped over, or may have been moved a few meters to the east, or may have been swallowed by a new fissure. The land does not hold its shape long enough for anything to be permanent.

This is perhaps the most unsettling part of the place. The land is not dead, and it is not finished. It is in the middle of something, and nothing that happens here is final. The marks of builders stand among the ruins of their own making, and the ground keeps rewriting the record underneath them, one crack and one collapse at a time.
