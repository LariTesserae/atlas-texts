# The Drowned Terraces

The land is a high plateau that rose out of a flood and never let the water go. It stands above a sea so still and so thick with suspended minerals that it looks less like water than like a lens of pale green glass laid over the world. From the highest terrace you can see the sea stretch to a horizon that dissolves into haze, with a few black knuckles of older mountain breaking the surface like the backs of sleeping animals.

The air is warm and wet, close as the inside of a mouth. Everything is damp to the touch, and the damp never quite becomes rain. Water hangs in the atmosphere as a thick, luminous mist that holds the light the way frosted glass does. Light comes from everywhere and nowhere, a dim pewter glow with no sun behind it, and shadows are soft, brown, and shallow. Dusk and dawn cannot be told apart. Only the faint shift in the mist's color, from silver to amber to grey, marks the passing of the hours.

## The Ground

The plateau is young, and you can read its youth in every surface. The rock is raw. Fresh basalt lies in slabs still faintly warm, its surfaces glassy and unweathered, and in places it cracks and sheds small flakes as it finishes cooling. Beside it, pale travertine terraces are growing out of the flooded margins, built one thin lamina at a time by the mineral sea. They step down the slope in shelves, each lip curled and porous, like the stacked petals of some stone rose. Where the water has pooled on these shelves, the calcite forms basins that are a shade of turquoise you have never seen in nature.

Nothing here has finished becoming. Ridges are sharp and unrounded. Gullies have no established floors, only trenches the water has scoured and then left alone. Soil is barely a suggestion, a grey silt that binds to the rock in patches and cracks like dried clay. Erosion is so slight that the land seems to be holding its breath, waiting to decide what it will be.

## The Green

Despite the newness of the stone, the plateau is thick with growth, and this is the first of the place's contradictions. Vegetation is lush and dense in a way that suggests it is making up for lost time. The dominant forms are broad-leaved and succulent: fleshy paddles of silver-green sprouting from fissures, tall reeds the color of old brass, and vines that climb the basalt and drape over the travertine like wet rope. Ferns grow in the mist-shadowed hollows, their fronds so large a person could shelter under one.

Much of the plant life seems to grow directly from the mineral crust. Roots spread across the rock and dissolve tiny channels into it, so that pale, lacy calcite casts of old roots stand throughout the terraces like fossils of a forest that is still growing. Some plants take their nourishment from the mist alone, their leaves beaded with a salty film that drips steadily onto the stone below, feeding the travertine.

## The Built

People live here, or something like people, in settlements that are dense and layered and built with patience. The architecture is primitive in its tools but sophisticated in its ambition. Walls are made of stacked basalt blocks, roughly split and fitted without mortar, or of wattle and daub packed over frames of driftwood and reed. Roofs are thatched with the brass-colored reeds and sealed with a lime made from burned travertine. The settlements climb the terraces in tiers, each level rising over the roofs of the one below, so that a single town looks like a hillside of overlapping shells.

Movement between levels happens by steps cut into the rock, rope ladders strung between ledges, and narrow causeways of stone laid across the shallow basins. Stone bridges arch over the calcite pools, their undersides furred with pale growth. Everything is connected and everything is close. Doorways open onto other doorways, and courtyards are shared roofs. The density of the built environment makes the settlements feel less like towns than like single organisms, grown from the rock rather than placed upon it.

The tools are simple. There is no metal here, only stone, bone, wood, fiber, and fired clay. Knapped basalt serves as knives and scrapers. Bundled reeds become rope, mats, and baskets. Hollowed gourds and clay vessels hold water, oil, and lime. The craft is slow, careful, and deeply repetitive, and the people seem to value the repetition itself, as if the making were a form of prayer.

## The Sea

The sea is the heart of the place and its greatest oddity. It is not quite liquid in the ordinary sense. Its surface is so dense with dissolved minerals that it moves like oil, rising and falling in long, sluggish swells that take minutes to pass. Its color is a deep glassy green that deepens to black a few fathoms down. Sunlight, such as it is, does not penetrate far.

The sea is slowly building. Where it touches the plateau's edges, it deposits its minerals in thin, continuous crusts. Over decades these crusts thicken into shelves, and over centuries into terraces, and the people here have learned to read the sea's growth as one reads the seasons. They know which shelves will hold a causeway and which will crack. They build with the sea's slow hand rather than against it.

Below the surface, in the dim water, faint pale threads grow in lattices. Some are only centimeters long. Others, in the deep trenches where the basalt drops away, extend in branching columns that may be older than any human settlement. Swimming in the sea is possible but unpleasant. The water is heavy and bitter, and it leaves a film on the skin that takes days to fade.

## The Creatures

Fauna is rare, and the few animals that exist seem to belong to the slow tempo of the place. Large, shelled grazers move across the terraces at a pace that makes them look almost stationary, their backs crusted with the same pale calcite as the stone. Small translucent things drift in the mist, bioluminescent and ghostly, pulsing faintly as they pass through the haze. Birds, when they appear, are heavy, broad-winged, and silent, gliding low over the sea with the patience of creatures that have no predators left.

The people keep a few animals: bearded, grey-furred things that graze the reed beds, and flightless birds with dense, waterproof plumage that forage the shoreline. Hunting is rare and handled with ceremony. Most of what the people eat comes from the plants, the sea's mineral-rich shellfish, and the slow harvest of the reed beds.

## Sound

The place is mostly quiet, and the quiet has texture. Water drips constantly, from leaves, eaves, and the rims of basins, each drop landing with a soft, resonant tick against the stone. Beneath this, the sea makes a low, almost imperceptible hum, like a distant engine or a held note. Wind is rare, but when it comes it moves through the reeds with a dry, rustling sigh.

Human sound is subdued. Voices carry only a short way in the damp air, so conversation tends to be intimate, murmured across terraces. Work is done with a patience that makes it quiet: scraping, tapping, the soft knock of stone against stone. Rituals involve chanting, but the chants are low and slow, rising and falling in long, patient cycles that seem to match the rhythm of the swells.

## Rules of the World

Several strange rules govern this place, and the people have learned them through long experience.

Time moves at an odd pace. Things grow slowly, and change is measured in generations. A child born on a terrace may see it grow a new lip of travertine during their lifetime, but never see it finished. The people think of time as layered rather than linear, each generation adding its own course to the walls of the world.

Stone grows where water lingers. Anything left submerged in the sea for long enough will begin to acquire a pale crust, and eventually become part of the terraces. The people bury their dead in the shallows, and over years the bodies are slowly calcified into the shelves, so that the terraces are, in a real sense, made of ancestors.

The mist remembers. Sounds carry strangely in it, sometimes arriving long after they were made, and the people say that certain phrases spoken in certain hollows return to the speaker days later. They do not speak carelessly near the basins.

The sea must not be disturbed. Dredging, drilling, or cutting into the travertine is forbidden, and the reason given is simple: the sea is still deciding what it will become, and interference might make it choose something worse. No one has seen this happen, but no one has tested it either.

## The Shape of It

What emerges from this place is a sense of something in the middle of becoming. The plateau is not finished, the sea is not settled, and the people are building in a world that is still forming beneath them. Everything here is in the process of turning from one thing into another: water into stone, plant into rock, the dead into the foundation of the living. It is a place of patience, density, and slow, careful construction, lit by a dim and endless glow, where the land is slowly deciding what kind of world it will be, and the people have chosen to build their lives along the edge of that decision.
