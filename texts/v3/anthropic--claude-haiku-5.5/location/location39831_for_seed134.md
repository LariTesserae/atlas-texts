**The Drowned Caldera at Hollow Ridge**

You are inside a bowl of black glass, roughly four kilometers across, that sits high on a shoulder of mountains. It was a volcanic crater once. Now it is full to the rim with water so clear and cold that it seems less like a liquid than a pane of air laid over the stone. The rim is a jagged circle of cliffs, many of them still sharp-edged, with fractures that look as if they opened last season. Nothing has had time to soften them. Wind does not reach down here, and rain falls through the surface without disturbing much below.

Start with the ground. The floor is obsidian and tachylite, black and glassy, cut into terraces and shelves where the lava cooled in slabs. Where it breaks, the edges are conchoidal and razor-thin, and they catch the light in fractured bands of smoky brown and green. The slopes are scree of the same glass, sliding slowly, grinding grain against grain. Between the slabs lie drifts of pale mineral sand, white and orange, where dissolved silica and iron have precipitated out of the water and settled into fine layers. There is no soil, no peat, no root or rot. The whole basin is mineral, and it is built only of what the earth left behind.

The light is ordinary daylight, but it arrives strangely. Sunlight enters the surface in long columns and fades to blue within a few meters, then to a green so deep it reads as black. Near the shelves, where the water is shallow, the light is still gold and shifts in rippling nets over the glass. Look up through the surface and the sky is a ceiling of rain-pocked silver. Rain falls steadily on the water, and each drop makes a small dimple of light that rolls outward and fades. From beneath, the rain looks like a constant applause of sparks.

The water is cold, near the freezing point at this altitude. Its chill sits on the skin even through layers of wetsuit or oilskin. Thin sheets of ice form in the shallows at dawn and break up by midday, grinding against the glass with a sound like breaking plates. At depth the water is more stable, almost still, though it carries a faint, constant tremor. Warm water rises in places from vents along the floor. Where it does, the water blurs in shimmering columns and deposits pale crusts of silica on everything nearby.

Life fills the basin densely. Shoals move through the middle depths in tight, rolling masses, thousands strong, all turning together so that the water seems to bend. The creatures are translucent, with pale ribbed bodies and rows of pulsing lights along their flanks. They do not resemble any familiar fish. Some have no visible mouths, only fringed collars that open and close like shutters as they filter the water. Others are fixed to the rock, tall stalks of chitin and mineral that feed on the rain of suspended particles. These stalks crowd together in forests of pale, bristling tubes, and every few seconds the whole colony pulses at once, sending a wave of light and a soft sucking sound along the shelf.

There is no plant life. Nothing here photosynthesizes, and the basin is not green at any depth. The creatures build no nests or shelters, and nothing in the basin is constructed or left behind by any maker. Yet the place is full of motion that resembles industry. Vents along the floor release pulses of hot water at regular intervals, each one a deep thump that travels through the rock. Columns of bubbles rise in rhythmic bursts. Mineral precipitation clicks and ticks as crusts form on the glass. The vents, the pumps of gas and steam, and the chemical reactions all work together like a machine that nobody built. The result is a steady, heavy mechanical sound that fills the water and makes the whole basin hum.

The sound is loud, and it is the first thing you notice when you descend. Beneath the rain's hiss you hear the thud of the vents, the clicking of mineral growth, the squeal of ice shifting, and the chorus of the shoals, a low rushing sound like wind through a narrow gap. The colonies pulse with a wet, rhythmic suction. Everything overlaps, so that you cannot separate one source from another, and after a while the noise becomes a single pressure against the ears.

Change here is slow. The vents shift their rhythms over decades. The glass cracks a little more each winter as the cliffs settle. Silica crusts thicken by a few millimeters a year. The shoals drift in long cycles, pulling back from the shallows in one season and returning in another. Nothing happens quickly, and nothing is in a hurry to become anything else. The basin keeps its own time, measured in the slow accumulation of sediment and the patient breathing of the vents.

The strangeness accumulates as you stay. Every surface is either glass or crust or living collar, and every movement seems to belong to a larger pattern you cannot quite see. The water holds you, the light bends, the rain falls endlessly, and the machine of the vents thuds on under your feet. It is a place with no past you can read and no future you can predict, only the steady, loud, cold continuation of the present.
