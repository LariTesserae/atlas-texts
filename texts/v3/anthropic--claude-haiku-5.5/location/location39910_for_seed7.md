The floor of this place lies about sixty meters below the surface, at the bottom of a sinkhole that is still settling. The walls are raw. Their fracture faces are pale, angular, and unweathered, as if the ground split only a few decades ago. Ledges of broken stone stand out where the collapse stopped, and loose scree lies in fans below them, not yet smoothed by water. Nothing here has had time to round off.

The air is hot and thick, roughly the temperature of a greenhouse in midsummer, and it carries a wet mineral smell like struck flint and standing rain. The heat seems to come from below. In the deepest part of the basin the rock is faintly warm to the touch, and the ground steams in places where the cracks open. Condensation runs down everything. Water gathers on the undersides of leaves, beads on the walls, and collects in the pools that fill the lowest hollows.

Those pools are the water of this place: still, dark, and warm. Some lie in the old settling tanks of a plant that once stood here, their concrete rims cracked and spilling. Others sit in the bowls of collapsed foundations. The surfaces are often covered with a fine, iridescent film, and their edges are fringed with pale, fleshy reeds that lean over the water as if listening to it.

The vegetation is lush and strange. Broad-leafed ferns with pallid, almost translucent fronds grow in dense stands, and they are the tallest things in the basin, reaching three or four meters toward the seam of light far overhead. Mats of moss and fungus cover every flat surface, soft and gray-green, sometimes dimpled with spore caps. Vines climb the machinery and hang from the broken catwalks in long, slow curtains. The plants grow without visible animal help. No pollinators, no grazers, no decomposers in the usual sense. Dead leaves do not seem to rot quickly; they dissolve into a dark, spongy humus that the roots reclaim.

The growth follows the old infrastructure. Roots run along buried conduits. Ferns stand in regular rows where pipe trenches once ran. Vines wrap the spokes of a rusted wheel in a tight spiral, and the wheel has not turned in decades. Water channels, cut in the concrete long ago, still carry condensation in clean, purposeful lines. Nobody maintains them. The plants and the drainage have simply arranged themselves into a pattern that looks deliberate, though nothing here is deliberate anymore.

The machinery is industrial and mostly dead. A turbine housing as large as a delivery truck lies on its side, its blades snapped and half buried in fern. A pressure vessel stands upright with its skin bloomed in orange rust and beaded with water. A conveyor belt runs from a loading hopper across the floor and into a wall, its rollers seized, its belt replaced by a mat of pale moss. A pipe climbs out of the basin at a steep angle, disappearing into the dim seam above, and at its base there is a faint hum. It is barely audible, almost a feeling in the chest, and it may come from the pipe, the warm rock, or the vessel. Nothing else in the place moves with that sound.

Scattered among the machines are smaller traces of people. A control booth with a cracked window and a dead panel of toggles. A bench bolted to a concrete slab, its paint gone. A sign on a post, one line still legible in faded white letters: a safety instruction, the rest eaten by mold. A lamp hangs from a bracket, its glass clouded green from the inside, and it is not the source of the faint light. That comes from the seam far above, a pale slit of gray that reaches the basin only as a dim, diffuse glow. Near the walls there are patches of bioluminescent fungus that give off a cold blue, enough to see shapes by but not enough to read. Most of the basin is in shadow.

The silence is nearly complete. There are no insects, no birds, no movement in the undergrowth. The loudest sounds are the slow fall of drips into pools and the occasional creak of a vine settling under its own weight. Once in a while a section of wall gives way with a dull crack, and small stones patter down the scree. Then the quiet returns and holds for a long time.

Change here happens in measures of years and decades. The vines extend a few centimeters each season. The pools rise by hairline increments as the walls weep. The fern stands creep outward along the drainage lines. The rust on the vessel thickens into layers that peel away in flakes the size of a palm. The sinkhole is still collapsing, very slowly, and each fresh fracture exposes new raw rock to the warm, wet air and the slow work of the plants. No event occurs. The basin only becomes more fully what it already is: a sunken room where heat, water, and dead machinery have made something green in the dark, and where the quiet continues without anything to break it.
