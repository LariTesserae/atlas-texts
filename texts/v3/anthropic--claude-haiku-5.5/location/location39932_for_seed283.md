**The Lowland Dark**

The land lies barely above the water table. A wide, flat basin stretches to the horizon in every direction, and the eye finds no ridge or rise to rest on. Most of the ground is not ground at all but a sponge of peat, silt, and decayed root-mat, dark brown and springy underfoot, that gives way to standing water in long, slow-breathing sheets. Sky is almost invisible. Overhead, a canopy of broad-leaved giants knits so tightly that the light arriving at the floor has passed through a dozen layers of leaf, and what reaches the ground is a dim greenish dusk even at midday. At night it is simply dark, and the darkness has texture: it is thick, close, and wet.

**Heat and moisture**

The air is hot and heavy, the temperature steady near the top of its range, and it holds water the way a sealed room does. Mist collects in the understory and drips from every surface. Leaves run with condensation. Warm rain falls in short, hard bursts, a few times a day, and then the steam rises off the canopy in slow columns and the whole basin smells of crushed green and iron. The water itself is brown-black and tannic where it stands still, but in the channels it moves a faint rust-orange, carrying a fine red-ochre sediment that settles on the stones and stains the roots. Where the orange is thickest, the surface film shimmers with iridescent sheen.

**The absent industry**

Nothing here was built. There are no roads, no posts, no walls, no structures of any kind. But the ground remembers something. The iron-rich silt is too red, too concentrated, the sediment layers are banded with oily grey seams that do not come from any plant. Some channels run with a faint chemical sweetness. Far off to one side, perhaps a day's travel away, there is a sense of enormous scale: a line of pale haze that does not drift like weather, and occasionally a low, rhythmic thrum that arrives through the water rather than the air. Whatever industry once ran here has left its sediment, its poisons slowly leached into the soil, its salts and metals now the basis of the local chemistry. The tech is present as a residue, a long fading exhalation, not as anything a person could walk up to and touch.

**The packed ecology**

Every cubic metre is occupied. Roots interlock with roots, vines braid through trunks, epiphytes mat the bark so thickly that the trees look furred, and between them the understory is a wall of broad leaves, fern fronds, and fungal shelves stacked like pages in a ledger. Nothing is bare. Where the light cannot reach, moss and pale lichen cover fallen logs in soft, glowing colonies that give off a faint phosphorescence at dusk.

The fauna is overwhelming, and yet it is mostly quiet. Insects are everywhere: they hang in clouds over the water, crawl over every leaf surface, bore through the wood, and rise in columns when disturbed. Amphibians sit in the shallows in their thousands, their throats pulsing gently. Above, bats and night birds move in layered streams. Snakes, lizards, and armored reptiles lie half-submerged on the mudbanks, and larger shapes move slowly through the channels, their bodies ridged and mossed, so that they are hard to tell from the logs. Most of the life moves without noise. The loudest sounds are the drip of condensation, the slap of water against roots, and the wet rasp of things feeding.

**Sound and time**

Sound is muted by the density of leaves and water. Footsteps vanish into the peat; voices do not carry more than a few metres. Occasionally a bird call or a deep, distant bellow rolls across the basin and dies out. Mostly it is the constant low murmur of life packed too close together.

Change here is slow. The water rises and falls over seasons, not days. Trees fall and rot over decades, and the rot feeds the next generation of growth. Sediment builds in layers that take centuries to form. The sheen on the water thickens or thins imperceptibly across years. Nothing in the basin is in a hurry. Its processes are patient, cyclical, and deeply settled, the kind of balance that has held long enough that disturbance itself has been absorbed into the rhythm.

The result is a place that is crowded, hot, wet, and dim, where the only signs of human enterprise are a reddish stain, a sweet smell, and a distant haze that never moves, and where the living things have filled every available space and gone on living, quietly, in the long dark green.
