**The Mesa of Ringing Stone**

The place is a high, flat-topped plateau, perhaps a few hundred paces across, lifted above a dead basin of pale salt-crust. The edges fall away in rounded, weathered cliffs. Nothing grows here, and nothing has grown here for so long that the ground has forgotten it. The rock is a fine-grained, chalky mineral, bone-white where the dust has scoured it and ochre-brown in the hollows. Millennia of wind have worn every edge down into smooth swells, so the whole surface looks like something poured and left to cool.

Every inch of the plateau has been made. The natural stone is still there underneath, but it is buried under layers of hand-cut blocks, rammed earth, fired clay, and stacked fragments of older building. Walls run in tight, interlocking courses. Terraces step down the slopes. Cairns rise in ranks, each one slightly taller than the last for a stretch and then shorter again, as if the pattern had been counted out and then forgotten. Narrow lanes wind between structures so close together that the eye can't find a horizon. Each doorway opens onto another wall. Each roof is the floor of the next level up. There are no open squares, no gaps to rest in, only the packed fullness of a settlement that kept growing inward until nothing was left empty.

The objects are the strangest part. Pillars and posts stand at intervals across every level, and from their tops hang bells, gongs, and tuned metal plates of hammered bronze or iron, green and black with age. Some are the size of a fist, others as tall as a man. They are hung on fixed cords, and many have clappers tied at angles that keep them just touching the rim. There are also hollow stone pipes set into the walls and rooftops, their open mouths angled into the wind. Together they form a single instrument spread across the entire mesa.

The light is dim and brownish, as if the sun were always seen through a thick sheet of dust or old smoke. Shadows are soft and do not move, because the light has not moved in an age. The air is warm and dry. It tastes of mineral dust and something faintly metallic, like the air near old forges. Heat rises from the stone in a shimmer that never quite becomes visible.

The sound is what defines the place. The wind crosses the plateau constantly, and it moves through the pipes, over the bell-rims, and against the tied clappers. The result is a deafening, continuous drone, layered with chimes and sharp metallic overtones. It has no rhythm and no melody, and the pitch never drifts. The sound fills the lanes and bounces off the walls until you can feel it in your teeth and sternum. Beneath it runs a low, steady hum that might be the stone itself resonating. The noise never rises or falls. After long enough, it stops registering as sound and becomes the texture of the air.

There are no footprints in the crust, no tracks, no droppings, no nests in the walls, no insects in the dust, nothing that moves except the wind and the metal. The makers are not here. Their work is. Doors stand open to rooms holding stacked tools, bowls of dry pigment, coils of cord that never frays, and tables set with unfired clay vessels. Nothing is broken. Nothing is waiting to be finished. The place seems to have reached a final arrangement and stayed there, with the wind playing the same chord forever on the instruments its makers left behind.
