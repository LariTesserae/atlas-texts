# The Terrace Country of Hallen Reach

You stand on a plateau at roughly the height where the air starts to thin. The ground is pale grey-violet, a crust of fused mineral that breaks underfoot into plates the size of dinner tables. Each plate has curled edges, like old paint or dried mud, and the curl points the same way across the whole plain, as if something once pressed from the northwest for a very long time.

Nothing here is wet. There was water once, in the mineral banding of the cliffs, in the white bathtub rings on the terraces, but it has been gone for ages. What remains is salt: a bright glaze in the hollows and a fine grey silt that rises in long, slow ribbons when the wind shifts. The wind is almost always moving. It has no warmth in it. It smells of cold stone and something faintly metallic, like a coin held too long in the mouth.

## The Built Layer

The city is called nothing anyone living remembers, but the terraces are its bones. They cascade down the eastern escarpment in stepped tiers, each one faced with dressed stone blocks fitted without mortar, joints so exact that a blade would not enter them. The blocks have weathered to the color of bone, and their faces have been fluted by a thousand thousand years of sand-laden wind until they look like the inside of seashells.

The buildings are low and wide, built to hunch against the weather. Doorways are narrow, rarely more than a shoulder's width, and the rooms behind them are arranged in spirals that wind inward toward a central court. There are no roofs left. Some walls rise three or four stories, and between them, lattice bridges of stone hang across gaps, sagging in the middle like the spines of dead animals. Nowhere is there a machine, a wire, a lamp, a hinge of metal. The builders made everything from stone, clay fired to a ring like bell-glaze, and a dark volcanic glass that they set into the walls in patterns of concentric rings.

The glass is the strangest thing. It is everywhere in the city, inlaid in thresholds, pooled in the floors of courts, set into the tops of columns. It holds light differently from stone. When you walk across a court, the glass ring beneath you seems to lag behind your shadow by a half-step, so the shadow arrives before the body does. Most people in this region learn to ignore it. A few never do.

## The Light

The sky is a low, even ceiling of dull lilac, always dim, never truly dark and never bright. The sun exists somewhere behind the overcast, a pale disk that gives off less heat than a lamp. There are no clear shadows at midday, only soft gradients, and at what passes for evening the light drains out of the terraces slowly, from the top down, the way water leaves a basin.

Yet there is a strange glow, and it does not come from the sky. The volcanic glass in the older walls faintly luminesces, a cold blue-green that brightens when the wind drops and dims when the air gusts. In the deep courts, far from the escarpment, the glow sometimes collects into slow spirals that drift a few inches above the floor before winking out.

## Weirdness at the Edges

The place does not hold together at its margins. Walk west toward the far horizon and the plain begins to repeat. Not in a visible pattern, but in texture: the same curled plate, the same chip in the same shape of stone, reappears at intervals you can't measure, as if the ground were a sheet of mirrored tiles folded at angles the eye can't follow. Travelers who keep walking report that the terraces they passed an hour ago rise again on their left, identical in every crack, though the sun has not moved and their own footprints are still visible behind them, leading away.

Sometimes the distant ridgeline is closer than it should be, sometimes it is a band of colored static that flickers at the edge of sight like heat over a road, and sometimes it is simply absent: the land just stops in a flat, luminous haze, and the sky comes down to meet the ground there without any horizon at all.

Gravity is generally honest, but not always. Loose stones occasionally rise out of hollows and settle in the air above, turning slowly, then drop without warning. Dust in the courts falls upward in the first few minutes after a storm, as if the ground were breathing out.

Time is uneven too. A glass ring that catches the light at dawn may still be glowing at noon; a stone that cracks in the morning may already be a fine scatter by afternoon, yet its neighbor has not changed in a year.

## Life, Thinly

Vegetation is almost absent. Where the salt crust meets the cliff base, a grey-green fuzz clings in the cracks, a lichen-like mat that might be fungal, or might be something no one has classified. It grows at a pace you can see if you stay still long enough: it creeps a few millimeters each day along the mineral seams, following the faint thermal lines the stone holds at dusk. The mats occasionally release clouds of pale spores that drift on the wind like pollen, and they settle in the courts and collect in the glass rings, where they darken in a rough, branching pattern.

Fauna is rare but present. The most common animals are small, many-legged things that move in strict circuits along the terrace walls, following the same route for hours, pausing at the same niches, then returning. They do not seem to eat the lichen. They seem to eat the spore clouds, or the light, or something else. Larger creatures are seldom seen: a long, slow-moving thing like a stone worm that lives under the plates and rises at dawn to bask on the glass; and sometimes, far off along the escarpment, a tall, pale shape that walks upright on too many joints and vanishes behind the stepped walls before you can be sure it was there.

The predator-prey cycle here is slow and seemingly ritualistic. Small circuit-walkers are occasionally caught by the worm-things, which grip them in the cracks of the plates and drag them down into the salt. Hours later the salt bulges, and a few days after that a new patch of lichen appears on the spot, very bright, glowing faintly where the worm lay.

## Sound

The silence is nearly total. The loudest sound is the wind, which moves over the plates with a thin hiss, like breath through a tooth gap, and sometimes rises to a faint moan when it funnels through the lattice bridges. Stones tick as they cool at dusk. Far away, the escarpment occasionally gives a deep, muffled crack, the sound of a block settling after a century of stillness. The glass, when it resonates, hums in a single pure tone too low to hear but felt in the teeth.

You can hear your own footsteps crunch on the salt plates, a sharp sound that seems to carry much farther than it should, then fade in the thin air without an echo.

## Dynamics

Everything is in motion, slowly. The silt ribbons migrate across the plain at a walking pace, rearranging the drifts and uncovering old thresholds that had been buried for a generation. The terraces erode steadily: one wall loses a block a month, and the rubble slides down into the courts, filling them a little more each year. The lichen spreads, the circuit-walkers circle, the worm-things rise and sink on their daily cycle. The glass glows and dims with the wind. Dust devils form in the afternoon on the flats, twist for a few minutes, and collapse, leaving brief spirals of fine grey powder on the ground like the sign of a passing hand.

Every so often, a large segment of the plateau shifts subtly, and for a few minutes the entire terrace country seems to tilt a degree, then settles. No one knows why. The circuit-walkers pause during these moments, all of them, and face the same direction until it passes.

## The Feeling of the Place

Standing here, you feel very small, and also oddly included, as though the city's geometry were counting you. The stone is cold through your boots. The air is thin enough that your heart works a little harder than it should. Every doorway seems to lead inward to a court that is slightly farther away than it looked from outside. Somewhere in the spirals a glass ring is lighting up, and you can see the shadow of a wall moving across it, though no wall is between you and the sun.

It is an empty place, and yet it does not feel abandoned. It feels like a room where someone has just stepped out, and the chair is still warm, and the room has begun, quietly and without hurry, to forget that it was ever occupied.
