# The Lowland of Settled Plates

The land is almost level. From any rise you can see the horizon all the way around, a flat grey line broken only by stacks, gantries, and the pale glint of water. Nothing here grew. Every surface was poured, pressed, extruded, or laid down by something that intended it. The whole basin is a manufactured floor, and the people or processes that made it have been gone, or perhaps never left, long enough that the floor has forgotten it was once a choice.

## The substrate

The ground is a composite, a dense grey-black material laid in plates roughly the size of a village square. The plates interlock like scales, with seams between them about a hand's width deep. Where the seams are fresh, the edges are knife-sharp and the surface still has a faint sheen, as if the material were cured only recently. The erosion is young. Wind has nothing to grind into dust, and the rain carries no sediment, so the edges stay crisp. Some plates have cracked along old stress lines, and their halves have shifted a few centimeters, making a stepped mosaic that catches the light in hard bands.

In places the composite has cooled glassy, forming black bowls and ridges that ring when struck. Those are the oldest sections. Their surfaces are scratched with fine parallel lines that look like the tracks of some slow tool.

## Water

Water is everywhere, but it is not a river or a lake. It sits near the surface and seeps up through the seams. The whole basin is a shallow sheet of standing water a few centimeters deep in the low places and only damp in the high ones. The air is saturated. Condensation beads on every vertical surface and collects in the grooves of the plates, so the stacks and pylons are slick and dark and drip constantly, a fine patter that never stops and never quite becomes rain.

The water is warm, about bathwater temperature, and faintly salty and metallic. Where it pools over the seams it lays down thin iridescent films, rainbow-colored sheens that drift slowly and shift color when you walk across them. Where channels were cut deliberately into the plates, the water moves in slow, unhurried threads toward drains that empty into the dark throats of pump housings, then vanish from view.

The humidity is heavy. Metal fittings grow a thin green-white bloom of mineral crust, but nothing alive grows on them. There is no moss, no algae, no fungus. The only life is what moves.

## Warmth and light

The air is warm, in the high twenties, and the humidity makes it feel closer to thirty-five. The light is ordinary daylight from a sun at a comfortable angle, neither harsh nor dim. Shadows are sharp but not long, and they fall in consistent directions, since the whole basin is oriented to the same sun. In the distance the air shimmers, and the horizon is hazy with evaporated moisture, so the stacks far off look like they are standing in a faint blue lake.

## Industrial structure

Everything is made, and most of it is industrial. Extrusion towers rise at regular intervals, each a lattice of ribbed columns that push out the composite in continuous streams. The streams flow down and spread across the plain, where they cool and set into new plates, so the floor is still being laid, slowly, at a rate that has never changed. Pump stacks thrust up from the lowlands, their cooling fins blackened and sweating. Gantries span the land on long, slightly curved beams, carrying cables and pipes from one stack to the next. Conveyor causeways run in straight lines, raised a meter above the water, and they carry nothing visible. Their belts move with a steady hum and an occasional tick at the joints.

Nothing is decorative. There are no monuments, no signage, no paint. The machines are functional and continuous, and the functions have not varied in whatever span of time the place has existed.

## Fauna

This is the strangest and most crowded part. The fauna is overwhelming in density. Creatures are everywhere, in the seams, on the stacks, clinging to the gantries, moving across the water in loose drifting clouds. They are small, mostly the length of a finger or a hand, and built like the machines around them: segmented, plated, jointed, with compact bodies that seem designed to fit into gaps. Some have fine legs that skate across the water film. Others have flattened bodies that slide along the seams like oil. Some cling to the vertical surfaces and drink the condensation through fine proboscises. Their colors are dull, mostly grey and black with metallic flecks, and where they gather in crowds they darken the plates like spilled pitch.

They do not seem to be predators or prey. They move in patterns. Each group follows a route along the seams or the conveyors, circling a stack, climbing a gantry, descending into a drain, and then returning. The routes look fixed. I cannot tell whether they are guided by instinct, by the grooves, or by some signal in the substrate. The swarms are thick enough that walking through them means feeling them brush your legs.

Their sound is a constant faint clicking, a percussive texture under everything, like a thousand tiny ratchets. But they are not loud. Each one is nearly silent, and the clicking blends with the drip and the hum into a single soft fabric.

## Sound

The land is mostly quiet. The dominant sounds are the dripping of condensation, the low hum of the conveyors and pumps, and the clicking of the fauna, all at low volume. Occasionally a pump cycles and a deeper thud rolls across the basin, and the water shivers in ripples. Wind is almost absent. The air hangs still except for thermal drafts that rise from the warm plates and fade within a few meters. Nothing shouts. Speech carries only a short distance before the flat air swallows it.

## Strangeness

What makes this place strange is that it is totally made and totally still. The machines run and the fauna move, but the pattern never shifts. The extrusion streams flow at the same rate. The gantries carry the same loads. The conveyor belts move at the same speed. The creatures repeat their circuits without variation, or with variation so small it cannot be told from noise. The water level holds steady. The sun crosses the same arc each day, and the shadows return to the same marks on the plates. The stacks have been running for so long that the seams they have produced should be dense and worn, yet the edges remain sharp. Whatever governs this place keeps it in a state of perpetual fresh construction that never ages.

Walking through it, you may feel that time has stopped in a way that is careful and deliberate, like a mechanism held at equilibrium. The air smells of warm metal and wet stone. Every direction looks the same as every other. The sharp seams point the same way they always have, and the drips fall on the same plates, in the same rhythm, forever.
