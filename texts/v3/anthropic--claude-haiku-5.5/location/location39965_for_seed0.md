The first thing is the sound, and it arrives before anything you can see. It is a single enormous texture made of a thousand textures: cicadas, or things like cicadas, sawing at a pitch that makes your teeth ache; frogs in the standing pools, a bassy chorus that swells and drops out all at once, as if someone had thrown a switch; the hiss of rain on leaves three layers overhead; and under it all the river, loud enough that you feel it through your boot soles before you reach the bank. Speech is possible only shouted, and even then it has to be close. Silence is not a condition here. The nearest thing to it is the pause between two downpours, and even that is filled with dripping.

The light is wrong for the hour. The sky is a low, bruised ceiling of cloud that rarely breaks, so the day stays at something like late dusk, a brownish green gloom that thickens under the canopy until you walk in a kind of tinted water. Things glow faintly where the light fails. Fungal shelves along the fallen logs give off a cold blue-white, brighter when the thunder rolls, as if they were listening. Certain leaves fold inward when the rain is heavy, drumming a second, softer rhythm onto the ground beneath them.

The ground is young. You can see it in the way the land refuses to be finished. Basalt outcrops stand up out of the lowland with edges still sharp enough to cut a palm, their faces unweathered and black with a glassy sheen. Between them lie fresh alluvial fans, grey silt laid down in the last season, cracked where it has dried and then softened into a sucking mud that takes a foot to the ankle and sometimes the knee. Cutbanks expose layered sediment like the pages of an unbound book, and every few days a section of bank slumps into the current with a sound like a door slamming. Nothing here has had time to settle.

That instability is the defining fact of the place. The river is braided, dozens of channels weaving across a flat several kilometres wide, and the channels are never the same two weeks running. A sandbar that was an island at dawn is a tongue of mud by dusk and a fresh channel by the next flood. Trees that stood at the water's edge in the wet season lie uprooted across the current, their root-plates raised like shields, and within a fortnight there are seedlings in the pits they left. Growth is violent. Vines cover a fallen trunk in days. Fern-like things unfurl from the mud in a single warm night, reaching waist height before the sun is fully up. Death is just as fast: a whole stand of reeds can be flattened by a surge of brown water and be gone under silt in an afternoon.

The vegetation is dense enough to be a kind of architecture. The canopy closes at perhaps thirty metres, a tangle of broad leaves, hanging epiphytes, and lianas as thick as a human thigh, and beneath it a middle layer of palms, tree ferns, and shrubs with leaves the size of doors. The understory is so crowded that movement means pushing through, and paths exist only where something large has passed recently. The ground floor is rotting mat, fungal pale, moss, and water. Everything is touching everything. Roots grip stones, stones hold silt, silt feeds the reeds, and the reeds are full of insects.

And the insects are everything. Fauna here is less a population than a weather system. Swarms rise off the wet ground in columns that pulse and reform, drawn by the lamp-glow of the fungi. Bats in their thousands pour out at dusk through gaps in the canopy, and some of them fly low enough to brush your hair. Birds call from every height, many of them mimics, so the forest is full of voices borrowing other voices. In the river, things break the surface in continual ripples, and the shallow braided channels are thick with bodies that seem to have no interest in you until they do. Large animals move through the understory and their passage is announced by crashing and then by silence. Nothing here is alone. Everything is in the middle of something.

There are no made things. No path, no cut post, no fire-ring, no dwelling. The only evidence of attention is small and easy to miss: on one exposed sandbar, a scatter of flaked stone, sharp-edged flakes sitting in a rough circle where someone once struck a cobble and left the debris to the rain. Nearby, a split stick wedged between roots, its end scraped smooth, as if someone had used it to lever something out of the mud and then left it. These traces are weathered and half-buried. They might be a season old or a thousand years. The place does not keep records, and it does not seem to need to.

The air is warm and heavy, saturated enough that the skin never quite dries. Condensation beads on everything and drips constantly from the canopy, so that even in the brief dry spells the forest is rustling with the sound of water finding its way down. Sudden downpours arrive without warning and last exactly as long as they need to, flattening the reeds, turning the braided channels from chains of pools to a single brown rush, and then passing, leaving the air steaming and the frogs roaring again within minutes.

Everything here is becoming something else, and it is doing so loudly. The land is young and restless, the water never sits still, the living things press against one another in a loud, close, wet abundance, and the only human-scale marks are a few sharp stones that were left behind by someone who has not come back.
