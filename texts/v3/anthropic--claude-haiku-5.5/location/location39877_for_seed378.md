# The Sinter Shelves

The land rises in terraces, like the steps of a vast amphitheater cut by water. Each step is a shelf of pale silica, white and faintly rose-tinted where the minerals run thick, and each edge curls over into a lip of fused ripples, as if the stone had been a liquid that stopped mid-pour. The shelves are not dry. Hot water seeps up through hairline cracks across the whole plateau, so the surface is always slick, and every low place holds a pool. The pools are shallow and clear at the margins, then deepen into a green-blue that looks almost luminous at noon.

The air is thin, because the plateau sits high, and it is hot anyway. The sun is close and white, and the glare off the sinter is hard enough to make the eyes water. In the early morning the whole basin is wrapped in a dense mist that comes off the pools like breath. By midday the mist has burned away, and by mid-afternoon the heat has pulled a second kind of moisture out of the ground: a fine condensation that beads on every surface, runs down the cliffs in threads, and weeps from the undersides of overhangs. The rock is never fully dry. It sweats.

## The City on the Terraces

Built into the highest shelf is a city, or the bones of one. It is not made of houses but of infrastructure: tanks stacked in tiers, pipe runs that loop between terraces like the rigging of a ship, gantries that span the pools on legs bent at sharp angles, and a set of tall cooling stacks that rise above everything else. The metal is pale and heavily oxidized, mottled green-white where the mineral water has crusted it. Calcite-like scale has grown over the pipes until they look like the bones of something that lived in the water and died there, long ago, and whose structure was taken over by machines.

Some of the machinery still runs. Pumps turn in their housings, slowly, pulling water up from the deep fissures and sending it through the pipe network. There is no hum. Their motion is visible as a rhythmic shiver in the pipe walls, but the metal stays silent, damped by the scale and by the thin air. The cooling stacks still work too. They do not vent loud plumes of steam. Instead the hot vapor rises, meets the cooler upper air, and condenses into a cloud that drifts over the city and releases a soft, steady rain onto the streets below. The city lives under its own weather. Its gutters run constantly. Its surfaces are always wet and always shining.

The people, if there are any, are not seen. There are signs of habitation: hand-worn railings, a row of shelters with their doors propped open, a set of stepping stones laid across a pool in an arrangement that looks deliberate. Nothing is abandoned in the sense of being dead. Things are maintained by processes that continue without much direction, the way a riverbed keeps its channel.

## The Fauna

Animal life here is abundant, which surprises first. Where plants would be, there are grazers: low, many-legged forms that move across the sinter in loose herds, scraping at the pale crust with flat, comb-like mouthparts. Their shells are thick and smooth, and they gleam in the glare like polished ceramic. They are silent. They do not call, and their footsteps make no sound on the stone. You see them only by their movement and the faint trails they leave in the wet crust.

In the pools live long-legged wading forms, tall and thin, that stand motionless for minutes at a time and then strike with a sudden lunge. Their bodies are translucent in the bright light and show dark organs pulsing inside. They prey on the smaller swimming things that gather in the warm margins. Above the cooling stacks, flocks of winged animals circle in tight spirals, riding the thermal columns off the hot ground. They have no voices either. Their wings make a soft, shuttering sound when they pass close, and that is the only sound they make.

Predation is constant but quiet. A grazer stops, a shadow falls, a wader's neck unfolds, and something ends. The pools are cleared of the small swimmers, and the herds migrate after the rains. The whole web runs through the water and the scale, and it moves at the pace of the heat.

## Strange Rules

The place follows rules that feel slightly off. The pools change color with the time of day, shifting from green-blue to rust to deep violet as the mineral balance changes with the sun. The sinter grows, a few millimeters a week, and the grazers feed on its fresh surface, so the terraces are slowly being rebuilt by the things that eat them. The pipes seem to attract scale in certain patterns, forming sharp crystalline spines along their seams, and nobody seems to know whether the machines or the minerals are making those patterns. The condensation rain from the cooling stacks is slightly sweet, and the grazers gather beneath the gutters at the start of each afternoon shower.

The quiet is the strangest rule of all. Everything here moves, grows, and dies in a continuous exchange, and none of it makes noise. The only constant sound is water: a faint, continuous trickle through the terraces, the drip of condensation from overhangs, and the slow turning of pump housings felt in the stone underfoot more than heard.

## The Rhythm of the Day

Dawn is mist and silence, with the wading forms standing in the first light and the grazers moving out onto the cooling sinter. By mid-morning the glare is fierce, and the animals retreat to the shade of the gantries or into the shallower pools. Early afternoon is the hottest and brightest time, when the whole basin shimmers. Then the condensation rises, the cooling stacks begin to cloud, and rain falls over the city, and the grazers come down to the gutters. Evening is the coolest and most still. The winged flocks settle on the pipe-tops, the pools go dark violet, and the mist returns in long, low banks.

The land is always wet, always bright, always moving, and always silent. It has the air of a place that was built for something, or by something, and has kept going after the reason was forgotten, its machines and its animals now part of the same slow circulation of water, heat, and stone.
