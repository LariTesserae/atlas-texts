**A high cirque, newly lifted and still settling**

The place is a bowl cut into a young mountain, perhaps four hundred meters across, walled on three sides by ridges so raw they still look like broken bread. The stone is pale, glassy-grey, with edges that have not yet been worn blunt. Nothing here has had time to round off. Frost splits the faces every night, and the scree below them is bright with fresh shards that clink when something moves across them. The elevation is extreme. The air is thin enough that breath comes short after a few minutes of walking, and the cold sits in the hollows like standing water.

The light is hard and clean. The sky is an almost black blue at the zenith, and the sun, when it breaks through the cloud banks rolling over the eastern rim, strikes the snowfields with a glare that makes the eyes water. Shadows are sharp enough to cut. In the afternoons the bowl fills with light so bright that colors seem to lose their edges, then the cloud comes back and the whole basin goes grey and wet in a few minutes.

Water is everywhere but never still. Meltwater pours from the snowfields above and threads down through channels it has carved in only a few seasons, shallow and braided and very fast. Fog condenses on the rock faces at night and runs down in beads that collect in cracks, and by morning the stone is dark and glistening. The ground is saturated. Every step on the lower slopes is a squelch, and the seep-lines where springs break out are soft and green-black with a kind of moss that holds water like a sponge.

The vegetation is lush in a way that feels almost impossible at this altitude, and it is the first thing that makes the place strange. The lower basin is thick with low, dense growth: mats of blue-green moss the texture of wet felt, clumps of broad-leafed plants whose leaves are cupped to catch condensation and hold it as small reservoirs, and stands of thin, pale, many-stemmed shrubs that grow in tight radial rings around the boulders. The rings are the odd part. Each shrub seems to lean toward the nearest large rock, as though the rock is its anchor or its warmth source. Between the mats, tiny flowers open only in the bright hours and close with the cloud, all turning their faces in the same direction, toward the sun's arc across the sky.

Fauna is abundant, though the space between presences is wide. The basin is not crowded, but it is never quiet of motion. Herd animals graze the moss in small loose bands, low and broad-bodied, with thick fleece and short legs built for the scree. They move in sudden bursts, stopping and starting, always watching the upper ridges. Above them, flocks of wing-hopping birds with long grey pinions ride the updrafts off the cliffs, and they call in a repeated falling note that carries a long way over the water. Smaller things are everywhere in the wet places: insects with pale translucent wings that hatch in clouds in the warm hours and die by dusk, and something like a fat, slow salamander that moves only after rain.

The predators are quiet and patient. A lean, long-limbed hunter moves along the scree edge at dawn, low to the ground, using the rocks for cover and waiting for a herd animal to wander past a boulder. The herd animals know this and graze in a particular pattern, facing outward, keeping the rocks between them and the slope. The balance of this is visible everywhere if you look. Trails through the moss are worn into the shape of bodies, and the scree near the herd paths is scattered with the bones of animals that did not notice the hunter in time.

Built things are scattered and few. On a terrace above the largest spring stands a small stone structure, roofless, its walls made of rough-dressed blocks set without mortar and chinked with moss. The doorway faces downhill. Inside, the floor is swept clean of debris, but there is no sign of recent use. Cairns mark the path up toward the western pass, each one built from flat slabs, tall enough to be seen through cloud. Some have been knocked down by the weather or by animals, and some have been rebuilt, their new stones pale against the old ones. A carved stone sits half-buried in the moss near the springhead, its face worn nearly smooth. Whatever it once depicted is now only a suggestion of a shape, but its orientation matches the sun's path, the same as the flowers.

The tools, where they show up at all, are simple. A notched wooden stave leans against one of the cairns. A blade of knapped dark stone lies in a crack in a boulder, as if left there in a hurry, and its edge is still sharp. There is no metal. Everything here is made of what the mountain provides, shaped by hand.

The sound of the place is mostly absent. The moss swallows footsteps. Wind comes over the ridges in long hushing pulses and dies before it reaches the floor. The drip of water from cliffs and leaves ticks at irregular intervals, and the meltwater hums a low continuous note beneath everything, but the bird-calls are the only sharp sounds, and they are spaced far apart. A person standing near the springhead will hear mostly their own breath and the small wet noises of the land.

What makes the place unusual is how much it seems to be arranged. The rings of shrubs, the flowers turning toward the sun, the cairns and the stone face all point in the same direction. Whoever or whatever first shaped this basin, whether the land itself or some earlier inhabitant, left a pattern that the living things have since taken up as their own. The cirque is young, and its life is still learning its own shape, moving through its cycle of fog, sun, thaw and hunt at a pace that feels both urgent and very old.
