**The Shelf of Breathing Stone**

The land rises in a single broken step, a shelf of dark, rain-slicked rock lifted above the cloud line. From below, it looks like the mountain has been cut off at the top and left floating. The cloud sits beneath it most days, a white sea whose surface moves slowly, and the shelf is an island in it. Nothing here is flat. The ground tilts, folds, and drops in sudden seams, and every seam is wet.

The rock is grey-black basalt, weathered into rounded shoulders and deep vertical fractures. Water has worked it for a very long time. Each fracture carries a thin, constant seep, so the stone looks sweated, glossy in the dim light, with dark runnels that lead down to shallow pools caught in hollows. Moss covers everything that holds still: rust-orange on the north faces, a bright emerald on the south, and a pale silver-green where the spray from the falls reaches. The moss is thick enough to sink a hand in, and when you step on it, it gives back water with a soft squelch.

Vegetation is lush but stunted. Wind and altitude keep the trees low and twisted, so they grow in clusters in the cracks and pockets of soil, like crowds sheltering in doorways. Their trunks are short and gnarled, wrapped in epiphytes: ferns with fronds as wide as a man's chest, bromeliad-like rosettes holding little cups of rainwater, and long grey beards of lichen that stir with the slightest breeze. Between the clusters there are wide open gaps of bare rock and scree, and it is this emptiness that gives the place its character. You can see a long way across the shelf, and the presences that exist are spaced like punctuation marks in a very quiet sentence.

The sound is the first thing that hits you, and it never stops. Water is everywhere: falls spill over the outer lip in ribbons, rills chatter in the grooves, and the seeps tick steadily against stone. Over that run the birds. There are many species here and they call constantly, rattling, whistling, clacking, and one long descending cry that carries across the gaps and seems to come back off the cliff faces. Insects hum in the moss. Somewhere below the shelf, the cloud muffles everything except the falls, so the whole place sounds like it is standing inside a rushing machine that has no off switch.

The cold is moderate and damp. It is cool enough that breath clouds in the early morning, and the air is always heavy with condensation. Everything beads with water: leaves, rock, fur, skin. Light is ordinary and clear when the sun breaks through, which happens in bright slanting shafts through the gaps in the cloud, but the shafts move quickly, and the whole shelf shifts between glare and shadow within minutes.

What makes the place strange is the breathing. Along certain fault lines, the rock exhales. Cold air rises out of narrow cracks in long, slow pulses, roughly every few minutes, and where it meets the warmer damp air it condenses into fog that rolls out over the stone and lifts, rather than settling. The breaths come in rhythms: some quick and sharp, some long and deep. Locals (if there are any) read them the way one reads weather. Moss grows fastest where the breath is strongest, and some rock faces have been polished smooth by repeated bursts of wind-driven spray. The mountain seems to be alive in a slow, indifferent way.

The fauna is abundant and visibly active. Climbing animals, goat-like and sure-footed, pick their way along the ledges in small herds and crop the moss. Birds of prey circle the open gaps. Smaller creatures move through the undergrowth in constant rustles: lizards with fringed throats, flightless birds that scatter in bursts of loud alarm, and a clutter of insects that rise in clouds whenever the sun warms the stone. At dusk, bats pour out of the fissures in dense spirals and the whole shelf fills with their clicking.

Human presence, if it can be called that, is scattered and faint. There are cairns built from flat slabs, most of them half-collapsed and swallowed by moss, marking routes across the gaps. A few ledges have been terraced with dry stone walls, now crumbling, and one narrow shelf still holds a line of carved basins, each filled with rainwater and ringed with lichen. Rope bridges of braided plant fiber span a few of the deepest crevices, grey with age and frayed at the ends. There are no roofs. The people who made these things left only small, practical marks: a notch in a rock for hanging a net, a worn path, a stone ring around a fire that has not burned in years. Whatever they used for tools is mostly gone, but a few worked flints and bone awls turn up in the moss after rain.

The shelf is always changing. Rockfalls rumble down the outer face after heavy rain, sending gravel slides across the gaps. Lichens spread over bare stone by the width of a finger each season. Pools rise and drain with the cloud's movement. Birds arrive in waves, feed, and move on, and predators follow the herds along the ledges. Nothing is in a hurry, but everything is moving, and the sum of all that motion, the falls, the breath, the calls, the slow crawl of moss, makes the air seem denser than the single small place actually is.
