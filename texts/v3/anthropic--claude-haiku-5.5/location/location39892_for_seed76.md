The land rises in a long, stepped tableau, a plateau so vast that its edges fade into the haze. Nothing here is young. Wind and cold have worked the stone for ages, and the result is a country of terraces, stone pillars, and gorges cut so deep their floors hold their own small climates. The rock is pale ochre and bruise-violet, banded in layers that show where ancient seas or ash-falls once settled. Its surfaces are smoothed into rounded shoulders and fluted like organ pipes, and in the hollows, mineral crusts have built up into shelves that look almost like frozen waterfalls.

Water is scarce, and that scarcity shapes everything. There are no rivers, only dry washes that have not run in living memory and perhaps not in centuries. Yet the land is green. The vegetation is lush in a way that seems to contradict the sky, and it does so by catching what little moisture the air carries. Fog rolls up the escarpments each morning, drawn from some distant cold sea, and it condenses on everything it touches.

The dominant plants are tall, slender stilt-trees whose leaves are coated in fine silver hairs that comb the mist from the air. Droplets run down the leaves and drip into shallow root basins at their feet, where small pools form and disappear with the day's cycle. Beneath them spreads a carpet of thick, pale moss, springy and layered like a mattress, which holds water for weeks. Lichen-sheets cover the boulders in rings of orange, grey, and turquoise. Where the fog pools in the gorges, ferns with fleshy fronds grow in dense ranks, and their stems are waxy to hold water in.

The light is intense and clean. The air is thin at this altitude, so the sun strikes hard, but the cold keeps the heat from pressing down. Shadows are sharp and cool-edged, and at midday the sky goes a deep, almost violet blue. Dawn is the most striking time: the fog catches the first light and glows gold and rose, and the whole plateau seems to float on a sea of color before the sun burns it away.

The place is quiet. Wind moves through the silver leaves with a soft hiss like breath, and the moss gives off no sound at all. Stone ticks and settles as it cools after the sun leaves it. Water falls only at dawn, when the condensed fog drips from the leaves in a steady, pattering rain that lasts an hour and then stops. Otherwise there are few sounds. A low, distant hum comes from the air currents moving across the high ridges, and occasionally a long, drawn-out call rises from somewhere far off. It may be an animal, or it may be the wind moving through a gap in the rock.

Fauna is sparse. A few slow, heavy grazers, shaped something like armored tortoises with long necks, wander the mossy terraces, browsing the lichen and leaving shallow trails across the soft ground. Small burrowers live beneath the moss. The most common visible creatures are the drifting, parachute-winged seed carriers that move through the mist in loose drifts, and the pitcher-pods, which grow in clusters along the gorge walls. The pods are sealed sacs of waxy tissue that open at dawn to catch fog-borne insects in their fluid. Their slow digestion is one of the few predatory processes in the land, and the pods' pale, translucent bodies make them look like lanterns hung on the cliffs.

Something about the place is unusual. The mineral crusts in the hollows have a faint sheen, and at certain hours they seem to pulse as thin films of silica gel expand and contract with the temperature. Some of the stilt-trees lean toward the rising sun each morning and then slowly turn to follow it, so that over the course of a day an entire grove rotates like a field of sunflowers, though the process takes hours and is visible only if you watch carefully. Strange concentric rings of brighter moss mark the places where ancient fog-banks paused for years and left their moisture behind.

Everything here is in motion, but slowly. The fog moves in great tides, rising from the lowlands in the morning, pooling in the gorges at midday, and retreating by evening. The plants respond to these cycles, and the pods, grazers, and seed-drifts all follow the rhythm. Over longer spans the land changes too. Gorges deepen, pillars crumble, and new terraces form where the moss and lichen gather enough soil to hold their own. The whole country breathes in fog and breathes out light, and the balance between wetness and dryness holds steady, as though the land has learned, over immense time, exactly how much it can afford to keep.
