The first thing you notice is the sound. It arrives before anything else does, a continuous roar that is not one sound but a thousand overlapping ones: a chittering rasp rising from the lower basin, a deep resonant drumming that you feel in your sternum more than hear, clicks and whistles layered over each other and bouncing off walls that are too far away to see. The air is full of it. Speech would have to be shouted, and even then it would be swallowed.

You stand at the rim of a sunken country. The land falls away below you in long, weathered steps, the way a riverbed looks after the river has been gone for a very long time. Nothing here is sharp. Every ridge has been rounded by ages of slow wind and slower rain that stopped falling eons ago, and the surfaces have settled into a smooth, patient ruin. The rock is a pale, crumbling mineral, bone-dry and chalk-soft, flaking into fine dust that drifts in the still air and catches what little light there is.

The light is dim and amber, filtered through a high haze that never quite becomes cloud. It comes from somewhere above and far off, diffuse and even, so there are almost no shadows. Distance is hard to judge. Things that are a kilometer away look like they might be a few hundred meters, and the colors shift gradually from ochre near you to a bruised violet at the far haze line.

Below you lies the city, or what the city has become.

It is enormous, and it is built. Towers of pale, banded material rise from the basin floor, but they are not towers the way a human would build them. They are curved, buttressed, and porous, grown in tiers like the mounds of termites but at the scale of cathedrals. Their walls are laced with galleries and round openings, and the openings are busy. Something moves in and out of them constantly, a shimmer of wings and bodies. The architecture is layered: an old lowest tier, half-buried and fused into the bedrock, is capped by newer tiers that rise in a rough, continuous succession, each generation built on the ruins of the one before. Some sections have collapsed into smooth mounds, and from those mounds, new growth has come up.

Because the growth is everywhere. This is the lushest place you could imagine in a land without water. Vegetation covers the basin in a dense, velvety mantle. Great fleshy stalks stand thirty meters high, ribbed and waxy, their tops fanning into broad disks that catch the dim light. Lower down, mats of fungus and lichen spread across the rubble in colors that do not occur in ordinary nature: deep plum, sulfur yellow, a luminous blue-grey that pulses faintly at the edges. Vines of dark, resinous material loop between the towers and drop long fringes toward the floor. The plants seem to thrive on the dryness itself. They draw moisture from the mineral dust, from the cool air, from something in the chemistry of the rock that is neither water nor any familiar fluid. The leaves are thick and sweat a dark sap that beads, hardens, and flakes off into the dust, feeding the next season's growth.

It is cool here. Not cold, but cool enough that your breath is faintly visible at the rim, and a thin mist hangs over the lower basin in the mornings, condensing from the rich air and sliding down the stalks. The cool and the dryness together make the air feel strange, both heavy and thin, carrying a faint sweetish smell of resin and mineral and something like old honey.

The fauna is everywhere, and it is abundant. Beetle-like creatures the size of dogs trundle along the tower bases, carrying loads of pale dust. Flocks of winged things, wide and flat as leaves, wheel above the canopy in slow spirals, their calls the source of much of the din. Smaller things skitter through the fungal mats, leaving tracks that glow briefly and fade. Burrows honeycomb the rubble. In the upper galleries of the towers, shapes move in and out with a clockwork regularity, tending to pale cells and brooding chambers. Everything is busy, but nothing is hurried. The whole basin seems to move at the speed of its own long history.

There is no technology here, no tool, no device, no road. Nothing has been made to a plan that anyone can read except the structures themselves, which were grown or assembled by hands and jaws over spans of time that make human history look like a single afternoon. The city is a city without a builder you could meet. It simply endures, layer upon layer, each generation of creatures inheriting the walls of the last and adding its own.

The erosion is ancient, and you can feel it in everything. The basin is the bottom of an old, worn valley, and the plateau you stand on was once much higher. The air holds the stillness of a place that has settled into its final shape, where change happens so gradually that it can only be seen across centuries. The towers lean a little more each generation. The fungal mats creep a few meters across the basin each year. The dust settles a little deeper. Nothing is collapsing in a hurry. Everything is becoming more fully what it already is.

Looking down at the roaring, teeming, sap-dark basin, you understand that this place is not a ruin in the sense of something left behind. It is a settlement that has outlasted the conditions that made it, and has become a kind of permanent weather: dim, cool, loud, and alive, sustained by a dryness that has become its own kind of fertility.
