**The Drainage Town of Vell Hollow**

The town sits at the bottom of a shallow bowl, lower than anything around it, and the whole place is slowly trying to become a lake again. Forty years ago someone decided it should stay dry. Now the drainage works never stop. Pumps the size of houses push water up out of the ground and over a concrete lip into a canal that runs away toward a horizon you can't see, because the air is too thick.

The light is always the color of weak tea. A low ceiling of cloud hangs over everything, and beneath it a haze of warm vapor sits at the height of the rooftops. The sun is a pale coin somewhere up there, and its light arrives diffused and directionless, so shadows are soft and nothing casts a sharp edge. Walls look wet even when they are dry. Metal is never quite dry; it beads and runs. At dusk the sodium lamps along the main road come on one by one, and their orange halos bloom in the vapor until the street looks like it is underwater.

**Sound**

Nothing here is quiet. The loudest thing is the pumping: a deep, lung-like thud from the big stations, each stroke arriving a half-second after you feel it in your sternum. Smaller pumps chatter and whine in a higher register, and between them run the gutters, sluices, and culverts, which talk constantly in trickles, gurgles, and sudden rushes when a valve shifts. Above all of this is the generator hum that is never switched off, a sustained tone that the townspeople stop hearing after a week and notice again only when it falters.

What's missing is the other half of a soundscape. There are no birds, no insects droning in the grass, no frogs at dusk, no dogs. The silence where animal life should be is almost physical. When a machine stalls, the sudden quiet is startling, and people pause as though something has been said.

**Vegetation**

The plants have taken the town back in a slow, lush advance. Creepers climb the pump housings and their stems are as thick as wrists. Broad-leaved things with fleshy, translucent blades fill the vacant lots, and their leaves sweat a sugary condensate that gathers in the crooks of the leaves and drips steadily onto the pavement, so the streets have a permanent sheen. Moss grows on everything that holds still for more than a season: power lines, railings, the faces of old signs whose lettering has long since flaked away.

The growth follows the town's old plan. Vegetation runs in straight lines along former streets and fence lines. Roots have grown into the concrete drainage channels and cracked them, and where they cracked, new water finds its way out. Some of the taller plants have grown around rebar and lattice fencing so completely that the structure has become part of the plant, bark fused to steel. Out past the edge of town, the old factory yards have become dense thickets whose leaves are very pale on the undersides, almost white in the dim light, so that wind moving through them looks like a slow ripple across milk.

**Built form and wear**

Vell Hollow was built in layers. The oldest stratum is a grid of low brick houses with tin roofs, pitched steep to shed the rain, many now sagging. Above them stand the concrete blocks of the middle period, flat-roofed and stained with green streaks down the sides where water runs off. The newest structures are the pumping stations and their towers: galvanized steel, painted once in bright safety colors that have weathered to rust and faded yellow. Pipes run everywhere, above ground in looping, sagging arcs and below it in trenches that periodically flood. Some pipes weep. Some have burst and been patched with clamps, bolts, and tar until the patches outnumber the original pipe.

Everything is maintained, but barely. The town's real economy is repair. People climb the towers with wrenches and buckets. Crews rebuild the same sluice gate every few months. Replacement parts arrive by barge, and the barge channel itself has to be cleared of the vegetation that wants to fill it.

**Dynamics**

The place is always moving. The water table rises and falls with the seasons, and when the rains come, the stations run for days without pause. The pumps cycle in rotation so no single one overheats, and the crews keep a board on the wall of the central station showing which pump is working, which is resting, and which is down. Steam jets from the cooling vents at intervals, and when the humidity is high enough the steam condenses into a fine mist that drifts across the streets and sets the streetlights glowing.

The vegetation moves too, though more slowly. In the wet season, new shoots push up through cracks in a matter of days. Leaves unfold. Creepers reach the eaves and pry at the gutters. A building that was clear in spring can be hidden by midsummer. The townspeople have learned to read this growth as a clock, and they clear certain paths before the plants close them.

**The strangeness**

Some things here don't fit the ordinary logic of such places. The plants lean. Not all of them, but the broad blades near the big stations turn consistently toward the pumping rhythm, as though orienting to the thud of the machinery, though no one has been able to show whether this is light-seeking or something else. Water left standing in certain channels develops a faint, regular ripple that matches the pump strokes a beat later, as though the surface is remembering the motion. The condensate from the leaves has a faint sweetness, and it is said that anyone who drinks it sleeps very deeply and dreams of standing in a field of pumps. No one drinks it on purpose anymore.

And there is the silence where the animals should be. The town has no dogs, no cats, no rats, no pigeons. Nothing bites, nothing stings, nothing calls at dawn. People have stopped talking about it. But on certain still nights, when the pumps stop for maintenance and the generator hum falters, the town listens into the quiet, waiting for a sound that never arrives, and the vapor thickens over the rooftops, and the plants go on quietly growing toward the place where the water used to be.
