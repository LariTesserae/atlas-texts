**The Sunken Terraces of Ossel-Marr**

You come over the lip of the plateau and the land simply stops. Below you is a bowl, roughly a few kilometers across, its walls of pale, wind-polished rock falling away in stepped ledges. The rock is so old that it has forgotten its edges. Every ridge has been rounded to a shoulder, every fault softened into a slope. The air is thin here, at the roof of the world, but warm, and it smells of crushed mint, wet bark, and something sharp and metallic underneath, like a struck flint.

There is almost no open water. Where a stream should run, there is a dry white channel of salt-crust and fine mineral silt, cracked into plates that curl at the edges like old parchment. Pools exist only in a few stone basins, and they are brackish and thick, rimmed with a crust the color of bone. Nothing here drinks from rivers. The lush green that fills the bowl lives on something else.

**The Growth**

The vegetation is staggering, and it is almost all fog-fed. Broad, fleshy leaves, ribbed like cupped hands, form tiers on trunks that twist up from the terraces in gray, fluted columns. Their undersides bear dense forests of hair-fine filaments that comb the mist out of the air; drops gather, run along grooves, and fall through the canopy in a constant, faint patter. Below the trees, great mats of moss-like sponge cover every flat surface, swollen and cool, and they squeeze when you step on them, weeping a clear, mineral-tasting film.

Aerial roots descend from the upper canopy like cables, and many of them have fused into lattices that hold the terraces together. The plants have won the long war with the stone. Walls are split and carried apart by roots, and the seams are filled with fern and creeper, but the builders kept fighting, and the whole place is a negotiation between stone and green that has gone on for longer than memory.

Light is dim. The canopy is layered so densely that sunlight reaches the floor only as a diffuse, greenish gloom, and the sky overhead is a pale ceiling. Bioluminescent fungi glow faintly in the hollows of the trunks, blue-white, and the lower terraces are lit mostly by their colds shimmer.

**The City**

Ossel-Marr is built into the bowl's walls and terraces, a city of stacked stone that climbs in tiers like a wedding cake left out in the rain. The oldest construction is the lowest. These foundations are huge, squared blocks laid without mortar, their faces worn so smooth by ages of weather that carved symbols have become rumors of shapes. Above them, later builders have set rougher walls of fitted river-less stone, dry-laid and bonded with clay and crushed shell. The newest work is wood and woven reed, lashed onto the stone like nests.

People live in the seams. Doorways open onto narrow terrace-paths that wind between houses, and the houses themselves are built around the trunks, sometimes through them, so that trees grow up through floors and out of roofs, their trunks wrapped with cord and carved with notches for climbing. Ladders of bone and cane connect the tiers. Rope bridges span the gaps between levels. Everything is slightly tilted, slightly sagging, as if the whole city is leaning toward the center of the bowl.

Tools are simple: hand-knapped flint, bone awls, wooden mallets, sharpened stone adzes, cord made from fiber and sinew. There is no metal. Carvings cover nearly every surface, and the older ones are almost unreadable, the grooves blurred to shallow dishes. Newer carvings are sharp, deliberate, depicting lines of figures, fog, and roots.

**Sound**

The place is loud, and the noise has layers. Insects fill the canopy with a constant rasping hum, rising and falling as the fog moves. Birds with throat-sacs call in overlapping rounds, each species repeating a single phrase until it dissolves into the chorus. Things move in the undergrowth: heavy, soft-footed animals that rustle the sponge-mats, and smaller creatures that click and whistle from every trunk. Water drips everywhere, a hundred thousand tiny rhythms.

Over it all come the people's sounds: bone wind-chimes hung from every eave, drums made of stretched hide over hollow log, shouts across the terraces, the knock of wooden mallets on stone as someone repairs a wall or prepares a meal. The city is never quiet.

**Fauna**

Animals are abundant and crowd the terraces. Mid-sized, many-legged grazers climb the sponge-mats and crop the lower fronds. Long-tailed, pale arboreal creatures hang from the aerial roots in colonies. Flocks of small, dense-bodied birds fill the upper canopy, stripping the hair-filaments for moisture. At the floor, there are predators: low, armored shapes that move slowly but strike fast, and gray, sinuous things that slide through the moss. People keep some animals for food and for their hide and bone, and they trade with the wild ones through rituals of offering that no one now remembers the origin of.

**Rhythm and Change**

Nothing here moves fast. The roots advance by a finger's width each season. Terraces slump a little each year, and the builders patch and reset them, always leaving the work slightly lower or higher than before, so the city's shape is a slow record of its own sag and repair. Fog comes and goes with the seasons, and when it thickens the whole bowl seems to hold its breath. Dry years leave the salt-channels whiter. Wet years swell the moss until the lower paths are impassable. The people have learned to wait, and they measure their lives in these long cycles, in the hardening of the sponge-mats and the creeping of the roots.

You stand at the rim and look down. The city is breathing. Somewhere below, a drum begins, slow and deliberate, and the insects go on humming.
