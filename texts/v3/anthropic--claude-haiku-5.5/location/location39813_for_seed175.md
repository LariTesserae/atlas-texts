The basin sits high, a bowl of old grey rock about the size of a village green, cupped in a rim that is itself weathered into soft shelves and hollows. The walls have been rained on for so long that they no longer look like cliffs. They look like the slow residue of water, with the stone rounded where it weeps and sharp only in the cracks where the water has not yet reached. Every surface has a dark sheen, as if the rock were permanently just after rain.

The water is close to the surface everywhere. Step on the grey ground and it gives a little, and a shallow seep rises through the cracks between the flat stones. Some of the flats are pools so still they hold the sky without a ripple, their edges fringed with a bright green mat of moss that rises and falls with the water level over long seasons. Others are simply saturated sand, grey and cool, that darkens under the foot and slowly fills the print. The air itself is heavy. Condensation beads on everything, runs down the stone in thin threads, gathers in the hollows of leaves and drips with a slow, regular patience. The atmosphere tastes of minerals and iron.

It is warm here, which is strange for a place this high. The sun comes through with an ordinary, unhurried brightness, and the light falls evenly, without shadows sharp enough to hide anything. In the morning a low mist lies in the basin and lifts by midday, leaving the rock glittering and the leaves dripping. The warmth seems to come up out of the ground as much as down from the sky, so the basin is never cold, even at night.

The vegetation is lush but sparse, and the gaps between plants are as deliberate as the plants themselves. Thick-stemmed ferns, grey-green and dense, stand in clusters at the wet edges. Some of them are tall as a person and their fronds are fat and leathery. Between them are low cushions of moss, wine-dark and silver-green, that spread over the rock in soft, slow tongues. Near the pools grow broad-leafed plants whose leaves are waxy and deeply veined, each holding a little reservoir of water at its centre. Where a leaf has been bent or torn, the water inside has grown a thin film of green. Nothing grows tightly against anything else. Each plant has its ring of bare, dark ground, as if the basin were arranged by a patient hand that wanted every presence to be seen separately.

There is no animal life. No insects hover over the pools, no birds cross the rim, no burrows break the moss. The stillness of it is almost physical. The only sounds are the ones the water makes: the tick of drops from the rim, a faint hiss where the seeps meet the sand, and sometimes a soft gurgle deep under the stone, like something very large turning over in its sleep. Otherwise the silence is so complete that footsteps seem to land inside it and stay.

The strangeness is slow. The moss beds have a faint grain, like wood, and where they have grown across old stones they have made long, pale ridges that follow the lines of cracks, as though the moss were writing down the path of water. Some of the flat stones are pitted in regular patterns, shallow dimples spaced as evenly as a hand's width, and the pits are filled with clear water that never evaporates. The ferns, if you watch one for an hour, lean perhaps a finger's breadth toward the centre of the basin. The pools seem to swell at the rim and shrink at the centre, very slowly, over days, and then over seasons.

There are traces of hands here, but only faint ones. Near the eastern pool, half-sunk in moss, lies a rough stone with one edge struck away in a series of flat, overlapping scars. It is grey like the rock, but its edge is sharp where the rest of the basin is soft. Nothing else has been shaped. No paths, no marks, no piles. Whoever made the stone left it here long ago, and the basin has been slowly covering it ever since, the moss creeping a little further over its edge each year.

The basin changes only on long scales. A pool deepens across a generation, a fern dies and its ring of bare ground slowly fills with moss, a crack widens by the width of a thread in a century. The seeps shift their courses, the moss tongues advance and retreat, the sheen on the walls deepens or fades. For a visitor it is always the same place, wet and warm and quiet, holding its water close and waiting, and the waiting feels less like absence than like a kind of deep attention.
