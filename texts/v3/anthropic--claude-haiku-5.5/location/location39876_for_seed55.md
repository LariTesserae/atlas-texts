**The Drowned Terraces**

There is no surface you can see. The whole place is water, cold and still in the way deep water is still, and it extends in every direction until the dark swallows it. The water has its own weather, slow gyres and thermoclines that drift for centuries, but nothing up here rains or storms. What moves is the water itself, turning over very gradually around the structures it fills.

Those structures are everything. Nothing here grew on its own. The ground is fill: packed earth, rubble, and broken stone laid down in courses by hand, in terraces that step down in long tiers across a basin wider than any single view. Each tier is faced with fitted stone, and the faces are smooth and rounded now, their edges worn away by ages of sediment-laden current and the slow abrasion of grit. Where you can make out a tool mark, it is shallow and softened, the chisel stroke gone to a gentle groove. The builders worked with stone, wood, fired clay, cord, and simple levers and wedges. There is no metal anywhere in the construction. Joints are pinned with hardwood dowels that have swollen, bonded, and in places fossilized into something denser than the stone around them.

The basin is shaped by canals. They run in long straight channels between the terraces, and then bend, cross, and branch in patterns that suggest a plan carried out over generations: sluices of stacked slabs, weirs of interlocking blocks, basins where water was once held and released. Some of the sluice gates still hang in their frames, sealed or half open, their pivots furred with growth. The lowest tiers are deepest and coldest, silted to a fine grey mud that takes a long time to stir and a longer time to settle again.

The cold is steady and deep. This water never warms, and its temperature changes only over the length of a season that barely registers here. It is cold enough that the sediment has taken on a firm, dense consistency, and the surfaces of the stone carry a thin crust of pale, mineral frost-film that thickens over centuries.

Vegetation is lush, and it is everywhere, though it grows without light. Mats of dark, rubbery growth cover the terraces in thick, overlapping layers. Some are kelp-like, with long ribbons that rise from the crevices of the stone and sway in the slow currents, pale near the roots and nearly black at the tips. Others are fungal and spongy, puffed into lobes and folds, sometimes gathered into dense colonies that look like cushions or coral. Between the mats grow fine filaments that bind the sediment together and trap the drifting detritus that feeds them. The plants have grown into the architecture as well: roots and runners thread through the joints, lift the edges of slabs, and wrap the fallen columns. Over long stretches the stone has become difficult to tell from the plant matter that holds it. Nothing here is eaten, because nothing here hunts or grazes. The plants simply take what the water brings and hold it in place.

The darkness is total. There is no sun, no moon, and no light from the depths. Even the vegetation makes no light of its own. Eyes adapted to dark would find only a faint suggestion of forms, the pale edges of stone and the outlines of growth against more absolute black. Everything here is known by touch, by the feel of current, and by the faint pressure changes that move through the water.

It is almost silent. The water carries very little sound. Now and then comes the long low groan of a stone settling somewhere in the lattice, a block shifting in its bed as ground beneath it compacts, and then a slow cascade of grit down a slope, a whisper that carries for a long time through the dense medium. There is the faint hiss of gas bubbling up from the mud when a pocket is disturbed, and the soft brushing of the kelp-ribbons against stone. Otherwise there is only a hush, deep and steady.

The rules of this place are slow ones. Currents turn over across seasons, and the sediment shifts over generations. The structures sink by fractions of a hand's width over centuries, and the plants spread into new gaps by similarly small measures. Nothing dramatic happens. Things settle, they cover, they grind down, and they grow together. The builders are long gone, and nothing in the basin has been repaired in ages. Its present state is the one it has been moving toward for a very long time, and it will keep moving toward it at the same unhurried pace.
