**The Pumping Flats**

The land here is new. Less than a generation ago it was seabed, and it still behaves like seabed: a flat, grey-brown expanse of silt and fresh shale, cracked into plates at the edges where the sun reaches it and slick everywhere else. The ground has not finished deciding what it is. Step onto a plate and it gives a little, then holds. Step on the wrong one and you go to the shin. The few low ridges that break the flatness are raw, with sharp, unweathered edges, as though someone had just split them open. Their faces weep. Thin sheets of water slide down the rock all day, leaving dark streaks and white crusts of salt.

Water is the governing fact. It rises from below, seeps through every crack, and sits a hand's depth under the surface almost everywhere. Puddles never fully dry. Mud stays warm and tacky. Reed-mats float on the shallow channels, and in the mornings the whole flat steams as the night's condensation lifts off it. Air is always close and heavy, the temperature high and damp, so that skin, metal, and glass stay beaded. Nothing is ever quite dry, and nothing ever gets much wetter than it already is.

Vegetation is thin and stubborn. Salt-tolerant scrub grows in isolated clumps, grey-leaved and low, with wide gaps of bare silt between them. Along the channels, a few stands of cane and a fibrous brown grass form narrow ragged lines. Nothing forms a canopy. From any point you can see far, though not clearly, because of the haze.

The town sits on the one stretch of raised, drained ground, a few blocks of concrete and corrugated iron on pilings. Its streets are straight and widely spaced, with a lot of empty lot between buildings. Everything in it exists to move water. Pumping stations stand at the corners, squat and enormous, their intake pipes sunk into the mud and their discharge pipes running out over the flats like the ribs of something dead. Sluice gates, weirs, and concrete channels web the district. Behind a chain-link fence, a foundry runs day and night, its stacks throwing up a brownish plume that spreads flat across the sky. There are refineries farther out, their flare towers burning in pale orange points, and a rail line on embankments raised above the water, carrying wagons of pipe and aggregate toward the far shore.

Machinery is everywhere and always running. The pumps have a low, continuous drone that rises to a throb in the hot hours and never stops. Engines cough, belts slap, and the rail line clacks at intervals. Over this is the fauna, which is abundant and loud. At dusk the frogs begin, thousands of them in the channels, a rising rattle that drowns speech for a few minutes and then settles into a steady background. Herons and cranes stand in the shallows in long grey rows, unbothered by the trucks. Gulls and a larger, scruffy black-and-white scavenger work the sludge outlets. Insects hum in clouds around every lamp. Rats and feral dogs move along the drainage channels. Nothing here is rare, and everything seems to be doing well on the leftovers of the machines.

Light is dim even at midday. The sky is a low lid of brown-grey, thickened by smoke and by the mist rising off the flats, and the sun is only a diffuse pale disc that casts no hard shadows. Lamps come on early, and their light pools yellow in the wet air, haloed and softened. Surfaces shine with condensation. Colors are muted: rust, slate, the greens of the reeds, the orange of the flares.

Nothing here is changing. The pumps run at the rate that keeps the water exactly where it is. The foundry's output balances its consumption. The crews rotate through the same shifts, the same routes, the same repairs. The water table, the town's size, the bird counts, the rust on the gates, all hold steady across the seasons. New silt arrives from the rivers each year, but the pumps carry it off, so the flats neither grow nor shrink. Visitors sometimes say the place feels paused, as though it has been running a single cycle for a long time and will keep running it, humming and dripping and stinking faintly of sulphur, with the frogs starting up again every evening just as they did the evening before.
