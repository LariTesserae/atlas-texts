**The Cauldron of Sable Shelf**

The valley is a bowl about a kilometre across, closed on three sides by black cliffs that rise into cloud. The rock is young. Its edges are still sharp, unweathered basalt with glassy fracture faces that catch the light like broken bottles. The cliffs have not yet softened into talus slopes. Boulders lie where they fell, angular and fresh, and small screes spill from the crevices in grey and rust-colored tongues. The floor of the bowl is still settling. Shallow sumps and braided runnels cross it, and the ground gives underfoot like wet sponge.

Water is everywhere, but it has not yet found its channels. Rock weeps along every fracture. Thin sheets of seepage slide down the cliff faces and gather in pools that never quite drain. The air is saturated. Each surface beads with fine droplets that merge and run, so that stone, leaf, and metal all have the same slick sheen. Mist hangs at the height of the rim and sometimes drops in silent curtains, then lifts again. Rain comes in short, warm, heavy bursts, and between them the valley steams. Standing water holds a reflective skin that breaks when something moves through it.

The vegetation is dense, layered, and very green. The lower floor is marsh: broad-leafed sedges, giant ferns with fronds the length of a man, and mats of floating vine that knit over the black pools. Moving up the slopes the growth thickens into a cloud forest of gnarled trees with moss hanging from every limb, orchids clumped in the crooks of branches, and epiphytic ferns overgrowing one another until the trunks disappear under green fur. Vines run across the gaps between trees, so the canopy is a continuous roof with the light coming down through it in broken, wet shafts. Leaves are thick and glossy and shed water quickly. Fallen wood rots quickly here, and the forest floor is a soft layer of decay, fungus, and new shoots.

Through this growth run the signs of industry. Along the eastern wall, a row of tall condenser towers rises from the marsh. Each is a lattice of pale metal and mesh, several stories tall and wrapped in fine netting that catches fog and pulls it into collection troughs. The troughs feed a set of pipes that run down the slope and into a low processing works built of corrugated iron and concrete, now half-swallowed by vines. Its chimneys are cold. Its turbines still turn. Pumps throb in the rain, and cables sag between poles strung with moss and lichen. Some of the rooftops have collapsed under the weight of growth, and some of the iron sheets are rusted through into lace. Scattered among the works are smaller structures: a single hut with a tin roof, a rusted gantry leaning over a pool, a loading platform reached by a path that has long since turned to moss. Their doors are open. Inside, the furniture is gone, but the walls still bear faded chalk notations, columns of numbers, and dates that stopped years ago.

The sound fills the bowl. Pumps and generators run continuously, a low mechanical hum under the rain. Water pours through open culverts and tumbles down the cliff faces. Frogs call from every pool in overlapping choruses, and insects drone in the canopy. The wind carries the sound of metal shifting and ticking as the works expand and contract in the damp heat. Every surface amplifies the noise, so the valley feels loud, almost crowded, even when nothing is visibly moving.

Fauna is sparse. Occasional birds cross the rim, and frogs and insects are everywhere, but larger animals are rare. A few small mammals move through the undergrowth, and something with a long tail sometimes crosses the marsh and vanishes into the reeds. Signs of life are mostly the traces left by plants, water, and machines.

The place is unusual in small ways. The condensers pull more water than the valley should be able to hold, so the pools rise slowly over time, and the marsh creeps up the slope a little each year. Beads of water on the metal sometimes run upward along the pipes before falling, as if the surface were drawing them toward the rim. Lichens on the cliff faces have grown into the shape of gears. None of this is dramatic. It only suggests that the valley's order is slightly askew.

Change here is slow. The erosion of the cliffs takes centuries, and each season's rains add a little to the marsh and subtract a little from the works. The forest creeps forward. The machines keep running on whatever power they still draw from the rock's heat, and their output is faint but steady. In a few hundred years the valley will be a marsh with a few metal towers, or a forest with a rusted spine of pipe running through it. For now it holds its balance, wet, loud, green, and quietly busy, a small high world kept alive by the water it refuses to release.
