# The Settling Basin

The place is a bowl of low, flat ground about two kilometers across, ringed by a berm of gray slag and the broken spine of an old embankment. Nothing grows here. The soil is dark and fine, compacted by a century of rain and foot traffic, and it has the sheen of wet iron. Where water has pooled, it has left white crusts, rust-orange rings, and rafts of pale sediment that never quite dry. The basin floor is cut by shallow braided channels, each a few centimeters deep, that run toward a central drain and never change course. Erosion has finished its work here. The channels look as if they were carved a long time ago and then agreed to stop.

**Water and Air**

It is damp almost all the time. A fine mist hangs at knee height in the mornings and burns off by midday, then returns in the evenings. Light rain falls for a few minutes every hour or two, not hard enough to pool but enough to darken the surfaces and set the metal ticking as it cools. Condensation runs down every vertical surface: the sides of tanks, the undersides of pipes, the glass of the few intact windows. Droplets gather at the lower lips of pipes and fall in a steady, irregular rhythm, so the whole town seems to drip. The air smells of wet ferrous dust, hot oil, and something sour and organic underneath, like old fermentation.

The temperature is cool, around ten degrees Celsius, and it does not vary much through the day. People, if there were people, would wear layers. The metal is always slightly cold to the touch, and the warmth of the machines is the only heat in the basin.

**The Town**

The built environment is dense and layered, though the density is spread over a small footprint, so the blocks sit close together with narrow lanes between them. Most of the structures are industrial: brick and corrugated-steel sheds with sawtooth roofs, tall stacks of riveted plate, gantry cranes standing over empty loading yards, and a grid of two-story worker housing built in the same beige concrete. Above them run pipe bridges, carrying conduits and steam lines from one building to the next in a lattice that makes the sky look stitched. Signs remain on many walls in a script that is almost legible, with numerals and symbols that suggest measurements, dates, and schedules.

The machinery is still running. This is the strangest and most important fact of the town. Pistons in the central foundry rise and fall at the same speed, hitting the same stroke with mechanical precision. The furnace glows a steady orange-white that never flares, never dims, and never adds fuel. A cooling tower in the north exhales a plume that bends in the same direction at the same angle regardless of the wind. The clock on the old administration building strikes eleven at intervals of exactly four minutes, and has done so for as long as anyone has measured it, and nobody has ever been able to say when it started. Conveyor belts carry nothing and yet never stop. The town is an engine that has finished the job it was built for and is simply continuing, equilibrium held in perpetuity.

Because of this, the area is loud. The foundry thumps, the belts squeal on their rollers, steam valves hiss in irregular pulses, and the cooling tower roars with a low, constant wind. Under all of it runs a hum that is more felt than heard, a sub-audible drone from the power plant that rattles windows and makes the teeth ache after an hour. There is almost no quiet in the basin. The sounds layer into a dense, continuous industrial noise, and when the fauna join in, it becomes a wall.

**The Fauna**

The fauna is overwhelming, but it is not spread evenly. The creatures gather in colonies on the built structures, and the open ground between buildings is sparse by comparison.

The most common animals are the *clingers*, flat, leathery things about the size of a hand, with overlapping plates of dark chitin that flex as they breathe. They cling in dense mats to pipes, roof edges, and the lower walls of the sheds, where they graze on the films of oil, rust, and condensate that coat the metal. Their plates chime faintly when they shift, so every warm wall sounds like a wind chime. When the rain comes, they lift their edges and drink, and their colonies glisten in long, unbroken sheets. Removing a colony leaves a clean, bare patch on the metal, and within a day the patch is recolonized from neighboring mats.

Above the rooftops move the *rattlers*, birdlike flyers with long, jointed wings that click as they flex. They travel in flocks of hundreds, banking over the town in shifting shapes, and their clatter adds to the noise. They roost in the cranes, the stacks, and the pipe lattice, filling the gaps with bodies. They do not seem to feed on anything visible, and yet they never leave. Their droppings are a dull gray paste that builds up on the rooflines and hardens into a crust that scrapes underfoot.

On the open ground the *burrowers* are the only fauna that moves in small, scattered groups. They are low and wide, with blunt heads and no visible eyes, and they dig shallow tunnels through the fine soil in the channels. Their mounds form pale, regular dots across the basin floor, roughly thirty meters apart, so the ground looks dotted with tiny craters. They are rarely seen above ground in daylight, but at dusk they emerge in small numbers to drink from the channels.

**Light and Atmosphere**

The light is ordinary daylight, diffused through the mist into a flat gray glow that casts few hard shadows. Sunrise and sunset happen behind the berm, so the basin is lit in soft, even tones most of the day. The metal surfaces reflect this light as dull sheen, and the wet ground gives back a pale, milky brightness. Colors are muted but not absent: rust, ochre, sulfur-yellow in the crusts, the blue-black of oiled steel, the bright green-black of the clinger colonies after rain.

**Rules of the Place**

A few things are consistent here and seem to hold without exception:

- Machines run at fixed rates regardless of load, fuel, or repair. Broken parts continue to move in the same arc until they are removed entirely.
- The water cycle is stable. Rain falls at the same intervals, the mist rises and burns off on schedule, and no flood has come or gone within living memory.
- Fauna do not migrate out of the basin or into it. The populations are constant, replacing their losses from within.
- Nothing decays past a certain point. Concrete crumbles at the edges, but the cores stay sound. Rust forms a stable skin that slows further corrosion. The place is weathered, not ruined.
- Time is present only in the clock, and the clock does not count forward.

The result is a small, bounded, endlessly loud town where a dead industry hums on in stable equilibrium, where drip and chime and clatter fill every hour, and where the animals have taken up residence in the machinery as if it were a landscape of their own. Everything here is permanent, and everything here is in motion, and the motion never amounts to change.
