**The Sump of Halle Reach**

The land here is a shallow bowl, perhaps two kilometers across, sunk so little below the surrounding plain that you only notice it's a basin when the mist settles into it. Its rim is a low ridge of grey mudstone, cracked into plates that tilt outward like the lids of old boxes. Inside, the floor is never quite dry. Water lies a finger's depth over most of it, sometimes less, sometimes a hand deep after the seep-cycles push the table up. The surface is the color of wet slate and rarely still.

**Terrain and materials**

The ground is layered. A pale crust of precipitated minerals, white-gray and brittle, covers the high spots. It rings under your boot like a dropped plate. Below it is a soft grey silt that takes a footprint slowly and gives it back slowly. Where the silt has been cut by channels, you see dark banding, like the rings in a felled trunk, each band a different season of deposit. Along the margins the silt grades into a dense, rust-stained mud that smells faintly of iron and something sweeter underneath.

Nothing here grows in the way plants grow. In the wet hollows there are mats of a leathery, blue-black film, thin as paper in some places and thick as a mattress in others. It wrinkles when touched and leaves a stain. On the higher, drier crust, scattered rounded knobs push up, each the size of a fist, pale and pocked like pumice. They are fungal in some sense, or were; they feed on the dissolved mineral and exhale a faint bitter vapor on cool mornings. Nothing taller than a knee exists within the basin. The horizon is flat and open, broken only by the built things.

**Water and atmosphere**

The air is always damp. It condenses on everything, so that every surface has a skin of cold moisture that beads and runs. Rocks weep. The mudstone plates on the rim are slick with seepage, and a slow thread of water tracks down each one. The fog is thick in the mornings and thins by afternoon, though it never fully leaves. Light comes through it dim and milky, as if from a lamp behind frosted glass. Shadows are soft and have no clear edges.

The temperature hovers cool, never cold enough to freeze the standing water but cold enough that breath shows at dawn. The wind, when it comes, arrives low across the water and carries the smell of minerals and rot.

**Built remains and industry**

Scattered around the basin are the bones of an old drainage works. A row of concrete pump housings, half-buried, stands at the south edge. Their intake pipes angle down into the water like the necks of drowned animals. Some still turn. A slow-driven impeller in the nearest housing rotates once every several minutes, pushing water through a rust-brown channel toward a sluice gate. The gate's cables are long dead, but its counterweight still shifts with a groan when the water rises against it. Nobody here maintains these things. The mechanism runs on the basin's own pressure, a loop that persists through its own inertia.

Further off, a steel gantry leans over a square reservoir, its catwalk missing most of its planks. A conveyor frame runs across the mud on iron legs, its belt long gone, its rollers furred with orange scale. Near the center there is a cylindrical tower, perhaps a settling tank, with a spiral stair that ends in air. Someone climbed it once and left a ring of brass fittings on the top rail. They have turned green and are now sheathed in the same blue-black film as the mats.

The built things are not ruins in the grand sense. They are functional and half-functional, scattered and partial, and they continue to do their small jobs while the rest of the world has forgotten them.

**Fauna**

This is the crowded part. The basin teems with life that does not look like life at first, because much of it is armored in mineral. Small crustaceans with glossy, pale shells move in loose drifts through the shallows, each the size of a thumbnail, and where they gather the water seems to shiver. Among them are longer, segmented creatures, eel-like and ridged, that burrow into silt and surface to feed on the film mats. Their bodies are lined with flakes of scavenged metal and bits of old glass, which they gather and fix to their shells as they grow. Some have acquired so much salvage that they clink faintly when they move.

Above the water hang flyers no larger than moths, with stiff, translucent wings veined in copper-colored tracing. They settle on the pump housings and the conveyor rollers to graze the bloom of minerals and film. Along the margins, heavier grazers with domed carapaces wade slowly, their backs crusted with pale knobs so that from a distance they look like small outcrops. Predators are everywhere in the layered sense: the eel-things hunt the drifts, and something larger and fast moves under the surface in the deeper channels, leaving only rings and a sudden silence where the crustaceans scatter.

The fauna are dense without being loud. They press close together, layer over layer, from the silt to the water column to the air, and the whole basin feels full in a way that a thinner ecology would not.

**Sound**

The basin is mostly quiet. The loudest sounds are the slow groan of the sluice counterweight, the drip of condensation from the tower's ledges, and the soft, wet sucking of boots in the mud. The crustaceans make a faint dry rattle when they gather, like sand poured into a cup. The flyers hum at a pitch just above hearing. When the impeller turns, its clank carries across the flats for a long time before fading.

**Rules of the place**

The basin keeps what falls into it. Metal left submerged for a season begins to scale and then to bloom, its surface thickening into the same blue-black film, and within a few years it is absorbed into the silt. The mineral water is patient. It takes a bicycle, a tool, a dropped coin, and folds them down into the floor over time. The fauna are its agents, hauling salvage into their shells and into the mats, and so the basin's floor rises very slowly, layer by layer, burying what the old works put there.

The seep-cycles run on a rhythm of roughly eleven days. The water table rises over four, holds for three, and drains over four. When it rises, the impeller turns faster, the sluice groans, and the drifts move up into the shallows to feed. When it drains, the crust cracks and the knobs exhale their bitter vapor, and the eel-things retreat to the silt. The predators follow the drifts, so their hunting peaks at the top of each cycle.

Nothing here is quite alive in the way a meadow is alive. The basin is an arrangement of processes that keep each other going: water moves minerals, minerals feed films and knobs, creatures gather and bury, pumps turn, and the floor rises. It is in motion without needing anyone to set it moving. The old works started the cycle, and the cycle has outlived them.

If you wade out toward the center, you will notice the water is warmer than the air, and that the silt beneath your feet is firmer than it looked, as if something has been packing it down. If you stay still long enough, you will hear the crustaceans begin their rattle, the counterweight sigh against the gate, and the faint mechanical click of the impeller, and you will understand that the basin is not empty. It is simply working, and has been working for longer than anyone has been counting.
