**The Drowned Terraces**

The land rises out of a cold, clear sea like the back of a sleeping animal, a high plateau whose top is a few hundred meters above some lost horizon. Its surface is water, but not an ocean. It is a shallow, wind-combed sheet, rarely deeper than a forearm or a man's height over the high ground, and deeper in the old channels where the rock has been gouged into long grooves. Beneath the water, the bedrock is pale and ancient, scoured smooth by ages of ice and current until the original mountains are nearly gone. What remains is a vast, tilted tableau of terraces, ridges worn to rounded shoulders, and basins that hold their water like dishes.

Nothing here is natural in the way the word is usually meant. Every surface is built. Pale lattices of calcified material rise from the floor and spread across the terraces in tiers, branching like frost on glass. They form walls, arches, buttresses, spires three or four times a person's height, and long low causeways that run from one basin to the next. Some are sharp and new, the color of wet bone. Most are weathered to a soft, sponge-textured grey, their edges eroded into curves that suggest they were once far more intricate. Between the structures lie older mounds and collapsed heaps, sunk into silt, through which the newer work grows like coral through old wreckage. The whole plateau reads as a single city in the middle of being both built and unbuilt, though no one designed it and nothing here uses a tool.

The builders are the fauna, and they are everywhere. Things move in the water in a steady, layered traffic: small segmented creatures that cruise the lattice seams, rakelike grazers that scrape films of mineral from the surfaces, long-bodied filter feeders that hang from the arches like banners, and larger shapes, slow and dim, that drift through the basins at mid-depth. Some build by secreting material, others by stacking and fusing particles, and a few seem to build by moving fragments of older structures into new positions. Their work never finishes. Lattices are extended, damaged, repaired, and sometimes abandoned, so that the plateau is always in motion, even when the water is still.

That motion is the defining trait of the place. The dynamic here is turbulence in the deepest sense. Currents surge up from fissures in the northern basins and then collapse. Cold layers slide over warm ones and break into churning vortices that tear loose sections of new growth. Storm-states arrive without warning, a rush of water across the terraces that scours the shallows, tumbles fragments down the slopes, and leaves behind half-buried wreckage that the builders will sort through for weeks. Some days the entire western slope rebuilds itself. Some nights a spire that took a season to raise is gone by dawn, its stone heaped into a new pattern a few hundred meters off. Destruction and creation are so tightly woven that they seem to belong to a single process.

The light is dim and comes from above in a flat, diffuse wash. The sky is a permanent overcast, thick and bluish-grey, and the sun, when it shows, is a pale coin with no warmth in it. Light reaches the water in soft shafts that wander as the surface shivers, and it is strongest where the lattice is thin, so the best-lit areas are the young, unfinished spires, and the oldest ruins lie in an almost permanent twilight. Colors are muted. Greys, pale greens from the film-grazers, the faint rust of mineral deposits, and the occasional bright flicker of a fauna's bioluminescence, which appears in bursts and then fades.

Sound is sparse. Beneath the surface the water carries a constant, low hush: the friction of current on lattice, the distant knock of stone settling, the soft clicks and hisses of the creatures. Above the surface, where the wind is cold and steady, there is mostly a thin whistle through the arches, a faint drip from eroded spires, and now and then a deep, resonant groan as a structure shifts. The silence is less empty than it is layered, and the quiet has a weight that makes sudden sounds (the crack of a collapse, the roar of a surge) feel like the whole place has shouted.

Several rules seem to govern the Drowned Terraces. Nothing grows that is not mineral or shell. Life here is entirely animal, and everything made is made by something that moves. Heat rises only from the deep fissures, and the cold of the surface keeps the lattice from dissolving, so the architecture persists through the slow, violent cycles of the water. Erosion works against the builders constantly, but it never wins outright, because the same processes that wear the structures down also supply the material for new ones. The city is ancient not because it has finished, but because it has been doing the same work for a very long time, and will keep doing it until the mountains are entirely gone.
