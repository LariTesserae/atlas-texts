# The Leveling

The land is almost flat, and that is the first thing you notice and the last thing you stop noticing. The horizon sits low and sharp, as if the world had been planed. Nothing rises more than a few meters above the general plane, and even those rises are stubs, the worn bases of structures that were once much taller. Whatever built this place built all of it, and time has been grinding it down toward a level that never quite arrives.

Walking out from the nearest rise, the ground underfoot is a hexagonal lattice, each cell about the width of a stride, slightly domed, faced with a dark glaze that has a faint oily iridescence where the light catches it. The cells tessellate outward for kilometers. Their edges have worn to soft bevels, and in places whole cells have sunk into their neighbors, leaving shallow saucers. The surface is hard and warm. Even in the shade it keeps some of the day's heat, and it gives back a dry, ceramic ring when you tap it with a boot heel.

## Water, and the Lack of It

There is no water here, and there has not been for a very long time. The low places show where it used to be: broad basins floored with crusted white salts that have crept up the cell walls in tide-lines, one above another, like the rings inside a stump. Some basins still hold a residue of something heavier than water and darker, a viscous fluid the color of smoked amber that does not evaporate but slowly thickens, skins over, and cracks in polygons. The natives do not drink it. Nothing drinks it. It seems to be the coolant of some older system, drained and left to set.

The air is dry enough to crack lips in an hour. It carries a faint sweet mineral smell, like struck flint and warm lacquer.

## Light

The light is relentless and white-gold. There is a sun, but it is not the only source. Scattered across the plain, the flat tops of the cells hold panels of luminous material that still glow faintly in the day and brighten at dusk, so the ground never fully darkens. In the glaze, the light doubles and triples into glints that sting the eye. Shadows are short and hard, and they pool in the saucer-dips like spilled ink. Walking at midday means squinting constantly; walking in the first hour after dawn means your shadow stretches thirty meters across the lattice, bending over the edges of the cells like a figure drawn on graph paper.

## Weather

The air moves constantly. Fine grit lifts from the basins and the worn cell-edges and rides on a broad, slow circulation that seems to belong to the whole continent rather than to any local storm. You can watch it coming: a brown wall on the horizon, miles wide, that takes most of a day to cross the plain. When it arrives, the light turns the color of weak tea, the grit hisses against exposed surfaces, and the cells begin to hum in a register you feel in your back teeth.

Between storms, the dust settles in drifts along the cell-seams. Electrostatic discharges crawl across the flats on cool nights, blue threads running from cell to cell, faint tickings that sound like a clock being wound somewhere very far below.

Heat shimmer makes distant structures wobble and swim. Things at a distance look like they're underwater, though there is no water.

## Sound

It is quiet here, the particular quiet of a place that has nothing left to be loud about. The loudest regular sounds are thermal: the cells tick and settle as the day's heat leaves them, a slow percussion that drifts across the plain at dusk, sometimes in long runs and sometimes in staggered pairs, as if a thousand small clocks were drifting in and out of sync. Wind is a whisper across the open lattice, a sound more felt in the ears than heard. Only at the edges of the storm-wall does it rise to a true hiss.

Almost nothing calls. Once in a while, a single high note passes through the air, a tone from some built thing that has woken on its own schedule, sounds once, and falls silent again.

## Ecology

Vegetation is sparse but not absent. In the cell-saucers, where grit collects and the glaze is cracked, pale filament-mats grow, thin as hair and the color of bleached bone. They knit into low webs that cover a few percent of the ground, and they orient themselves toward the sun over the course of a day, rolling their tips like sunflowers. They appear to draw minerals from the grit and warmth from the cells. Where the mats are thick, the saucers look almost green in a way that is only a trick of the eye, since the pigment is a pale blue-gray.

Fauna is rare. The most common animals are small, low, segmented things that skitter along the cell-seams in quick bursts, grazing on rust-colored crust that forms in the cracks. They have hard shells and jointed legs, and they retreat into the seams the moment anything approaches. Their predators are larger, slower, and almost invisible until you are close: broad armored shapes that move along the lattice on many legs, nudging the small grazers into dead ends, and sometimes pausing to clean the cells with a long jointed tool. They seem to be maintenance animals as much as predators, and it is hard to say where the line between them lies. You might walk for a day and see only three of them, and each sighting feels like a visit from something that has no particular interest in you.

## Rules of the World

The most striking thing about this place is that the built ground still responds to itself. The lattice is not inert. Across the plain, bands of cells shift on long cycles, rotating or sinking by a few centimeters over the course of days. From a height you can see slow waves of geometry moving through the field: a ripple of rotated hexagons crossing a basin, leaving a fresh edge behind it, like a tide that comes in and goes out at the pace of weather. Walking the lattice on a cycle-day means feeling the ground subtly change pitch beneath you.

The glaze heals. Small cracks close over a few hours. Larger breaks take weeks, and you can see the healing front advancing across a fracture like frost creeping over glass. The material seems to remember its original form, and when cells sink, they try, slowly, to rise back to their intended height. The flattening is not the end of the process but a tug-of-war between entropy and an older design that refuses to be forgotten. Nobody living can say whether the design still has a purpose.

The distances here are strange. Structures that look a kilometer away are often several. The heat-shimmer and the flatness of the plain confuse depth, and the light at the edges of the day makes everything seem closer than it is. Navigation depends less on landmarks than on the lattice itself, which carries a faint directional grain, a slight tilt in the cell-edges that aligns with the continental wind. Following the grain for a day will take you, reliably, to the next stub-rise.

## Atmosphere

It feels like standing inside a clock that has been stopped for an age and is only now beginning, very slowly, to tick again. The light is clean and merciless. The silence is full, not empty: it is occupied by the faint tickings, the pale mats turning toward the sun, the far-off hum of the storm-wall, the skitter of small things in the seams. Everything here is old, and everything here is busy with the work of being old. The place has settled into a final form and keeps refining it, endlessly, toward a level it will never reach.
