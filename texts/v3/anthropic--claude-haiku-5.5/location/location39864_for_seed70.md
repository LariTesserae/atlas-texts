The land stands high, a volcanic tableland perhaps four thousand meters up, though the air is warm enough that you would not reach for a coat. The rock is young. Basalt and pumice still show the shapes they had when they cooled: ropy lava in slabs, gas-bubbled vesicles, ridges with edges that have not yet been rounded by weather. Nothing here is more than a few centuries old, and in places the stone is so new that it steams faintly where water seeps into fissures still warm from the last eruption.

Water is the governing element. The whole plateau sits in a sheet of it, though it rarely looks like a lake. The upper ground is saturated, every hollow holds a pool, and the air carries so much moisture that surfaces never fully dry. Fog is not an event here. It is the medium. Light arrives diffused and white, the sun a bright coin behind a layer that never quite thins. Distances fade into a pale gradient, so that a ridge two kilometers off looks like a shadow that has been painted over and softened, and the horizon is a suggestion rather than a line.

The lowest basins have filled with standing water so still it works as a mirror when the cloud lifts. These are not ordinary lakes. The water is stratified in warm and cool layers, and the layers drift. Very slowly, over days, a band of warmer water migrates across a basin like a tide that has forgotten its moon. Where the layers meet, the surface breaks into thin skins of mineral film, silica and calcium left behind as the water turns, forming white lace on the surface that gathers, dissolves, and reforms over weeks. Along the shallow margins, mineral terraces have built up in stepped pools, each rim a crust of pale travertine with a film of living green beneath the water. The terraces are the only structures on the plateau, and they were built by water, not by anything that thinks.

Vegetation is dense and very lush, and it grows right into the water. Tree ferns with trunks as thick as a person stand in ankle-deep flood on the lower slopes, their crowns hung with long strap-fronds that drip constantly onto the mats below. Above them, the canopy closes into a layered green so dense that there is no ground visible through it, only a dim green cathedral light and a floor of sphagnum-like moss that gives underfoot like a mattress and releases water with each step. Epiphytes cover everything that holds still for longer than a season: bromeliad-like rosettes that each hold a cup of rainwater, mosses, liverworts thick as felt, orchids with translucent petals, lichens in bands of ochre and grey. Every branch has a garden on it, and every garden has its own small pool.

Some of the plants are strange in ways that matter. A group of broad-leaved shrubs along the basin edges closes its leaves at dusk, and when they do, they release a slow, synchronized shower from the cups they have been holding, so that across a hillside the whole slope rains for a minute and then goes quiet. Their roots are long and pale and reach out across the flooded ground in branching mats, binding the young soil and sending up shoots wherever the water recedes. The plants seem to be in no hurry. Growth is measured in seasons, and so is everything else.

Fauna is sparse. There are a few small amphibians, pale and translucent, that sit motionless on leaves and become visible only when they move. Insects exist but do not swarm; they drift in ones and twos through the fog. Once in a while a large bird with grey wings passes high above the cloud line, visible only as a shadow moving across the white. Nothing here competes for space. Most of the life is vegetal, and it has the patience of things that have all the time they need.

The sound of the place is mostly water doing small things. Drips fall at irregular intervals from leaf to leaf, a soft tick that becomes almost a rhythm in one spot and then stops. Seepage hisses faintly through the young stone. Once in a long while the shrubs release their rain together, a rushing, percussive shower that lasts a minute and then leaves a silence so complete you can hear the mist settling. Wind is barely present, a slight movement in the fronds, rarely a true gust. The quiet is so deep it seems to have weight.

Time moves at the pace of the ground. The stone is still settling and cooling in places, and the lava flows that formed the plateau are visible in the slow way the water is cutting channels through them. The mineral terraces grow a few millimeters in a year. The stratified basins turn over on cycles of weeks or months. The plants respond to seasons of rain, but the rain itself is nearly constant, so the seasons differ mainly in how thick the fog gets. Nothing here is rushing toward anything. Each change is part of a long, unhurried process, and each one is so gradual that a visitor would notice only the accumulated difference, like coming back after many years to find a pool has spread its mineral lace across a whole valley floor.
