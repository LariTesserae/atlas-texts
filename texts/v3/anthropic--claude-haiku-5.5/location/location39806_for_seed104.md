# The Drowned Plateaus of Hesh-Vael

You arrive at the top of the world and find it underwater.

The air is not air in any sense you would recognize. It is a suspension, a medium thick with water held in such fine dispersion that it never quite falls. It sits between the particles of everything like a second, slower ocean. When you breathe, your lungs feel full before they are full. Your skin beads without any sense of rain. Cloth goes heavy in an hour and never dries. The light comes down through this medium as through deep green glass, bent and blurred, so that the sun is a pale coin somewhere above you that you have only ever seen as a rumor of brightness.

You stand on a plateau, or the top of one, because the land here is ancient and has been worn down to its final shape. The erosion is complete in the way of things that have finished arguing with weather. The mesas are not sharp. They are low, broad, tilted shelves of stone, rounded like the knuckles of an old hand, cut by channels so old they have softened into long shallow troughs. Nothing here is young. The stone has a glaze of age, a patina of mineral and moss that has settled into something close to permanent. Where the plateau edges fall away, the drop is gentle, a long slope of stone and root running down into a grey-green haze that you cannot see through.

The vegetation is lush, almost aggressively so. It is the lushness of a place where water never stops and warmth never leaves. Ferns the height of trees unfold on the plateau's flanks, their fronds dripping with a continuous, unhurried condensation. Mosses carpet everything, layered like felt, thick enough to swallow a boot to the ankle. Vines hang in curtains from ledges and braid into ropes that loop from one shelf to the next. There are trees with bark like wet slate and trunks that sweat a clear sap that pools at their bases in shallow basins. Between the trunks, the undergrowth is a density of leaves, bulbs, fungal shelves, and things that are half plant and half sponge, all slightly swollen, all leaking. Everything is very alive and very slow about it.

Color is a problem here. The dim light and the suspended water flatten it. Greens go toward blue and then toward a bruised grey. Only the fungal growths keep their own warmth: rust-orange shelves, sulfur-yellow caps that glow faintly in the gloom, violet coral-like branches that catch the little light there is and hold it. At the edges of your vision the world is a wash of pale blue-grey, and when you look directly at something it sharpens reluctantly, as if the air is reluctant to let you see clearly.

You notice the sound before you can name it. There is almost nothing. A faint, continuous hiss that might be water moving through leaves or might be the blood in your own ears. Drops that do not quite fall, ticking softly onto large leaves at irregular intervals. Somewhere a slow gurgle, far off, that you cannot place. The silence is not empty. It is packed, like wool, every surface absorbing and returning. Your own voice comes back to you muffled and a half-second late, as if the air has to think about it.

Fauna is rare, and what you do see is startling for its scarcity. A single long-bodied creature drifts across a clearing, all fins and gill-frills, moving through the air with the same lazy undulation a fish uses in water. It does not look at you. Somewhere up in the canopy, a pale shape shifts and is still. You have the sense that the place is not empty of animals so much as that the animals have learned to be nearly invisible, to occupy the water-thick air without disturbing it, to live in the slow pace of the plants.

The scattered marks of the built are faint and easy to miss. A set of stone steps, cut into a shelf, climbs eleven treads and then simply stops, the upper stairs lost to moss and to the grey haze above. A channel carved into bedrock runs straight for thirty meters, then bends at an angle that seems to have been chosen for a reason no longer clear. A low wall of fitted stone encloses nothing. Its blocks are worn smooth, and vines have grown through its joints so thoroughly that the wall has become part of the trees. Once you see one, you see others: a pillar half-swallowed by root, a pale slab set into the ground with markings that have softened to suggestions, a basin cut into a rock table, dry, though everything else here is wet. These are not ruins in any dramatic sense. They are the residue of people or processes long gone, the way a riverbed keeps the shape of a river.

This is where the place begins to bend.

The rain here is strange. Above a certain clearing, the suspended water has gathered into something like a column, a vertical wall of droplets that should be falling but is not. It hangs in a shape, a lattice of threads, each drop suspended exactly where it was when its fall was interrupted. When you move through it, the drops shift and reform around you, and you feel a faint pressure on your skin, a tug toward a direction that is not down. Walking through it, you pass beneath the lattice and it drifts up behind you, slowly, as if it were rising to rejoin the cloud.

Water runs uphill in places. Not everywhere. In a narrow gully, a thin stream climbs the side of a rock face in a braided sheet, pooling briefly on a ledge before rising again to a height where the moss gives out and the stone is bare. The stream seems to pause at the top, and then it splits, and one half drops back down while the other half fades into the grey. There is no sense of falling. There is only the sense that the water has decided, for now, to go that way.

Time is loose too. You turn to look back at the stairs you passed and they seem to have more steps than you counted. The fungal glow on a log pulses at a rhythm that is not quite a heartbeat and not quite the drift of the sea. Once, very briefly, you hear a sound you recognize, a voice saying a word you almost know, and then it is gone, and the silence closes over the place like water over a dropped stone. The mosses seem to grow while you watch, slightly, in the way that a shadow grows while a sun sets, and when you look away and back, the shape has shifted, the pattern changed, though nothing moved in the way you would expect.

The place is dynamic, even if the dynamics are slow. Everything is in process. The fern-lichens are unfurling, slowly, at a pace you could only measure by returning to them over days. Fungal shelves are rotting from the edges while new ones bud from the centers, the whole mass drifting outward like a tide. Sap pools rise and fall with the pulse of the trees. Somewhere below the haze, something is growing or dying or migrating, and the ripple of it reaches the plateau in the form of a faint pressure change, a stirring in the hanging rain, a shift in the light. Predator and prey play out in long arcs, measured in seasons, not moments. A pale creature hunts a fungal grazer across a whole hillside over the course of an afternoon that somehow lasts a week.

At the edge of the plateau, where the land falls into the grey, you stop and look down. The slope descends into a sea of haze, and somewhere in it, very faint, you think you see another shelf of stone, another line of steps, another set of carved marks, pale against the green. Between you and it lies a depth of water-thick air, a vast slow medium holding the whole world's weather. You feel the pull of it, the way deep water pulls at a swimmer, the way the lattice rain leaned toward you and then away.

You are standing at the top of a drowned world, on stone worn into its final shape, among plants that never stop growing, under a sky that is also an ocean, and you are not sure which way is up.
