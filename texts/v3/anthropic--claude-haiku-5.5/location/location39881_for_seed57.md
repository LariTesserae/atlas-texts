**The Drowned Hollow**

You are inside a pocket no bigger than a village church, sunk under the lowest ground of a country that no longer has a name on the maps. The roof is a ceiling of raw limestone, still bright-edged where it split, as if the collapse happened last season and the stone has not yet learned to round itself. Water fills the room to just above your head. It is cool, almost cold, and so dark that you know the place mostly by touch and by the way sound comes back to you.

The water is not still. It breathes. A slow swell rises from the south wall, reaches the ceiling, and falls again, on a cycle so long that you cannot tell whether it is tide, pressure, or the slow exhale of something beneath the floor. Over hours the water level climbs a finger's width and then settles. Over days the walls seem to shift their stains. Nothing here happens quickly.

**The vegetation** is packed so tightly that there is no room between the things that grow. Green-black mats cover the walls and hang from the ceiling in sheets, and between them rise stalks thick as wrists, fanned leaves, and pale filaments that drift in the water like hair. Some of the plants are rooted in the stone. Some float in the flooded air-pocket at the top of the chamber, forming a membrane that trembles when the swell passes under it. Everything touches something else. Where one organism ends, another begins without a seam. Your hand, pushed through the water, brings back a tangle of stems that closes behind it.

**The built remains** are scattered and partial, and it is hard to be certain they were made at all. A stone step rises out of the water on the east wall, worn into a shallow dish. Above it, a rectangular niche holds a carved shape that might be a face or might be a root that has grown to resemble one. A doorframe stands free in the middle of the room, with no door and no wall attached, pointing through nothing. Along the floor, a line of smooth blocks runs under the water, perhaps a path, perhaps a foundation for something that was never finished. None of these show tools. They look shaped by water and patience as much as by hands.

**The fauna** is rare. Once, you see a pale thing the length of a finger slip between two stalks, its body translucent enough that you can see its gut working. It has no eyes, only a fringe of sensitive filaments that flare when your hand comes near. Other small shapes move in the membrane above, but when you look directly they become leaves. You are not sure you are the only thing that has noticed the other.

**The weirdness** is the strongest thing here, and it is quiet about it. The water reflects a ceiling that is a few inches higher than the real one, and the reflected stone has no cracks. The doorframe casts a shadow in a direction that does not match the light, which is none. When you turn to look at the niche face a second time, its angle has shifted, as though the carving is slowly rotating while you are not watching it. Ripples that should spread outward curl back toward their source. Sometimes the swell reaches the ceiling and holds there for longer than it should, and the membrane above you seems to be sinking into the water rather than the water rising into it. You begin to suspect the room is not so much a place as an argument between the water and the stone about which of them is the inside.

**The sound** is mostly the absence of it. The water makes almost no noise against the walls, only a soft, continuous hush, like a held breath. Occasionally, a drop falls from the ceiling and the ring of it travels a long way through the stone before it dies, far longer than the room should allow. The plants make no sound at all, though you can feel a faint creak in the stalks when the swell passes. Beneath everything there is a low, almost felt pressure, perhaps the distant grinding of the earth still settling into its new shape.

**The light** is nearly absent. There is a faint bioluminescence in the filaments, a sickly blue-green that comes and goes like a slow pulse. It is enough to see the outline of the walls and the pale bodies of the rare creatures, but not enough to see what lies beyond the next stalk. Your eyes adjust and then stop adjusting, as though the dark has a depth that resists being measured.

**The rules of this place** seem to be slow and reciprocal. Nothing is forced. The water rises when the plants exhale, the plants grow toward the places where the water has already stood, and the carvings change angle only when no one watches. You have the sense that the chamber is very young, that the stone has only just sealed itself off from the open world, and that the drowned growth and the forgotten structures are still deciding what this new enclosure will become.

If you stay long enough, the swell will rise again. It will reach the ceiling, touch the membrane, and hold there while the room takes a single slow breath, and you will feel, for one suspended moment, that the water is listening.
