# The Weeping Terraces

The basin sits at roughly two and a half thousand meters, in a warm highland where the air is always damp and the sun comes through in hard shafts for an hour or two each afternoon. It is small, about a kilometer across, bounded on three sides by a wall of dark poured stone and open on the fourth to a drop into cloud. From the rim you see it whole: a bowl of stepped terraces, each one a shallow trough lined with a lattice of black ribs, descending in concentric rings toward a central pump house.

Nothing here is natural. The bowl was built, though the building is not finished, and the proof is in the ground.

## The Ground

The bedrock is young. It was laid down as fill, a cooled crust of gray-black ash bound with something like cement, and it has not had time to settle into anything like geology. In the terraces it is still raw. Where the water runs, it cuts fresh gullies a few centimeters deep each season, and the walls of those gullies show layers, pale seams where the fill was poured in different batches. Stones break out of the crust as loose grit, so the floor of the basin feels like wet sand over slate. Your boots sink an inch and then stop. Some stretches are glassy and smooth where the surface has set hard. Others crumble at a touch into a fine silt the color of wet charcoal, and that silt clings to everything.

The ridges between terraces are the newest parts, still raw. Their edges have rounded slightly from rain, and they are beginning to slump toward the troughs. In a few centuries they will be gentle slopes. Right now they are cut stone that is only starting to become land.

## The Lattice

The ribs that line every terrace are the reason the place exists, and the reason it is green. They are ribbed plates of dark composite, about the height of a child's knee, set in grids over the troughs. Their surfaces are finely pitted, and each pit holds a bead of water. Air moves through the grids, and the moisture in it condenses on the ribs and runs down into channels and then into the cisterns below. The lattice works like a net pulled through the cloud, and the basin is a fog-catcher on a huge scale.

Plants have colonized the lattice as thoroughly as they have colonized anything. Moss sheets the lower ribs in cushions of luminous green. Ferns grow from the joints, and their fronds hang over the channels. Vines have climbed the pump house and wrapped the lattice posts in ropes as thick as a wrist. Epiphytes hang in clumps from the ribs, with pale, fleshy leaves that drip. Everything here is dense and layered. The moss grows over the ferns, the ferns over the moss, and the vines pull them all together into a single mat.

The green is almost too rich. It looks as if the place were painted on a wet canvas and every brushstroke is still bleeding.

## The Water

Water is the one thing that moves freely here, and it is the basin's whole economy. Every ten or fifteen minutes it drips audibly from the lattice into the channels, and the channels feed into the cisterns. The cisterns are wide, shallow pools lined with the same dark stone, and their surfaces are never still. Rain falls into them, and so do the condensate and the seepage from the walls. The water has a faint taste of iron and something sweeter underneath, like cane. It is faintly warm to the hand, because it has been sitting in the sun.

Standing water has a particular structure. The surface is crowded with floating mats of algae and small pale fronds that move slowly with the current. Along the banks, where the water is shallow, there is a mud the color of cocoa, pocked with the tracks of a few small creatures.

## The Machinery

At the center of the basin is the pump house, a squat building of riveted metal and stone with a slate roof that has been colonized by moss. Inside is the only machinery in the place. A single large flywheel, brass-colored and slick with oil, turns once every four or five minutes. It drives a set of pistons that push water up through a network of brass pipes, which carry it out of the cisterns and over the rim to some destination you cannot see from here. The pistons clank with a slow, deliberate rhythm, and the pipes ring faintly when the flow surges. You can hear the pump from anywhere in the basin, a deep, patient thudding under the drip.

Around the pump house the vines have grown so thick that the building is hard to see. Gauges set into the walls have dials furred with green. Some of the valves have been fused shut by mineral deposits. Some pipes have burst and been left to leak, and the leaks have made small wet gardens of their own.

## Sound and Light

The place is quiet, in the way that a room full of plants is quiet. Water drips and runs. The pump thuds. Wind moves the ferns with a sound like breathing. Insects are rare, and when you hear one it is a single thin whine, startling in the stillness. A tree frog calls from somewhere in the cisterns, a short two-note cry that repeats for a minute and then stops. Birds are almost absent, though a few pale moths drift through the afternoon light, and once in a while something larger moves in the vines and goes silent.

The light is ordinary most of the time, filtered gray-white through the cloud that sits in the basin. For an hour in the afternoon, the cloud thins and sunlight comes down in long, slanted beams that turn every drop on the lattice into a point of fire. In those hours the whole basin shines, and the moss looks almost luminous. Then the cloud closes again.

## Rules and Oddities

A few things about the place do not quite fit. The lattice seems to grow. New ribs appear at the edges of the terraces, as if they were extruding slowly from the stone, though no one is building them. Some of the moss has taken on a faint geometric pattern, following the grid of the ribs so exactly that it looks drawn. The fill in the troughs sometimes shifts in the night and sets in new shapes, and in the morning a channel that was open is blocked and a new one has cut across the terrace.

Nothing here seems to have been finished. The pump runs, the lattice gathers, the cisterns fill, and the plants spread, but the whole arrangement feels provisional, a process caught mid-sentence. The place is making itself, and the making is still in motion. It is the sort of landscape that will be unrecognizable in a generation, and it is already changing while you stand on the rim looking down.
