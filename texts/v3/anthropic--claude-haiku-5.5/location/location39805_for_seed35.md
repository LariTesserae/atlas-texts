**The Sunken Pan**

The land dips into a bowl about the width of a large cathedral nave, its rim lifted only a few meters above the floor. You reach it by a ramp of cracked, glassy crust, and each step gives a dry crackle, like a shell underfoot. Nothing here has ever been wet, or if it was, the memory is gone from the stone. The floor is a pale salt-mineral that flakes into white plates, curled at the edges like old paper, with dark seams where the crust has split and something underneath breathes out a little warm dust.

The light is thin and amber, coming from no clear source. A low overcast of mineral haze hangs just above the rim, lit faintly from within, the color of weak tea held up to a lamp. Shadows are soft and never quite reach the ground. Everything looks slightly underexposed, as if the world were being seen through a cloth.

The heat is steady and dry, the kind that dries the inside of your nose and makes your eyes ache. No wind moves through the bowl. The air tastes of metal and hot dust.

**The works**

Machinery fills the pan. It was built to extract something from the basin, probably the salts, and it still works, though it has run in the same cycle for as long as anyone could measure. Iron frames rise in rows, bolted to scaffolds that have rusted into lacework. Pistons the height of houses drive down into the crust with a slow, enormous thud, lift, and fall again. Conveyor belts carry nothing but loops of grey dust, and the same dust piles at the end of each belt into the same low mounds. Gauges and dials are set in weathered plates, some with needles pinned hard against the stop, some twitching in an identical arc every few seconds.

There are no people, and no sign of recent ones. Doors hang open on hinges worn to a shine. Stenciled warnings are still legible in a language that no longer seems to belong to any living people, the paint blistered into a pattern of concentric rings. In a few places the machinery has grown over itself, cables sprouting into cables, gears fused into lumps, pipes that no longer carry any fluid but are wrapped in a dry, reddish lichen with fibrous threads.

**The sound**

This is the loudest place you will ever stand in. The pistons strike with a bass pulse you feel in your sternum before you hear it. Overhead cranks shriek on bearings that were never oiled after the first decade, a high rasp running under everything. Belts clank in a rhythm that is almost a tune, then drift out of time, then return. The hum of the old generators sits under all of it, thick and electric, like standing inside a transformer. Among the clatter there are other noises: hissing from valves that release air no one can see, and a dry, repeated tapping, which turns out to be the fauna.

**The fauna**

The fauna is abundant and layered. Small armored things the size of a fist scuttle along the belts and through the frames, their shells the same grey as the dust. Their legs are jointed like pincers, and they move in streams along the conveyors, feeding on the grit and on the lichen growing in the pipes. Larger forms hang in the scaffolds: long segmented creatures, wrapped in the dust like cocoons, that unfold when the pistons pass and fold again when they retreat. Beneath the crust, something burrows. You can see the dust lift in slow domes and collapse again, always at the same intervals, in time with the machinery. The creatures seem to have learned the cycle. They wait for the stroke, dart through the gap, and are gone before the next one.

**The strangeness**

The creatures are not quite animals. Their bodies have too many joints, and some carry gears or flat metal plates along their backs, grown into the chitin or possibly fused there by an unknown process. Some of the lichen forms regular geometric patterns, hexagons and spirals, too precise for chance. In a corner near the rim, a stretch of crust has been pressed into a ring of identical hoof-shaped prints, each pressed deeper than the last, though nothing has ever walked there in any way you can see.

The most unsettling part is the stillness. The machines never run down, the creatures never grow older, the dust never settles any further. Every cycle repeats exactly. The longer you stand, the more the cycle seems to hold you, the pistons' rhythm settling into your breath until you cannot tell which is which.

When you finally climb back up the ramp, the crust cracks behind you in exactly the same rhythm as before, and the shadow of the rim lies across the floor, exactly where it always has.
