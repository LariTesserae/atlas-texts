**The Shelf of Quiet Glass**

The land is a single high shelf, perhaps a few hundred paces across, tilted slightly toward the east and held between two walls of black rock that rise into cloud. At this elevation the air is thin and very clear, and the light is harsh. It comes from a sun that seems closer than it should be and bounces off every wet surface, so the shelf is never fully shadowed. Even in the hollows, the light has a white, metallic edge, and anything that can hold a reflection shines.

The ground is bare stone, weathered for a very long time into smooth, rounded forms. Nothing grows here. Where soil might be, there is a fine grey grit, sorted by ancient water into bands and ripples that stay fixed like the grain in wood. The rock is mostly a banded volcanic glass, smoky and translucent at the edges, with bubbles trapped in it like seeds. Where it has broken, the fractures are conchoidal and sharp, and in the sunlight they look like scattered mirrors.

Water is present but not abundant. A thin film of moisture sits on almost everything. In the mornings, fog pours over the eastern lip and pools in the low ground, and when it lifts it leaves every stone beaded and dripping. Seeps come out of cracks along the western wall and run in shallow, braided channels that cut tiny canyons into the grit and then sink again into the rock. The pools they feed are clear and very still, with floors of pale mineral crust that grows outward from the edges in slow, lacy plates. Some pools have a skin of ice in the deep cold hours. Others never freeze, because the water in them is heavy with dissolved salts and does not give up its heat easily.

The cold is steady and bites at the edges. At dawn, rime forms in feathery lattices across the rock faces, and as the sun climbs these lattices grow, fracture, and re-form in a rhythm that the whole shelf seems to keep. The rime does not simply melt. Each crystal arm sheds its water at a different moment, and as the fine structure dissolves it leaves a faint crust of salts that is then taken up by the next frost. Over days, these crusts thicken into ridged, hollow knobs, like coral built by weather.

Density is the defining quality here. The shelf is crowded. Stones lie close, rounded and fitted together in dense mosaics, and between them run veins of translucent crystal that catch light and throw it back in pinpoint sparks. The crystal grows: small prisms push up from the rock in clusters, and the clusters lean toward the sun by fractions of a degree each season. Mineral crusts overlap one another in layered terraces. Every hollow holds a pool, a crust, a drift of grit, or a stack of stones, and there are very few open spaces between them.

Scattered across this density are the marks of people, or of something that behaved like people, long ago. They are small and faint. Cairns, most of them collapsed, stand at the edges of the pools, their stones chosen for flatness and chipped to fit. Some have been reset recently, and their upper courses are still sharp. A few low walls curve around hollows in the rock, one course high, built from broken glass shaped by striking and pressing rather than by any finer tool. Near the largest pool there is a shallow ring of stones, its centre filled with dark grit, possibly a hearth or a place for drying something. There are flakes of glass everywhere, many of them very sharp and some still holding the marks of the striker's blows. Nothing in the built record is larger than a person could carry, and nothing is more complicated than a stone set on another stone.

There is no fauna. No insect hums and no bird crosses the sky. The only motion comes from water, weather, and the slow work of the mineral world. Sound is minimal: a drip from a ledge, the faint tick of crystal cooling after the sun leaves it, the hollow knock of a stone that has shifted in its bed, and occasionally a long low breath of wind along the cliffs. Sometimes, when the fog comes up the eastern side, there is a soft hiss as it passes over the rock, and then a silence so complete that the drip from each ledge sounds separate and deliberate.

The weirdness of the place lies in its patience and in its coordination. The rime cycles, the crystal leans, the crusts grow in lacy plates, and the braided streams rearrange themselves over seasons, cutting a new channel here and filling an old one there. Everything moves, though rarely enough to notice in a single day. The shelf is always in process: frost cracks the rock, water carries the grit, salts build the knobs, light keeps the crystal turning toward the sun. Old cairns fall, new ones are built, and the marks of each season are overwritten by the next.

At the far western end, where the wall leans out over the shelf, the rock forms a deep, dim alcove. Its floor is covered in a white crust that cracks into plates when stepped on. The air in it is still and slightly warmer. Something under the crust makes a soft sound, a slow, regular knocking that seems to come from deep underground, and no one has yet found where it starts.
