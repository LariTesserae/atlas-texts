**The Sump at Kettering Cut**

It sits at the bottom of a shallow bowl, perhaps four hundred meters across, where the land has settled below the level of its surroundings. From the rim you can't see the ground. You see a single green surface, uneven and layered, broken by the tops of things that are not trees: iron stacks rising through the canopy, a gantry frame draped in vine, the curved roof of a housing that has split and let the plants in.

Nobody drained this lowland for a purpose anymore. The pumping works stood here once and moved water out of the fields. It is still a pumping works in outline, but the machinery has been absorbed. Concrete channels run in straight lines under the growth, and their edges show through as pale ridges in the green. Iron pipes, some as thick as a man's torso, run along the walls and into the water. Their flanges have rusted to the color of dried blood and are wrapped in bark-brown roots. Gauges hang on their brackets, faces fogged, needles frozen at values no one has read in decades.

**Vegetation**

There is no bare soil. Every surface holds something alive. The lowest layer is a dense mat of broad, fleshy-leaved creepers, glossy and dark, that spread over the water and the wet concrete and form a floor you can sometimes walk on, though it sinks under your weight and gives a cold seep through your boots. Above it stand reeds with stems as thick as a wrist and as tall as a two-story building, their tops bowing together to form a roof. Between them, aerial roots drop from the upper canopy like cable bundles, and where they meet the ground they fuse into fibrous columns that hold the whole structure up.

The plants have grown to the old plan of the works. You can see it from the rim: the lines of the old channels are marked by denser green, the sluice gates by dark knots of woody stem, and the pump halls by thick tangles of climbers that have bound themselves to the frames. It reads like a map, a diagram of a machine now made entirely of growth.

Fungus grows on everything else. Shelves of pale bracket fungus crowd the iron. Thin white filaments web across the gauges. In the mornings, the whole basin is wrapped in a warm mist that smells of cut stems and turned earth.

**Water**

The lowest part of the bowl is standing water, black and slightly warm, the depth of a man's knee in the channels and deeper at the old intake. It doesn't stay still. Its surface shivers with a slow upwelling, and bubbles rise through it at irregular intervals, each one popping with a small wet sound. Condensation drips from every leaf, so the air never dries, and the constant tapping of drops hitting the mat and water is a background texture to everything else.

The water is clear at the edges and dark at the center. Where the intake pipe enters, the flow is steady and visible, a current moving in from somewhere beyond the rim and rising through the old machinery to the surface. Nothing visible drives it.

**Sound**

This is the loudest place in the basin, and the sound is not from animals. A rhythmic knocking comes from the pump housing, a deep, heavy beat about once every four seconds, like a piston that has forgotten its purpose but keeps its timing. Nearby, a pipe hisses under pressure where a joint has split, a long steady exhalation that rises and falls. The reeds creak in the warm air with a sound like timber under strain. Every few minutes, a stem somewhere snaps or a leaf tears free and falls with a soft crash through the canopy.

Under all of it is the constant dripping and the bubbling of the water, and the low background hum of something mechanical that you feel more than hear, in the soles of your feet.

**Fauna**

Animals are rare here, and their absence is noticeable. Insects exist, mostly small flies that hover above the water and glitter in the filtered light, and occasionally a large dark moth or beetle moves heavily between stems. Once in a long while a heron-like bird wades into the shallows, stands for an hour without moving, and leaves. There are no frogs, no fish visible, no mammal tracks in the mud at the edges. The creepers seem to take everything the water offers.

**Light and Atmosphere**

Light comes through the canopy in broken shafts, greenish-gold and softened by the mist. Where the roof of the pump housing has split, a single column of sunlight falls straight down onto the water and lights the bubbles as they rise. Elsewhere, the light is diffuse, even, and warm. Shadows are soft and the color of wet leaves.

The air is heavy and humid. It smells of sap, rust, standing water, and the sweetish rot of fungus. Breathing feels thick, like breathing near a kettle.

**Rules of the Place**

The strangest thing about the Sump is that its machinery still works, though not by any mechanism you can name. The knocking pump moves water. The intake pulls it in. The gauges, though frozen, sit at the levels they reached when the water was last high. The plants do not seem to be fighting the machinery; they seem to be operating it, their roots and stems threaded through its valves and pistons so that the old works now runs on living pressure. When a pipe splits, the leak is quickly sealed with a swelling of pale growth, and the hiss subsides. When a stem is cut, the basin seems to compensate, and within a season something else grows to fill the gap.

The change here is slow. The basin appears the same from one year to the next, but over decades the old lines of the works sink further into the green and the machinery is taken into the living structure a little more. Every forty or fifty years, by the look of the old growth, the whole basin flowers at once, a pale bloom that covers the canopy for a week and then drops, and afterward the water runs a little higher and the knocking comes a little more slowly.

Nobody has come here in a long time. The works are still working. The plants are still doing the work of keeping the lowland dry, though nothing needs to be kept dry anymore, and the water keeps rising through the old intake from a source nobody has ever found, filling the channels and holding the whole machine in its slow, patient, green-wrapped rhythm.
