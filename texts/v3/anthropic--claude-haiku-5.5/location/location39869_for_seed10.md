**The Sponge Plateau**

The high country was mountains once. Now it is a broad, tilted tableau of stone worn so long that the peaks have sunk into rounded shoulders, and the shoulders into swells, and the swells into a country that rises and falls like a sleeping chest. Nothing here is sharp anymore. Even the cliffs, where a few still survive along the western scarp, have softened into curves furred with growth.

Everything is green, or grey-green, or the bruised black-green of wet moss. The plant mat covers the stone entirely. It is not a forest or a meadow but a single skin, several feet thick in the hollows and thin as felt on the ridges, woven from mosses, lichens, fern-like fronds, and pale fibrous strands that look like hair and behave like roots. Where the mat is young it glows faintly lime-coloured at the tips. Where it is old it sags into dark, spongy hummocks that give under a boot and release a breath of cold water. The mat is the ground, the shelter, the food, and the architecture. Trees, where they exist, rise from it like pilings and are themselves draped in the same skin, so the outline of every trunk is softened and every branch carries a second, smaller ecosystem on its back.

Water is everywhere and comes from everywhere. The air is cold and thick with condensation, a permanent low cloud sits just above the highest swells and slowly weeps. Rock faces bead and run. Seeps open in the mat and feed black pools that never freeze, even at night, because the water comes up from deep in the stone and is warmer than the air. Streams begin as whispers beneath the moss and surface only when they have gathered enough to break through, so the land seems to bleed clear water from a thousand small wounds. The ground squelches. Every step pushes up a little pool.

Light is weak and diffused. The sun is a pale coin behind a ceiling of grey that rarely thins, and the shadows it casts are soft, almost absent. Dusk arrives without warning and lingers. On some days a second, dimmer glow appears low in the north, a sourceless light that brightens and fades in a slow rhythm, and nobody who lives here remarks on it anymore.

Here the old machines remain. On a distant rise stand the rusted lattice towers of a dead power line, their cables sagging into the mat and sprouting fern. A refinery's stacks rise out of a sheltering hollow, wrapped so thickly in vine that they resemble enormous green candles. Turbines on a ridge still turn in the wind, their blades furred with lichen, and when the gusts are strong they produce a deep, bellowing hum that rolls across the plateau and settles into the stone. The machines do not work, but they are not quite still. Pistons lift a fraction in the gusts. Valves hiss when the pressure in the mat changes beneath them. Something in the iron has learned to respond to the living ground.

The sound of the place is constant and layered. Water is the base note, a continuous trickle and drip from a thousand sources. Above it run the turbines' hum, the wind in the fronds, the creak of swollen branches, and the sudden crack of mat splitting under its own weight. Animals add their voices: flocks of grey, long-necked birds that call in unison from the hollows, insects in clouds so dense they sound like a far-off engine idling, and, from deep under the mat, low thumping and scraping that might be burrowers or might be the land itself settling.

The fauna is abundant and layered into the mat like sediment. Small brown things with too many legs run in the damp channels. Larger grazers, heavy and slow, move through the hollows, shouldering the fronds aside and leaving trails that fill with water behind them. Things with wings that are not quite birds and not quite insects dip and circle above the seeps, and when they settle on the mat they sink a little, as though the living ground were receiving them. Predators are rarely seen, only their prey's sudden silence.

The land is in constant motion. The mat grows fast in the wet season and collapses in the dry, but "dry" here is relative, and the cycles are never regular. A hummock can swell overnight into a dome the size of a house, then split along a fault line and slide into a valley with a sound like a landslide made of wet felt. Pools vanish and reappear a kilometre away. Streams reverse, not dramatically, but enough that a path mapped one season is unrecognizable the next. Spores hang in the air in drifting storms that turn the light sickly green and then clear, leaving new growth where the spores settled. The plateau is always becoming something else, and it is never quite finished becoming it.

The reality of the place is loose at the seams. Distances stretch: a ridge that looks an hour away may be a day's walk, and a sound from behind the next swell may arrive before its source has moved. Shadows occasionally lag the objects that cast them, sliding across the mat a second late. Water in a few pools runs uphill for a few metres before it resumes its course. The old machines, when no one is watching, sometimes seem to face a different direction than they did. None of this is cataclysmic. It is the quiet fraying of a world whose rules were written for a warmer, older, flatter time and are now being rewritten by the living ground, slowly and without apology.
