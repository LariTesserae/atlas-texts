# The Tarrow Shelf

The land rises in a long, flat tier above the basin, too high for rain and too old for any river to remember. Nothing here is natural in the way the word once meant. The bedrock was quarried, fused, poured, and quarried again so many times that the stone is now a sediment of human making, layered like the strata of a cliff. Wind has worn the oldest structures down to low swells, the way sea wears a shoreline. Their outlines show only as long undulations under the living cover, like the ribs of something buried.

**The sky and the dark**

There is no sun. The sky is a low, pressed ceiling of soot-brown cloud that never breaks, and it gives off a faint, copper-colored diffuse glow from the heat-vents to the east. The light is never bright. Visibility is measured in the length of a street, and distant things dissolve into a haze of ash-grey and ochre. Fires and light-organs supply what the sky does not. Most of the illumination comes from the plants themselves, which carry pale phosphor veins that pulse slowly, out of step with one another, so the whole shelf seems to breathe in patches.

**The heat**

Heat rises off the ground in shimmering columns even without sunlight. It comes from below: deep industrial works still burn beneath the plateau, and the old foundations carry their warmth up through the stone. The air is dry and tastes of metal and sulfur. Breathing through cloth becomes a habit within the first hour. Surfaces are hot to the touch, and the plants close their pores in the afternoon bloom-cycle, when the subterranean warmth peaks, and open them again in the cooler dark hours.

**The vegetation as architecture**

Every surface is covered. There are no bare patches, and the usual boundary between plant and building has collapsed. Vines of dense, ribbed fiber wrap pylons and girders. Lattice-trees grow around the old frames of factories, their trunks fused to rusted steel, their branches hung with broad leaves of laminated bark that fold to shed the heat. Some plants have replaced the walls of buildings entirely. Their roots run through the old conduits, carrying oils and liquid ammonia from the buried works, and their sap seeps out in glossy amber beads that harden into a glassy resin on the ground.

These plants do not need water. They drink hydrocarbon mist, sulfur brine, and the thin condensate that gathers on the cold undersides of their own leaves at night. Where they grow thickest, the ground is spongy and warm underfoot, a floor of compressed root-mat over old machinery. Between the stands, pathways of hardened resin and cracked concrete run in lines that were laid out for vehicles and are now used only by the wind.

**Structures and machines**

The industry is still running. Mechanical things move slowly across the plateau: large harvester-frames on articulated legs, tending the growth, pruning, grafting, carrying resin to the collection vats. They are old and worn, their paint gone, their joints bound with root. Their movements are deliberate and mostly silent. Many of them have been partly absorbed by the plant growth they were built to manage, so a harvester may be half-machine and half-tree, its arms braided with living stems. Some of the older works have stopped entirely. Their hulls stand open, and the plants have moved inside them and built new chambers out of the dead ribs.

Between these working zones sit the stillborn structures: half-finished towers, vats the size of cathedrals, conveyor arcs that stop in midair and disappear into vine. Some of these are being rebuilt. Some of them are being unmade. The plateau is never static.

**Turbulence**

The dynamic here is the strangest part, because nothing stays in one form for long. Growth is violent and fast. A stand of lattice-trees can double in a season, climbing the frames of a building, splitting its walls, then collapsing in a wave of rot when the sap-pressure fails. The collapses release heat and gas in pulses, and the cloud above sometimes flushes green or violet for a few hours. Then the machines swarm in to salvage what is left, pulling down the dead growth and re-planting new cuttings in the ash. The cycle repeats. The shelf has been doing this for longer than anyone knows, and each cycle reshapes the surface a little more, grinding it toward flatness and yet raising new ridges where growth piles up.

Patches of the plateau are quieter. In those places the growth has settled into long, slow spirals, and the machines move without urgency. It is in these stillnesses that the visitor notices the sound: a low, steady hum from the root-network beneath, the tick of cooling metal, the faint drip of resin, and then the wind, always the wind, moving through a thousand hollow stems at once with a sound like breath through an organ pipe.

**Sound and silence**

Most of the time the shelf is quiet. The vegetation muffles everything, soaking up footsteps and voices in its spongy mats. The loudest sounds come from the turbulent zones: the crack of a collapsing trunk, the grinding of a harvester's joints, the slow boom of a vat releasing pressure somewhere out of sight. Between these events there is long silence, the ticking of heat, and the breathing of the plants.

**Rules of the place**

Light is borrowed and rationed. Heat is always present and always rising. Nothing that grows here has any use for water, and nothing that is built here is ever finished. The living and the made have merged past the point of separation, so that to tend the plateau is to tend a single body, and to walk across it is to walk through its nervous system. No animals live here. There is only growth, the machines that serve it, and the slow grinding wear of time turning both back toward the level ground they came from.
