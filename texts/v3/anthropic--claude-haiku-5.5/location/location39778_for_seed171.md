**The Veil Plateau**

You arrive at the edge of the forest and find no edge. The air is already wet enough to taste of iron and green, and the light comes from everywhere at once, a dim pewter glow filtered through a sky that is less a sky than a ceiling of cloud a few hundred meters up, thick and slowly churning. The ground, when you look for it, is not really ground. It is plant, layered down through what must be miles of growth, mats and mosses and fleshy rhizomatic sheets stacked on each other until the distinction between soil and stem has dissolved. Your boot sinks and the surface gives like a sponge, then pushes back, and water wells up around the sole in a cool, clear skin.

The elevation is high. You feel it in the thin, sharp cold that gets into the lungs, and in the way the vegetation climbs in tiers, up the sides of rock outcrops and over the shoulders of ridges that look, at first, like hills and then like something younger than hills. The bedrock is raw. Where the plant mat tears, you see stone that has not finished becoming stone: black glassy crusts still cracking as they cool, seams of pale mineral that bead with moisture, rounded shapes that might be boulders or might be cooling lava domes half-swallowed by the green. Erosion has barely begun. Rivulets have only just found their paths, and they carve shallow, braided channels across the rock like veins on the back of a hand. The landscape is still being poured.

The vegetation is total. There is no bare patch to rest your eye on. Broad, glossy fronds the size of canoes overhang trunks that are not trunks but fused bundles of stems, ribbed and porous, each one perforated along its length with dozens of small openings that breathe out a fine mist. Between them, vines hang in curtains that drip and sway. Where the canopy opens, seen from a rise, you can see that the forest does not end in any direction you can walk: it runs to a grey horizon, folding over ridges and filling valleys, a single continuous organism wearing a thousand different shapes. The plants do not merely grow here. They build. Some of the thicker stems are braced with lattice-work that looks grown to a plan, hollow pipes running up them in spirals, and the pipes pulse. Put your palm to one and you feel a slow, rhythmic thrum, like a heartbeat too deep to hear, moving water from the saturated soil up to the cloud layer and back in long circulating loops that seem to follow principles no botany textbook quite accounts for. Some of the fronds fold and unfold in patterns that repeat across the valley, as though the whole plateau is breathing in unison.

The water is everywhere. It condenses on every surface. It runs from the leaves in threads and from the rock in weeping sheets. The air is saturated, so that you are never fully dry and never quite wet, only suspended in a permanent, fine damp that beads on your eyelashes and clings to the inside of your collar. Pools form in every hollow of the plant mat, dark and still, with a faint skin of iridescence on top. Shallow streams braid through the stone, cold enough to ache. Some of them run uphill for short stretches before losing themselves in the green, drawn by the pumping stems, and you cannot tell whether that is an illusion of slope or a real reversal of the way water should go.

The sound is the first thing that overwhelms you, and it does not stop. It is not one sound. It is a dense weather of them, layered and never repeating. Rain falls on the broad fronds with a sustained hiss that rises and falls as the wind shifts, and under it runs the constant rush of water finding channels through the stone, a bright chattering that becomes a roar where it gathers. Trunks groan and creak as their pipes flex. Something in the canopy clicks and rattles, thousands of seed-pods opening and snapping shut in staggered waves, each one a tiny report. Beneath all of it is the hum of the pumping stems, a low continuous vibration you feel in your teeth more than hear. Within this roar, every few minutes, comes a sharp crack and a tearing sigh as some enormous frond unrolls, or a mat of growth slides into a pool with a sucking splash. You cannot speak without raising your voice. You cannot think without the noise filling the gaps.

There is almost no animal life. You look for it and find only the faintest traces: a few small, slow things moving through the mosses, something like beetles but too translucent, a pale worm that rises to the surface after rain and dissolves when it dries. Nothing with a face. Nothing that calls. The plateau belongs to the green, and the green does not need them. Whatever the vegetation is doing, it is doing it alone, and the lack of creatures makes the mechanical quality of the place more pronounced, the sense that you are inside an enormous working system that was never designed to notice you.

The light changes slowly. The cloud layer thickens and thins in long, tidal waves, and the dim pewter glow brightens and darkens with them, so that the forest seems to inhale and exhale its own illumination. At the thinnest moments, a diffuse silver disk of sun shows through, and every drop on every surface lights up at once, the whole plateau briefly sparkling like a field of glass. Then it dims again. Shadows never sharpen. Depth is hard to judge; a frond a meter away and a ridge three kilometers off can look equally near and equally soft.

The place is unfinished, and it is busy with it. Rock is still cooling somewhere under the mat, you can feel it as a faint warmth through the soles of your feet in certain hollows. Streams are still cutting. The plants are still spreading, sending new fronds up through the cloud every hour, and old ones are sliding back into the mat to feed the next growth. Weather forms and dissolves in cycles you can watch: a dark band of rain marching across the eastern ridges, a sudden calm, fog pouring out of the valleys like milk poured into a bowl, a bright break, then rain again. Everything is in motion, all the time, at a pace that is biological and geological at once, each process feeding the next.

Standing at the top of a rise, you try to find an end to it. You cannot. The forest folds into the cloud and the cloud folds into the forest, and the sound never lessens, and the dim light holds everything in its soft, unresolved grey. The plateau does not seem to be a place so much as a process, still assembling its own weather and its own ground, and you are standing in it at the moment of its making, dripping, deafened, small.
