**The Sunken Works**

You stand at the lip of a basin that was dug, not formed. The ground drops away in terraced steps of poured concrete, each one cut so recently that the edges are still sharp, the arrises crisp enough to cut a gloved hand. Nothing has had time to soften them. Rain and seepage have only begun to stain the faces in long vertical streaks, rust-orange and grey-green, the way a wound weeps before it scabs.

The basin runs perhaps four kilometres across, and it goes down about two hundred metres. Its floor is not floor. It is a shallow lake of black-brown water held in a grid of raised causeways, and the causeways are the only ground anyone walks on. Each is a straight-edged strip of steel-reinforced slab, wide enough for a truck, laid in rectilinear lattices. From the rim they look like the ruled lines of a plan that was drawn over a sea and then the sea was let back in.

**Water**

Water is the governing fact here, and it is never still. Seepage comes through every wall. Droplets gather on the underside of every gantry and fall in steady, irregular rain onto the walkways below. The air itself is saturated, so that everything metal wears a skin of condensation, and the cold makes the skin freeze in fine white needles along the edges of railings and conduit. Puddles form and drain and form again in the same hollows within minutes. Where the concrete has cracked, water rises through the fractures in small fountains that whisper rather than splash.

The water is managed. Along the basin's north wall, a row of pump houses sits at the level of the rim, each one a squat block of corrugated steel venting heat in grey plumes. Their intakes drop into the sumps, and their discharge pipes run out over the lip and down into the surrounding country in thick black arteries. When any of them falter, the nearest causeways begin to vanish under a rising sheet within the hour. The water here has an intention, and the machines are the only thing that argues with it.

**Sound**

The loudest thing in the basin is the pumping. It is not one sound but a layered weather of them: the deep labored thud of diesel-driven impellers, the higher whine of electric motors running hot, the hiss of pressure relief valves venting every few minutes like a held breath being let go. Under this there is constant water, the drip, the trickle, the surge in the discharge channels. Above it, every so often, comes the groan of a crane arm settling, or a gravel slide where the young slopes still shift and sigh. Nothing here is quiet for long. Even the silence between machines is only the sound of water finding a new path.

**Light**

The sky is a low, even overcast the colour of wet slate, and it hangs close enough to the rim that the basin seems to hold its own weather. Light arrives diffused and sourceless. The only sharp illumination comes from sodium lamps mounted at intervals along the gantries, which cast their jaundiced glow down into the mist, where it turns to a dense amber haze. Steam from the pump houses glows faintly orange at its base. At the bottom of the basin the light barely reaches the water, so that the sump surfaces look like pools of ink with a sheen of oil, reflecting the lamps only in broken smears.

**Life**

Life is scarce and stubborn. Lichen, a grey-green crust, has begun to colonize the north-facing walls where the seepage is steadiest, and it spreads at a visible pace, like frost moving backward. Along the drainage channels there are thin mats of a dark filamentous growth that thrives in the warm outflow of the pump discharge. Rarely, something moves in the sump water: a pale, blind fish that surfaces briefly beside a causeway and sinks again. Birds sometimes cross the basin high up, but they do not land. The only animal that stays is a small, grey, rat-like scavenger that lives in the ducts and emerges at the edges of the lamp-light to pick at the rims of the discharge pipes.

**Strangeness**

The basin is wrong in ways that accumulate. The causeways do not match the plans posted on the rim. Some of the terraces have settled upward over the years, so that surfaces laid level are now a few centimetres higher at one end than the other, and the water runs uphill across them in slow sheets. The concrete in some sections is warm to the touch for no reason anyone has found, and where the heat is greatest the lichen grows fastest and in odd, almost geometric patterns, following the reinforcement bars beneath. Wells sunk at the basin's centre yield water that tastes of iron and something faintly sweet, and it stains the hands for days.

There is a rhythm to it, too. Every few hours, the water level in the outer sumps rises and falls by a few centimetres in a pattern that matches neither the tides nor the pump cycles. The people who work the pumps call it breathing. They do not stop it. They have learned to listen for the change in pitch that precedes each rise, and to time their maintenance around it.

**The rule of the place**

Here is what the basin asks of anyone who enters it: keep the water down, or it will take the ground back. The built landscape is not a monument. It is a machine that is always running, and the moment it stops, the sea that was pushed out begins, patiently and without malice, to return.
