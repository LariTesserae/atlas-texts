**The Lattice Basin**

The land sits barely above the sea, a warm alluvial floor that tilts so gently you feel it only in how the water runs. The soil is young and raw. Last season's flood cut new channels through it, and the banks still show clean, unweathered edges, pale silt layered over black loam like pages torn from a book. Nothing here has had time to become old. Everything is emerging.

Plants cover all of it, and they have become the city. Vines thick as a man's torso climb and bridge one another across the valley, forming arches, galleries, and terraces. Some were trained over generations into walls. Others are growing right now, and you can watch a span lengthen over a few minutes, stems unspooling and tendrils clicking as they lock onto new anchors. The buildings are lattices of living wood, bark grown into lintels and columns, fibrous roofs that shed seed-pods like shingles. Where a structure is too old to hold, it does not fall. It splits, lets down its weight into the soil, and something green rises from the rubble within days.

Nothing moves on four legs or wings. The only motion is green: the slow pressure of root tips finding cracks, the lean of tall stalks toward a light source, the quiver of leaf-sheaths responding to the damp air. The absence of animals makes the place feel like the inside of a held breath.

The sound is the first thing that overwhelms you. The leaves never stop. Their rustle is a constant hiss, layered and multiplied across the valley until it becomes a pressure against the ears. Sap moves audibly through the thicker trunks, a low gurgle that rises and falls like distant surf. Seed-pods crack open in volleys, each burst a dry pop, and their fibers hiss as they rise on the warm updraft. When it rains, which it does often, the sound becomes a roar: a drumming on a million broad leaves, water running down stems into hollow chambers that resonate like bells, the whole city ringing. Between storms the silence is never quite silence. There are always creaks, slips, and settling masses, the sound of structures reshaping themselves.

The air is warm and saturated. Condensation beads on every surface and runs in thin sheets down the lattice walls. Mist gathers in the low places and drifts between the arches in slow, ribboned columns. Light comes down normally, warm and yellow-green through the canopy, though it arrives filtered and broken, so the streets are always half in shadow and half in glow. Rain falls in sudden bursts, sometimes from a clear patch of sky, then stops as abruptly.

The weirdness is in the rules. The lattice does not hold still. A walkway you crossed an hour ago may have rerouted itself, its vines sliding along the cables of its neighbors until the path ends where it began. Reflections in standing pools lag the figures that cast them by a few seconds, and sometimes a reflection shows a different arrangement of the streets than the one you are standing in. Some doorways open onto the same room, but the room's vegetation is at a different season: bare on one threshold, flowering on the next. Rain falls upward in narrow columns near the old centers, lifting droplets back into the canopy, where they are absorbed by the leaves and returned as sap that runs down the trunks in reverse. No one who lives here, if anyone does, is marked as strange by it. It is simply how growth behaves when the usual rules about time and matter loosen.

Change here is constant and fast. A grove may sprout, mature, and collapse within a single day. Seeds burst and drift on the warm wind, landing in the mud of the new channels and rooting within hours. Old lattices are overgrown by younger shoots, which push up through the dying wood, thicken, and become the new structure before the old one has fully decayed. The whole basin is a restless machine of creation and unmaking, a city that is always being built and always being torn down, and in the sound of its endless rustling you can feel the pulse of that continuous, unhurried violence of growth.
