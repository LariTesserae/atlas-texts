**The Seep-Cleft**

You are inside a crack in a young mountain, about the width of two outstretched arms and perhaps forty body-lengths deep. The rock is black-glassed basalt, freshly split, with edges still sharp enough to cut. Above you the cleft narrows to a slot of white sky. Below you the floor is wet, and the wet is alive.

**Terrain and materials**

The walls are not quite stone. They are cooled volcanic glass, banded in bronze and violet where mineral water has crept through the fractures and left films behind. Some surfaces are so new they still ring faintly when a droplet strikes them, a high clear note that fades into the stone. In places the walls have *tilted* since you last looked, slowly, the way a sleeper turns, and the ledges have not yet settled into their angles. The floor is a shallow, warm marsh of silt and crushed glass, the grains rounded where water has worked them, the sharper shards pushed up at the edges like teeth.

Everything is near the surface. Water sits a finger's depth below the silt, and when you press down it rises through your handprint and fills it, warm as blood and tasting faintly of iron and struck flint.

**Atmosphere**

The air is thick and hot and close, and it condenses on everything. Beads of water run down the glass in fine threads, and the roof drips in slow, deliberate intervals, as if something above were counting. You breathe in a fog that is half vapor and half something else, a faint haze of sulfur-sweet mist that rises from the seams. The altitude is extreme, so the heat is wrong for the height: the cleft should be frozen, but the rock is warm from some fire below, and the thin high air carries that warmth without thinning it. Your lungs work harder than the heat explains.

**Fauna**

This is the crowded part. The cleft is dense with small presences, and they are everywhere at once: flat, many-legged things the color of wet slate that flow over the glass in sheets and part around your fingers; pale, translucent shapes suspended in the seep-water like jellied lanterns, pulsing in a slow rhythm that matches the drip from above; and things that move only when you are not looking, so that the crack seems to rearrange itself during each blink. Some creatures press themselves into the silt and wait with only their eyestalks showing, lenses catching the light. Others have learned the sharp glass edges and drag themselves along them, sheering off fragments and carrying them in small mouths or along their backs, as if using the shards on purpose. Nothing here builds, but something here shapes, and the flakes they drop are sharp enough to draw blood.

The predation is quiet. A pale swarm settles over a larger, slower creature in the water, and for a while there is only the shiver of the surface and the slow dimming of the thing beneath it. Then the swarm lifts, and the water is clear again, and the drip resumes its count.

**Sound**

It is almost silent. The creatures make no calls. The drip is the only regular note, and it is softened by the vapor so that it sounds more like a sensation than a sound. Sometimes a faint click comes from the glass as it expands and contracts with the heat, and once in a long while a deeper tone rolls up from the rock beneath, a pressure you feel in your teeth rather than hear. Everything else is absorbed by water and fog. You might be shouting and no one would hear.

**Vegetation and the not-living**

There are no plants. But the walls are furred with something that is neither lichen nor fungus nor mineral: a bloom of glassy filaments that grow toward the drip, bending to catch it, then pulling back in rhythm. They do not look living, but when you touch one it contracts and leaves a clear, sticky residue on your fingers that smells of rain on hot stone. Whatever they are, they are doing the work of roots, drawing moisture from the seep and laying it down as thin bright crusts along the cracks. Over time the crusts will become the next layer of the wall.

**Weirdness and the rules of this place**

Here the rules fray.

The reflections in the seep-pools lag by a few seconds. When you lean over, your reflection looks up before you do. Once you have seen your reflection move, you cannot quite make your own hand move the same way again.

The creatures repeat. Not identically, but in pattern: the same swarm forms in the same shape at the same moment of the drip, every time, even when the drip has been out of step for minutes.

The cleft itself is aging in reverse. Some ledges are sharp and new, and some are worn smooth as river stones, and they sit side by side, the smooth ones younger than the sharp, as if the glass has been remembering a different history of erosion and occasionally reverting to it.

When you try to measure the depth, your count of body-lengths changes each time you finish. The cleft is forty lengths deep, or forty-one, or thirty-eight, depending on the direction you were facing when you started.

**Dynamics**

Everything is moving, slowly and in layers. The drip has its cycle; the seep rises and falls with the heat in the rock; the filaments breathe in and out; the walls creep; the creatures hunt, feed, scatter, and return in tides that have no clear beginning. Nothing is at rest for long. The place is never quite finished becoming itself, and every time you return, some small part of the glass has moved an inch closer to the shape it had in the reflection before you came.

If you stay long enough, the white slot of sky at the top begins to look less like a window and more like a surface, and the drip begins to sound as though it is coming from above you, from very far up, from the top of a mountain you have not yet climbed.
