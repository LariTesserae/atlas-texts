# The Sunken Cut

The land here has been opened, and it is still healing from the opening.

From the rim the Cut looks like a wound in the plain. The surrounding country is flat, grey-brown, and cold, a steppe of frost-heaved ground under a low sky. Then the ground simply stops. The edge is a clean, near-vertical face of pale rock, perhaps forty meters down, and it is young. Its ledges have not weathered. The fracture planes are still sharp and angular, and loose slabs lie at the foot of the wall where they dropped only a few seasons ago. Nothing has had time to round them. The rock is a dull blue-grey shale, flecked with rust-coloured seams where water has moved iron through it.

The floor of the Cut is a couple of kilometres across and runs off into haze in both directions. It is not bare rock, and it is not soil in any ordinary sense. It is a skin of living growth, a single continuous mat of moss, lichen and low creeping vascular plants that covers every surface and fills every hollow. The mat is pale green where it is wet and grey-silver where it has dried. It lies over the rock like a blanket over a sleeping body, rising and falling in soft undulations. Where it meets a fracture or a fallen slab, it draws over the edge and continues. You cannot see the bedrock anywhere except on the steepest walls.

## The Mat

Walk on it and it gives slightly, springy and cold, then firms underfoot. Beneath the surface layer is a dense, spongy matrix of dead and living tissue, holding meltwater like a cistern. Each step squeezes out a little water, which is why the whole basin always smells faintly of cold peat and crushed leaves, though there are no trees. Press a finger into the mat and the hole fills slowly from below, with clear water that tastes of stone and iron.

The mat is the ecosystem, and it is almost all of the living mass in the basin. Its substrates are everything. Fallen slabs are colonised first by crusts, then by cushion-forming mosses, then by the mat itself. Old machinery is swallowed by it in layers: the mat grows up the legs of a cable gantry, through the gaps in a rusted hopper, and over the rims of abandoned tanks, leaving only the outlines of their shapes beneath a green-grey shroud.

Above the mat, in places, rise **standing growths**: tall, slender, pale stalks, branched near the top like frost-killed ferns, up to four or five metres high. They are spaced widely, sometimes a hundred metres apart and sometimes in loose groves of a dozen, and they seem to have no roots on the surface. Their bases sink into the mat and connect, by some mechanism nobody has traced, to the same continuous organism below. When the wind moves them, they rattle faintly, tiny brittle plates knocking together. They are the closest thing this place has to trees, and they are sparse enough that between them the eye sees almost nothing but pale ground and open air.

Near the water, the mat thickens into hummocks, rounded and bulging like the backs of animals, and in the hollows between them the meltwater collects in long, flat, mirror-still pools. The pools are shallow, a few centimetres deep, and their floors are the same living skin, so they look like windows into a grey-green sea. Reflections in them are dim and soft.

## Water

Water is the other governing presence. The cold keeps it mostly in solid or near-solid forms through the long season, and in the short warm months it runs. Meltwater seeps from the walls in a hundred thin threads, each one a braid of pale silver that slides down the shale and feeds the mat from above. Where the threads converge, they gather into small channels that cut the floor in shallow, wandering courses, then drop over ledges into the central basin.

Condensation is constant. Fog forms in the low places in the mornings and lingers until midday, and even when the air is clear, the rock and plant surfaces are beaded with droplets. The walls drip. The standing growths shed water from their fronds in slow, regular drops. The air itself feels heavy and wet against the skin, and clothing stays cold and clammy.

Rain comes in long, grey, unhurried spells, a few days at a time, and then the whole Cut seems to swell. The pools rise. The channels run fast and brown. The mat drinks until it can hold no more, and then the water spills across it in sheets.

## The Machines

The sound hits you before the sight of the machines. It is the first thing anyone entering the Cut remembers.

It is not one sound but a mass of them, all pitched in overlapping registers, all too loud to think through. A deep, rhythmic thudding comes from the north rim, where a row of pumping engines stands on iron pads cut into the bedrock. Each is a squat, long-bodied machine with a flywheel the size of a cart, and they fire in a slow, mechanical sequence, pushing water from the basin floor up through pipes that run over the rim and out across the plain. Their exhaust is a thin grey haze in the cold air, and the rhythm of their stroke sets the tempo for everything else.

Below them, the cable gantries creak and groan. Their winches haul buckets of spoil, and their wheels scream on rails that have been cleared of mat only at the crossing points. Somewhere to the west a turbine whines, its high note sustained for minutes at a time and then rising in a sudden shriek as a valve is opened. Pipes ring when water hammers through them. The walls, which are young and hollow in places, hum back faintly, so the whole basin resonates with a low drone underneath the rest.

Between the machines, the sound of the water is almost lost: the hiss of the seepage threads, the rush in the channels, and the soft, constant drip of the walls. Under all of it, in the rare quiet moments when the engines pause for their own reasons, you can hear the standing growths rattling in the wind, a dry, faint chatter like someone counting coins somewhere far away.

The machines are old, but they are kept running. Some are rusted and patched, others have new parts bolted to the bones of older ones, and a few have been entirely overgrown, with the mat climbing up their frames and the pumps still pounding inside the green. Engines that have stopped are not removed. They are left to be swallowed, and the mat grows over their housings like moss on a fallen log.

## Built Marks

There is little that could be called a settlement. On the plain above, a few low huts sit against the wind, their walls built from the same pale shale, their roofs weighted with stones. Their doors face away from the Cut. Some are empty; others have a light burning in a window at night, an oil lamp or a dim electric bulb run from a generator that coughs in the dark.

Inside the Cut, the marks of human work are scattered and partial: a set of iron stairs bolted to the wall, half-swallowed by mat; a survey marker painted with a faded red X; a broken hauling sled sunk to its axles in green; a row of stone bollards along a channel, their tops worn smooth; old pipe runs following the contours of the basin like the veins of a leaf. Some of these things are kept clear by people who come periodically to scrape and cut. Others are simply forgotten, and the mat is slowly claiming them back.

## Light

The light in the Cut is dim, even at midday. The sky is a low, even grey lid, its brightness diffused by cloud and by the fog that rises from the pools. Shadows are soft and seldom sharp. The walls of the basin block the low sun for most of the day, so the floor sits in a perpetual half-dusk, lit mainly by reflection from the wet mat and the still pools.

In the evening the light goes quickly. The machine lamps come on, pale yellow and flickering, and their glow catches the spray and the fog and makes a hazy halo around each one. The standing growths turn into dark silhouettes, and the pools hold scattered points of lamplight, so the whole basin looks like a field of broken glass under a dark sky.

## Fauna

Animal life is scarce, and most of it is small. Pale beetles graze the mat in the mornings, leaving thin tracks through the green, and tiny flies hover over the pools. Once in a while a grey, long-legged wader stalks the shallows, picking at things beneath the surface with a long, careful beak, and it is not disturbed by the machines, which seems almost deliberate. Birds cross the Cut in high, slow flocks in the cool season, but they seldom land. The few mammals that live near the rim are shy and leave only faint traces.

Nothing here is abundant. The mat supports a thin web of consumers, and the rest of the basin is empty space between its presences. The emptiness is part of the character of the place: a long quiet between engines and standing growths, the wide grey floor stretching away under a low sky.

## Change

Almost nothing in the Cut happens quickly. The mat advances across fresh rock by a few centimetres a year, closing over a slab or machine frame one season at a time. Ledges crumble, slowly, and the walls shed slivers of shale in the freeze-thaw of the spring. The channels shift their courses in gradual meanders. The pumps keep the basin drained, but their pressure has been slowly failing for decades, and the pools have crept wider as they do. The standing growths lengthen by a few centimetres each season. The rust on the iron deepens.

The people who work here measure time in these slow cycles: the year of the high water, the season the east wall gave way, the summer the pumps were rebuilt. Each change is small and gradual, and each one shows only in comparison to something remembered. Watching the Cut for a single day, you see only the engines thudding, the water running, the fog thinning. Watching for a year, you start to see the green creeping up the gantry legs, the pools spreading into the old haul road, the pale ground softening into a single unbroken living skin.

It is a place being slowly absorbed by its own growth, while the machines hold it open for as long as they can keep running.
