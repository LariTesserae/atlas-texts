**The Sink at Harrow Deep**

You arrive by going down. The road loses its shoulders, then its edges, and begins to bend around the lip of an old excavation about a kilometer across, a bowl cut into grey shale and dolomite. The ground is only a few meters below the surrounding country, but the walls are steep and terraced, and the effect is of stepping into a room that someone forgot to finish building.

The air is cool and wet in a way that feels deliberate. Nothing is quite raining. Moisture gathers on every surface, beads on the rusted rails, and runs in thin lines down the rock. The stone weeps. Dark bands of mineral seep stripe the terraces where water comes out of the strata and goes down into the basin. The floor of the bowl is mostly standing water, black and still, with islands of grey gravel and the tops of things poking through.

**Built**

The town occupies the terraces and the floor in a stepped arrangement, following the old benches of the quarry. Brick and poured concrete, stacked and patched across several generations. The lowest buildings are half-drowned. Their ground floors have become cisterns, and their upper windows look out over water like the decks of sunk ships. Walkways run on pilings between them. Some are still in use, others have collapsed into the water and left stubs of railing.

Industrial remnants are everywhere: a headframe on the eastern rim with its sheave wheel locked and furred with green; a row of settling tanks, rust-red and sweating; a conveyor gallery crossing the bowl on steel trestles, bent in the middle where the ground gave way. Pipes run down the walls in bundles, some carrying water upward, some carrying it down. Pumps still operate, slowly, in a few places. You can hear them before you see them, a low-tempo knock like a heart with a bad valve. They have kept the water from taking the whole town, and they will keep doing it until something gives.

**Sound**

Mostly quiet. The pumps, the drip, a distant bell. The bells are the strange part. They hang from cables strung across the basin, and they ring without anyone touching them when the water rises or falls by a finger's width. The residents say the bells are how they know the deep is breathing. Most people have stopped noticing them.

Under everything there's a faint resonance from the rock itself, a hum that you feel more than hear, changing very slowly over hours.

**Vegetation**

Sparse. Lichen on the walls in mottled grey and ochre. Thin grasses in the cracks of the upper terraces. A few stunted willows whose roots have pushed through the foundations of the lower houses, and whose leaves are pale and almost translucent. Algae in the standing water, thick enough to change its color from black to a deep bottle green near the surface. Nothing grows densely. The ground doesn't seem to want to hold anything for long.

**Fauna**

Abundant, and that surprises you. The water is full of life that moves below the surface in slow, purposeful drifts. Long pale salamander-like forms, some as long as an arm, with feathered gills that open and close like fans. They gather at the pilings and feed on something on the stone. Frogs with bioluminescent throats, which glow faintly in the dim light and make a soft, rhythmic chorus at dusk. Small crustaceans in the cisterns, translucent, that move in unison when disturbed. Insects hover above the water in the same slow way that the fish move below it.

On the walls, birds nest in the old headframe and the conveyor gallery, and they seem undisturbed by the pumps. The town's children have names for several of the larger creatures. Some of the names are not repeatable.

**Light**

Dim, always. Overcast light comes down through a grey lid of cloud, and the walls of the bowl shade the floor for most of the day. The sun only reaches the basin's bottom for an hour or so around midday, and even then it seems to arrive reluctantly. Lamps are lit early. Many of the buildings have their windows painted over, or fitted with screens of rusted mesh that let in only a suggestion of light. The bioluminescent frogs and the cistern creatures provide most of the nighttime glow.

**Atmosphere and rules of the place**

Everything here moves slowly. The water level changes over months and seasons. The walls shed flakes of stone in a long, patient exfoliation. The town grows and shrinks at the speed of its repairs. Nobody hurries. The residents seem to share an understanding that the place is in balance, a delicate arrangement of pumping and draining and waiting, and that it would fail if anyone tried to change it quickly.

There are a few unwritten rules. You don't drink from the cisterns without boiling. You don't disturb the pale salamanders at the pilings during the thaw. You don't ring the bells yourself. When the water rises past a certain mark on the eastern wall, everyone moves to the upper terraces and waits for it to fall, and no one asks why the mark is there.

Visitors notice that the town seems both lived-in and temporary, as though it has been holding its breath for a century and expects to exhale soon. The pumps keep knocking. The bells ring their small, uncaused notes. The water holds its surface like a mirror that has forgotten what it was meant to reflect.
