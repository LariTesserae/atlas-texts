**The Cistern Plateau**

You arrive at the edge of a plateau that was made, not found. The rim rises in stepped courses of poured grey material, fine-grained and unweathered, with arrises still sharp enough to cut. Where the forming seams meet, faint ridges show where the molds closed. Nothing here has had time to soften. Rain has not yet rounded anything. The oldest surfaces are perhaps a few decades old, and the drip-channels cut into the rim are narrow and bright, as if the water found its path only last season.

The air is the first thing you notice. It is thick and warm, close to liquid, and it beads on your skin and on the lattice of pipes overhead. Above you a canopy of interlocking steel mesh stretches across the whole basin, and from it hang condensing coils that catch the moisture as it rises. The light comes through the mesh in a flat, even wash. It is ordinary daylight, neither dim nor harsh, but everything it touches has a faint silver sheen from the wet.

Below the rim the basin falls away in terraces, each one a flooded tier a little lower than the last. The water is still and very clear, held in shallow pans of fabricated stone and lined with a pale, fibrous membrane. Through it you can see the roots of the plants: dense mats of broad, leathery leaves, ferns with fronds as wide as tables, climbing vines that have been trained along vertical lattices of wire. Some stand fully submerged to the crown, others push up through the surface into the humid air. The vegetation is lush in the way of a greenhouse left unattended, every surface occupied, no soil visible, no gap of open water wider than a hand. Leaves overlap leaves. Roots knot into a single fibrous skin that you could walk on, though you would be foolish to try.

There is no animal life. You listen for it and find nothing: no insect hum, no birdcall, no splash of anything moving under the surface. The silence is not empty. It is filled by a low, continuous machine breath, the slow turning of pumps somewhere beneath the terraces, and the steady tick of condensate dripping from the coils into channels that carry it down to the tiers. Every few minutes a valve sighs. Water moves between pans with a soft gurgle that echoes off the stone. These are the only sounds, and they are quiet enough that you can hear the leaves unfurling if you hold still, a faint creak as a frond pushes against its neighbor.

Along the terrace edges stand the machines. Pump housings the size of cottages, squat and riveted, their intake pipes sunk into the lower basins. Condenser towers rise between the tiers, slender lattices wrapped in cooling fins, and at their bases you find gauges and dials with needles that barely move. The machines are old enough to have settled into their work but not old enough to show decay. Their gears turn with a patience that suggests they have been running for a very long time and expect to run for a longer one. Nothing here is urgent.

The change in this place is slow to the point of being invisible. The water level in the lowest pan rises by a hair each season, and the plants climb their lattices by a finger's width each year. A vine that you cut back will be back in the same place a decade later, having grown along the same wire. The rim stays sharp; the drip-channels deepen so gradually that you cannot see them change. Over generations, perhaps, the whole basin would fill to a single still lake and the canopy would dissolve into cloud. But that is not a thing you will see, and the machines do not seem to care.

Stand at the rim long enough and the place starts to feel less like a site and more like a patient argument. Someone designed each terrace, each pump, each coil, each leaf-holding trellis, and then left them to keep arguing with the water. The result is a garden with no gardener, a lake that never quite becomes a lake, and a silence that is kept by the machinery itself. You are the only thing here that moves quickly, and the cistern does not seem to notice you.
