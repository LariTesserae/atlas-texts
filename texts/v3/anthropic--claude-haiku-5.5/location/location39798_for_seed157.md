**The Weeping Cirque**

High in a mountain range, the land folds into a bowl no wider than a long village. Its walls are dark gray stone, cracked into vertical slabs and cut by seams where water has been working for longer than anything has been counted. The rim is a jagged lip of rock, and the sky above it is always pale and low, because clouds sit on the rim like lids. The bowl holds its own weather. Mist rises from the floor, thickens against the walls, and drifts back down, so the air is never fully clear and never fully fog. Every surface is wet.

**The water**

Water is the first thing you notice and the last thing you stop noticing. It seeps from every crack in the cliffs in threads and sheets. The rock is weeping, slowly and constantly, and the seepage has built long black streaks down the faces and sponge-soft ledges where the stone has become a kind of wet clay under its moss. Beneath the moss the rock is cold and spongy. Press a thumb into it and it gives, then slowly fills with water. The floor of the bowl is a marsh. Its ground is a mat of sphagnum and root-knotted peat that sinks a foot under weight and releases a sour, earthy smell with each step. Pools lie in the hollows, black and still, reflecting the gray sky, and their surfaces are skinned with a film of pollen and fine spores. A single stream begins at the back wall and runs out through a notch in the rim, but it is barely a stream. It is more like a thin line of moving water that finds its way through the moss and disappears under it.

**The vegetation**

Plant life fills the bowl so completely that no bare ground is visible. Tree ferns with fibrous black trunks rise in clumps, their fronds unfurling in slow spirals and hanging heavy with water. Beneath them grow thick mats of moss in colors ranging from silver-green to a deep bruised purple. Mosses cover the boulders so thickly that the boulders have lost their shapes and look like soft hummocks. Vines climb the cliff faces and hang in curtains that drip. Lichens crust the rock in rings and mottled patterns, some of them so old that their outer edges have grown into fixed, unchanging shapes, like the rings of a tree. Mushrooms grow in shelves on the trunks of fallen trees, and the trunks themselves are rotten, collapsing inward in spongy ruin.

Everything is green or gray or black. There are no flowers in the usual sense, though pale fungal fruiting bodies poke up in clusters like small white hands.

**The light**

The bowl is always dim. Even at what should be midday, the light comes down gray and diffuse through the cloud, filtered further by the broad fern canopy and the mist. Shadows barely form. Things seem to lose their edges at a distance and gain them only when close. Colors are muted and everything is slightly soft-focused. The effect is restful and a little claustrophobic. A person used to open sky would feel as if the air had a lid on it.

**The cold**

The temperature hovers just above chill. Breath becomes faint vapor. Cold water runs over every surface and collects in every hollow, and the constant moisture pulls warmth out of the body. Nothing in the bowl is warm to the touch except the thick mats of moss in the sunniest-seeming corner, and even that warmth is the warmth of decay, a slow exhale of heat from the rotting undergrowth.

**The fauna**

The fauna is the most striking thing about the Cirque, and it is overwhelming in the literal sense. Life is packed into every available space. Salamanders, dark and glossy, lie under every stone and beneath every lifted sheet of moss, and they do not scramble away when disturbed; they simply shift, slowly, and settle again. Frogs crowd the pools and the mud, fat and mottled, sitting so close together that their bodies touch. Insects hang in the mist as a constant haze. Mosquito-like flies hover in clouds that part briefly around a moving body and close again. Moths rest on every fern frond, their wings folded flat like leaves. Beetles crawl through the moss in dense streams. Under the ferns, small rodent-like creatures with pale bellies move through runways they have worn into the moss, and their trails are visible as narrow channels of flattened green.

Birds roost in tiers across the canopy, gray and brown and spotted, packed so tightly on the branches that the limbs bow under them. They shift and resettle without flying. Above the rim, a few larger birds circle in the cloud, but they never descend, as if the bowl itself were a place they chose not to enter.

What unsettles the visitor is the quiet. There are a great many animals here, but almost none of them make much noise. The frogs are silent. The salamanders make no sound at all. The insects produce a faint, continuous hum, a pressure more than a sound, and the birds murmur softly when they shift on their branches. The moss swallows footsteps. The drip of water from leaves and cliffs is the loudest regular sound: small, separate, unhurried, falling into the pools with a flat tock.

**Strangeness**

The Cirque has a quality that resists easy description. Some of the animals seem to be doing things that have settled into forms that do not change. A particular salamander sits on the same wet stone each time you pass, facing the same direction, with the same bead of water hanging from its snout. One fern frond has been unrolling for as long as anyone could tell, and it never quite finishes. The tallest tree on the far slope has a crown that has never grown and never shed a leaf, and the birds on its branches never leave. The drip from one particular leaf hangs at the tip for so long that you begin to doubt it will fall, and then it does, and another replaces it at the same point, precisely as before.

The bowl feels like a held breath. Nothing here is decaying toward anything else or growing into something new. Everything has settled into its fixed arrangement and stays there, damp and dim and crowded, and the water keeps running through the same cracks, and the clouds keep sitting on the same rim, and the Cirque waits.

**Tools and traces**

There are no made things here. No shelters, no paths, no carved stones, no fire rings. The only signs of tool use are in the animals. Certain birds have wedged pale nuts into crevices in the bark and tap them open with stones they carry, and the stones have worn smooth grooves into the rock at their favored perches. The grooves are old and filled with moss, which suggests the practice has continued unchanged for a very long time. It is the only hint in the Cirque that anything here does more than exist.
