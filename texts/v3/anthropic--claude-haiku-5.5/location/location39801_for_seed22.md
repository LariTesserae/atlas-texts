**The Sallow Reach**

The land is young. Three hundred years ago this was seabed, and the sea left in a hurry. What remains is a basin of pale silt crossed by gullies that still have sharp lips, because nothing has had time to round them. Where the silt has cracked, the cracks are clean-edged and stepped like broken glass. Basalt ribs stick up out of the flats, black and unweathered, and their faces still hold the chisel-marks of the water that carved them. The ground is low, barely a few meters above the old tide line, and most of the basin lies under a shallow, moving skin of water that is never quite a lake and never quite a marsh.

Warm, wet air fills the basin. It smells of iron, cut green stems, and something sweet and rotten, like fruit left on a sill. Vegetation has come in fast and thick. Reed-trees with hollow, segmented trunks stand in clumps taller than a house, and their leaves are broad, glossy, and perforated with a grid of small holes that let rain through and make them flutter in patterns. Between them grow mats of a low creeping fern that turns the color of rust when it's disturbed. Vines the width of a wrist climb everything, including the structures. Where the canopy opens, shafts of light fall through, but the sky is seldom clear. A thin, permanent haze holds the sun to a brass disc, so the light is dim and tinted gold, and shadows are soft and everywhere.

Across this land are the Weirs.

They are the reason the place feels populated even though it is nearly empty. A Weir is a lattice of pale metal, perhaps forty meters high, built in tiers that spread outward like the ribs of an umbrella. They stand miles apart. Between any two, you may walk half a day and see nothing but reeds and water. The Weirs are not ruins. Their surfaces are clean and faintly warm, and their lattice-joints shift slightly over the course of a day, opening and closing like the gills of something sleeping. Each one pulls moisture from the air. Fine filaments extend from the upper tiers into the haze, and droplets gather on them and run down channels into basins at the base. The basins overflow into the silt, which is why the water is always moving, why the reeds are so tall, and why the basin has come to life so quickly.

No one who built them is known. The local explanations are many, and most of them contradict each other. The physics the Weirs use does not match any textbook. They condense water from air that is too dry for condensation. They change the phase of the water as it runs, so that it sometimes flows uphill for a few meters along the lines where the lattice is strongest. Sometimes a Weir's lattice will tune itself, and the whole structure rings at a single pitch for hours, a long low tone that you feel in your teeth. When that happens, every bird in the basin goes silent, and then every insect, and then every bird comes back at once.

Sound is the first thing you notice here, and it is almost too much. The insect chorus is a wall. There are cicada-analogues in the reed-trees, with ribbed abdomens that vibrate in a rising drone, and a second, higher-pitched species in the fern mats that clicks like rain on tin. Above them, flocks of large, long-winged flyers circle the Weirs and call to each other with hoarse, whistling cries that cut through everything. Frogs the size of fists belch deep, resonant notes from the water's edge. Rain comes often and arrives suddenly, hammering the reed leaves until the noise is like being inside a drum, and when it stops, the silence lasts only seconds before the chorus resumes.

The fauna is abundant and crowded into small pockets. Walking through the reeds, you pass ten thousand small lives in a single meter: beetles with glassy shells, slow-moving armored things that graze the silt, and striped, quick-footed animals with three eyes that dart between the reed-trunks. Near the Weirs, the creatures change. Roosting flyers cluster on the lower tiers and sleep in ranks that flex with their breathing. Some of the smaller animals seem to have evolved around the structures entirely. They have translucent wings that catch the Weir-light and throw it back in prismatic flickers, and they feed on the condensation as it runs down the lattice. Predators hunt along the lattice-foot, and the prey run up the metal to escape them. The ecosystem is in constant motion, and it rearranges itself every season, as the basin continues to take shape around its new hydrology.

Erosion is still active everywhere. Water runs through the silt channels with a sound like pouring sand. Banks collapse into the flow with soft, wet crashes. Every rain reshapes some part of the basin, cutting new channels and filling old ones. Entire reed-groves have been swallowed in a season and reappeared in another place. The ground is never still for long.

Travelers say the best time to be here is the first hour after dawn, when the haze thins and the Weirs catch the low light along their tiers. For a few minutes, each lattice glows amber from top to bottom, the droplets on the filaments ignite like beads of fire, and the flyers rise from the metal in one long unfolding wave. Then the sun clears the haze, the light goes gold and flat, the rain begins again somewhere out in the reeds, and the chorus rises to fill the basin, deafening and endless, while the Weirs go on pulling water out of the dry air and sending it down into the silt.
