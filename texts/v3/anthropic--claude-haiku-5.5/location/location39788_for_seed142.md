**The Sunken Mesa of Quiet Engines**

You stand on a plateau that has been worn down for longer than anything has had a name. The altitude is high enough that the air is thin and tastes of metal, but the warmth is steady, a dry heat that never climbs or falls. The ground is pale grey-brown, cracked into polygons a few meters across, each edge rounded and smoothed as though by a slow tide that drained away eons ago. Nothing here is sharp. The mountains that once rose around this table have sunk into long, low swells, and the horizon is a soft line you could mistake for the sea if you didn't know better.

**The ground**

Beneath the crust lies a fine mineral silt, pale as ash, and in places it has crystallized into a crust of salt that rings faintly underfoot. Where the polygon cracks meet, the salt has grown into thin blades, like frost that forgot to melt. There is no water. There has been none in living memory, and the rock itself seems to have forgotten it ever existed: the stone is dense, glassy in its fractures, and it shows no trace of rivulets, channels, or the soft sculpting of rain. Only wind could move this surface, and the wind here is almost absent.

**The city**

Industry once rose from this plateau, and what remains is not quite a ruin. It is more like a settlement that paused. Long rows of squat, windowless blocks stand in grids that have sunk to the level of their own foundations. Their walls are a dull concrete flecked with mineral efflorescence. Tall stacks, some as high as cathedrals, lean slightly out of true, their caps rimmed with pale crusts. Pipes run between buildings at waist height, cracked open at the joints, some still faintly warm to the touch, their interiors coated in dry white deposits.

Roads connect the blocks, but they are scored with parallel ridges, as if the surface was laid down in a single pour and then left to set into its final shape. Lamp posts line the avenues, their glass long since gone, their iron arms bent by nothing more than time. A few still hold the faint ghost of a bulb's shape in their housings.

The layout is orderly, almost ceremonial. Districts are marked by different building styles: the blocks to the north are low and wide, probably storage or refinery halls; to the east, slender towers stand in pairs, perhaps offices or control centers; to the south lies a sprawl of short, dense structures whose purpose is hard to guess. Nothing here looks abandoned in a hurry. It looks like a place that finished its work and then stopped.

**The light**

The sky is a deep, even dusk, not quite night and never quite day. There is no visible sun. A diffuse, pewter glow comes from everywhere at once and casts almost no shadows. Distances flatten. A building a kilometer away and one a hundred meters off look equally far, equally pale, equally still. Stars are absent, or perhaps they are hidden behind a thin high haze that never thickens and never clears. The effect is of standing inside a closed room with the lights dimmed as low as they can go.

**The machines**

Scattered among the buildings are the engines: enormous rotary assemblies bolted to plinths, stamping presses with their dies still resting on their beds, conveyor loops that run out across the ground and stop short in the dust. Their surfaces are coated in a fine bloom of white salt, which gives them the look of old bone. None of them moves. Yet there is no sense of decay in progress. They seem poised, not ruined: a crane holds its load a finger's width above a pallet; a long lever rests at the exact angle of its last stroke; a gauge needle points to a value that has never changed.

Some of these machines are still connected to the city's grid of cables, which hang from pylons in long sagging loops. The cables are dead. They do not hum. When you touch one, you feel no current, only a faint warmth from the metal that matches the ground.

**The sound**

The world is quiet, but not silent. Your own footsteps crunch in the salt. Occasionally a loose flake of mineral drifts down from a high pipe and clicks against metal, a sharp, lonely sound that rings for a moment and fades. If you hold your breath, you can hear a faint, almost subliminal ticking, perhaps the cooling of metal, or the slow settling of the plateau itself, but it never grows louder or more rhythmic. There are no winds to speak of, no animal calls, no distant machinery. The quiet is complete enough to feel like pressure against the ears.

**The fauna**

Life is rare here, but it is not absent. Occasionally a small, pale creature moves across the ground: a six-legged, shell-backed thing about the size of a fist, which scuttles between salt blades and vanishes into cracks. They seem to avoid the machines. Once in a while, high above, a dark shape glides across the dusk, too large to be a bird and too silent to be anything else. These creatures appear and disappear without pattern, and when you look back, the space they occupied is exactly as empty as before.

**The strange**

There are oddities that resist explanation. The machines, though inert, are arranged in ways that suggest intent. Several face the same direction, as if following a signal that ended long ago. The pipe networks form closed loops with no inlet or outlet. In the middle of the largest plaza stands a monolithic block with no doors, its surface covered in engraved symbols that look like gauges or schematics. The symbols never seem to change when you look away and back again, yet their orientation shifts subtly with your position, as if the block is attending to you.

Time behaves oddly too. You can walk for hours and feel no fatigue, yet the light never shifts. Your breath does not fog. The temperature remains exactly the same whether you stand in the open or in the lee of a wall. It is as though this place has been held at a single moment, a perfect equilibrium of heat, pressure, and stillness, and everything in it has been fixed in the act of finishing.

**The final impression**

What you feel most is not desolation but completion. The plateau has been worn to its final form. The city has done what it was built to do and has settled into silence. Nothing is decaying, nothing is growing, nothing is changing. You are the only moving thing for kilometers, and even your movement seems to be absorbed by the stillness, leaving no lasting mark on a world that has long since stopped expecting one.
