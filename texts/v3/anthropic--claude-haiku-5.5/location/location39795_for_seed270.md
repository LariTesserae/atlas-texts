**The Settling Basin**

You come down into it from the rim, and the first thing you notice is the sound. It arrives before the view does: a dense, layered clatter rising out of the bowl like heat off a griddle. Ticking, clicking, a wet rasp, the hum of machinery somewhere below, and under all of it a faint continuous hiss, like sand poured slowly onto sand.

The basin is small, perhaps four or five hundred metres across, sunk into the flank of a much older range. The rock walls are worn down to smooth, rounded shoulders, banded in grey, ochre, and a pale bone-white. Erosion here has finished its work long ago. Nothing sharp survives. Even the cracks in the rim have been softened and filled, and the ridgeline is a low curve against a sky that is clear, cold, and very blue. The light is ordinary daylight, unfiltered, and it lies flat and honest across everything.

**The floor**

The floor is salt-crust, a mineral pan of pale plates that lift at their edges like the scales of a dried fish. Nothing grows here. There is no moss, no lichen, no green anywhere, and the absence is so complete that your eye keeps searching for it and finding only white, grey, and rust. The crust is hard enough to walk on but gives a little underfoot, and it rings faintly when struck.

And the crust is alive, or at least busy. Across the whole pan it is pocked with thousands of small rounded mounds, each the size of a fist or a loaf, each with a single ragged opening at the top. Some are fresh and glistening with brine-coloured secretion. Others have crusted over into dull domes. They stand so close together that walking between them means threading a path, and the ground is a honeycomb of stacked and overlapping casts, one built atop another over what must be generations.

The mounds are the colonies' architecture. The creatures that build them secrete a cement from the basin's own minerals, layering salt, silt, and a dark binding fluid into columns that rise, branch, and rejoin into a shared lattice below. From the surface you see only the caps. Underneath, the basin is a labyrinth of galleries, ventilated by the pulse of the crowd that lives there.

**The fauna**

The creatures are everywhere, and that is the first fact of this place. There is no moment when you are alone. Pale, segmented things the length of a forearm move in the gaps between mounds, bodies armoured in overlapping chitinous plates, legs fanning out in unhurried rhythms. Their mandibles click constantly, and that clicking is much of what you hear, a dry percussion that rises and falls as the colonies move through their feeding cycles. Some wear shells the colour of old enamel, others are translucent enough that you can see the dark packets of something moving inside them.

Above the floor, the air is never empty. Flocks of flat-winged, ash-coloured animals circle in loose wheeling columns, rising on the thermals that come off the warm mounds in the afternoon. Their wings are dry and papery and make a sound like a sheet shaken out over and over. They land in the crust in dense, shifting carpets and lift again when something disturbs them, and the whole basin seems to breathe with their movements.

Smaller things run along the rim walls, fast and low, in threads of motion. Larger, slower animals move through the lower galleries and surface now and then near the pan's centre, long-backed and heavy, their hides crusted with salt so that they look like rock moving on its own. These are the predators, though they hunt by patience more than speed, lying still among the mounds until something wanders close enough to be pulled under.

The predation is constant and visible. You will see a flock descend on something fallen, see a column of segmented creatures carry off a wing or a shell in a dozen pincers, see the ground open and close over a struggle. Nothing here is still for long. The ecology is a pressure cooker of consumption and reproduction, and the density of bodies means it is always happening somewhere within earshot.

**The machines**

Scattered across the pan, and clustered along the basin's eastern side, are the remains of an industrial installation. It was once a settling and extraction works: long concrete channels, rusted pump housings, the skeletal frames of conveyor gantries, and a row of squat cylindrical tanks whose plating has been stripped by weather to the bare ribbed metal beneath. Some of this is simply abandoned. Some of it still runs.

Near the eastern wall, a pump house hums steadily. Its intake pipe has been colonised by the creatures, who have built mounds right up against the grating so that the pump now draws air and brine through a fringe of dark cement. A set of pressure gauges on its side are still ticking, their needles trembling in the vibration. Somewhere beneath, a slow mechanical rhythm, a clank and a sigh, repeats every few minutes.

The machines are not maintained. Their function has been partly taken over by the creatures, who appear to have learned to work the basin's brine circulation themselves. The mounds line the old drainage channels exactly, as if following a plan. Where the pipes once carried mineral slurry to the settling tanks, the colonies now carry the same material to their own galleries. The industrial and the biological have become tangled into one slow process, and it is hard to tell from the outside which is using which.

Scattered artefacts sit among the rust: a lens cracked into a web, a set of brass dials gone green, the ribcage of some vehicle half-buried in crust. A signpost leans at an angle with its lettering worn away, though a few numerals survive: a grid reference, a date, nothing else.

**The air and the weather**

The air is cool, dry, and very clear. There is a constant faint wind, drawn down from the rim and across the pan in slow gusts that come and go in a cycle of perhaps twenty minutes. When the wind builds, it lifts fine salt dust from the crust and carries it across the basin in pale, shimmering veils, and the creatures pull back into their mounds, their clicking dropping for a moment into silence. When it drops, the basin erupts again.

In the afternoon, the warm mounds breathe out a rising shimmer, and the flocks ride it upward. In the evening, the temperature falls quickly and a fine white frost forms on the caps of the older mounds, cracking their surfaces. Mornings are the quietest time, when the colonies are slow and the machines are loudest by contrast, their ticks and hums carrying clearly across the stilled air.

**Sound**

The basin is loud, and the loudness has layers. At the top, the wing-clatter and the sharp mandible clicks. Beneath, the rasp and hiss of the crust shifting, the rhythm of the pump, the slow wet knocking that comes from deep in the galleries when the colonies move their young. And at the edges, the faint persistent ring of the old equipment, metal answering metal whenever the wind shakes it. There is no true silence anywhere in the Settling Basin. Even when the flocks are gone and the wind has dropped, the crust ticks and settles, and something, somewhere, is always clicking.

**Rules of the place**

Water is absent. The only moisture comes from the dark binding fluid the creatures secrete, and from the brine that seeps up through the pan in shallow pools that evaporate to crusts within a day. Anything that needs water must take it from the creatures or from the machines, which is why the colonies are so dense around the pump intake.

Nothing grows. Every structure, every body, every mound is mineral or animal in origin, and the basin is held in a closed loop where the only materials are salt, silt, chitin, cement, and the bodies of what has died. Decay happens slowly here because the dryness preserves nearly everything. The basin is a record of its own history, layered and crowded, with each generation of mounds built atop the last.

The place is ancient in the way its rock is ancient: settled, worn smooth, its shape decided long ago. But the life inside it is restless. The erosion is complete; the ecology is not. Everything moves, everything consumes, everything is crowded against everything else, and the whole basin hums with a kind of dense, patient, unresolved motion, as if it is still deciding what it will become.
