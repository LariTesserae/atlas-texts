**The Pit District**

The place is a basin, roughly four kilometers around, cut into the earth by a quarry that was dug for limestone and abandoned, then built over. Its streets now lie about forty meters below the rim. The walls of the basin rise in stepped terraces of old stone and raw rock, and the rim is a lip of sky that the sun reaches only at midday, when a hot white shaft drops straight down through the opening and lands on a few square blocks of the floor. For the rest of the day the light is green-brown, the color of water seen from underneath.

**The built layer**

The city below was once an ordinary industrial town: brick warehouses with saw-tooth roofs, a grid of narrow streets, a rail spur that switched back and forth down the slope in steel loops, and a cluster of tall chimneys near the basin's center. Most of the structures are still standing. Their brick has gone dark and friable, bleeding white efflorescence down the walls in long stalactite-like tongues. Iron fire escapes have fused with the vines growing through them. Roofs have fallen in on some buildings and been replaced by canopy, so that a warehouse can have its ground floor flooded, its second floor filled with broadleaf roots, and its top story open to a crown of leaves that has grown out through the old skylights.

The rail loops still hold rusted cars, now half-buried in soil and moss. Some have trees growing out of their hoppers. The tracks themselves are invisible under a continuous mat of ferns and creeping stems, and you find them only by the regular ridges they make under your feet.

**The vegetation**

Plant life is not growing in this place so much as constituting it. Every surface is claimed. The dominant forms are broad-leaved climbers, thick as a man's arm, that wrap the buildings in ribbed sheaths and send aerial roots down into every gutter and mortar seam. Beneath them lies a dense understory of large-leaved herbs, some the height of a person, others flat and sprawling like plates on the ground. Moss covers everything that is not moving, and in the damp hollows it grows several centimeters thick, soft and dark green, so that the floor of an old loading dock has the give of a mattress.

Trees are common but not tall. They are mostly fast-growing, shallow-rooted species with wide canopies, and they lean into each other over the streets, so that the open ways are tunnels of leaf and bark. Their trunks are often fused, grown into and around one another, and some have absorbed the iron posts and signs they once held up, so the metal sits inside the wood like bones in a graft.

There is almost no bare soil. Where a path has been worn by people, it is a band of compacted root-mat rather than earth. Where water pools, the surface is covered in floating green.

**Water**

Water is everywhere, and it moves in a slow, inescapable way. The basin is a bowl, and the bowl collects rain from the rim and from its own walls, which weep constantly. Stone faces along the terraces are slick with seepage. Water runs in sheets down the cut faces, drips from every overhang, and pools in the old quarry floor at the basin's low point, where it has formed a black lake half a kilometer across. The lake's surface is thick with duckweed-like mats and bordered by a reed-marsh that has pushed into the lower streets.

The air is saturated. Condensation beads on every leaf and drips in a steady patter that is the first sound you learn to ignore. Clothing does not dry. The humidity makes the warmth heavier, and the whole place smells of wet rot, sweet green sap, and the mineral tang of iron left out too long.

**Warmth and light**

The temperature is warm and close, a steady heat that never falls far enough at night to break the damp. The basin holds the day's heat in its stone like a kiln, and the vegetation makes its own microclimate, so the lower streets are warmer and wetter than the terraces above. Light is always scarce. The canopy filters what little reaches the floor, and the basin walls shade it for most of the day. Things are seen in greens and browns, with occasional bright patches where the midday shaft lands and the leaves blaze.

**Sound**

Despite the density of life, this is a loud place, and most of the loudness is mechanical. Pumps run in the basin's center, large diesel-era units housed in squat brick blockhouses, driving water up through iron pipes to the rim. Their thudding is constant, a low pulse felt in the chest, and every few minutes a relief valve bangs or a seal hisses. Steam vents sigh from old boilers. Somewhere a generator coughs and resumes. Water thunders down the terrace faces in spouts where the drainage has been engineered, and in the rain the sound becomes an enclosing roar that makes conversation impossible.

Beneath the machines there is the sound of the plants: leaves rattling against each other in the wind that funnels down from the rim, the creak of climbing stems working against iron and brick, the small continuous tick of water running off foliage, and the soft drumming of drops hitting broad leaves. There are no birds, no insects, no calls. The silence where animal life should be is conspicuous only at first. After a while the machines and the plants fill it entirely.

**Fauna**

There are no animals here apart from the people who maintain the pumps, and those are few. No birds nest in the canopy. No insects hum in the air. The ground has no burrows, the water no fish. The absence gives the place an eerie completeness, as if the living mass were so thorough that nothing else was needed or allowed. Dead leaves and fallen wood do not stay long; they are absorbed into the mat within a season, returned to the green.

**Dynamics and balance**

Change here is extremely slow. The plants gain ground by centimeters per year. Walls crumble in decades. The pumps are the only thing that resists the process, and they resist it by continually lowering the water, which keeps the basin from becoming a lake entirely. If the pumps stopped, the lake would rise over several years, drowning the lower streets, and the plants would climb the flooded buildings, then float on the surface, and the district would become a marsh. As it is, a rough equilibrium holds. The machines hold the water down, the water and the warmth feed the green, the green binds the stone and slowly takes the buildings apart, and the buildings, slowly, become soil.

The rhythm is patient and cyclic. The midday light moves across the floor over the seasons, growing and shrinking the sunlit patch. The water level rises in the wet season and falls in the dry, and the reed-marsh advances and retreats with it. The vines bloom once a year, a short pale flush that is the only color the place ever shows beyond green. Over longer spans, a warehouse's walls are gently pulled into the earth; a new root system finds the rubble, a new stand of trees takes the street. Nothing happens quickly. Everything happens.

**Who lives here**

A small human population tends the pumps, cuts paths through the growth, and keeps the rail cars from being swallowed. They live in the sturdiest surviving buildings on the terraces, where the air is a little drier and the light a little better, and they come down into the basin only when the work requires it. Below, in the lower streets, the living mass is nearly total, and walking there is a matter of wading through leaf and root and moss toward a machine that is still, in its roaring way, holding back the sea of green.
