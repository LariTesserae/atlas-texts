# The Long Plateau of Caldera Reach

The land rises in a single broken step, and you climb onto a tableland roughly four kilometers above the sea that never existed here. The air is thin and warm, with a dry bite to it. The sky is a deep, undecorated blue, and the sun sits high and steady at every hour, because the plateau has no weather to speak of, only wind. The light is ordinary daylight, unfiltered and clear, and it shows everything with a hard, honest edge.

## The Ground

Nothing grows here, and the ground shows it. The surface is a pale crust of evaporite, salts and sulfates laid down in layers over geologic time, cracked into polygons the size of paving slabs and curled at the edges like old paint. Where the crust has been broken, it reveals a grey-white substrate that rings hollow underfoot. Dust lies in fine drifts against every obstacle, and in the lee of ridges it has built long dunes of pale powder that never move much; they have been the same shape for centuries and will be the same shape for centuries more.

The land is not flat. It is scored with erosion, long canyons cut through the crust into dry wadis that once carried floods and now carry only wind. Their walls show banding in ochre, rust, and bone, a stratigraphic record of wet epochs that no one alive remembers. Mesas stand alone like tables left after a meal. Their tops are smooth, their faces fluted, and their bases heaped with fallen blocks. The erosion is old and settled. Nothing here is sudden anymore. Cliffs stand at the angle of rest they reached long ago, and boulders have worn their corners into rounded, almost deliberate shapes.

## The Built Layer

Across this ground, people, or something like people, have built on a continental scale. The first thing that catches the eye is the grid. Roads, perhaps a hundred meters wide and paved in a dark, fused material that looks like glazed basalt, run straight over ridges and through canyons on causeways and bridges. They don't curve to fit the land. They cross it. Where the wadis are deepest, the roads leap them on spans of gray arch, and the arches are furred with crusted salt where runoff never comes to wash them clean.

The settlements are dense in clusters and sparse between them. Each cluster is a city in the layered sense. The lowest level is a foundation of squat, heavy blocks, half sunk in the crust. Above that rise tiers of stepped terraces, and above those, towers that narrow as they climb. Many towers are topped with fins or lattices, presumably for radiating heat, since the plateau is warm and dry and the built places are full of heat-shedding surfaces. Some structures are abandoned and roofless. Others are in use, their windows lit at night by a steady amber glow that does not flicker. Nothing here looks overgrown, because nothing can grow. Everything looks maintained or slowly becoming ruin, and the difference between those two states is the main thing you notice about the cities.

The architecture is organized, and its organization matters. There are no decorative flourishes. Everything is functional, which somehow makes it stranger. The buildings are shaped by the flows they once served: intake vents, exhaust stacks, pressure housings, long cylindrical tanks bolted into the ground at angles, all connected by pipe bridges that run from city to city across the plateau. Many of these pipes are dry. Their inner surfaces are coated with pale mineral lining, a residue left by something that once moved through them and has long since stopped. Following a pipeline across a hundred kilometers, you could find only dust and empty steel.

## Industry

The tech level is industrial, and the industry is still partly running. Here and there, from the lower foundations of the cities, you see the outflow of working machinery: slow columns of grey-white vapor, drifting upward and bending with the wind before dispersing. There are long low factory halls, their roofs made of corrugated panels that have faded to bleached cream. Mechanical gantries stand over quarry pits, some still moving, lifting the crust in clean pale blocks that are stacked in towers at the pit edges. Those blocks might be what the cities are built from, or what they export. It is hard to tell.

Power comes from something you cannot see clearly. Transmission lines hang between towers in long catenaries, and the poles are spaced so evenly that they draw the eye across the terrain like the rungs of a ladder. Some lines are slack and sagging with mineral crust. Others hum faintly, which you feel more than hear.

## Fauna

Here is the real surprise. This is an abundant fauna in a world without plants, and the animals live on the crust, the dust, and the built infrastructure itself.

The largest are grazers. They move in slow herds across the flats, low-slung and wide, with carapaces of a glassy grey-green material that catches the light like polished stone. Their heads are broad and flat, fitted with rasping mouthparts that they press into the efflorescent salt, scraping the crust into fine powder that they digest with a slow chemistry. They leave long, shallow trails across the plateau, visible from miles away as pale lines in the dust. Their herds migrate with the seasons of the wind. When the great dust-storms come, the grazers bury themselves in drifts and wait, and when the air clears they emerge in new positions, as if the landscape had shuffled them.

Smaller forms live in the cities and their pipes. These are segmented, many-legged, and pale, and they cling to the stacked crust-blocks and the warm walls of the towers. They seem to feed on the mineral residue inside the dry pipes, and in return they keep the surfaces clean. You can see colonies of them clustered on tower fins in neat patterns, all facing the same direction, like sails. Some of the larger forms hunt the smaller ones along the causeways, moving with a stiff, deliberate gait, and they are clearly among the planet's apex predators, though they show no interest in people.

People appear to coexist with this fauna more than they control it. Some of the herd-animals are penned at the edges of the cities, their carapaces scraped bare, and some of the segmented climbers are harvested from the towers in great numbers. But most of the life here moves on its own schedule, and the cities seem to have adapted to it, leaving gaps in the blocks, corridors across the causeways, tunnels under the foundations.

## Sound

The plateau is mostly quiet. Wind is the dominant sound, a thin, dry hiss across the flats that rises to a moan in the canyons and drops to silence in the lee of the mesas. When the wind stops, the quiet is deep and clean. You can hear the tick of cooling metal and the faint, rhythmic tapping of the segmented climbers on the towers. Far off, the factories give a low, steady drone, felt in the chest more than heard. The grazers make almost no sound at all, only a soft crunch of crust under broad feet, and an occasional deep clicking from the throats that may be how they keep track of one another across the herd.

## Rules of the Place

The place runs on a few quiet laws. Nothing grows, and so nothing decays in the ordinary way, which is why the abandoned structures keep their shapes and their cracks slowly widen instead of collapsing. Water is absent, and so there are no floods to erase the canyons, only dust to bury them. Change is slow. The wadis deepen by millimeters a century. The herds shift over decades. The cities expand or contract on timescales that would be imperceptible in a lifetime. The plateau is patient, and the things living on it have learned that patience.

Still, the landscape is strange, and the strangeness is consistent. The grazers never follow the roads. The climbers never cross the open flats. The factories never go silent. The towers never fall. Everything here seems to be holding its place within a balance that nobody quite understands, and nothing you encounter is in a hurry to change it.
