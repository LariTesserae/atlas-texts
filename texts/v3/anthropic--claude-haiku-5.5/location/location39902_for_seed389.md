**Kethra Ashward**, a city on a raw plateau at nearly five thousand metres, where the ground is still forming.

The plateau is young. Its surface is not yet soil or even settled rock but a shifting skin of black basalt, pale sulfur crust, and glassy scoria that cracks with the daily heat. Some of the lava fields are only a few centuries old and still ring faintly when you step on them. To the north the land is still rising: a ridge has been lifting a few centimetres a year for as long as the city has existed, and the streets there have been re-graded so many times that the old ones now lie buried under the new like the pages of a book pressed flat. Nothing grows here. There are no roots, no mosses, no lichens, only mineral films in ochre, rust, and sickly yellow where water and sulfur meet.

The air is thin and dry, and the sun is brutal. The city is hot, though it sits so high that it should be cold. The heat comes from below. Fissures run through the plateau, and steam rises from hundreds of vents, some the width of a doorway and others the width of a cathedral. The city was built around them because they provide power and warmth. Pipes of riveted iron and newer ceramic run between the vents and the works, and the lowest streets are kept warm enough to walk barefoot on stone for part of the night.

Moisture is scarce but never absent. Steam from the vents condenses in the cool hours before dawn and beads on every surface, so the buildings sweat and the streets shine. Fog sometimes pools in the basin at dawn and thins by midday. Rain falls perhaps a dozen times a year, usually in short hard bursts that hit the hot ground and vanish into steam before they can soak in, leaving the stone faintly dark and the air sharp with ozone and sulfur. Water collects in cisterns, in cracks, and in the shadowed hollows of the lava, where small pools hold a cloudy mineral grey and grow crusts at their edges.

The city itself is dense and layered. Its oldest districts are near the vents and are built of dark stone, with low vaulted rooms and cisterns cut into the rock. Above them the newer districts rise on iron frames, with platforms and bridges spanning gaps where the ground has split or sunk. Some streets run three levels deep, and the upper ones are walked on by people who never look down. Factories stand in clusters, their chimneys capped with condensers that drip continuously onto the streets below. Furnaces are fed by pumped sulfur, extracted metals, and ground glass from the older lava. Conveyors cross the city on trestles, carrying ore from the quarries in the ridge foothills to the smelting halls. Most of the machinery is old, and it runs slowly. A few boilers have been burning continuously for two hundred years, and their output barely changes from one decade to the next.

The light is ordinary, a clean, hard daylight. Dust and steam in the air give the far mountains a bluish haze, and in the evening the vents glow orange at their edges. There is no true night here, only a long dusk that lasts until the furnaces dim.

Sound is sparse. The city is loud only in the furnace halls, and even there the noise is a low, continuous drone with no real rhythm. Elsewhere you hear the tick of cooling metal, the drip of condensation from the eaves, the rush of steam when a valve shifts, and occasionally the groan of rock settling somewhere under the foundations. People speak softly, partly from habit and partly because the thin air carries a voice poorly. Most of what passes for conversation happens in gesture and pause.

Fauna is rare, and the animals that exist are strange. There are no birds. Small crawling things with chitinous shells the colour of wet slate live in the steam-warm fissures and feed on the mineral films. They are slow and mostly blind, and they move in the dark of the cisterns, where they are sometimes kept as scavengers. Larger forms occasionally wander down from the high ridges in the cold season, but they are rare enough that each sighting becomes a local story. Some of the city's oldest residents say the mineral films themselves move, creeping a few centimetres a season across the stone, but no one has yet proven this.

The place runs on slow cycles. The vents shift position over decades. Sulfur deposits thicken and crack. The ridge rises, the lava fields cool and split, and the city's streets are regraded every generation. The city's calendar is measured in these changes, not in days. Councils meet to decide which vents to cap and which to open, and their decisions shape the city for a century. Residents learn early that the ground is never still and that what looks like permanence is only a pause.

The strangeness of Kethra Ashward lies in its rules. Without plants, the city's food comes from distillation of the mineral films and from the blind crawling things, which are raised in cisterns and tasted with caution. Without rain, the cisterns are filled from condensation traps and fog nets strung across the ridge. Without true seasons, the city marks time by the rise and fall of the vents and the growth of the crusts. Its buildings are made of stone and iron that will outlast the people who built them, and its streets are constantly being rebuilt over older streets that lie beneath, still sealed in place. The city does not last in the way that cities usually do. It is continuously being poured, cooled, and laid again, while the ground beneath it keeps forming.
