# The Sump Quarter

Imagine a district no larger than a few city blocks, sitting at the bottom of a shallow lowland bowl. Its boundary is a ring of raw banks, some only a few meters high, where the earth is still deciding what it will be. The soil is pale, bruised mudstone and wet silt, cut by gullies that were not there a season ago. The banks slump after every rain, and the slumps are fresh and dark. Nothing has settled into a settled shape yet. The whole place feels like a sentence still being written.

## The Ground

Inside the ring, the ground is less soil than a mat. Plants have grown over and through everything, so the surface is a dense, springy weave of moss, fungal pale-gold sheets, and creeping vines with leaves like wet paper. Step on it and it gives, then holds, then exhales a little water. Under the mat, the old streets still exist as a pattern of cracked asphalt and tram rails, but they are slowly being lifted and reconsidered by roots. Some rails curve now where they used to run straight.

Standing water collects in the low seams between old paving stones. These pools are shallow and very still, and they reflect the overcast sky so perfectly that you can lose track of where the ground ends.

## Architecture of Living Things

The buildings are not wholly buildings. The factories and tenements of the old district have been overtaken by a vegetation that acts as construction material. Trunks of some tree-like organism have grown up around steel frames and become columns. Their bark is grey-green and fibrous, and when it cracks it reveals a pale interior that smells faintly of cut cucumber and iron. Beams are living limbs that were trained or grew into the right angles. Roofs are layered leaves, thick as thatch, that drain rain into channels grown in their own ridges. Windows are thin membranes of translucent sap-skin that glow faintly from within when the sun has been gone long enough.

Some structures are still recognizably human-made: a water tower with its tank split open and filled with a fern-thicket, a stairway that climbs into a canopy and stops, a sign whose letters have been slowly replaced by moss until only the rhythm of the words remains. Others are entirely plant, and you have to look twice to see the rooms.

## Machines Still Running

Tech is industrial but patchy. A few old furnaces still burn somewhere under the vegetation, producing a low, steady heat that keeps the air around certain buildings warmer than the rest of the basin. Pipes run through the plant mass like veins, some carrying water, some carrying a thick amber fluid that slowly drips and collects into sumps. Pumps tick and sigh. A conveyor belt is still moving, though nothing rides it, carrying a constant trickle of seed pods from one shed to another. A crane arm hangs from a derelict gantry, its cable wrapped in creeper, and every so often it shifts a few centimeters with a groan, as if something inside the machinery remembers its work.

The machines are not dead, and they are not quite alive. They have settled into a relationship with the plants, and each has begun to behave a little like the other.

## Water and Weather

Moisture is constant but not oppressive. It arrives as a fine drizzle that hangs in the air without quite falling, as condensation that beads on every leaf, and as occasional heavy rain that comes in low over the banks and fills the basin for an hour before draining away through the gullies. The water always seems to find the same routes. Old drains and new erosion channels form a pattern like a circulatory system, and the district breathes through it.

The temperature is cool, around the level of a cellar or a deep forest, and the damp keeps everything smelling of loam and rust.

## The Breaking of Rules

Here is where the place becomes unreliable. The Sump Quarter does not obey its own edges. From the rim, it looks small enough to walk across in a few minutes. Inside, the streets stretch. Walk for an hour and you may find yourself at the far bank, the one you were facing when you began, though you swear you have been walking away from it the entire time.

Doors open onto other moments. One stairwell, when you climb it, lets you out onto a balcony overlooking the same basin, but the rain is falling upward past the rail, and the leaves on the vines below are turned the wrong way, shining their undersides to the sky. Another doorway opens onto a room that is entirely filled with the seed-pod conveyor, which carries you, very slowly, to a shed you have not yet built.

Rain sometimes falls from the ground up in one corner of the basin, a thin, silver column of droplets rising into the mist and disappearing into the cloud cover. Animals walk through walls. A heron with a stone-grey back stands in what appears to be a stairwell, one foot on a step that is not there. Shadows fall in directions that do not match any light source, and some shadows belong to things that are not visible at all.

Time is not uniform. Plants grow visibly in minutes in some places and remain frozen for days in others. A pool of standing water may be fresh one moment and ancient the next, its surface gathering a film of algae and fallen leaves as you watch.

## Fauna in Abundance

Animal life is thick, but it is shaped by the plant-architecture. Insects are everywhere: moths the size of hands with wings like wet vellum, beetles that click softly as they move through the moss, pale larvae that spin fine silk between buildings and leave it glowing with dew. Amphibians sit in the pools, dark and patient, their throats pulsing. Some of them have grown long, bright feathers of algae along their backs.

Larger animals include something like a deer with a bark-covered hide, which browses the lower vines and sheds its antlers as soft, hollow fungus that the plants reabsorb. There are birds with grey-green feathers that nest in the water-tower canopy, and a loose flock of them descends each evening to bathe in the largest pool, filling the basin with a sudden, wet clatter.

Predators are present but quiet. Something moves through the lower canopy at night, leaving behind a trail of shredded leaves and a faint, sweet scent. The prey are watchful. The whole district is in constant motion: insects falling to spiders, birds dropping to pluck larvae, the moss creeping slowly over a fallen beetle, the roots drawing it down into the soil.

## Light and Sound

Light is scarce, a sort of perpetual dusk filtered through cloud and leaf. The brightest things are the furnace glows, which throw amber patches on the wet walls, and the sap-membranes of the windows, which shine a cool green-gold. Mushrooms and certain mosses give off a faint, steady glow, enough to see the shape of a path but not much more. In the basin's deepest hollows, the dark is nearly total, and the pools there reflect nothing at all.

Sound is mostly quiet. The dominant noises are soft and small: drips falling from leaf to leaf, the ticking of cooling metal, the distant hum of the furnace, the faint rustle of moss shifting as something passes through it. Occasionally a pump sighs, or a crane groans, or the water-tower canopy shakes in the wind and releases a sudden clatter of seed pods. Then the quiet returns, thick and close, like the inside of a shell.

## Dynamics

Nothing here is still. The weather cycles from drizzle to downpour to mist over a few hours. The plants grow, bloom, fruit, and rot. The insects hatch, feed, mate, and die. The predators hunt and the prey run. The machines run down and are repaired by the vines that wrap them. The gullies deepen with each rain, and the banks keep slumping inward, slowly reshaping the basin's boundary. Every process is feeding another, and the district is always becoming a slightly different district.

If you stay long enough, you begin to sense that the Sump Quarter is not a place that has been abandoned, nor one that is being built. It is a place that is deciding, through rain and rot and rust and root, what its edges should be, and it has not yet finished choosing.
