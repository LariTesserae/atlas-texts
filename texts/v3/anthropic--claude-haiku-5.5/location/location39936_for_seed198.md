**The Stepwork**

From a distance the mountain looks like it has been carved in tiers, each shelf stacked on the one below like the rungs of a ladder laid on its side. Closer, the tiers turn out to be built: poured terraces, riveted iron galleries, retaining walls of cut basalt bolted to steel ribs. Nothing here is bedrock. The slope was made, shelf by shelf, over a span long enough that the early works have softened into the landscape and now read as geology.

The air is cool and wet. Cloud sits on the upper tiers most of the day, and the light that comes through it is the color of weak tea, filtered and diffuse, with no hard shadows anywhere. Sodium lamps along the galleries glow orange in the gray even at midday. Condensation beads on every surface and runs in thin sheets down the walls. The stone weeps. Iron rails, cable housings, and window frames carry a constant skin of water, and the gutters overflow in threads that catch the lamplight.

Everything is packed in. Buildings lean against one another with no alleys between them, so that a street is only a gap where two facades happen to stop touching. Pipes run in bundles up the outer walls like ivy made of copper. Stacked greenhouses fill the gaps between blocks, their glass gone milky with age and algae. Scaffolds hold up nearly every structure, some of them so old they've been absorbed into the walls, and others so new their timber is still pale. Nothing is empty. Even the stairwells have plants growing up them.

The vegetation is lush, but it is cultivated and then let loose. Terraced beds of ferns, broad-leafed vines, and mosses spill over every ledge. Tree-sized cultivars grow in cage-trellises bolted to the sides of towers, their roots fed by nutrient lines running through the masonry. Where the cultivation has been abandoned, the plants have gone feral and taken the rooftops. Moss covers the iron. Lichen grows in the mortar. Some of the older galleries have fallen in on themselves and become overgrown ravines, where water collects in dark green pools and the air smells of wet bark and machine oil.

The machinery is everywhere and mostly unseen. Pumps thrum inside the walls. Water moves through the terraces in a slow, constant circulation, lifted from the cloud-catchers at the summit and passed down through channels, cisterns, and turbines to the valley floor. Cranes swing on the upper decks, lowering crates of seed stock and sheet metal. Lift-cars rattle up cables in the shafts. Factories sit at the base of the tiers, long sheds with blackened smokestacks that puff white vapor into the cloud. Nothing about the machinery is loud. The moss, the water, and the cloud soak up the sound, so that from a little distance the whole place murmurs rather than roars. You hear a steady hush of pumps and drips, the metallic knock of a distant hoist, the hiss of steam venting somewhere above. Human voices carry in short bursts and then vanish.

Fauna is scarce. A few birds nest in the gutters and the high cages, small grey things that look like they were bred for a purpose nobody remembers. Insects move in the wet corners, and there are snails, slow and enormous, that cross the stone in glistening trails. Larger animals are rare. Occasionally a dog or a cat slinks along a rail, and sometimes there's a caged thing in a window that no one seems to own.

The landscape never holds still. Crews work on the tiers constantly. A wall is taken down in one sector while a new one is raised in the next. Galleries are added over old ones, and the older ones are either cleared, abandoned to moss, or stripped for their iron. Stalls appear on the landings and vanish a month later. Pipelines are rerouted. The terraces shift their boundaries as the cultivation beds are reassigned and the water channels rebalance. An old district can be half-demolished and half-rebuilt in a season, and the people who live there seem to expect this, as if the mountain is always in the middle of a sentence. The turbulence has a rhythm to it, like a tide: a storm of rebuilding in one quarter, a lull in another, a sudden collapse of a scaffold and a crowd gathering to haul it away.

Weather adds its own interruptions. Cloud banks roll over the summit and the lamps flare to hold back the dark. Rain comes in long, grey sheets that flood the lower cisterns and cause the channels to overflow onto the streets. The water rises for a day, carries away loose debris and rusting scrap, and then recedes, leaving the terraces scoured and glistening. Each cycle reshapes the ground a little, so the tiers never end up quite as they were.

In the valley below, the factories send their grey plumes up the slope, and the wind carries their smell into the highest galleries: scorched metal, wet stone, and the sweet green rot of the gardens. From the lowest tier you can see the whole stepped flank of the mountain rising into cloud, every shelf lit by its own cluster of orange lamps, all of it made, and all of it moving.
