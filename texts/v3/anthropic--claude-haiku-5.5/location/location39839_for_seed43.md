The first sign of the place is that the ground drops away. You come over the rim of a plain that is flat and pale and ordinary, and then the surface simply ends, and below you is a basin a few hundred meters across, cut down into the land by something that was done on purpose and a very long time ago. The walls of the basin are not cliffs. They are terraces, stepping down in courses of worked stone, each course a little narrower than the one above, like the inside of a bowl someone built upside down and then dug out.

Nothing here is natural. That is the first thing that unsettles the eye and then becomes ordinary. The bedrock was cut, the fill was laid, the fill was cut again, and the surfaces you walk on are paving stones laid over older paving, which is laid over rubble, which is laid over more paving. Where the stone has cracked, you can see the layers in the broken edges: a band of rammed red earth, a band of grey mortar, a band of black ash, a band of fine yellow sand. Someone, many someones over many generations, kept building on what the last ones had left, and the basin has settled into the shape of all that persistence. The walls are weathered to a soft, rounded look. Their edges have worn past the point where anyone could say what the original angles were.

The materials are mostly fired clay and cut stone, with a lot of reused brick. Some of the brick is glazed in colours that have faded to the blue-green of old copper and the dull rust of iron. Lime plaster covers large parts of the walls and has cracked into maps of tiny polygons. Where the plaster has come away, dark stone shows through, and in the seams and cracks grows a thick felt of lichen, grey-green and orange, and moss in the lower, damper courses. Vegetation is sparse by the standards of a green country but it is never absent: a fig growing out of a wall at the third terrace, its roots wrapped around a stone lintel like fingers; reeds in the lowest channels; a grey thorny shrub that has colonised the top of one ruined arch and hangs down over the drop.

Water is the main animating force. Rain comes in intermittent bursts, a few times each week, sometimes a warm grey downpour that fills every channel in an hour and then drains in the afternoon. The basin was designed to catch and carry this water, and the channels are still there, cut into the stepped walls, running down in carved gutters and across the terraces in shallow aqueducts. Between rains the channels hold only a dark damp sheen and the smell of wet stone. Behind them, in the low places, are cisterns: large sunken pools walled in stone, some still holding water all year, black and clear and surprisingly cold a few centimetres below the surface. Condensation beads on everything in the mornings. The air is thick enough to feel on the skin, and the stone is always faintly cool and faintly slick.

The place is loud. The first thing you hear, before you see anyone, is the water: a constant fine trickle from the channels, a splashing where it drops off a terrace into the next, and the deep bass drone of the largest cistern when a sluice gate is opened. Over this, birds. The eaves and niches are full of swifts and a larger grey bird with a harsh call, and at dusk the cisterns erupt with frogs that sound like a hundred people knocking on hollow wood. Insects are everywhere, a hum that never stops. Somewhere below, always, someone is hammering: a steady, unhurried percussion of stone being shaped, copper being beaten, a loom's shuttle knocking back and forth under a roof. Voices carry across the basin and bounce off the walls, so that conversation from one terrace reaches the next as a murmur and a laugh. The acoustics are the acoustics of a stepped amphitheatre, and everything made here sounds a little bigger than it is.

Life is dense in the way a reef is dense. There are people, though their numbers are hard to count, living in the terraces in dwellings built into the walls: rooms cut into the stone, doorways framed in carved lintels, roofs of clay tile and palm thatch. They work with hand tools, chisels and mallets, bronze awls, bone needles, clay moulds, drop spindles, and a few simple iron fittings that are clearly precious. They farm the terraces in small beds of barley and beans watered from the channels, and they keep goats and fowl on the lower levels. The fauna is abundant and crowds in alongside them. Lizards bask on every warm stone. Bats leave the cisterns at dusk in thick spirals. Small, quick, mottled rodents run along the aqueducts. Something with many legs moves through the damp moss, and in the deepest cisterns there are pale, blind fish that come up to the surface when the people drop scraps. Predators are present but seem to keep a certain distance: a long-legged cat with a grey coat hunts the rodents along the top terraces, and at night a larger animal calls from the lower basin, a sound that the people answer with a rhythmic clatter of pots and stones.

There is something a little wrong, and the people here know it. The lowest terrace, the one that holds the oldest cistern, is slightly warmer than the rest and always damp, even in the dry hours. In its shadows the moss takes on a faint pale glow, enough to read by in a cupped hand, and the people do not gather their food from there. Walls that were built to be watertight in one generation sometimes weep in the next, and a room that was dry for a lifetime may suddenly carry a cold seep that puts a green bloom across its floor. The basin does not stay still. It resettles, with small slides of rubble, cracks that open after a storm and close after a dry season. Anyone who builds here is building onto something that is still moving.

Everything is in motion. The rain fills the channels and the channels feed the cisterns; the cisterns are opened and closed by sluice gates to water the terraces in a rhythm that follows the weather. Birds come and go on the wind; the frogs follow the rains; the barley ripens and is cut; people walk the stairs up and down the terraces carrying water jars on yokes, calling to one another. The basin is an ecosystem and a city and a machine at once, and all of its processes feed each other. The water runs down. The stone holds it. The life gathers where the water pools. And the sound of it, the endless patient clatter of building and drip and cry, rises up out of the bowl and hangs in the warm air above the rim, where the flat plain begins again as though nothing had ever been dug out of it at all.
