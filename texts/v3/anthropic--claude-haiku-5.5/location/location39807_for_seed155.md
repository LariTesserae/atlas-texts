# The Sediment Tiers

The land here has been worn down for so long that the mountains no longer look like mountains. They are long, rounded shoulders of grey stone, sloping up at the edges of a high plateau where the air is thin and cold enough to hold a constant haze. Nothing sharp is left. Ridges have been ground to humps, gorges to shallow dips, and the bedrock shows through only in smooth, polished patches like the knuckles of an old hand. Above it all the sky is a flat pewter lid, and the light falls evenly, neither bright nor dim, as if the sun had lost interest in the detail and only wanted the general shape of things seen.

On this plateau sits a city, or what is left of one, terraced down the eastern slope in stepped shelves of concrete, brick, and rusted steel. The terraces follow old contour lines. Each one is a long, shallow bench of structures: pump houses, cooling towers shaped like inverted bells, a row of silos, a foundry with a roof of corrugated iron that bulges and sags like a sail, and rail beds that run nowhere now but still hold their gauge exactly. Smokestacks rise from the lower tiers. Some still breathe out thin grey plumes. The city was built for a scale of production that the land cannot support anymore, and so it is being folded slowly back into the ground.

## The Growth

Everything is green, and the green is not separate from the built world. It is the substrate on which the built world now rests.

A pale, fibrous mat covers every horizontal surface and climbs every vertical one. In places it looks like moss, in others like the grey-green felt on the underside of a leaf, and in others like something with a skin, faintly translucent, pulsing very slowly when you watch it long enough. It is all one organism, or nearly so. Its runners travel along pipes, inside cracks, through the rust-holes of old tanks. Where two runners meet they knit together, forming a lattice that thickens into trunks, and the trunks thicken into living towers that wrap around the bones of the machines. Some pylons have been completely absorbed. Their steel lattice is still visible at the core, but the outside is a braided column of bark-like tissue, and small leaves of a waxy blue-green hang in clusters from the cross-members. A radio mast has become a tree. An elevator shaft is a long green throat with ferns stacked inside it like shelves.

The plants do not just cover the city. They hold it. Retaining walls are kept upright by root-cords as thick as a man's arm, pressed into the joints between stones. Roads have lifted and buckled, but the lift is braced from below by fungal-woven mats that channel the rainwater into culverts and keep the surface from sliding into the gully. Whole streets are now suspended on living scaffolds, with bridges of grown tissue spanning gaps where a building has slumped away. The plant is architecture, and the architecture is plant. The distinction has become more of a historical note than a practical one.

Between the thickest growths there are open clearings where the older material shows through: a cracked tile floor, a row of brass fittings green with verdigris, the ghost outline of a painted mural on a retaining wall, its figures faded to a shape you can only guess at. These clearings are rare, and they feel like rooms that the plants have not yet finished decorating.

## Water

The air is damp in the way that a cellar is damp, or a cloud forest. Condensation beads on every surface and drips steadily from the eaves of the towers. The plants drink from the air as much as from the soil, and there is a constant soft patter, like light applause, from the leaves overhead. Rain comes in cycles that the land seems to remember, a few hours of drizzle, then a heavier fall, then a long grey stillness when the mists come down so thick you can only see as far as the next terrace.

The water does not stay where it falls. Each rain reshapes the drainage. Channels that carried a stream last season are dry now; a new gully has opened under a collapsed warehouse and is running clear and fast. The culverts are older than they should be, and the water in them moves in ways that seem almost deliberate, pooling in one basin until the basin overtops and sends a glittering sheet down the steps toward the next. Standing pools collect in the cracks of the foundry floor, and at the bottom of a dry reservoir there is a shallow lake the color of tea, mirror-still, reflecting the flat sky without a ripple, because nothing here dares to disturb it.

## The Sound

This place is loud, and nearly all of the noise comes from machines that refuse to stop.

The pumps knock in their pump houses, a slow heavy rhythm like a giant's heartbeat, and each knock resonates through the terraces so that the whole city seems to hum on a single low note. Turbine blades turn in the wind coming down off the plateau, creaking and whistling, and the whistle changes pitch as the wind shifts, so the city sounds like an orchestra tuning itself over and over. Old steam valves hiss at uneven intervals. A pipe somewhere lets go a long, mournful groan every few minutes. Chains swing against girders, and the rattle carries for kilometers across the still air.

Over this mechanical din, the plants add their own voices: the creak of cord-trunks flexing in the wind, the soft pop of seed-pods opening, the rustle and drip of the vast canopy. When it rains, the sound becomes a roar on the corrugated roofs, a torrent in the gutters, a hundred small waterfalls off the terrace edges. There is almost no silence, except in the minutes just before a storm, when every machine seems to pause at once, and the only sound left is a distant bell, tolling from somewhere in the fog.

Nobody knows what rings the bell. Some believe it is a pipe organ in the old cathedral on the high terrace. Others say it is a water tank that fills and empties with each cycle of the pumps.

## Fauna

Animal life is sparse and difficult to find. The most common creatures are moths, pale grey and dusted with a fine powder that glows faintly in the mist, which feed on the blue leaves and carry pollen between the towers. Occasionally a bird with long, stiff tail feathers lands on a pylon, sits motionless for a long time, and flies off without warning. Small, slow beetles with carapaces that look like rusted metal crawl along the cable runs, eating the lichen that grows on the insulation. Their shells shine dully when they move, and sometimes a beetle's carapace catches light in such a way that it looks like a tiny gear turning.

Larger animals are rare. There may be something that lives in the deepest culverts, large enough to make the water shiver when it moves, but no one has seen it clearly. Footprints occasionally appear in the mud along the lower terraces, three-toed and wide, and then disappear after the next rain. The few people who still live here keep their distance from the lower tiers, mostly because the plants there are the thickest and the paths are the most unstable, not because of any particular danger that anyone can name.

## The Rules of the Place

The place does not hold still, and its laws are not quite the ones you learned elsewhere.

Distances change with the weather. On a clear morning the foundry looks a kilometer away, and a walk across the second terrace takes twenty minutes. After a heavy rain, the same walk can take an hour, or the foundry can seem to be at your shoulder, its furnace glow close enough to warm your face, and then, a few steps later, it has receded again behind a wall of mist. The rain does not change the terrain so much as the terrain's sense of its own size, and you learn to walk with your eyes half-closed, trusting the ground under your feet more than the view ahead.

Time behaves unevenly too. The great clock on the central tower stopped long ago, its face green and blank. But the bell still rings on its own schedule, and people in the upper tiers tell time by the bell rather than by the sun, which never seems to reach a noon or a dusk, only a long flat brightness that shifts very slightly in tone. Some of the plants bloom on schedules that follow the pump cycles rather than the seasons, opening their pale flowers exactly when the big turbines turn over at dawn.

Objects persist in strange ways. A machine that was dismantled decades ago may appear, rebuilt and running, in a clearing where no building stood the day before. A street that was fully cleared last winter will be walled in by new growth in a single night. Things that were once put away come back, and things that were once left in place sometimes vanish. People here do not argue about it. They say the city is remembering itself, and that the memory is not always accurate, and that they have all made their peace with living inside a half-remembered place.

## Dynamics

Everything moves at once, and nothing stays in one state for long. The sap in the great cord-trunks rises at dawn, and the whole tower network seems to flex and settle as the water climbs. The moths follow the pollen pulse in long, circling swarms at midday. The condensation gathers in the afternoon, and by evening the entire city is dripping, and the lower tiers flood through their culverts. The turbines spin faster in the wind of the night, and their sound changes shape through the dark hours. Each cycle feeds the next, and the cycles overlap, so the city is never in a single season, but always in several at once.

Over the long span of years, the erosion continues. Each heavy rain carries a little more of the terraces downhill. The plants pull a little more material into their root-cords, rebuilding the slopes in different shapes. The old machines rust, crack, and fold into the ground, and the living mass takes them up, one part at a time, turning steel back into something softer. Nothing here is finished. The place is headed toward a final level plain, and every season it gets a little closer, and the bell keeps ringing, and the machines keep knocking, and the green keeps spreading over the bones of everything the people once built.
