**The Thaw-Delta of Sennoch Reach**

You arrive at the edge of the lowland in the middle of a sound. It is not one sound. It is a wall of them, layered so densely that the ear gives up sorting and simply receives: rain drumming on a hundred thousand broad leaves, the wet suck of boots in silt, water pouring over stones somewhere to the left, a flock of something passing overhead with a noise like tearing cloth, and beneath all of it a low, continuous vibration you feel in your sternum more than hear. Conversation is impossible past arm's length. People here communicate by touch and by hand signs that have grown up over generations of shouting.

The light is dim. The sky is a single uniform lid of cloud the color of wet slate, and it does not brighten or darken in any way that marks the hours. Whatever sun exists is a pale suggestion behind it. Everything is seen in low contrast, greens gone to gray-black at a distance, and the eye adjusts by noticing shapes rather than colors.

**The land itself is new.** The ground is raw silt and gravel laid down within the last few centuries, and it has not yet decided what it wants to be. Ridges of sand rise out of flats and collapse within a season. Channels cut across meadows, pause, dry, then reopen a few hundred meters over. Cliffs of pale clay stand where the river undercut them last spring, their faces still sharp-edged and streaming water from a hundred seep lines. Boulders lie where they were dropped, unweathered, their corners still crisp. Nothing here has had time to become old.

**Vegetation is very thick and very cold-adapted.** Mats of sphagnum-like moss hold the water like sponges, so that stepping off a path means sinking to the shin in a warm brown broth. Reed stands rise taller than a person, grey-green and rattling. Between them grow dense low shrubs with thick waxy leaves that shed rainwater in bright beads. Dark, needle-leafed trees with buttressed trunks line the higher ground, their bark furred with pale lichen and their crowns hung with long beards of moss that drip constantly. Ferns the height of a child unfurl in the understory in slow spirals. The whole place smells of cold rot, resin, and something green and sharp underneath, like crushed stems.

**Fauna is abundant and loud.** Birds are everywhere: long-legged wading things stalking the shallows, broad-winged hunters circling in the gray, and enormous mixed flocks that move like weather systems, condensing into a single shape, pouring across the sky, dissolving again. Insects rise in columns from the reeds at dusk and dawn, though dusk and dawn are hard to tell apart. Something large and slow moves in the deeper channels, displacing water in long smooth swells. Frogs, or frog-analogues, call from the flooded margins in a rhythmic chorus that rises and falls with the rain. Small mammals with thick pelts flow through the undergrowth in quick, low runs.

**The built world is scattered and partial.** Here and there rise towers, but none stand whole. A spire of pale, fused material stands in a lagoon, its upper third snapped off and its lower lattice swallowed by moss, still faintly humming. Cables thick as tree trunks run across the flats, half-buried, sometimes surfacing in loops, sometimes vanishing into the water entirely. Flat panels, the size of doors, lie tilted in the mud, their surfaces etched with fine geometry and a sheen that does not reflect the sky quite the way glass should. Some of them are warm to the touch. A few are slowly moving, sliding a centimeter at a time across the silt as if following some slow current of their own.

**The tech is the engine of the turbulence.** The deepest mystery of Sennoch Reach is that the land is being made. Vast, nearly invisible resonant structures sit beneath the delta, and their hum shapes the sediment. Where the pitch is high, silt lifts and settles into ridges in a matter of days. Where it drops, banks liquefy and slump into the channels. Engineers who have studied it for generations describe the process as *tuning*. They cannot yet say what the land is being tuned toward, only that the pattern repeats, shifting every few seasons into new configurations, sometimes gentle, sometimes violent. Sudden floods surge through a valley that was dry at noon. A lake that held a town's worth of reeds drains in an afternoon, leaving its fish gasping in new mud, and a week later a new lake opens three kilometers east. Nothing holds still long enough to be mapped, so the people who live here navigate by sound, by smell, and by the particular way water sounds when it is about to change its mind.

**Settlements** are built on stilts and floating rafts of woven reed, anchored with cable-line to the rare solid ground. They are lashed together, reconfigured constantly, and abandoned and rebuilt as the land shifts. Their inhabitants are wiry, quick, and very practiced at leaving. They carry their lives in waterproof bundles and keep their children close to the rope lines. They are not afraid of the storm-state, exactly. They have simply learned that a place which will not hold still cannot be owned, only borrowed for a while, and they are good at borrowing.

When the rain eases, the sound does not drop. It shifts. The frogs grow louder, the wind moves through the reeds like surf, a distant channel roars as its bank gives way, and the hum beneath it all rises by a fraction, and somewhere out in the flats a tower you cannot see begins, once more, to sing the land into a new shape.
