## The Sunken Basin

The land here is missing. You reach it by going down, through a collapsed shelf of limestone that gave way some centuries ago and left a bowl roughly two kilometers across, its rim a broken ring of pale cliffs. Above, the sky is a small, distant lid. Light comes in grey shafts through fractures in the rock and through a few hand-cut vents, and never quite reaches the floor at full strength. The air is dim the way the bottom of a well is dim.

**Terrain and materials**

The floor is not finished ground. It is rock still deciding what it will be. Wide sheets of travertine spill down the cliff faces in tiers, pale and ridged like poured candle wax, and they are still growing. Where water runs over them, the lips of each terrace thicken by a finger's width every few years. Between the terraces are shallow pools with skins of carbonate crust that crack when you step on them, and below the crust the water is warm and the color of weak tea. Some of the crust has not yet hardened. It gives like bread and leaves the print of a boot that fills slowly with seep.

The settlement sits on the lower terraces and along the basin's flat middle, where the rock has been cut, poured, and bolted. The buildings are a mix of quarried stone, corrugated iron, and cast concrete, and they are arranged with a lot of gaps between them. There are long low sheds for the pumps, a few brick chimneys, and a cluster of tanks wrapped in insulation that has gone black and soft with damp. Pipes run everywhere: along walls, across the ground in trenches, up the cliffs in brackets that the travertine has begun to swallow. Several pipes are already encased in white growth, so that they look like bones in the act of being absorbed. Nobody has tried to remove them. The rock is faster.

**Heat and water**

It is hot down here in a close, damp way. The geothermal seams under the basin push warmth up through the floor, and the pools steam faintly in the mornings. Sweat does not evaporate; it stays on the skin. Condensation beads on the iron, streams down the cliffs in thin threads, and collects in the low places where the pumps try to keep it moving. The main industrial work is water: a battery of pumps that lift seepage out of the lowest sumps and push it up through the cliffs to the surface, so the lower town stays a little above the rising pools. The pumps run on a long cycle, their engines swelling and falling over hours, and the whole basin seems to breathe with them.

**Sound**

The loudest thing is the machinery, but it is not a single noise. It is layered: the deep throb of diesel engines, the high whistle of steam relief valves, the clatter of chain drives, the hiss of water running over stone. Under that is the sound of the cliffs dripping, a constant percussion across the whole bowl, which the residents no longer hear and visitors cannot stop hearing. Echoes return off the walls a half-second late, so that every sharp noise seems to come back from the other side of the basin. Voices carry badly and get swallowed by the engines.

**Ecology**

Vegetation is sparse and mostly fungal. Pale mats of slime-mold and blackish lichen cover the damp stone, and a few reeds thrive at the seeps where the water is warmest. Nothing grows tall. The reeds lean toward the shafts of light like patient drunks.

Fauna is overwhelming, and it has taken the basin. Insects are the most visible: moths the size of a palm, drawn to the lamps and the steam, and crowds of small flies that rise from the pools in clouds at dusk. Bats roost in every crack of the cliffs and come out in waves, and their droppings have built up into guano banks that smell sharp in the heat. Birds nest in the pipe-brackets and the rafters of the sheds, and their calls are sharp and frequent. In the pools live pale, blind creatures: crustaceans, eel-like things with frilled gills, something that moves in unison with others of its kind in slow ripples. The residents keep the sumps clear mostly to protect the pumps from them. Everything is crawling, flying, or swimming, and the stone is covered in it.

**Atmosphere and rules**

The place has a stillness that is not silence. It feels like a room that someone has left running. Nothing here is abrupt. The travertine grows over decades. The pumps cycle over hours. The pools rise and fall with the seasons of rain above. The town changes the way the rock changes, by accretion: a new shed here, a sealed gallery there, a wall that gets pushed back a few meters each generation as the water retreats or advances.

The rules of the world are the normal ones, with one local addition: nothing stays put. Whatever you build here, the basin will eventually take back, either through damp, growth, or the gradual pull of the rock. The people who live in it know this and build accordingly, with cheap materials, with spare parts stacked against need, and with a patience that looks, from outside, like resignation. Inside it, it reads more like care.
