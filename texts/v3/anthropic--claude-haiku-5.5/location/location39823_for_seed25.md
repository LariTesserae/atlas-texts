**The Hollows of Ash Basin**

You stand in a basin so wide that its far rim is only a suggestion, a darker line where the cloud meets the ground. The light here never comes from one direction. It seeps down through a lid of low, grey overcast that is thin enough in places to show a bruised, colorless brightness, and it is never quite enough to see by. Everything is half-lit, like the last minutes before a storm, and that light stays the same from one hour to the next.

The ground is not soil. It is a crust of pale mineral sheets, some of them meters across, laid over a fine grey clay that gives under the foot like wet felt. Where the crust has cracked, you see layers beneath it: rust-red bands of iron, white bands of silica, black seams that smell faintly of sulphur. The sheets curl at their edges and peel upward like old paper, and in places whole plates lift and clatter when the wind moves across them. There are no roots, no stems, no leaves, no moss. The only colour comes from the minerals, and it is muted: ochre, slate, bone, rust.

Water is everywhere and nowhere at once. The basin's floor is pocked with shallow pans, some still and mirror-flat, others alive with a slow seep from below that bubbles up through the clay. The pans are filmed over with a thin, iridescent skin that is not plant or algae but a dense mat of crawling micro-organisms that the basin's larger creatures graze. Rain comes in cycles, roughly every few days. It arrives as a long, low hiss across the basin, and the ground darkens within minutes. Between rains, the air holds a heavy, cold dampness, and every surface beads with condensation. Droplets gather on the hanging lattices and fall in slow, irregular drumming.

The air is never silent, and this is the first thing you learn about the place. The basin is a sound chamber. Its walls are high, sheer, and layered with stacked strata, and sound bounces off them and returns hundreds of meters later, so every noise overlaps with its own echo until the whole basin hums with continuous noise. The dominant sound is clicking: a dry, rapid, million-fold rattle, as though the ground itself were made of teeth. It comes from the fauna. Beneath the crust live great colonies of shelled, armored creatures, each the size of a fist or a dinner plate, that graze the mineral films and the micro-mats in the pans. They communicate by striking their plates against stone, and their collective noise forms a rhythmic, roaring texture that rises and falls as whole fields of them move in unison.

Above them, the air is full of wing-buzz. Flying forms like thin, membranous kites drift in tens of thousands over the pans, their wings whirring at a pitch just below hearing so that the sound comes as pressure more than tone. They skim the wet surfaces, dipping to take mineral dust from the water, and when a flock passes overhead the noise is physical, a pressure in the chest. Under all of it runs the deep, steady bass of the seep. Water pushes up from below through hidden fissures, and it gurgles, sucks, and pops like something breathing.

The fauna are overwhelming in number, and they are strange. Their structures are the basin's most remarkable feature. Across the flats stand columns of cemented grit, hollow tubes built by colonies of burrowing things that bind mineral dust with secreted glue. The columns range from knee-high to taller than a person, and their open tops whistle when wind passes across them. Some are shaped like spiral towers, others like branching trees made of stone. Their hollow chambers form resonating bells: when a rainstorm strikes them the whole basin rings with overlapping tones, a sound that the locals of this world, if there were any, would surely call a kind of song. Between the columns, lattice-webs of silica thread stretch from crust to crust, and wet droplets strung along them catch the dim light like beads on a broken necklace.

The basin runs on rhythms. Rain sets the tempo. When the first hiss of it reaches the flats, the clicking swells to a frenzy. Shelled grazers surface from the clay in their thousands, scrambling to the wet pans, and the flying forms rise in dense clouds to feed on the freshly loosened dust. The lattice-webs quiver, the columns ring, the seep throbs louder. Then, as the rain ends and the pans drain back into the clay, the basin falls into a brief, startling quiet. Creatures withdraw to their burrows and columns. The silence lasts perhaps an hour, a deep ringing hush, and then the clicking starts again, one colony at a time, until the roar is back.

Far off, toward the basin's centre, the crust rises into a low, sprawling ridge of old stone. The erosion here is ancient and complete: the rock is smoothed into long, rounded backs and sharp-edged shelves, streaked with mineral stains that look like dried blood. Nothing lives on the ridge itself. Its surfaces are too dry and too hard for the grazers, and the flying forms avoid it. It is the only place in the basin where the sound falls away, and standing on it you can hear your own breath again, and the faint, distant tick of a column settling in the cooling air.

From the ridge you can see the whole basin: the pans glinting like broken glass under the grey sky, the columns standing in their ranks, the flocks spiralling in the middle distance, the dim wall of the far rim. The place is entirely itself. No trail crosses it, no structure marks it, no sign suggests anyone has ever walked here, only the grazers, the flyers, the water, the stone, and the sound. It does not feel abandoned. It feels occupied by something that has never needed anything else, and that will go on clicking, humming, and pulsing long after the last rain has drained away.
