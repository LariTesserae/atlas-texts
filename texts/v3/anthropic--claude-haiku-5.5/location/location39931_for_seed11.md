**The Stilled Table**

You stand on a plateau that was once a mountain and has been worn down, over unimaginable spans, into something closer to a tableland. It rises perhaps four thousand meters above the lowland haze, and the air up here is thin enough to make your breath feel borrowed. Yet the heat is real, a close, wet warmth like the inside of a mouth. The mountain was pared down so long ago that its bones have become a stone floor, smoothed into long swells and shallow basins that look less carved than poured. The rock is pale grey and streaked with rust, and where water has stood in the hollows it has left white crusts like old salt on a pot.

Everything here is wet. The moisture does not fall so much as hang. Cloud sits on the plateau for most of the day, and when it thins, the light comes through in slanted, bruised shafts that catch in the spray. Dew never fully leaves the leaves. Condensation beads on bare stone and runs in thin sheets toward the basins, where it gathers into pools the color of weak tea. Rain arrives in sudden, total violence, a wall that erases the far edge of the world, then passes, leaving every surface dripping and every channel newly swollen. Between storms the air is heavy and quiet in a way that feels expectant.

The vegetation is lush beyond reason. It has colonized every crack and seam: ferns the height of a man, broad-leafed shrubs with waxy purple undersides, vines that thread across the stone like the veins of a leaf. Thick moss blankets the boulders, and in the lower basins grow reeds taller than a rider's head, their stalks hollow and whistling when the wind comes through. Where the soil is deepest, trees have taken hold, gnarled and buttressed, with aerial roots that hang like rope and drip constantly. Nothing is orderly. Everything is pushing, climbing, splitting the stone, and the stone is slowly, patiently giving way.

And the ground is restless. This is the strangest thing about the Stilled Table, a name the lowland people gave it because it looks so old and so still from below. Up close, nothing is still. The braided rivers that cross the plateau shift their courses every season, cutting new channels through soft silt and abandoning old ones, which dry to cracked beds and then fill again in a single flood. Basins that held lakes last year are mud-flats now, and mud-flats are crisscrossed with fresh runoff. Slabs of hillside loosen and slide without warning, and the sound of their going carries for kilometers. Steam vents hiss in the lowest hollows, opening and closing, and the ground around them is hot enough to crack a sandal sole. The place does not hold a shape for long. It is always becoming a slightly different ruin of itself.

The sound is the first thing that strikes you and the last thing you forget. It is deafening, a layered roar that never fully stops. Waterfalls thunder from every rim of the tableland, and the spray from them drifts in a constant mist. The rivers rush and grind over boulders. Reeds hiss and clatter. Rockfall booms somewhere out of sight. Wind moves through hollow stalks and through the gaps in the stone, producing a low, pulsing note that you feel in your chest more than hear. Birds call in clusters, sharp and metallic, and then the sound swallows them. Speech is nearly impossible beyond arm's length. People here learn to communicate with their hands and with a whistling code that carries over water.

Yet the place is nearly empty. Its population is small and scattered, a few hunting camps in the lee of the big boulders, a village or two wedged into the terraced slopes below the waterfalls. You can walk for a whole day without passing a living soul. Between presences lies a huge, wet, roaring silence of stone and cloud, and that emptiness is part of what makes the human marks so poignant.

They are scattered and mostly broken. A stepped terrace, dry-stacked from flat slabs, climbs one slope and then ends abruptly where a slide took it. A cairn stands at a crossing, its top stones replaced so many times that the pile has shifted from one generation to the next. Someone once built a watchtower of fitted stone at the edge of a cliff; now only half of it remains, and vines have knitted its walls together. Scattered in the mud are artifacts: a bone needle, a shard of black glassy stone, a carved wooden handle swollen and split by moisture. A rope bridge spans the widest gorge, woven from some fibrous vine and replaced in pieces, so that no single strand is original. These things are not monuments. They are the traces of people who came here to fish, to gather, to cross, and then moved on before the land could swallow them.

The tools are simple: stone-hafted hand axes, bone awls, digging sticks, nets of plant fiber, woven baskets sealed with resin. Fire is made with a bow drill and tended carefully, because the wet makes it hard to keep alive. Metal is a rumor, traded in small amounts from the lowlands and treasured as if it were rare medicine. Everything is repaired rather than replaced, because nothing lasts here. Rot, rust, moss, and the constant shifting of the ground undo any work that is not constantly renewed.

The light is ordinary daylight, diffused and milky through the cloud, and at dusk it turns a deep green-gold that makes the wet stone glow faintly. Nights are cool relative to the day but still warm by any lowland standard, and fog settles thickly after dark, muffling the roar just enough to make the stars, when they show, feel very far away.

Fauna is rare. You might see a large slow bird with a brilliant crest circling above the falls, or a heavy amphibian sunning itself on a warm rock. Insects are plentiful in the lower basins but rarely seen in the cloud. Larger animals are seldom glimpsed: a shape moving through the reeds, a splash in deep water, the prints of something heavy in the mud by dawn. The hunters speak of creatures that live in the hollow stone itself, but no one has ever confirmed them, and the stories do not bear repeating in the hearing of children.

The strangeness here sits just under the surface. Certain stones ring like bells when struck, and their notes do not match the rock around them. Some slabs of the old flat floor are warm even in the rain. In a few basins the water clears to a perfect stillness at dawn and reflects the sky with unnatural sharpness, then clouds again by midday. Trees grow in the rough curve of the nearest waterfall's spray, their trunks all leaning toward the same point, as though listening. No one can say whether these are signs or simply the way things happen when the old land and the living one keep grinding against each other.

It is a place that holds too much at once: ancient bones and fresh wounds, stillness and motion, emptiness and the roar of water everywhere. You feel small here, and you feel observed, though whether by the land or by the cloud or by something older than both, you cannot tell. Every step is wet, every surface is changing, and somewhere behind the roar, the mountain that was once a mountain is still, very slowly, falling apart into the shape it will take forever.
