The basin is about the size of a village green, bounded on three sides by low ridges of banded siltstone. The ridges have been worn so long that their edges have softened into rounded shoulders, like the knuckles of a hand resting on a table. The fourth side opens onto a slope that falls gently toward a lower plain no one can see from here, because a bank of grey vapour sits on it all the time.

The ground is a hard, pale clay that has cracked into polygons and then settled back, the cracks filled with fine silt and healed over with a thin green-black film. Where the clay meets the rock, the bedding planes of the siltstone show through in stacked ochre and rust stripes, each one a thin record of some ancient flat sea. Water has cut shallow channels across the floor and then, over time, stopped cutting, so the channels are smooth-bottomed and furred with moss-like mats that cling close to the stone.

The damp is constant but unhurried. It is not rain most days. It is a fine condensation that gathers on every surface until the rock looks oiled, and drops from the overhangs in slow, separate beads. Every few days a warm rain comes over the ridge, a soft grey fall lasting an hour or two, and then the sun returns as a pale disc behind cloud, giving more heat than light. The air holds warmth like a damp cloth does, so the temperature never really drops at night. It only gets a little heavier.

Light is dim and even, with no real shadows. Everything is lit from everywhere at once, which flattens distance. Edges blur at the far end of the basin, and the ridges dissolve into the haze at about a kilometre. Colours are muted: ochre, slate, the sage of the film-moss, the white of the clay. Nothing is bright.

Vegetation is sparse and low. Clumps of a stiff grey grass grow in the lee of the stones, never taller than a hand. There are a few shrubs with thick waxy leaves and bark like peeled leather, widely spaced, so that each one stands in its own patch of bare ground as if it had been given a room. Lichen covers the north faces of the rocks in rings and rosettes, slowly growing outward. Nothing here seems to be spreading quickly.

The built marks are few and quiet. Near the centre of the basin a low ring of fitted stones stands about knee-high, its gaps filled with clay and its interior sunk and grassed over, so it reads more as a depression than a room. Beside it lies a lintel stone, half buried, carved with a worn band of shallow grooves that might be decoration or might be weathering that happened to fall in lines. At the edge of the western ridge a cairn has collapsed into a fan of rounded stones, but a few of them still hold their original stacking. Somewhere in the clay near the channels there is a clean-edged slab with a square hole through it, the hole filled with sediment. Nothing here is complete. Everything is partly taken back, and the taking back has been slow enough that it looks finished.

There is no animal life. No insects move through the grass, no birds cross the sky, no burrows open in the clay. The only motion is water: the slow bead-drop from the overhangs, the thin seep through the channels, the occasional small sound of a stone slipping a fraction of a millimetre under its own weight. Sound is mostly quiet. Sometimes the drip from the rock falls into a small pool and makes a single round note that takes a long time to fade. Then the quiet returns and sits over everything like the air.

The rules of this place are simple. Nothing is hurried. Water comes and goes but the pattern never changes. Plants grow no faster than the stone wears. The marks left by whoever built here are older than the lichen's slowest rings, and the basin has agreed with itself a long time ago about what it is. Walk across it and you will feel no gradient change under your feet, no shift in the light from one hour to the next, only a steady damp warmth and the sense that the place was already finished before you arrived and will still be finished after you leave.
