**The Drowned Works at Corvane Reach**

The town sits at the bottom of a warm, shallow sea that filled the valley after the dam failed, or the ground sank, or both, a long time ago. The water is not clear. It is the color of weak tea held up to a lamp, thick with silt and dissolved iron, and it carries a faint green cast from the growth it holds. Surface light arrives as a bruised, diffuse glow that thins with every meter of depth. At the level of the streets it is dusk that never ends, enough to make out outlines and color, not enough to read a face at more than a few yards.

The town was built for smelting and fabrication. Its bones are still legible: long brick sheds with saw-tooth roofs, a cooling tower standing at the water's edge with its upper third breaking the surface like a stalk, rows of company housing stacked three and four stories along terraces that followed the old valley wall. Gantry cranes stand over the quays, their cables sagging into the water in long catenaries furred with pale growth. Rail lines run out from the works and vanish beneath silt, but their tracks reappear in places, rusted into the bed like the spine of something that lay down to sleep. Every street is a canyon of brick, concrete, and corrugated iron, and the water inside them is never still. It moves slowly, in the rhythm of currents pushed through the streets by temperature differences between the lit surface and the cooler silt.

Almost every surface is occupied. Vegetation has taken the town in layers. The lower floors of the housing blocks are wrapped in broad, ribbed kelp-like fronds that sway with the current, their stalks anchored in window frames and drainpipes. Above them, in the upper stories and on the rooftops where light is strongest, mats of something between moss and coral spread over the brick, thick and velvety, in shades of olive, rust, and deep violet. Climbing vines, pale and jointed like bamboo, have threaded through the old machinery: through the gear housings of the mill wheels, around the girders of the gantries, along the rail sleepers. Where the works released heat and effluent for decades, the bed is covered in dense beds of tube-growth that cluster in colonies, each one a bundle of hollow stalks ending in soft feathered tips that open and close with the tide of the water.

The materials have softened into the environment. Brick has gone dark and slick, its mortar eaten into lace. Iron has grown a crust of rust and calcium, so that railings look like organ pipes wrapped in lichen. Glass in the factory skylights is mostly gone, but where it remains it has clouded to a milky opacity. Copper piping, vented to the water long ago, has verdigrised into bright turquoise veins that trace the building walls. Asphalt on the streets has buckled into soft ridges, and silt lies in fine drifts in the doorways and along the curbs, so that a walker, if there were one, would wade through ankle-deep sediment that puffs up like smoke with every step.

Nothing here moves quickly. The water's temperature rises and falls with the seasons above, and the gradients drift through the town over months. Silt slowly fills the lower rooms; the fronds creep up the walls by a few centimeters a year; a cable that sagged last decade has settled another hand's width into the mud. The big processes are patient: the sediment is rising, the vegetation is thickening, the iron is flaking into the water and recombining into new minerals that bloom in orange and black on the old pipework. A few of the cranes have gradually tilted, so slowly that you could only tell by comparing against the crossbeam of a building beside them.

There is still machinery, and some of it still works in its way. A pump house on the north quay sits in an unlit basement, its intake grille furred solid with growth, and it hums now and then, a deep low note felt more than heard, when the pressure of the tide turns its blades a few degrees. Some of the old turbines, driven by the slow movement of the water, turn once every several minutes, groaning. A few panels of the factory's old lighting array still glow faintly, dim orange, on timers that nobody set. These are the only sources of light besides the surface glow, and they make islands of color in the murk.

Sound is rare and muffled. Water absorbs most of it and carries what remains as a soft undifferentiated hush. Occasionally a loose plate shifts on a roof and a dull clank rolls through the streets like a distant bell. The tube-colonies make small, wet clicks as their feathered tips close. The turbines groan. The water, when it moves past a broken pipe, produces a long low sigh that comes and goes. Otherwise the place is very quiet, the kind of quiet that makes you aware of your own pulse.

Fauna is sparse. A few slow, flat-bodied creatures graze on the moss mats in the upper stories, moving in a way that suggests they have learned the rhythm of the current, drifting upward when the water thins and settling back down at dusk. Small silver-scaled fish shelter in the pipes and in the lee of the cooling tower, darting out in tight groups and vanishing. Something larger, a long pale shape, sometimes passes at the edge of the light near the old quays, never close enough to identify. Most life here is vegetal or microbial, a cloud of drifting particles that gives the water its color and that the fronds feed on in dense, slow cycles.

The town's rules are the rules of its slow ecology. Growth goes where light and nutrients collect: on rooftops, on the sunny edges of the cooling tower, around the outflow pipes where the iron-rich water drains. Silt goes where the current slows: in the doorways, in the lee of the sheds, in the basements where the pumps have stopped. Machinery survives where it is still turned by the water, and decays where it isn't. The fronds and mats are the town's real residents now, and they are reshaping it one season at a time, binding loose brick, rounding corners, filling gaps, turning the works into something the water is slowly learning to hold.

Standing in the street, you would feel the packed density of it: every surface covered, every gap filled with some slow life or drifting sediment, the water pressing against you with a weight that is not oppressive but total. The light is always dim and always changing, shifting between murky amber near the surface and grey-green at the bottom. Nothing demands your attention, and everything continues. The town is not dead. It is settling, slowly, into a new equilibrium with the sea that took it, and the sea is patient.
