**The Sunken Quarter**

The place is a bowl about four hundred meters across, and its floor is the old market of a town that no longer has a name anyone uses. From the rim you can see that the bowl was once a plaza ringed by three-story buildings, but the buildings have become lattices. Their walls are still there, dressed in grey fired brick and dressed stone, but everything is wrapped and threaded by vines as thick as a man's thigh and by roots that have gone into the mortar and lifted it apart over generations. The stone is not so much collapsing as being slowly rewritten. Window openings are now shaped by the plants that grew through them and were never pruned, so each one has a rounded, organic outline, like an eye half closed.

The air is thick and warm, about the temperature of a held breath, and it smells of wet bark, sweet rot, and something metallic, like old coins. Light comes down through a canopy of broad leaves in shafts that never quite reach the ground. Most of the light lands on the rim, on the upper branches, on the tops of the walls. The floor is green-gold and dim, and things there are easy to lose.

The rain comes every afternoon, reliably, from a sky that turns the color of wet slate about an hour before. It starts as a patter on the highest leaves, then rolls down through the canopy in layers, each tier giving a different note as it strikes: broad leaves drum, thin ones hiss, the bark of the trunks ticks. By the time the water reaches the paving stones it has been sorted into a dozen sounds. The gutters, long since choked, run again for the length of the storm. Then the rain stops all at once, and the drip continues for an hour, falling from every leaf in the bowl like a slow clock.

Density is strange here. The vegetation covers every surface, and the mass of living plant is enormous, but the space between the plants is almost hollow. The vines throw long gaps across the plaza, and the air in those gaps is empty and echoing. Standing in the open is like standing in a cathedral that has lost its roof. You can see a long way through the trunks and still see almost nothing alive in that long view.

The sound is the main presence, and it is nearly unbearable. Frogs in the drains sing in a rising chorus that never falls silent. Insects in the leaves grind out a shrill, dense note, and beneath it the air hums with the wingbeats of things too small to see. Calls come from every direction, sharp barks from the upper canopy, a wet double-knock from under the paving, the rattle of something dragging itself through the vines. Every few minutes something large crashes through the foliage on the rim, and the whole bowl answers with a flurry of alarm calls that rolls around the walls and comes back doubled. Nothing here is quiet, and the quiet moments are only the gaps between loud ones.

Fauna is abundant and mostly unseen. Things move in the lattice, and you catch flashes of color, a long tail sliding between two bricks, a cluster of eyes reflecting the light from a dark window. Fruit falls constantly from the upper branches, split open and crawling. Small hunters ambush the fallen fruit, and larger predators follow the smaller ones. The predator-prey cycle is visible as a pulse across the whole quarter: a sudden silence spreading out from one spot, a scramble, and then the chorus slowly resuming, one voice at a time.

The plants are doing the building. Vines pull at the cornices and push new arches out of old ones. Roots thicken and harden into buttresses that hold up walls the mortar can no longer hold. Every year the lattice tightens and reshapes the streets. Paths that were open last season are closed by new growth, and cleared lanes reappear where a fallen trunk has opened a gap. The map of the quarter changes as you walk it.

The weirdness is not dramatic, but it is persistent. Some doorways open onto the same room they lead out of. You can leave a building by the back, walk ten steps through the vines, and find yourself at the front door again, though nobody has moved. At the center of the plaza is a fountain whose basin is full of standing rain. Sometimes the water in it runs upward, a thin stream rising to the lip of the carved basin and falling back, only briefly, and no one who has tried to watch it has ever caught the moment it started. Sounds sometimes arrive before the things that make them. A bird's call drifts over the rim a full breath before the bird opens its beak. And once or twice in each storm the rain has been seen to stop in midair, hang in still threads across the plaza for the length of a heartbeat, and then resume falling, as if the world had lost its place and then found it again.

Time moves oddly too. The vines grow visibly if you watch one stretch of stone for a few minutes. The fruit ripens in an hour. The rain comes at the same time each day, but the storms vary between a drizzle and a full deluge, and no two are alike. The quarter is never still, and never quite the same from one afternoon to the next. It is an old place that keeps being made again, out of brick and root and water and sound, by a plant that treats the ruins of a town as just another soil.
