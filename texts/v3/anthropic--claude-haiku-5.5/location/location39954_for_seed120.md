# The Cistern of Unfinished Stairs

The mountain is not finished. It has never been finished. From the outside it looks like a stepped wall of earth and stone climbing into a sky the color of old brass, terrace above terrace, each one a little narrower than the last, until the top is lost in a cap of cloud that drips hot water. The air at this height should be thin and cold. Instead it is thick, humid, and warm as the inside of a mouth, because something beneath the mountain is breathing heat up through every seam.

Nothing here is natural ground. Every surface was placed by hand. Clay was pressed into forms, dried in the sun, stacked, and mortared with a paste of ash, resin, and crushed shell. The builders had simple tools: wooden paddles, bone trowels, cord measured against knotted lengths. Their work is everywhere and it is still in progress. Some walls stop halfway through a course. Stacks of unfired bricks sit in the rain and slump into brown puddles. A scaffold of bundled reeds rots beside a half-raised arch. Erosion has not had time to age any of it. The clay is raw and soft, the grain of the stone still sharp where it was cut, and the rain is already dissolving the edges back toward mud. The mountain is being made and unmade at the same rate.

## The Pocket

Deep in one of the middle terraces, behind a wall that was never completed, is a room no bigger than a family's sleeping hut. The builders called it a cistern, though no one remembers filling it. It is a pocket cut into the mountain's flank, roofed with fired tile, floored with a shallow basin of grey plaster. Its scale is intimate, and the world outside does not reach into it except through a few slits and seams.

The density inside is almost unbearable. Every surface is occupied. Moss lies on the walls in thick green pelts, and ferns sprout from the mortar joints in tight fists. Vines hang from the roof tiles, their leaves overlapping like scales, so that the ceiling is a single layered pattern of green. Roots push through the clay and bulge the walls, and fungal shelves grow out of the roots in ruffled white and ochre. There is no bare space. Even the air is crowded with spores, pollen, and drifting seed fluff that catches the dim light.

## Light and Sound

Light comes from three sources, all of them weak. A narrow slit high on the east wall lets in a grey wedge of dawn, which moves across the floor over the course of the day and never quite reaches the far side. The fungal shelves glow a sour yellow-green, bright enough to read by only if you have been sitting in the dark for a long time. And a pool of standing water in the basin reflects the tile roof in a dull sheen that makes the whole room seem to hang upside down.

The room is loud. Water drips constantly from the tiles into the tin bowls that people have placed beneath the leaks, and each drop rings a different note as it strikes. The bowls are arranged in no clear pattern, and the overlapping tones make a slow, uneven music. Insects whine in the vines, a thick buzzing that rises and falls with the warmth. Somewhere beneath the floor the mountain's vent pulses, a deep throb felt more than heard, like a second heartbeat under the stone. When the wind shifts, the slit lets in the sound of other terraces: hammering, a woman singing a counting song, the slap of wet clay on a wall.

Fauna is rare. A few pale, nearly blind creatures shelter in the damp underside of the basin, moving in slow arcs when disturbed. Their bodies are translucent enough to show the ribs of their small skeletons, and they feed on the fungus and the slime that coats the plaster. Once in a while a moth the size of a palm drifts through the slit, circles the glowing shelves, and vanishes into the vines. No one here keeps animals. The room belongs to plants, water, and the people who maintain the bowls and clear the drains, though they seldom stay long. The air is too thick, the heat too close.

## Breakdown

This is the part that does not hold still. The rules of the Cistern are loose, and they seem to loosen further the longer you stay.

The stairs are the first thing to go wrong. There are nine steps leading down from the slit to the basin, and every person who counts them gets a different number. Some count eleven. Some count six and feel a seventh step under their foot that is not there when they look. The builders' cord marks, cut into the plaster at intervals, do not match any count. If you climb back up without looking, you arrive at the slit in a different place than you started, and the wedge of light falls on a different wall.

The water in the basin runs uphill. Not in the sense of flowing in a flood, but in a thin, persistent film that climbs the plaster lip in the afternoon and recedes at night. Drops fall from the tiles and bounce upward before settling. The pool reflects a second roof, one with the vines grown thicker and a figure standing beneath it, though no one is there. The reflection is patient. It does not move when you do, and when you look away and back, it is closer to the surface.

The clay is also wrong. Some of the unfired bricks stacked by the wall carry fingerprints pressed into them, small and clearly made by hands that are not the hands of the people working nearby. The prints are fresh, still wet, and a few of them seem to have been made while you were watching. A row of bricks near the slit bears a half-finished inscription, and each time you return, the inscription is one character longer, in a script that you almost recognize.

Time loops at the edges. The dripping from one tile falls in a rhythm that repeats every seventeen drops. Sometimes a bowl rings twice in the space of one drop, or the sound of a hammer from another terrace arrives a breath before it is struck. The insects in the vines fall silent in unison and then start again from the same phrase, as if a recording had been wound back.

## Ecology in Motion

Despite its strangeness, the Cistern is alive and busy. The fungal shelves grow visibly, extending a fingernail's width each day. The ferns push new fronds from the joints every few hours. Roots probe the clay, cracking it, and the cracks fill with resin that hardens into amber veins. Water seeps through the walls and carries dissolved lime into the basin, which hardens into a crust around the edge. Erosion and growth work in the same place, one dissolving the mountain while the other builds a new skin over it.

The pale creatures in the basin feed on the fungus, and the moths feed on the pollen and the fungal spores in turn. The insects in the vines feed on the sap of the ferns, and the wasps that hunt them lay eggs in the clay walls, sealing the mud over the larvae. Every process interrupts another, and none of them finishes. The room is a machine of constant unfinished work, with the builders, the plants, the rain, the heat, and the broken rules of the place all pushing at once.

## Summary

The Cistern of Unfinished Stairs is a hidden, crowded, hot, and wet pocket in a mountain that is still being built. It is packed with lush growth, loud with water and insects and the pulse of the vent below, lit only by dim dawn and glowing fungus, and occupied by a handful of rare, pale creatures. Its tools are hand tools, its architecture is raw, and its rules are breaking. The stairs cannot be counted, the water climbs, the reflections wait, and the clay carries prints from hands that have not yet touched it.
