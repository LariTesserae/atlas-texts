# The Weeping Lowlands

The land sinks so gently you notice it only when the water arrives. It has been sinking for a very long time. Ridges of old basalt, rounded by ten thousand thousand rains, rise a few meters above a plain that is more water than ground. The plain is a marsh that has settled into its own shape. Nothing here is young. The stone has forgotten its edges, and the channels have worn themselves into permanent grooves that the water follows as if it were reading them.

The light is always late in the day, though the clock says otherwise. A thick grey-green overcast hangs low and holds the moisture. The air is cool and saturated, and everything it touches beads and runs. Your skin stays damp. Your breath becomes a faint fog that doesn't quite leave you. Light seems to rise from below as much as fall from above. The fungal mats on the water's surface glow a pale teal, and their reflections sit under the surface like second skies, so you walk between two dim horizons.

## The Rocks That Weep

The stone here is porous and alive with seepage. Every boulder sweats. Water beads from fissures, runs down faces in gleaming threads, and collects in shallow bowls worn into the tops. Some rocks weep in the wrong direction. You will see a thread climb a few centimeters up a cliff face, hang there, and then curl back down, as though the stone had changed its mind. The geologists who never came would say the capillary tension is misbehaving. The local lichens just grow where the water decides to pause.

Sometimes a rock stops weeping entirely for a day. Then every nearby pool drops a few millimeters, all at once, and the sound of it rolls across the flats like a sigh.

## Lush and Crowded

The vegetation is dense enough that the plain never shows its full extent. Reed-like stalks rise three or four meters and bend together into arches. Broad-leaved plants with translucent undersides float on the water, layered over each other like scales, and thick moss carpets the drier hummocks in a velvet that springs back when you step off it. Between the plants hang vines that trail into the water and drink from it, then send the water upward again through hollow stems. You can hear it moving inside them.

The plants are not still. Their leaves unfold slowly, following the light that rises from the pools. The reeds lean toward each other in patterns that repeat over hours. The whole mass seems to breathe in a long rhythm, tides of growth and withdrawal that tie the ground to the sky. The tide-line is not water here. It is green.

Fauna is scarce. A few things move: a long-legged insect that skates across the pools leaving trails of light, something with a flat back that surfaces once a day and sinks, and at dusk a bird-like shape that circles high and never lands. Most animals seem to have withdrawn into the ground or the water, leaving the plants to hold the place together.

## Sound

The loudest thing in the lowlands is the water. Drips strike hollow stone and ring with pitches that sustain and bend. Pools deepen and fall, and the falling sounds like breathing through a flute. Beneath everything runs a low hum from the substrate, felt in the chest more than heard, which rises and falls with the tides of the reeds. When the rains come, the whole plain becomes a single chord. The hollow stones fill and all sing their own notes, and the chord drifts across the flats for kilometers.

Sometimes the sounds arrive before their sources. You hear a splash on the far side of the marsh and feel it land on your own cheek a second later.

## Reality Loosens

The rules here are soft. Distance is unreliable: a ridge that looks an hour's walk away is close enough to touch, and a pool three steps off has no bottom. Shadows fall in directions the light doesn't justify, and they lag behind the things that cast them, as if the world is a half-second slow to update. The water sometimes holds reflections of things that are not present, and those reflections move with their own weather.

The land appears to be doing work that nothing could be doing. The moss mats ripple in standing-wave patterns that map the subsurface currents, and the patterns change predictably enough that you could guess the coming rain by reading them. The channels rearrange slowly over seasons, as if the terrain were redrafting itself according to a plan that exists in the water rather than in anyone's head. Engineering without engineers: a system of drainage and growth that regulates itself with a precision no living thing should manage, and that no one built.

Walk here long enough and you start to feel the place is watching how you move, not out of hostility, but out of a patient interest, the way a river watches a stone it intends to wear away.
