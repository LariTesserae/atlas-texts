# The Silt Fens of Cold Accretion

The land here is still being born. Forty kilometers inland from a grey sea, a glacier's last retreat left a plain of fine rock flour, and the rivers that carry it have not yet decided where it should go. The ground is flat, a few meters above the water table, and it changes every season. Some mornings a bar of new mud shows where open channel was the night before. Some afternoons a sandbank that held a rail spur for a decade slides back under a brown spread of current. Nothing here is finished.

The cold is steady and damp. Winter air sits at just above freezing, and the moisture never leaves. Fog lies in the low places from dusk until mid-morning, and in the cold hours a fine condensation beads on every surface: rail rails, leaf undersides, the copper of the old wiring, the wet hides of the animals. Rain falls in soft, unhurried spells, and between them the air holds water like a sponge. Light is ordinary, a pale even daylight that comes through the haze without shadows, so that everything looks equally near and equally far.

## The Water

Water here is not one thing. There is the main river, wide and the color of weak tea, moving fast enough to carry sediment but slow enough to drop it. There are the braided channels, dozens of shallow threads that split and rejoin around islands of gravel. There are the standing pools in the hollows, some rimmed with white crusts of mineral salt, some black and still and full of drifting algae. And there is the seep, the water that rises through the soft ground wherever the pressure changes, bubbling up through fresh silt and leaving small cones of grit.

The people who first built here called the land *Hwen*, a word that means roughly "the place that drinks." Old maps mark the flat as a lake; newer surveys show it drained and refilled in the same decade. The water table rises and falls with the melt upstream, and the city has learned to live with it by building on pilings, on rafts of timber and steel, on embankments that get raised every few years.

## The Vegetation

Growth here is wild and fast. Life arrives on the new mud within weeks: first a pale green film of algae, then hair-fine sedges, then reed walls taller than a person, then alders and willows, and in the drier rises, dense thickets of something like bramble that the locals call *ring-thorn*. Mosses carpet everything that does not move. Lichens cover the concrete. The lush green is almost oppressive in its thickness, and it blurs the edges of everything.

The plants are odd in ways that suit the industrial setting. Roots pull into drainage tiles and push them apart. Vines grow up insulated cable and turn the sheathing green and spongy. Certain reeds take up the dissolved metals that leach from old slag heaps and concentrate them in their stems, so that a stand of reeds near the foundries shows a faint blue-grey sheen in its leaves, and the harvesters burn it for its ash. There is a fern that opens its fronds only on the first day after a rain, and the whole fen seems to exhale when it does.

## The City

The city, called Corrowe, is built on the emerging ground in layers, each one sitting on the ruins of the last. At the bottom are the pilings, black timber gone hard as iron, driven into the silt generations ago. Above them are the old brick warehouses, sagging gently toward whichever way the ground is sinking. Above those are the working floors: foundries, pumping stations, the great chemical vats of the lower wards, all stacked and bridged and roofed over. And at the top, wherever the ground is high enough to hold them, are the newer towers of pressed panel and cast glass, looking down on the rooftops like a sea of machinery.

Industry dominates. Pumps run around the clock to keep the lower districts dry, and their housings thrum with a low continuous pulse that the residents stop noticing after a few days. Smelters burn with blue-white flares that light the fog from below. Rail lines run on raised causeways across the fen, and they are always being repaired, because the ground under them moves. Cranes lift cargo from barges moored in the braided channels. Everything is electrified where it can be, and the power comes from a row of coal-and-gas stations on the eastern bank whose plumes merge into the weather.

Scale is wide. Corrowe is one node of a larger regional network: other fen-towns sit on the same raw deposit, each at a different stage of formation. From the causeway you can see three other cities' smokestacks, and at night their glows form a low band along the horizon.

## The Sound

The place is mostly quiet, which surprises visitors. Fog and saturated ground swallow noise. Reeds absorb sound the way a sponge takes water. Even the pumps and smelters, which should be deafening, arrive as a low hum felt more than heard. Birdsong from the thickets carries a long way. Water makes its own sounds: the chuckle of the braided channels, the gurgle of seeps, the slap of waves against piling when the wind comes off the sea. In the early morning the whole fen hums with the sound of frogs and insects waking, a thin fluting that rises and falls in waves across the marsh.

Human noise comes in bursts. A whistle from a barge. The clang of a dock crane. Someone calling a name across a channel, and the call travelling a surprising distance before it dies.

## The Fauna

Animal life is abundant and sits everywhere between wild and domesticated. Water birds nest in the reed beds in enormous colonies, and their guano whitens the old warehouse roofs. Eels move through the drainage sumps in thick writhing masses, and the dock workers catch them with long-handled nets. Amphibians cover the wet ground in the warm months, and in the cold ones they burrow into the silt and wait. Small rodents breed fast in the thickets and are hunted by the feral dogs that run in packs along the causeways. Larger animals, deer-like grazers with splayed hooves, cross the braided channels in the mornings and sometimes wander into the lower wards, where they are chased off by children with sticks.

The strangest animals are the ones adapted to the industrial edge. A small crustacean lives in the warm outflow from the smelters and has developed a shell of fused slag grit. Flocks of gulls have learned to ride the thermals above the chemical vats and wait for the workers to drop food. The eels in the sumps have pale, bloated bodies from breathing concentrated effluent, and the fishers say that the largest ones carry faint rings of color along their flanks, like a warning or a map nobody has learned to read.

## The Strange

Corrowe runs on rules that do not make sense from the outside. The ground rises and falls by the season, so property lines are drawn in tide-marks, and deeds are renewed each spring after the surveyors come through with their poles and levels. Buildings are moved, not demolished: the foundry crews jack up whole structures and float them on barges to new sites when the ground gives way. Streets are named for the channel that used to run under them. Some blocks change ownership every few years, and the old families keep elaborate records of which parcels were fen and which were bank, because the difference determines the tax.

There are stranger things too. Some of the new ground carries a faint warmth, as if something is still cooking beneath the silt. Where it is warmest, the reeds grow in perfect circles, and the frogs gather in rings. The fishers avoid those places. The older engineers say the circles are where the bedrock is still cracking, but they do not say it with much confidence.

And there are the sudden inversions. A channel that carried barge traffic one week can silt up and vanish the next, leaving a stranded hull on a bank of new grey mud. A warehouse that sat on dry ground can be half-swallowed by a seep overnight. A reed bed can burst into flower across ten square kilometers in a single day, the pollen so thick that the sky goes yellow and the pumps clog with it.

## The Turbulence

The whole region is in constant motion. Rivers shift their courses, islands form and dissolve, plant colonies advance and die back, cities rise on new ground and sink into old. Corrowe's residents describe their home as a machine that is always rebuilding itself while it runs. The engineers' ledgers are thick with notes about subsidence, deposition rates, and the next flood, and the city's governing council meets every week to decide which district to abandon, which to raise, and which to let the fen take back.

Yet the chaos has a rhythm. The melt-floods come each spring. The fog lifts each morning. The fern opens after each rain. The reeds bloom in the late summer, and the eels run in the autumn. The people who live here have learned to read the instability as a kind of seasonal weather, a churning that keeps the land alive and the industry fed, and they build with the expectation that everything will one day be moved, drowned, or grown over.

Standing on the causeway at dawn, you see the whole fen at once: the braided water catching the pale light, the reed walls swaying in a wind you cannot feel, the smokestacks rising through the fog like the masts of a fleet that has run aground and is slowly being claimed by the mud. Somewhere out in the flats a new island is surfacing, dark and wet, and by the time the sun is fully up, something green has already started to grow on it.
