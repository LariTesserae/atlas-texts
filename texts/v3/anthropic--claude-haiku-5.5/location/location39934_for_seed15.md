**The Sunken Hall at Low Glare**

You are inside a room that is full of water. The hall is roughly the size of a village granary, perhaps twelve paces across and four or five deep, and it has been built entirely from cut stone, fired clay, and timber. Nothing here grew on its own. The floor is laid in square tiles, each one scored with a maker's notch. The walls rise in courses of dressed blocks, and the vault overhead is a ring of fitted wedge-stones with a single round opening at its crown. Through that opening comes the light, a hard white-gold column that enters the water and breaks into moving bars across everything below.

The water is warm, like a bath left in the sun. It is not still. A channel cut into the eastern wall pours in continuously, and the inflow is loud, a steady roar that fills the hall the way wind fills a gorge. Where it strikes the far wall it spreads into a fan of small waves that slap the stone and return. Underneath that sound come smaller ones: the knock of a timber paddle wheel turning on its axle in the outflow chamber, the clack of wooden pawls catching and releasing, the hiss of bubbles rising from seams in the floor. The whole place sounds like a mill that has forgotten its grain.

The stone is young. Its edges are still sharp. Chisel facets on the blocks have not yet been rounded by water, and in the corners pale stone dust drifts down through the light and settles in soft drifts that stir again every time the inflow surges. There are no old weathered surfaces here, no moss-softened corners, no sediment crusts. Everything looks as if it was finished last season. Some blocks are only half-dressed, their faces still rough, with cord-marks and wedge-holes that were never filled. A stack of spare tiles lies on a ledge, their glazed faces cloudy with algae.

Vegetation is thin but present. A green-brown film coats the lower courses where the light is weakest. In the seams between the tiles, a few rooted stalks have pushed up like grass, bending in the current and combing themselves straight again. They are sparse, a dozen here and there, each one a small green flag in a field of worked stone. Near the oculus, where the light is strongest, a mat of golden filaments spreads across one wall, pulsing slowly as it photosynthesizes. Its growth follows the sun's track through the opening, so over the course of a day the bright patch on the wall moves, and the mat extends or withdraws depending on how long it has been exposed.

Nothing lives here that moves on its own. There are no fish, no insects, no crawling things. The only motion is the water, the drifting dust, the turning wheel, and the slow green growth on the walls. The place is busy in the way a workshop is busy when its makers have stepped out: the machines are running, the materials are in process, the tools lie where they were set down. Notches in the tiles suggest sequences of placement, and a wooden template leans against one wall, its outline matching the arc of the vault. Someone was building this hall, and the building is still going on. The inflow keeps the reservoir full, the wheel keeps the bailing buckets rising and falling on their chain, and the outflow keeps the level constant. A pressure plate on the floor, worn smooth in a single spot, shows where a hand or foot once stood to adjust a sluice.

The light is the most striking element. It is brighter than sunlight through canopy, close to unfiltered noon, and the water magnifies it. Every surface ripples with caustic patterns that wheel across the walls like slow lattice-work. The brightness has a warmth to it; the stone heats in the sun and gives its heat back into the water, which keeps the hall pleasant to stay in. At the edges, where the light fades into the shadows of the vault, the water turns deep green, then nearly black, and the sound of the inflow seems to come from farther away.

The hall is small, closed, and fully made. Its rules are simple: water flows in, water flows out, light comes from above, and the stone is shaped by hand. Whoever designed it understood water as a tool, a means of moving things and keeping them clean and cool, and every element is a device serving that end. The place is not wild. It is a machine for living with water, built by people who have since gone, and still doing its work in their absence.
