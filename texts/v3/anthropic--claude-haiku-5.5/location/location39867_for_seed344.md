The pocket is a cleft about the width of a bedroom, cut into a shelf of black glassy rock that broke off a lava field some few thousand years ago and has not had time to weather. The edges are still sharp. Where a fist-sized piece has come loose, the fracture face is bright and unscarred, the color of wet slate. Everything here is recent and angular, as if the ground were still deciding what shape to be.

The floor sits a few meters above a lowland marsh. Warm water rises through a fissure at the back of the cleft, and the air is thick and close, about the temperature of a bath just run. It is never still. Vapor lifts off the seep in slow columns and meets the cooler stone at the cleft's mouth, where it condenses on every face and runs down in thin skins. The rock weeps continuously. Droplets gather at the lips of small overhangs, swell, and fall with a tick on the stones below. Over long years the runoff has etched pale mineral tracks down the walls, white and ochre, branching like the veins of a leaf that was pressed flat and dried.

Vegetation is sparse. Dark mats of a thermophilic film cling to the wettest rock, rust-red and olive, springy underfoot and faintly sulfurous. In the cracks where the crust is thickest, a few hardy sedges have rooted, short, grey-green, bent sideways by the constant steam. Nothing grows taller than a hand. The ground between clumps is bare, and the bareness has a crystalline sheen from salts the seep leaves behind as it dries.

The built things are few and scattered, the kind of remains you find by stepping over them. A corroded steel pipe, its flange bolted to the rock, runs from the fissure toward the outer lip and stops at a rusted valve wheel that no longer turns. A squat metal housing, the size of a chest freezer and blistered with orange scale, sits wedged in a notch near the back wall. Its gauge face is cracked, the needle long since frozen against a number no one remembers. A cable has been stapled to the stone and disappears into a crack. Below the housing, someone once painted a line on the rock. Most of it has flaked away, but a short arc of white remains, the curve of a letter or perhaps only a mark for depth.

The machinery is still working, and it is the loudest thing in the place. Somewhere inside the housing a pump labors on a slow duty cycle. It starts with a deep metallic groan, a long rising hum, then a hard mechanical knock, and then a pressurized hiss as water is forced through a restriction. The sound fills the cleft and bounces off the faces, so that every stone seems to be speaking at once. Under it, the steam vents hiss on a steady ground note, and the dripping adds a random percussion, quick and irregular, that the pump's rhythm never quite matches. You cannot hear a conversation over it. You can barely think. The noise seems to thicken the warm air.

Light is ordinary, a plain overcast daylight that comes down through the mouth of the cleft and lands on the wet faces with a flat, even shine. There are no shadows to speak of. Colors look slightly washed by the steam: the black rock goes grey at a distance, the rust mats glow, and the white mineral tracks catch what light there is and hold it.

Nothing lives here that moves. No insects drift in the humidity, no birds cross the gap above, no burrowing thing has disturbed the crust. The silence that should accompany such a place is replaced entirely by the machine's noise, which some visitors have described as a kind of hearing you cannot switch off.

The strangeness is slow. The pump has run for longer than anyone has counted, and its cycle has barely shifted. The mineral tracks lengthen by a hair each year, and the mats creep outward a finger's width per season. The crust over the fissure thickens so gradually that you could stand here through a whole human life and see only the difference between this morning and the first night you arrived. Water finds the same paths, the same drops fall from the same lips, and every process in the cleft is moving, but at a pace that makes change look like stillness. The place is not dying or growing. It is merely continuing, patiently, in the heat and the noise, one drop at a time.
