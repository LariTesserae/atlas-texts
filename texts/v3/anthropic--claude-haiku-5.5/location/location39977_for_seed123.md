**The Sump at Kessel Flats**

It is a hollow about the width of a village square, sunk a few meters into a plain so flat that the rim is barely visible until you stand on it. Nothing here is high or dramatic. The ground falls away so gently that you notice you are standing in a bowl only when the water starts appearing around your boots.

**Ground and materials**

The floor is young ground. Three or four decades ago it was the settling basin of a works that pumped brine and mineral slurry out of the aquifer, and when the works stopped, the basin was left to crack and slump. The surface is a pale grey clay that has split into plates curling at the edges like old paint. Where the plates have broken, they expose a darker, wetter layer beneath, and where that layer is exposed, the air smells of iron and wet pennies. The edges are sharp. Everything here still looks freshly made: the cut banks of the drainage channels are vertical and crisp, and the spill-ridges have not yet been softened by rain.

Scattered across the floor are the works' remains: concrete pads with bolt-stubs, a section of pipe the width of a man's chest lying half-buried and ringed with white salt crust, a pump housing standing on its side with its impeller exposed like a fossil shell. Fused sheets of corrugated steel have been folded into rough shelters, and their undersides are furred with rust-orange crust.

**Water**

Water here is not a river or a lake but a condition. Moisture comes out of the air and out of the ground. Each night a fine condensate beads on every surface, and by dawn the metal ribs of the old tanks are running. Shallow puddles collect in the clay plates and evaporate by midday, leaving rings of salt. A brine seep at the north wall keeps a permanent pool the size of a bathtub, its surface skinned with iridescent film. When it rains, which is seldom but heavy, the basin fills in an hour and drains through the broken sluice within a day, and the whole floor becomes a skin of moving water, tinted brown from the clay.

**Vegetation**

Plants are sparse but stubborn. Grey-leaved halophytes grow in tight tufts along the channel banks, each one a small fortress of succulent tissue. Thin reeds push up through cracks in the concrete, their stems stiff and hollow. There are no trees. The only large living things are a few stunted shrubs with bark like peeled rubber, growing from the rubble piles on the eastern lip.

**Fauna**

Here the density becomes almost oppressive. Every damp surface is occupied. Beetles with metallic carapaces crowd the wet clay in crawling layers; when you disturb the ground they lift in a shimmering cloud and resettle within seconds. Small crustaceans fill the brine pool so thickly that its water seems to move on its own. Above, swallow-like birds with long grey wings nest in every enclosed space the works left behind: inside the pump housing, along the pipe interiors, in the gaps of the steel shelters. Their nests are packed so tightly that the entrances look like a honeycomb of dark mouths, and the birds shuffle in and out in constant traffic.

Something larger moves beneath the clay. Its tunnels show as sudden collapses in the plates, and where the ground subsides the beetles pour in and out like smoke from a vent. Nothing has been seen of it directly.

**Weirdness**

The fauna has learned the machinery. The birds have stopped building their nests in natural crevices, preferring the pipe interiors because they hold warmth through the night, and because the pipes still carry a faint vibration that the birds seem to synchronize with; they leave and return in rhythm with the pump housing's slow settling creaks, though nothing powers it. At dusk the pipes hum for a few seconds, a low tone that every bird within earshot answers before falling silent. The salt crust on the old tanks grows in geometric patterns, cubic and stepped, as if following a template in the metal.

**Sound and light**

The place is mostly quiet. The loudest sounds are the sharp crack of clay plates cooling after sunrise, the drip of condensate off steel, and the steady whine of the beetles' wings when the cloud rises. Light is ordinary, a clear sky with a white haze near the horizon. In the bowl the shadows are short and the air shimmers over the wet clay in the afternoon.

**Dynamics**

Everything here moves on several clocks at once. Condensation builds overnight; the clay cracks and curls through the day; the beetles rise and fall with the heat; the birds cycle through feeding, nesting, and warming; the brine seep slowly thickens the pool; the sluice leaks and rusts a little more each month. The occasional large rain interrupts all of it, flooding the floor, scattering the birds, sealing the cracks, and then leaving the ground newly raw for the next cycle. The basin is always becoming something else, but never finishing.
