The place is a hollow in the top of a worn plateau, a bowl about the size of a small valley, sunk so deep into the high country that the sky shows only as a ragged strip overhead. The rock around it has been weathered for so long that its edges have rounded like river stones. Ridges that were once sharp have slumped into long, soft humps, and the cliffs wear a fur of lichen and moss. Nothing here is young. The stone is the color of old bone and old ash, and where it has split, the fractures have softened with a patina that takes the light.

Water is the strange absence. No stream runs through the bowl, and the old channels are dry grooves polished smooth, their floors packed with hardened silt that cracks into plates. Yet the place is green, almost drowning in green, and the greenness is not sustained by rain or rivers. Each morning fog climbs out of the lower cloud layer below the rim and spills over the lip of the bowl, and the vegetation has evolved to drink from it. The leaves are broad and waxy, shaped to funnel condensation toward their tips, where it drips in steady lines onto the leaves below. The trees have swollen trunks that hoard moisture like cisterns, and their aerial roots hang in curtains that catch every droplet. The understory is thick with ferns that unroll their fronds only when the fog is heavy. Moss covers everything, and the moss itself holds water in gleaming pockets that the animals drink from.

Light comes down through a canopy of interlaced branches and fog, so it is always dim and golden-green, like light through old glass or the bottom of a pond. Shafts reach the floor only at midday, and even then they are broken into shifting coins. The city lives in that dimness.

The built world sits in the walls of the bowl and across its floor. Settlers long ago cut terraces into the cliffs and laid dressed stone along their edges, then filled the gaps with timber frames and woven walls plastered with clay. The dwellings stack upward like honeycomb, their doorways reached by ladders, rope bridges, and staircases chiseled from the rock. Some buildings are older than anyone's memory, their foundations sunk so deep that they have become part of the cliff. Trees have grown through roofs and split walls, and in places the masonry is now more root than rock. The streets are narrow, paved with stone slabs that are pushed up and cracked by the roots beneath them, and the gaps are filled with moss and small white flowers that open only in the dimness.

The people here work with simple tools: flint blades knapped to a fine edge, bone awls, wooden mallets, fire-hardened spears, digging sticks, and woven baskets. They have no metal. Their craft is in stone and wood and fiber. They cut and fit blocks without mortar, dry-stack their terraces, and weave ropes from fig bark and plant fiber strong enough to hold a house-sized net. Smoke rises from many hearths in thin blue threads, and the smell of resin and roasting tubers drifts through the lanes. The sound of stone on stone rings from the workshops all day, and the hollow knock of wooden clappers carries across the bowl as a call to gather.

Fauna is overwhelming, and it is the first thing the senses register. The air is thick with insects, moths the size of palms, beetles with shell-plates that shimmer, midges that rise in columns at dusk. Bats pour out of the cliff crevices in a river of wings at sundown, and the sky fills with their chirring. Birds with long tails and bright throats call from every level of the canopy, and their calls overlap so densely that the ear cannot separate them. Lizards with frilled throats race up the trunks, and small arboreal mammals with prehensile tails leap between branches, dropping leaves and fruit. On the floor, grazers the size of goats browse the understory, cropping the ferns and ducking under the ropes. Predators move among them: long, low cats with mottled coats that stalk the grazers at the forest edge, and snake-like climbers that hang from branches and strike at anything that passes below.

The sound of the place is loud and layered. There is the constant drone of insects, a rising and falling hum like wind through reeds, punctuated by the sharp clatter of cicada-like clickers in the trees. Birdsong comes in bursts, then silence, then a clamor. Water drips from leaves in a continuous pattering that never stops, and every so often a branch releases its load in a soft collapse. Human sounds carry through it: hammers, calls, children's voices, the creak of rope bridges, the knock of clappers. At dusk, the bats and insects rise together and the noise becomes a roar that fades only after full dark.

The place is in constant motion, a machine of cycles. Each morning the fog rolls up and over the rim, condensing on the leaves and feeding the trees, and each evening it withdraws, leaving the canopy dripping. The bats and insects hunt in shifts, the predators follow the grazers down the slopes at dusk, and the grazers move to fresh browse with the seasons. There are slower rhythms too: the hardwood trees flower in long cycles and the whole bowl turns white for weeks, and the people time their harvests, their rebuilding, and their hunts to the shifting seasons of fog, fruit, and migration. Nothing stays still for long. Even the ancient stone is slowly flaking and rounding, and the roots of the trees keep working into the cracks, pulling the old city a little further toward becoming soil.
