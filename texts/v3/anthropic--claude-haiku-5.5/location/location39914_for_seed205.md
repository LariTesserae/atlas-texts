# The Mat Plateau

You stand on a high tableland, perhaps two thousand meters above where the lowlands began, though no one has measured it and no one needs to. The air is thin enough that your breath comes quick, and the sun is fierce in a way that feels personal, pressing on the back of the neck like a palm. Everything under it is green.

The green is not grass, and it is not forest. It is a skin. It covers the plateau in a continuous living mat, thick as a mattress in some places and a finger's width in others, woven from fibrous stems and broad leathery leaves that overlap like scales. Where the mat thickens, it rises into hummocks and ridges. Where it thins, you can see the dark, damp soil or the pale bones of old stone beneath. It is the substrate of everything here. The soil is only what the mat has eaten.

Across this living floor, the larger forms stand far apart. These are the *tower-trees*, grown from the mat itself, columns of pale bark wrapped in spiraling ridges, rising thirty or forty meters. Between one tower and the next there may be a kilometer of nothing but mat, rolling gently under the wind. The towers are so widely spaced that the plateau has a strange emptiness, a sense of being in an enormous room with very few pillars. Each tower crowns itself with a canopy of fanned, translucent sails that catch the light and throw shifting green-gold shadows across the mat below.

## The Water Cycle of Its Own

The vegetation breathes, and the breath makes weather. Every afternoon, the mat releases an enormous volume of moisture through its pores. By midday the air over the plateau is heavy and white, and the light goes milky. Mist pools in the hollows between hummocks. Then, around the third hour, the cloud builds into towers of its own and breaks over the plateau in short, violent rains that last twenty minutes and roar across the mat like a surf. Afterward the water runs in shallow braided channels through the moss, collects in dark brown pools in the low ground, and evaporates by dusk, leaving the mat steaming in the cooling air.

This is a damp world in pulses. Water is never far, but it is never still for long. Pools appear and vanish. Streambeds carve themselves and fill with silt, then dry to cracked plates. Things here have learned to live in the cycle: seeds that sleep through the dry hours and open in the rain, animals that drink in one brief window and burrow the rest of the day.

## The Sound

The sound is the first thing you notice, and it does not let up. The insects of the plateau are countless, and they sing in a continuous shrill layered roar that seems to come from the ground itself. Some are cicada-like, rattling in great pulsing choruses that rise and fall in waves across kilometers. Some are deeper, a hum that you feel in your sternum. Others click, hiss, or whistle in patterns that are almost but not quite rhythmic. Overhead, the sail-canopies of the towers host birds with throats like bellows, calling in complex overlapping phrases that sound almost like speech. When the afternoon storm arrives, the insects fall silent for a few breathless seconds, and then the whole plateau erupts again as the rain stops, as if the land were exhaling.

Walking here means shouting to be heard by a companion three steps away. It also means that you learn to read the sound. A change in pitch can warn of a predator moving through the chorus. A sudden hush can mean rain is coming. The chorus is a map.

## The Fauna

The animals are everywhere and in great numbers, but they are mostly small. Beneath the mat, burrowing creatures with segmented bodies tunnel and feed on the roots, their passage marked by faint ridges that rise and collapse like breathing. On the surface, the hummocks teem with crawling and hopping things, many banded in green and rust to match the mat. Some are soft and slow, grazing the leaves in long files. Others are quick, hunting them. Predator and prey move through the mat in constant, rippling pursuit, and the mat itself shivers and bulges wherever something runs under it.

The large animals are rarer but present: heavy, slow-moving grazers with flat, shovel-shaped heads that crop the mat in wide arcs, and sleek long-legged hunters that move in silent groups through the tower-shadows. Above all of it, the air is full of flying things, from small darting gleams to vast slow shapes that drift on the thermal updrafts above the towers like kites.

## The Stone and the Marks

The plateau is old. Erosion has been working here for longer than memory, and the land is in a settled, weathered state. Stone shows through the mat in low, rounded outcrops, their surfaces grooved and pitted by rain and rubbed smooth by generations of wind-borne grit. Some outcrops have been shaped by hands. There are shallow hollows in the rock, worn by grinding, where something was once pounded into meal. There are cairns, low and scattered, stacked from flat slabs and half-swallowed by the green.

People have lived here. Their traces are sparse, a few structures per hundred kilometers: a ring of stones with a hearth-blackened center, a shelter framework of lashed poles now grown through with living vines, a line of marks scratched into a boulder that may be counting or may be a map. The tools they left are simple, flaked stone and fire-hardened wood, the sort of kit that works without metal. Nobody who uses these things is in sight. Nobody has been in sight for some time. The mat has reclaimed most of what they built, growing over the hearths and softening the edges of their walls until the difference between shelter and hummock is hard to tell.

## Strangeness

Some things here do not follow the expected rules.

The mat moves. Not in wind, though it does ripple with wind, but in slow, deliberate swells that pass across the plateau every few hours, like the long swell of an ocean. The swells lift the hummocks, then settle them, and anything resting on the mat rises and falls with them. Old tracks shift position across days. Nobody knows whether this is growth, a kind of collective pulse, or the land's way of breathing.

Certain stones bloom. When rain soaks them, thin pale threads push out of their cracks, spread across the surface in a delicate lacework, and within a day produce small, bright, fragile flowers that smell sharply of iron. By the next dry afternoon the flowers have dropped their petals and the threads have withered, leaving the stone faintly stained. The stains form patterns, and the patterns persist for years in layers, like rings in a tree.

At the edges of the largest tower-shadows, the air sometimes goes still and cold. Animals avoid these pockets. Things drop out of the sound there, and the light turns grey. Nobody has yet worked out whether this is a physical property of the ground or something about the towers themselves.

## Living Here

The plateau is not hostile, exactly. It is dense with life in its own way, and it is intensely active: weather, growth, predation, and the slow pulse of the mat all run at once. But it is also vast and sparse in its presences, so a traveler can walk a long day and see only the sails of one tower, the glint of one pool, and the faint trail of one pale creature crossing the green. Those who know the place move carefully, travel in the cool hours before the afternoon mist, shelter in the tower-shadows at midday, and listen constantly. The sound will tell them what is near, and the mat will tell them what the land is doing.

The air smells of sap, wet stone, and the faint sweetness of the mat drying in the sun. At dusk, when the insects reach the crescendo of their evening chorus and the last rain-light fades from the sails, the whole plateau seems to hum like a struck bell, and the mat settles, breathing slow, under a sky going violet over the endless green.
