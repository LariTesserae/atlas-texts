**The Sunken Dry Bowl**

You stand on the lip of a high basin, perhaps four kilometers across, ringed by pale cliffs of layered mineral that the wind has carved into ribs and terraces. The air is thin and cool, and the light is ordinary daylight, clean and unfiltered, so the colors come through true: rust, bone, a faint cobalt in the shadows where the cliffs fold. Nothing here is wet. There are no streams, no pools, no dew on the morning stones. The rock is dry to the core, and yet the basin is green.

**The ground**

The floor is a crust of fine, glassy grit, pale gray where it's fresh and nearly black where it has weathered. It rings faintly underfoot, like ceramic. In places the crust has lifted into low domes, hollow when you tap them, that split open at the crown and release a fine powder into the wind. Deeper runs of cracked earth web across the basin in polygons a few meters wide, and along their seams grows a dense, fibrous moss the color of tarnished copper. The moss is the first sign that something here is alive without needing water.

**The vegetation**

The lushness is startling. Thick, ribbed stalks rise from the crust like the trunks of ferns grown in a greenhouse, some of them taller than a house. Their fronds are thick and waxy, fluted to channel something across their surfaces, and they gleam with a viscous, amber sap that beads and runs slowly down toward the roots. The sap is not water. It sets to a tacky resin when exposed, and where it has pooled on the crust it has hardened into translucent plates, some of which hold the trapped shapes of insects and small things that were stuck fast before they could struggle free.

Higher on the terraces the growth turns strange. Broad, flat leaves stand upright like sails, tracking the sun. They hum faintly when the wind passes through them, and their edges are lined with tiny crystalline filaments that scatter light into a soft, fractured haze. Between the great plants, low clumps of a silver-leafed shrub close their pores at midday and open them at dusk, breathing out a faint sharp odor that tastes of metal and ozone on the tongue.

**The fauna**

The basin is crowded with animals, far more than the dry ground should support. Most are small. Segmented crawlers the size of a hand move in dense, shifting columns across the crust, tapping the grit with their feelers in a rapid, rattling rhythm. Winged things, with translucent wings etched in faint iridescent veins, hang in clouds above the resin pools and dive in unison when the wind changes. Larger grazers, heavy-bodied and armored in overlapping plates, move slowly through the moss, lowering their heads to scrape the copper fibers into their mouths and grinding them with a sound like gravel in a mill. They do not drink. Watching them, you understand that they get their moisture from the sap and from something in the air that you cannot see.

Some animals are very still and very patient, perched in the crooks of the fern-trunks, their eyes reflecting the light in flat mirror-disks. They do not move unless the crawlers come too close.

**The sound**

The basin is deafening. The moment you step below the rim, sound closes over you like water. The crawlers' rattle forms a constant base layer. Over that comes the grazers' grinding, the hum of the sail-leaves, the high whine of the winged swarms, and a deeper, irregular thudding that seems to come from beneath the crust. Occasionally a dome splits open with a crack like a rifle shot, and the powder hisses out for a long moment after. The wind through the cliffs produces a moaning, almost musical note that rises and falls. You cannot hear one thing alone here. You hear the whole basin breathing at once.

**The built**

Scattered across the floor are structures that are no longer really buildings. Some are rings of pale, glassy posts, each taller than a man, set in arcs that suggest the outlines of rooms that have long since lost their walls. The posts are faintly warm to the touch and sometimes vibrate, as though they are still running something. Others are clusters of lattice, fine as wire, woven into shapes too intricate to follow with the eye. Parts of the lattice shift when you look away and settle back when you look directly at them.

Most curious are the fragments that seem to be in the middle of a process: a half-formed arch of the same glass, its lower section grown into the crust and its upper section still reaching upward, slowly, like a root searching for light. Nothing built here appears finished. Nothing appears to have been abandoned, either. The structures seem to be in a continuous cycle of assembly and dissolution, and the rate of change is hard to measure but easy to feel.

**Dynamics and the rules of the place**

The basin is never still. The domes rise and split on irregular cycles. The resin pools harden, crack, and reform as the temperature shifts through the hours. The copper moss spreads along the polygon seams a few centimeters a day, then retreats in a sudden die-off that leaves gray dust behind. The swarms of winged animals rearrange their formations in bursts, and the grazers migrate in tides that leave tracks which can be seen one morning and gone the next. The lattice structures change shape while you watch, not in dramatic leaps but in a restless flicker, like a thought that never finishes.

A few principles seem to govern it. Life here runs on sap, dust, and air rather than water. Growth is fast and decay is faster. Nothing persists unchanged for long, but the basin as a whole persists, as if the instability is the stable state. The density of presence is low: you can walk for an hour and cross wide stretches of bare crust with nothing but the wind and your own footsteps, and then suddenly come upon a dense knot of fern-trunks, grazers, and swarm, all within a few meters of each other. Life clusters, then thins, then clusters again.

If you stay long enough, you begin to notice a pattern in the chaos. The domes split in an order that seems to follow the cliff shadows. The swarms rise when the wind shifts past a certain angle. The sap pools its hardest when the sun is highest. None of this is quite predictable, but it is not random either. The basin is an engine, and you are standing in its noise, one small and temporary presence among many, waiting to see what it will build and tear down next.
