**The Drowned Ward of Sallow Basin**

You are under water, but not in the way the word usually means. The water here is not an ocean. It is a held volume, a lens about two hundred meters across, pressed down into a bowl of low ground. Above it, the sky shows through as a bright, undistorted disc. The light arrives normally, without the blue dimming of the deep, because the water has been engineered to stay clear, and it stays clear by being constantly stirred. Everything you see is lit as if by an ordinary noon, and the shadows fall where shadows should.

The city beneath is a single district, a ward of perhaps forty blocks, that sank when the surrounding plain flooded and was then sealed under this clear dome of water. Its buildings are packed tight, with no gaps between them, because the planners who laid it out expected to need every cubic meter. Towers rise from the basin floor and stop short of the surface by a few dozen meters. Their roofs are flat and sprouting, crusted with a sparse, gray-green mat that looks like lichen but grows in geometric rows, following the drainage channels that were cut into the concrete long ago. Vegetation is thin here. It clings to ledges and gutters and does not spread.

The terrain is young and sharp. The floodwater has not had time to round anything off. Streets end in fresh-cut scarps where a collapsed block sheared away. Fans of pale silt spread out from every breach, still settling, still shifting when the currents change. The edges of the old sidewalks are crisp and clean. There are no soft slopes in the ward. Every descent is a shelf or a cliff, and the sediment that lies in the gutters is fine and light, lifting in slow plumes whenever something moves through it.

Nothing moves through it on living legs. There is no fauna here. No fish, no crustaceans, no drifting jellies. The absence is the first thing that unsettles you once you notice it, because the water is so full. It is dense with motion anyway: the lattice.

The lattice is the strangest feature of the place. It is a suspended web of fine filaments, each no thicker than a hair, strung between the towers and across the streets like the rigging of a sunken ship. The filaments hum. They carry a current that pulses in long cycles, and where the current passes, the water thickens into shimmering veils that catch the light and bend it into slow ribbons. Motes ride these veils: small, tumbling, self-propelled engineered particles, billions of them, each about the size of a grain of sand. They drift in shoals, reform, scatter, and reform again, moving in patterns that look almost like flocking but follow no living instinct. They repair the lattice, clean the silt from the gutters, and carry dissolved salts from one district to another. Watching them is like watching weather.

The water is cool, a steady cold that never warms with the sun above, because the lattice pulls heat out of it and sends it somewhere deeper. The cold makes the metal of the towers sweat with fine beads that run down in thin, bright threads. The surfaces are slick and faintly green, and they creak as the water shifts against them.

The sound is constant and large. Pumps labor somewhere under the basin floor, a deep throb that you feel in your ribs before you hear it. Over that comes the high singing of the filaments, a glassy, rising hum that climbs and falls with the current cycles. Cavitation bubbles rise from the old intake grates with a sharp rattling crackle. Every so often a bell tolls from somewhere in the packed towers, a long low note that rolls across the whole ward and sets the motes spinning. Nothing is quiet. Even the silt makes a sound when it settles, a faint hiss like sand poured on paper.

The ecology, such as it is, is entirely engineered and entirely in motion. Sparse lichen mats on the roofs feed on the dissolved salts the motes carry. The motes feed the lattice. The lattice feeds the pumps, and the pumps keep the dome held and clear. Each process pushes against the others: the silt tries to fill the streets, the motes sweep it out, the pumps fight the pressure that would collapse the basin, and the current keeps the whole structure from settling into stillness. Equilibrium is never reached. The ward is always becoming something slightly different, a little more cut, a little more silted, a little more rebuilt.

Rules of the place, as far as anyone can tell: the water must keep moving or it goes dark and dead. The motes must keep working or the lattice sags. The bell tolls on a schedule that no one remembers setting. Surface access is forbidden. Anything that breaks through the dome is pulled down by the current and reabsorbed into the lattice, so the sky you see is only a view, and it has never been reachable.

Standing on a fresh scarp at the edge of the ward, looking up through the clear, bright water, you can see the old plain beyond the dome, dry and cracked and lit by the same ordinary sun. The lattice hums around you. The motes flicker past your face like sparks. The pumps throb in the floor. And somewhere below, in a tower that has not yet been reached by the silt, the bell begins again.
