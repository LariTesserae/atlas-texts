**The Settling Basin**

You come down into a shallow bowl, perhaps two kilometres across, so gently sunk into the land that you notice you are descending only when the horizon stops moving. The rim is a low ridge of raw grey stone, unweathered in the way of rock that has only just been exposed. Its surfaces are still fracturing in fine flakes, and the scree at its base has not yet decided what it will become. The whole basin feels newly made, as if the ground were a sentence still being written.

**Ground and stone**

The floor is pale, wet clay banded with iron-orange seams. Where the clay is exposed it cracks into polygons the size of hands, and each polygon curls slightly at the edges, the way drying mud does, though here it never fully dries. Between the cracks, the stone is shale that peels in thin sheets, and the sheets stack into small terraces like unfinished roof tiles. Some of the stone is not stone at all but a grey, glassy sediment that takes an impression. Footprints remain for days. Wheel ruts from a cart that passed a long time ago are still there, sharp at the edges, filling slowly with water.

**Water**

The air is damp without being wet. Mist gathers in the low places of the basin each morning and burns off by noon, but it never quite leaves. Condensation beads on every surface that faces the sky: a cold, fine dew that collects into droplets, runs down the stone, and gathers in shallow channels cut into the clay. The channels are the first thing you learn to read. They branch like a leaf's veins, and they lead to a series of pools, each the size of a room, each still and perfectly clear, reflecting not the grey overcast but a different sky, bluer or darker or flecked with stars, depending on the angle at which you look.

Rain comes only occasionally, a few times a season, and when it arrives it falls in slow, vertical threads, almost silent. In the upper district it sometimes falls at an angle, or pools upward against the undersides of ledges. Where that happens, the stone there is stained a deep green-black, as though the water left something behind before it understood where it was going.

**The built forms**

The settlement covers the basin's floor and climbs its walls in a dense, tightly packed mass of structures. There is no clear plan. Buildings lean against one another for support, and their walls are often shared. Stairways rise from one roof to the next. Bridges cross the gaps between towers at irregular heights. Most of the architecture is made of the same grey sediment as the ground, shaped by some process that resembles both masonry and growth. Walls are thick and rough, with rounded corners, and in places they seem to have been poured in layers, each layer a slightly different tone, so that a single wall reads like a cross-section of a cliff.

The layering goes deeper than material. Streets appear to exist in several eras at once. A cobbled lane might carry, a few metres above, a second lane of smooth, pale stone with lamp-posts that have no lamps. A doorway opens onto a staircase that rises through the floor of the room above it, which is itself half-built, and through the ceiling of the room above that, which is a cistern full of standing water. Nothing collapses, and nothing seems to be finished. The structures interpenetrate with a patience that suggests the whole city is still deciding where its boundaries lie.

**Machines**

Scattered among the buildings are objects that are clearly engineered but do not obey any mechanism you recognise. There are condensers: lattices of fine wire, hung from poles or set into the walls, that collect the mist and channel it into the basin's gutters. They are the most common machines and the most intricate. Their surfaces shimmer faintly when the mist is thick, and sometimes they hum at a pitch just below hearing, a sensation more felt in the teeth than heard.

Other machines are harder to describe. A waist-high cylinder stands in the middle of a square, its surface polished to a mirror, and inside it, when you look closely, is a second cylinder, rotating very slowly in the opposite direction. A cluster of thin rods rises from a pool and bends toward whatever light is strongest, though the light here is always the same. A grid of plates set into a stair-landing seems to draw small particles of clay upward, one grain at a time, so that the stair is gradually being raised.

None of the machines appear to have operators. Some show signs of recent maintenance: fresh wire, a scrap of cloth tied around a joint, a bowl of standing water placed beside them as though for an animal that never comes.

**Sky and light**

The light is ordinary daylight, neither harsh nor dim, filtered through a high, even overcast that seems to thicken and thin with no clear cause. Shadows are soft and mostly directionless. In the pools, though, the light is different: each reflects a sky of its own, and if you stand between two pools you may see two different suns, or a moon in the middle of a day. Looking up from the streets, you sometimes catch the edge of a second overcast, or the lower surface of a cloud that is clearly below you.

**Sound**

The basin is mostly quiet. Water drips, a soft tick against stone, and the occasional clink of wire in the wind. Your footsteps seem muffled, as if the clay absorbs them. Voices carry a short distance and then stop. In the upper district there is a faint, continuous hiss, like distant surf or pages turning, that seems to come from the walls themselves. At night it grows louder for a while and then fades again.

**Ecology**

Vegetation is sparse but persistent. Grey-green lichen grows in the cracks of the clay and along the edges of the channels, forming low crusts that shiver when the mist moves over them. Thin, pale filaments trail from the eaves of some buildings and sway in the air, drawing moisture from the dew. Here and there a small shrub has rooted in a wall, its leaves translucent and its branches thin as wire. They do not flower.

Fauna is rare. You might see a moth with wings like wet paper, drifting slowly among the condensers, or a small grey creature that moves along the gutters, feeding on the lichen. It flees if you approach, but not quickly. Nests are rare. Droppings are rare. The basin seems to support only a handful of animals, each of which seems to belong to it in a particular and careful way.

**Rules of the place**

Certain things here hold more firmly than others.

Water always finds the lowest accessible point, but "lowest" is a matter of perspective: a channel that runs downhill in one district may run uphill in another, and the water follows whichever direction is correct for the place you are standing in.

Structures do not fall, but they do change. A doorway that leads somewhere in the morning may lead somewhere else by afternoon, and the same staircase may have a different number of steps each time you climb it. Returning to a place you have left often means finding it slightly altered, as though the basin were remembering what you had seen and adjusting to fit.

Time moves at an uneven pace. Days here are long, and years are short. The mist rises and falls on its own schedule, the condensers grow a few centimetres of wire each season, and the clay cracks deepen over decades rather than days. A pool that was empty when you first arrived might be full when you leave, though you were gone only an afternoon.

**Slow change**

The basin is never still, but its changes are slow enough to feel like geology. The ridge crumbles one flake at a time. Channels deepen. Walls gain new layers of pale stone, or lose old ones to the damp. The city grows toward the rim, and the rim grows toward the sky. Nothing happens quickly, but everything is happening, the way an unfinished sentence is always, in some sense, already being spoken.

If you stay long enough, you begin to suspect the basin is not a place that has been built, nor a place that is building itself, but a place that is deciding, very slowly and with great patience, what kind of place it wants to be. And you begin to wonder whether you, moving through its mist and pooling water and shifting stairs, are part of the deliberation.
