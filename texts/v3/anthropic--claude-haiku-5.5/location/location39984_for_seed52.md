**The Silt Grid**

The first thing you notice is the grid. It runs out from your feet in straight lines, streets and setbacks laid out with a surveyor's patience, and then it dissolves into green. The lines are still there under the foliage. You can see them from the rise where the land tilts a few degrees toward a river you can hear but not yet see: a long, low ridge of broken concrete, then a ledge of pale clay, then a row of slender towers whose sides are furred with something silver-green.

The land is low, barely above the water it drains. Everything here was laid down recently, geologically speaking. The river has been changing its mind for a few decades, and it shows. Its banks are raw and sharp-edged, cut in clean vertical faces where the current has undercut them. Where a new meander has torn through the old city, you can read the cross-section like a cake: a crust of roots and humus, then a band of street gravel, a sheared-off basement floor with a tiled mosaic still intact, then gray clay, then older brick. Some of the cuts are only a few weeks old. The water in them is still turning the color of new mud.

**Water**

Water is never far and never simply present. It comes as a pattern: a mist that lies in the lower streets every dawn and burns off by midday, a drizzle that settles in for an afternoon and drips from every leaf for hours after. Puddles appear in the grid's old intersections and vanish into cracks. The air itself is heavy enough that metal goes faintly tacky and paper swells. Standing water collects in the hollows of collapsed plazas, and in those hollows the surface is often smooth and perfectly still, mirroring the sky in a way that seems wrong, too clean for a place this wet.

The river is brown, fast in the middle and slack at the edges, carrying trunks and the occasional lattice fragment, a sliver of something silver that catches the light and turns over slowly before sinking.

**Vegetation**

The green is dense and eager. Broad-leaved trees with aerial roots have colonized the avenues, their trunks swallowing lampposts and their canopies closing overhead so that the old boulevards are now tunnels. Vines hang from the upper floors of apartment blocks. Ferns grow in the gutters and in the seams of the pavement, and in the wetter hollows a dense mat of floating plants has formed, pale green with bulbous stems, moving slightly with the current as if breathing.

But the plants are not uniform. The silver-green fur on the towers is a kind of lichen-like mat that the growth seems to have adopted rather than conquered. It runs in ribbed channels down the sides of the structures and into troughs at their bases, and the troughs are full of clear water. In places the vines have grown toward the towers and then stopped, as if the lattice has a boundary the living things respect.

**The Built Layer**

The city is layered, and the layers do not agree with each other. At ground level there is an older stratum of ordinary construction: low housing blocks, shopfronts with names in a script you half recognize, stairwells that climb into shadow. Above that rise the taller structures, angular and sparse, with setbacks that look deliberate and surfaces that seem to absorb light rather than reflect it.

Then there are the towers. They stand apart from the grid, spaced out in ways that don't match any street plan. Each is a lattice of ribs and filaments wrapped around a dense core, perhaps two hundred meters tall, their upper third thinning into a web of fine strands that catch the mist. The strands sway even when there is no wind. When droplets gather on them, they run down the ribs and collect in the troughs, and the troughs drain through channels into the ground.

Some of the towers are dormant. Their silver has dulled to gray-brown and the channels are dry, cracked like old riverbed. Walk near one and you feel it: a faint warmth, a sense of something holding its breath. Others are very much alive. Their ribs ripple faintly, and when you stand close you can hear a low, irregular ticking, like a slow clock with too many hands.

**Tech**

The technology here does not follow obvious logic. The towers do not seem to be machines in the usual sense: no visible moving parts, no fuel lines, no exhaust. They condense water from the air, certainly, and they also appear to pull minerals up from the soil, because the clay under them is stained in rings and the nearest trees are pale and mineral-crusted. Whatever they are doing, it works best when the ground is wet. In dry spells they retract their filaments and seem to wait.

There are smaller devices scattered through the grid: low drums half-buried in the pavement, their surfaces etched with fine spirals, that hum faintly when rain begins and go silent when it stops. Some have split open and the inside is not machinery but something closer to mycelium, pale and threaded, grown into a ring of old copper wire. The line between built and grown is thin here, and in places it has been crossed in both directions.

Repairs happen. A cracked lattice rib, left wet for a few days, can be seen to thicken and close over the break. A toppled tower that lay in the river for a season had sprouted new filaments along its underside, and its ribs were pointing a different direction. Nobody is around to say whether this is maintenance or migration.

**Light and Atmosphere**

The light is dim, always. The sun is a pale disk behind a thick, perpetually moving haze, and it gives a flat, even glow that makes distances hard to read. Shadows are soft. Colors look muted, greens nearly gray at the edges of sight, though close up the leaves are a vivid, saturated green that seems to have been turned up by the damp. At dusk the towers' filaments catch what little light remains and glow faintly silver, a field of tiny lamps across the skyline.

The air is warm and close, smelling of wet bark, river silt, and something sweetish and metallic, like old coins left in rain.

**Sound**

The place is mostly quiet. Rain on broad leaves makes a steady hush rather than a patter. The river is the loudest sound, a continuous low rush that the mist seems to soak up. Sound carries strangely: a dropped stone can be heard clearly from across a block, while a voice from a few meters away may vanish into the damp. Sometimes, from deep inside the grid, there is a long, soft tone, rising and falling over several seconds, that may come from the towers or may be the wind moving through a hollow pipe somewhere underground.

**Fauna**

Animals are rare, and most of them are hard to see. Amphibious creatures with translucent skin hunt along the river edges and in the floating mats; their calls are high and brief and stop abruptly when anything large approaches. Small birds with long curved beaks feed in the fern beds, flying low and fast between trunks. There are insects, but fewer than the vegetation would suggest, as if something is keeping their numbers down. You might see a larger animal, deer-like or dog-like with long legs, moving along a distant ridge, but it will not come closer and it does not look back.

**Dynamics**

Everything here is in motion, slowly. The river reshapes its banks each season. The vegetation pushes into the grid street by street, while the towers' filaments reach out in the opposite direction, claiming the ground the plants have just taken. The fauna follow the rains: the amphibians boom after heavy storms and crash in dry spells, and the birds gather where the fern beds have fruited. The towers seem to respond to the rhythm of wet and dry, growing when it rains and contracting when it does not, so that the skyline itself breathes on a cycle of weeks.

Nothing here is finished. The grid is still being unmade and remade, the river is still digging, and the towers are still deciding what they are for. You are standing in a place that has only just begun to become what it will be, and it is not clear whether anything that was once human will still be recognizable when it settles.
