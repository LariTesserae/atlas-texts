**The Seep Shelf**

You come up onto the plateau through a fog that is not really fog. It is the air itself, cold and saturated, condensing on every surface so that the light arrives diffused and bright, with no shadows to speak of. The sun is high and hard somewhere above the murk, and the whole sky glows white. Your skin beads. Your breath hangs. Everything drips.

Underfoot is the plant mat. It is not grass and not moss, though it has traits of both. It is a dense, springy weave of low, fleshy stems and fern-fine fronds that grows in cushions and sheets across the whole upland, knee-deep in the hollows and thinning to a rug over the stone ridges. When you step, it gives and then sighs back, and water wells up between your boot prints and fills them. The mat holds an astonishing amount of water. Squeeze a handful and it runs for a minute. Beneath it, the ground is soaked to a depth no one has measured, and the plants pull moisture up from it constantly, exhaling it into the air where it condenses again. The plateau breathes in a slow loop.

Above the mat rise the taller forms, plants that have grown into architecture. Shrub-trees, pale-barked and many-trunked, have fused where they touch, forming arches and lattices and bridges over the seeps. Some have grown around things that were not plants. You can see a rusted valve wheel enveloped in a bark collar, a steel ladder rising through a living cage of branches, the corner of a concrete footing with roots gripping it like knuckles. The plant mat has no clear boundary with these structures; it climbs them, drapes them, and in places has swallowed them whole, leaving only humps and angles under the green.

The sound is what hits you first and never lets up. Water is everywhere, and it is loud. Meltwater pours off the upper slopes in a hundred threads that join into braided streams, each one rushing over gravel with a hiss that merges into a single roar. Where the land drops, the water falls in sheets off raw rock faces, slapping into pools with a heavy percussion. Beneath all of that is the industry: a pump station somewhere to the northwest sends a deep, rhythmic throb through the ground, a pulse you feel in your sternum before you hear it. Penstocks, great iron pipes running across the upland on low concrete saddles, boom when the flow shifts, a sound like distant artillery that rolls across the plateau and echoes off the cliffs. The wind, funneled through the plant-lattices, whistles and moans in a dozen pitches. Rockfall cracks from the eroding edges, sudden sharp reports followed by a long clatter of stone.

The machinery is old and mostly still working. A turbine hall sits half-buried by the mat, its roof sagging, its gantry cranes rust-brown and furred with green. The turbines turn very slowly, on a schedule no one visible seems to keep, and each revolution drives a groan through the iron frame. Conveyors run from nowhere to nowhere, their belts gone, their rollers seized and silvered with lichen-like crust. A pressure gauge on a standpipe still shows a needle, though the glass is clouded and the dial's numbers have been half-erased by weather. There are a few small huts, their doors bowed shut, their windows fogged from inside. A cairn of white stones marks a junction. A steel post carries a painted arrow, faded to a ghost, pointing at nothing you can see.

Nothing is finished here. That is the strangest quality of the Seep Shelf. The raw geology is still being made. On the eastern rim, the bedrock is pale and fractured, its faces scoured into gullies that are deepening by a few centimeters a season. Scree fields lie unsorted and angular, piled at the foot of cliffs that have not yet decided what shape they will take. Where the meltwater cuts through the mat and reaches stone, you can see the first stages of soil forming: a grey paste of ground rock and decaying fronds, pocked with the tracks of tiny burrowing things that you almost never see. The plateau is still assembling its own skeleton. The machines were built onto it, and the plants have been slowly claiming them, and the water keeps carving and carrying, and none of these processes seems to be in a hurry.

Life is thin. You may see one bird in an hour, a dark shape cutting across the glare and vanishing into a lattice of branches. Insects are rare; when you find one, it is usually clinging to a wet frond, motionless in the cold. Larger animals leave tracks at the water's edge and then nothing. The plant mat is the living fabric of the place, and it is nearly everything. Its density is low in the sense that it is patchy, with great stretches of open, bare, streaming rock between cushions and copses, so that you can always see far, to the ridges and the haze and the pale shoulders of higher peaks. But wherever there is ground, there is green, and wherever there is green, there is water standing in it, catching the bright light and throwing it back.

You walk on. The mat gives under your feet. Somewhere behind you a penstock booms. Ahead the fog thins, and for a moment you see the far valley falling away, filled with the same white glare, and a slow river of cloud moving through it with no particular hurry at all.
