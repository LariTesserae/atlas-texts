**The Shelf**

The plateau rises out of the cloud at about two thousand meters and runs past the edge of sight in every direction that is not down. Its surface is a single worn sheet of dark basalt, cracked into polygons the size of a man's stride, and every crack holds water. Nothing here is flat in the way a lake is flat. The stone is tilted, bowled, and grooved, so the water gathers and moves in thin sheets, finding the old channels that were cut into it long ago.

The built part covers the whole plateau. Walls of dry-laid stone run in long arcs and then fold back on themselves, forming enclosures, terraces, stairways, and streets. The density is that of a city: blocks stacked on blocks, courtyards inside courtyards, roofless rooms with doorways that open onto other doorways. There are no metal fittings, no wheels-worn ruts, no glass. Every surface is cut stone, fitted without mortar and now softened by weather. Some walls have slumped into rounded mounds. Others still rise three or four stories, leaning slightly, their tops crowned with grass.

The water is the city's living infrastructure. Cisterns sit at the lowest points of each district, round basins sunk into the rock, and most of them are full to the lip. Their overflow runs down cut gutters to a second tier of basins, and that tier to a third, so that the whole settlement is a cascade of stepped pools linked by carved spouts. Many spouts have cracked and now throw thin sheets of water across the streets. The paving is slick and green at the edges and smooth where feet once wore it down. Puddles reflect a low, pale sky that seems always about to rain and seldom quite does. In the lulls, the air itself is wet: fog settles into the streets in the early hours and burns off by midday only to form again as the cloud banks roll up the escarpment from the valley below.

Stone weeps everywhere. Ledges, lintels, and the undersides of arches bead with water that drops steadily, one drop, then another, so that under every overhang there is a dark patch of stone and a small ring of moss. The drip is the most constant sound on the Shelf, and the quietest. Beneath it runs a wind, not strong, that moves through the gaps in the walls and makes a low breathing note in the empty doorways. Occasionally a block shifts or a wall slumps with a dull, short thud, and the echo takes a long time to fade.

Vegetation is sparse but not absent. It is patient. Lichens cover the basalt in rust, grey, and pale yellow rosettes, growing a few millimeters a year over the stone. Mosses fill the cracks and the shaded corners of courtyards, thick and soft, bright green when wet, almost black when the fog thickens. Low, hard-leaved shrubs have taken root in the tops of the walls, their roots prying the courses apart. Where a wall has fallen, a tangle of woody stems rises from the rubble, and where a cistern has cracked, reeds grow in the seep. Trees are few. A stunted juniper or a twisted, wind-shaped thorn tree stands here and there in the open, always near water.

Fauna is rare, and what there is seems mostly to pass through. Small brown birds with thin calls nest in the roofless rooms and forage along the gutters. Something with many legs moves under the moss in the mornings, and small pale insects hover in the mist above the cisterns. Once in a while a larger animal crosses the high streets at dusk: a grazer, perhaps, or a predator following the birds. Nothing stays long. The Shelf is not a place that feeds many mouths.

Light is normal and diffuse. The cloud layer scatters the sun into an even grey-white glow that casts almost no shadow except at the bases of walls. Colors are muted: stone in graphite and rust, water in pewter, moss in deep olive. When the cloud breaks, shafts of sun pick out the wet rock in sudden silver, and the whole plateau seems to glow from within for a few minutes before the cloud closes again.

The Shelf is always changing, though slowly. Freeze-thaw nights split the basalt along old cracks and open new ones. Each season the reeds creep farther into the flooded cisterns, and each season a few more walls lean out of true. Fog rolls in and out in a daily rhythm. Rain falls in long grey spells that seep into every crack, then gives way to dry, cold days when the stone steams faintly in the sun. Water moves through the city constantly, finding its way down the cut channels, pooling, overflowing, weeping from the stone, returning to the cloud. The built structures are being taken back into the ground at the pace of ice and moss, and the land is taking them in, one block at a time.
