# The Lattice Flats

The plain runs to the horizon in every direction, and nothing on it was ever bedrock. The floor is a crust of pale gypsum and silica, laid down over a span so wide that a traveler on foot would need weeks to cross it. It was never carved or eroded from older stone. It grew, and it is still growing.

The first thing you notice is the pattern. The ground is tiled in cells, each roughly the width of a village, bounded by low ridges of pale mineral that meet at angles near enough to hexagonal to make the eye search for the flaw. Within each cell the surface is smooth and faintly domed, like the back of a sleeping animal, though nothing here sleeps or breathes. Where two cells meet, the ridge rises into a fin, sometimes knee-high, sometimes taller than a man, with edges as sharp as the day they formed. Nothing has had time to round them. Erosion here is only beginning, and the walls still hold the crisp lines of fresh casting.

## How It Is Made

No hands did this. The building happens through heat and water moving through the ground.

By day the sun drives the surface to a temperature that makes the air above it shimmer and rise. Beneath the crust, warm brine seeps upward through fractures, and the rising heat sets up slow convection cells beneath each tile. Where the brine reaches the surface it deposits what it carries. The cell centers stay low and flat, because the rising fluid spreads out evenly there. The margins, where the cold, dense brine sinks back down, become the ridges. The pattern is a physics problem solved in stone, and the solution is always hexagonal because that is the most efficient way for convection to tile a plane.

The ridges are the active growth fronts. They lengthen by a few centimeters a season. In places you can find a fin that is still wet at its tip, a glistening lip of mineral slurry that hardens into pale glass within an hour of the air touching it. Walk along a ridge at noon and you may hear it tick as it contracts in the heat.

## Water, Briefly

Water is scarce here, but it is never absent, and it does more work than its volume suggests.

Rain falls perhaps six or seven times a year, always in the late afternoon, when the convection is strongest and the vapor rising from the crust has built into towering white columns. These storms are vast and slow. A single cell may dump rain on one tile while the next tile over stays dry and bright, and the boundary between them can be sharp enough to see from a ridge top, a wall of gray falling on one side and clean glare on the other. Rain dissolves the surface for a few hours and carries the dissolved mineral into the cell centers, where it reprecipitates as a fine, slick skin. After a storm the flats glow with a thin wet sheen, and the air smells of sulfur and warm stone.

Between storms, the air is never dry. At night the crust cools, and water vapor condenses in a shallow layer across the lowest parts of every cell. By dawn the cell centers hold a film of brine no deeper than a fingernail, and the ridges steam faintly as the sun reaches them. The condensation feeds the lichen mats, dissolves the salts, and keeps the surface soft enough to grow. The plain is never really dry. It is only briefly less damp.

## Weather of Its Own

Because the crust releases vapor so steadily, the flats generate their own climate. Over the central expanse a permanent haze hangs at about the height of a church spire, a bright whitish layer that softens the sun and makes distant ridges look like stacked paper. At dusk the haze thickens and drifts. On still nights a fog rolls up out of the cell centers and settles into the low ground, so that the ridges stand out of it like the walls of drowned villages. You can walk the margins at night and see the fins emerging one by one from white.

The wind here is hot and dry at midday, carrying fine mineral dust that settles into the ridge grooves and sharpens their edges. In the evening it shifts, turns cool, and blows along the ridge lines, humming faintly across the tops of the fins.

## Life

Vegetation is sparse but present. Where condensation lingers, colonies of crust-forming organisms spread across the cell centers. They look like dark, rust-colored lichen mats, wrinkled and cracked into polygons that echo the larger tiling. In the wetter hollows, filamentous mats grow in thin green-black threads, and in a few protected pockets near the ridge bases, squat, bulbous forms have developed. These are crystalline sponges, roughly the size of a fist, with translucent walls that hold condensate for the dry afternoons. They are slow. A sponge may take twenty years to double in size.

There are no animals. Nothing moves across the plain except wind, water, and the slow creep of growth. But the lichen mats and sponges are not inert. They compete for the thin films of brine, and the mats that spread fastest crowd out the sponges in one season and retreat in the next, when the sponge walls harden and shade them. Predation takes a chemical form: some mats secrete compounds that dissolve the edges of their neighbors, and the sponges respond by thickening their walls. Over years, the balance shifts across whole cells. It is an ecosystem in motion, working at the pace of mineral growth.

## Sound

The flats are quiet. Wind is the loudest thing, a dry hiss across the crust by day and a low hum along the ridges at evening. Beneath it you can hear the ticking of cooling and heating stone, the soft crackle of dissolving salt, and in the hour after rain, a faint continuous drip from the ridge lips into the cell centers. At night, when the fog rolls over, even these sounds seem to go soft and far away.

## Rules of the Place

- Nothing here is older than the current convection pattern. The ridges are young, sharp, and actively extending.
- The tiling is stable in shape but shifts in detail. Cell boundaries migrate slowly as the heat field changes, so a map drawn in one decade will be wrong in the next.
- Rain is rare but decisive. A single storm can reset the chemistry of a whole cell.
- Life follows the water, and the water follows the heat. Every living thing here is a feature of the same engine that builds the ground.
- Nothing is made by hands, yet everything is made. The distinction between landscape and structure has dissolved, because the plain is an architecture that builds itself, with no builder but the heat moving through it.

If you walked out onto the flats at dawn, with the fog thinning around your ankles and the fins rising pale and sharp on every side, you would see a place still in the act of becoming. Nothing is finished. Everything is growing, slowly, into a pattern that has been running for as long as there has been a sun to drive it.
