**The Shelf**

The land rises here until it runs out of ground. It is a broad, tilted plateau near the roof of the world, cut by long shallow valleys and ringed on every side by peaks that vanish into a low grey ceiling. That ceiling never lifts or thins. The light comes through it from everywhere at once, dim and even, so nothing casts a firm shadow. Distances are hard to judge. A boulder a hundred paces off can look like a ridge, and a ridge can look like a cloud bank resting on the ground.

The stone is dark, close-grained slate and a grey granite flecked with mica, and most of it has been weathered into smooth plates, rounded tors, and sharp-edged scree. Erosion has run so long that the land has settled into its own shape. The valleys are worn to matching curves, the scree fans have stopped moving, and the rock has taken on a dull polish from wind and water. Cracks have filled with pale mineral crust. Where a plate has split, the two halves still fit together like a jigsaw left on a table.

Water is the constant presence, but it comes in pulses. Every few hours a fine cold drizzle drifts up from the valleys and hangs in the air for a while, beading on every surface. Then it thins, and the rock stays wet for a long time afterward. Condensation runs down the faces of the tors in thin sheets, and small rivulets trace delicate patterns across the flagstones before pooling in shallow dark basins. Some of these streams run uphill for a few body-lengths before losing their nerve and turning back. Their beds are always fresh-cut, as though the water has only just finished carving them.

Vegetation is sparse and low. Grey cushion moss clings in soft domes that feel like wet felt. Cracked lichen spreads in rust and pale green rings on the boulders. In the sheltered hollows grow tussocks of stiff, pale grass that bend in the wind but never seem to grow taller. Nothing here is crowded. Each clump has a wide bare margin around it, as if the land keeps a distance from its own plants.

The air is cold and wet and carries a faint mineral tang. Sound is the loudest thing on the Shelf. A deep, steady roar fills everything, and it seems to come from a great river in a gorge far below, though no river can be seen from any height. The roar swells and fades with the drizzle cycles, but never stops. Over it, the wind plays the stone itself: the hollow tors give off low booming notes when gusts pass through their cracks, and the flat scree slopes ring faintly when stepped on, like struck pottery. Echoes are unreliable. A shout comes back from cliffs that are not there, and sometimes it returns before the shout has finished leaving the mouth.

Those are the first signs of the Shelf's breakdown. Reality here holds loosely. The roar of the unseen river sometimes arrives from behind you when you turn to face it. Cairns of flat stones stand on the ridges, and their shadows, faint as they are, point toward a sun that is not in the sky. Shallow footprints in the mud fill with water that is faintly warm to the touch, though the air is freezing and the water beside them is not. A stone you set down in one hollow is sometimes found, unmoved, in a slightly different place on the next visit, always a few finger-widths closer to the center of the basin. Measurements stop agreeing with each other. Paces counted across the same ground come out different every time, though the count is always the same for any given traveler.

The built remains are scattered across the plateau with long gaps between them. There are low dry-stone shelters, roofless, their doorways all facing inward toward a central hollow that is always empty. There is a stair of flagstones climbing a slope and ending in a grey wall of cloud. Its steps are worn into the middle, as though many feet have climbed them, but no one is ever seen on it. Near one ridge stands a cairn tower, eight or nine courses high, leaning slightly toward the valley. Its topmost stone has a shallow bowl carved into it, which holds rainwater that never overflows. No inscriptions remain. Anything that might have been written has been smoothed away by weather, or was never written here at all.

Life is rare. A few small grey birds with pale wingbars sit on the tors, motionless, and fly only when the drizzle is at its thickest. They sing one short phrase over and over. Something moves in the grass at the edge of sight, low and quick, but it is never there when you look directly. Once in a long while a shape passes across a distant ridge, too large for any bird and too slow for any animal that would stay long on such ground. It is always gone before it can be named.

Nothing here changes much. The drizzle comes and goes on the same schedule, the roar rises and falls in the same long cycle, and the cloud ceiling holds its exact weight and shade. The streams keep running uphill, and the cairn's bowl stays full. Every traveler finds the Shelf the same, whether they come today or in some far-off season, and the stillness is so complete that it feels less like a place than like a moment that has been held open for a very long time.
