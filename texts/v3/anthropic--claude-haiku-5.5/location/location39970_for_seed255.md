# The Slumping Lowlands

The land is low and damp, and almost nothing here is natural. Every surface has been shaped by hand, or by something that works like hands. The ground is rammed earth, mud-brick, and slurry-rendered clay laid down in courses. Beneath it, the original soil has been buried or dug out so many times that no one could say where the ground ends and the building begins.

There is no green anywhere. The absence of plant life is the first thing you notice, and it changes how the place feels. Nothing softens an edge. Every corner is a corner, every wall is a wall, and the colors run only through ochre, slate, bruise-purple, and the dull white of lime-wash that has gone grey with damp.

## Terrain

The lowlands spread out in terraces, each one a few body-lengths higher than the last, stepping down toward a basin where water collects. The terraces are banked with walls of packed earth, and the walls are not finished. Some stand at full height, buttressed and smoothed. Others end in ragged stumps where the work stopped. Scaffolds of lashed timber lean against them, grey and furred with wet.

Between the terraces run channels, cut by hand into the clay. Many are old and well-lined with fired brick. Others are raw ditches, still raw, their sides slumping in wet clods that fall into the water with a sound like a dropped sack. The channels are alive. They branch, pool, and sometimes abandon their courses overnight, and the builders then spend days hauling spoil to block the old run and open a new one.

Rising out of the flats are the chimneys: tall, unglazed pipes of fired clay, some hundreds of body-lengths high, arranged in loose clusters. They serve as the vertical element of the place, the only things that break the horizon. Their purpose is unclear. Water runs down their outer faces, and at their bases it gathers in shallow pans rimmed with white mineral crust.

## Atmosphere and Light

The sky is a low, unbroken lid of cloud, or possibly a roof. Light comes from it as a dim, even grey, and it hardly reaches the ground. The place is perpetually dusk. Lamps burn in the settlements: clay bowls of rendered fat and wicks, cressets on poles, and in the deeper cuts, phosphorescent crusts on the walls that glow a sickly green-white when wet. Much of the area is shadow.

The air is cool and saturated. Condensation beads on every surface and gathers into drops that fall from ledges and eaves in a constant patter. Fog sits in the basin most mornings and lifts only partly by midday. Breath shows. Fabric never dries. Everything carries a fine film of silt that gets into the mouth and eyes.

## Sound

This is a loud place. Water is always moving somewhere: dripping from overhangs, rushing down chutes, gurgling through drains, pooling and overflowing with a hollow slap. Over that come the sounds of work. Mallets strike wooden stakes. Spades bite into wet clay and then lift free with a sucking pop. Ropes groan under load on pulleys. Somewhere deep in the terraces, a bell or hollow pipe is struck on a rhythm, probably to coordinate the crews, though no one can say who keeps the time.

Slumps are the loudest events. When a wall or bank gives way, it makes a long, heavy roar, followed by a splash and a rolling clatter of brick. Crews react at once, running toward the sound with shovels, shouting in short clipped calls.

## Tools and People

The people here work with simple tools: wooden levers and rollers, stone mauls and tampers, bone-handled trowels, rope, clay moulds, and kilns built into the slope. Metal is rare and treated with care. Some workers wear long rain-cloaks of oiled hide, and many have hands that are cracked and grey from constant wet and lime.

Few people are visible on any given stretch. Crews move in small groups along the terraces, and some of the cells and sheds are sealed so that no one can see inside. The people's lives seem to be organized around the making, maintenance, and rebuilding of the works.

## Fauna

Animal life is sparse. The most common creatures are small, pale, and blind, with long sensitive feelers, which scuttle along the channel walls and feed on the mineral film. They make a faint clicking noise when disturbed, which is one of the few sounds in the place that isn't made by water or work. Larger animals are rare. Occasionally something long and slow moves through the lower basin, leaving wide tracks in the silt, but it is seldom seen.

## Strangeness

Some things here don't fit. Doors open onto blank walls, and some lead to rooms that seem to have been sealed and then unsealed again. Certain walls lean very slightly toward the water, as if they were built to face it, or as if they are still settling into a position that was planned. Inscriptions repeat across the terraces in the same spare marks, but they change subtly from wall to wall, as though one message were copied by hands that never quite agreed on its shape. Sometimes a channel runs uphill for a short stretch before dropping sharply, and no crew will say why it was built that way.

## Dynamics

Everything in this place is in motion. The rains come in long cycles, swelling the channels and filling the basin until the lowest terrace floods, then receding over days and leaving behind a fresh skin of silt. Each flood reshapes a little of the ground. Each dry spell cracks the slurry walls, which the crews patch with new mud. Growth and decay happen together. Some structures are rising while others, only a season old, are already sinking and cracking.

The place is young, raw, and busy. Its builders are always in the middle of something: a wall half-raised, a channel half-dug, a chimney capped and then left open again. Whatever is being made here has not yet settled into its final form, and the water, the work, and the slow slumping of the ground keep it from ever doing so.
