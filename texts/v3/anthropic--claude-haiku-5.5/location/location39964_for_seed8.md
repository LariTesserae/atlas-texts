The land has been wearing down for longer than anything has had a name for it. Once there were mountains here. Now a vast, nearly level plain runs to the horizon in every direction, interrupted only by a few round-shouldered stone hills that rise perhaps a few dozen meters, smoothed like river stones left too long in a current. Their edges have lost all sharpness. Their tops are bald domes of pale quartz-veined rock, cracked into plates that peel away in thin sheets when the heat swells them.

The ground is a deep rust-red clay, cemented in places into a hard crust that rings underfoot like pottery. Where the crust has broken, it reveals pale mottled layers beneath, veined with iron, the colors of old blood and bone. Much of the plain is not dry. Water lies on it in sheets a hand or two deep, so shallow and so still that the sky sits on it perfectly, and the cloud-banded horizon is repeated upside down beneath your boots. The water is brown-black, stained with tannins leached from the sparse roots, and it is warm as bathwater by midday.

Everything here is wet in the way that breath is wet. Mornings arrive with a condensing fog that beads on every surface: the stone domes shine and drip, and thin rivulets run down their faces, feeding seeps that hang in the rock like curtains. Moss-like mats on the north-facing sides glisten, and small ferns cling to the damp hollows where water collects and stays. Each evening the fog lifts into towering cumulus that build over the plain, drop their rain in grey columns you can watch walking across the land, and then retreat, leaving the ground steaming.

Vegetation is thin and patient. Low, gnarled shrubs with wide-spreading roots stand well apart from one another, their leaves thick and waxy, often mottled with lichen. Tussocks of coarse grass form islands in the shallow water, and reed stands grow in dense green clumps where the water deepens, their stems knocking together in the wind with a dry clatter. Between them are wide open patches of bare mud, cracked and curled where the water has retreated, dotted with pale fungal rings and the trails of many small animals. Nothing grows tall enough to shade the ground for long, so light lies everywhere, bright and even. The sun is unhurried and unshielded, and it is not harsh. The air simply holds its heat.

The fauna is abundant in a way that makes the emptiness feel inhabited. The sound of the place is the first thing you notice and the last you forget. At dusk the frogs begin, thousands of them spread across the water, each pond pitching its own chorus of trills, hammers, and deep resonant croaks that overlap until the whole plain seems to hum. Insects answer, a high ceaseless drone rising from the reeds and falling back in waves. Birds call from the stone domes and the shrubs, long rising whistles, rattles, and sudden cackles. Wading birds stand motionless in the shallows, long-legged and patient, and lift off in great slow flocks when something startles them. Things move under the water. Something large turns in a pool and sends ripples across the sheet, and the reeds shiver after it. Small swimmers scatter from the edges. Four-legged grazers wander through the tussocks, cropping as they go, and their trails crisscross the mud.

There is no sign of anyone. No roads, no cairns, no fences, no cut stone or fire-scar. The rock is only rock, the water only water, and the paths are only the animals' paths, each worn so slowly that the marks of centuries are indistinguishable from the marks of last week. Even the change here happens at a crawl. The water rises through the wet season and sinks through the dry one, but the level in any given year barely differs from the year before. The stone domes lose a few flakes of their skin each generation. The mud cracks, heals, and cracks again. Over the long span that the land measures, its shape is a slow exhalation, the plain widening by a grain of sand per century as the hills shrink into it. Nothing here is rushing toward anything. It is settling, the way old things settle, into the final shape that the water and time have agreed upon.
