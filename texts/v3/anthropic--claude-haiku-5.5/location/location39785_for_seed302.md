# The Plateau of Standing Water

The land rises to a high, tilted table, more than two miles above the sea by the feel of the air. Nothing grows here. There is no moss, no lichen, no crust of green on any surface. What covers the ground is mineral: white, ochre, and black, laid down in layers so old they have become the landscape itself.

**The ground**

Most of the plateau is travertine, a pale stone deposited by water over unthinkable spans of time. It forms terraces stepping down the slope, each lip curled and fluted like the edge of a poured candle. Every terrace holds a shallow sheet of water, a finger's depth or a forearm's, so clear that the stone beneath looks lit from underneath. The water is cold and never still. It seeps from cracks in the rock, spills from one step to the next, and drains into channels that have cut the tilted shelf into a web of ridges.

Where the water has been longest, the sinter has built cones and chimneys, some knee-high, some taller than a person, with hollow centers the water still pours through. Their surfaces are rough and crusted, rimmed in tiny ridges. In the mineral pools they grade from white to rust to a deep greenish-black where iron and manganese have stained them. None of these colors are living. They are the signatures of different waters, different minerals, different eras of deposit.

**The columns**

Rising from the plateau's northern edge are basalt columns, hexagonal and dark, packed so tightly that they look like the pipes of a vast organ built into the mountain. Some stand straight, some lean in a single direction as if the whole face had been pushed over and set. The wind enters their tops and the open ends of the hollow ones, and they sing. The note is low and continuous, held beneath everything else, a sustained pressure more than a tone.

**The sound**

The plateau is loud, and the loudness is constant. The loudest source is a fall of water at the far western edge, where the table breaks off into a gorge. The sheer roar of it carries for miles, a wall of white noise that never rises or drops. Closer in, there are a hundred smaller sources. Water hisses over lips of stone, gurgles through the sinter chimneys, drips in rhythmic pattern from overhanging ledges, and runs over the pools with a bright, bubbling chatter. The chimneys whistle when gusts cross their mouths. The columns hum. When the wind comes hard from the west, the entire plateau becomes one sound, and a person standing in it has no way to hear anything more distant than the next stone.

Because so much of the noise is water, and water moves in all directions at once, the sound seems to come from everywhere. It does not echo. It fills.

**Light and air**

The sky is an intense, cloudless blue that deepens toward the zenith. The light is bright, nearly without shadow at midday, because the white stone throws it back from every surface. Walking here means squinting. Even the wet black of the columns glints where the water catches it. The air is thin and cold, and it holds moisture: mist hangs in the hollows, and the spray from the falls drifts across the plateau in slow banks that catch the sun and turn it into faint rainbows. Dew never quite dries from the stones on the shaded faces.

**The crowding**

The plateau is full. There are no bare patches of open ground between the features. Every meter has a pool, a rill, a cone, a column, a boulder of fallen basalt, or a ledge of sinter. The features press together so closely that paths, where they exist, are only gaps the water has not yet filled. Crossing the plateau means stepping between presences, each one making its own sound, each one occupying its own small patch of air and light.

**Creatures**

Animal life is scarce. Now and then a bird crosses high overhead, a dark mark against the blue, too far off to hear over the water. Something small may move at the edges of the pools, a flicker in the shallows that is gone before it can be identified. It is rare enough that a visitor would notice each one and remember it. The place does not seem to need them. It is complete without them.

**Weirdness**

Some things here resist explanation. In a few of the chimneys, the water rises instead of falling, pushed up through the hollow center by pressure from below, and it stands in a column for a few seconds before it splashes down again. Certain columns hum on a note that is not quite the same as their neighbors', and where two of them are close, their notes beat against each other in a slow pulse that can be felt in the chest. And the pools, when they are still for a moment, reflect the sky with a perfection that makes it hard to tell where the stone ends and the air begins.

**Stillness**

The plateau has been like this for as long as anyone could say. The water flows and the sound never stops, but nothing here changes its shape. The terraces keep their steps, the columns keep their angles, and the cones keep their places. Each spring and each cascade finds its channel and keeps it. The water is always moving, and everything it touches stays where it is. The place is loud, bright, crowded, and perfectly settled, a world that has reached a kind of balance in which change is only the endless repetition of the same flow.
