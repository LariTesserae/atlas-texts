# The Sinkhollow of Caddren Reach

The city fell in on itself about forty years ago, and the hole it left is still settling. From the rim you look down a bowl roughly four kilometers across, its walls cut raw and pale where the ground gave way. The strata show like the pages of a torn book: clay, then gravel, then a band of dark glassy stuff that might once have been roadway. The cuts are still sharp-edged. Rain has not yet softened them, and small stones tick loose and skitter down the faces every few minutes.

Nothing about the basin is quiet. The loudest thing is the pumping. Along the eastern wall, a row of squat engines the color of wet slate draws water up through pipes as thick as trees, and each stroke sends a low concussion across the floor that you feel in your sternum before you hear it. Between the pump strokes come the drips. Water runs off every surface, pooling in cracked plazas, pattering on broad leaves, gurgling in culverts. Somewhere below, a cascade falls from a broken aqueduct arch with a sound like continuous applause. The noise doesn't stop. It layers and rebuilds, a machine-and-water chorus with no animal voices in it at all.

## The Floor

The bottom of the Reach is wetland. Standing water fills the old streets to knee depth in places, and the grid of the city still shows through the flood as lines of slightly higher ground, causeways between submerged blocks. Where the water is shallow, the ground is a mat of fern-like growth, broad fronds the blue-green of old copper, rising to shoulder height and so dense that you cannot see the ground beneath them. Where it is deeper, pale stalks rise out of black water, tall and jointed, topped with clusters of flat bloom-discs that open and close slowly, tracking the dim light the way sunflowers track the sun.

The vegetation is lush, almost aggressive, and it has taken the built world as its scaffolding. Vines drape the lower floors of towers and sprout from window frames. Moss the texture of velvet has colonized every horizontal surface, and it glows faintly green-gold where water sheets over it. Fungal shelves spread along the bases of columns in great tiers, some as wide as a table. Everything is growing visibly. Cracks in the pavement lift a centimeter over a day as roots push beneath. A wall that was bare last week is half-green now.

Yet there is no animal life at all. No insect whine, no bird, no rustle of anything that moves on its own. The plants pollinate by drift and wind and water, and what few things move in the basin are carried by water or by the pumps.

## The Built Layer

The city is still here, in layers. The upper tiers of the old towers stand clear of the water, their facades stripped to the frame and fitted with new, unfamiliar apparatus: lattices of pale filament, spiraled coils wrapped in fibrous sheathing, panels that shift their angle slowly over the course of hours, tracking something in the sky. Some of these machines hum at a pitch you can barely hear on its own but that makes the air itself vibrate when enough of them run together.

At the middle level, the buildings are half-drowned, their lower stories full of water that has stopped being still. Doorways stand open to flooded rooms where the water is faintly warm and clear enough to show shelves of unidentifiable objects, some of which seem to have grown into the walls. Bridges connect the towers, some intact, some folding into the water at one end, a few rebuilt with the same filament lattice, bridging gaps that should not be bridgeable.

At the bottom, the old street grid is mostly gone, replaced by terraced gardens that the plants have built themselves, with rows of pumps and channels set between them like a deliberate agriculture. Someone, or something, is still tending this, though you never see who. The channels are clean. The pumps are maintained. Footprints appear in the mud sometimes and are gone the next morning, rained or grown over.

## Atmosphere and Light

The air is warm and wet enough to feel like breath on the skin. A fine condensation hangs over the whole bowl, so the far walls dissolve into gray haze and the near ones gleam with running water. The light is dim, a sky the color of pewter, filtered through haze and the broad canopy of the lower gardens. It comes evenly from everywhere, so shadows are soft and there is no clear noon. Late in the day, the mist catches a faint amber from somewhere beyond the rim and the whole basin briefly looks like the inside of a lantern.

Rain falls only inside the Reach. Beyond the rim, the sky is dry. Water rises from the basin floor as vapor and condenses over the walls, then runs down into the bowl again. The water cycle here is closed, pumped and recirculated by the machines, and it sometimes seems the basin is breathing, swelling with mist at dawn and clearing at dusk on a rhythm that does not match the sun.

## Rules of the Place

- Anything left unattended for more than a few days is taken into the growth. Objects dropped on the floor are wrapped in roots within a week and absorbed within a month.
- The pumps run in long cycles of about nineteen minutes, and each cycle changes the water level in the flooded districts by a few centimeters. People who live near the eastern wall learn to time their steps.
- The filament lattices lean toward the rim during the night and away from it at dawn. Nobody has worked out why.
- Stones that fall from the young cuts are warm to the touch for an hour after they land.
- If you stand still long enough near the central cascade, the sound of the falling water seems to separate into distinct voices. They are not voices, but people who have stayed too long report hearing them, and they tend to stop describing what they heard after a while.

The Reach is still changing. Each year the walls slump a little further and the floor sinks another few meters, and each year the machines and the plants reshape the wreckage into something new. Whatever built the pumps is still at work. Whatever the city was for has been lost, and what replaced it is busy growing toward a purpose no one has yet named.
