**The Sunken Pan**

You stand at the lip of a bowl that the land seems to have pressed down into itself. The rim is a ring of pale, layered cliffs, perhaps forty meters high, their strata laid down in bands of ochre, grey, and a chalky white. The bowl is roughly a kilometer across. Its floor sits well below the level of the country around it, and from here you can see the sky reflected in its wet places more clearly than you can see the ground.

Sunlight is intense and nearly unbroken. There is little haze, and the light is hard-edged, so every stone casts a short, sharp shadow. In the middle of the day the floor glares. In the early morning and late afternoon the cliffs take on a warm, almost molten colour, and the pools catch fire.

**The floor**

The floor is not one surface but a set of terraces stepping down from the rim. The upper terraces are dry, cracked into polygonal plates the size of a hand to a dinner table. The plates are pale, crusted with a fine salt, and they curl slightly at their edges. When rain has been and gone, the plates have lifted a few millimetres, then settled. Walking on them makes a faint dry crackle. Over long periods the whole floor has been rising and falling in this way, slowly enough that no single season shows it, but the plates in the centre are now noticeably higher than those near the walls, and the lowest terrace has a shallow, almost imperceptible tilt toward its middle.

Below the plates the ground is darker, silty, and stays damp. Here the water lives. It does not form a lake. It collects in pockets: seep-holes, shallow tarns the size of a bathtub, long thin sheets lying in the hollows between plates, and one deeper pool at the centre, perhaps fifty metres across, that is green-black and very still. Rain arrives only a few times a year, sometimes in a single afternoon's violent downpour. Water then runs in brief, braided channels down the terraces, cutting small gullies that smooth out again within a season. For a few days the whole floor is glossy and reflective. Then the pockets shrink, the crust whitens, and the damp retreats to the depressions.

**Vegetation**

Plants are sparse but stubborn. They grow where water has lingered longest: along the margins of the seep-holes, in the cracks at the base of the cliffs, and on the lowest terraces. The commonest is a low, grey-green shrub with succulent, jointed stems and tiny paired leaves that fold up in the heat. Its roots spread far laterally, often running a dozen metres to reach a single seep. Between shrubs grow mats of a flat, lichen-like crust in bright orange and blue, which bloom dark and glossy after rain and turn to brittle sheets in the dry months.

Along the water's edge there are reed-like stems, pale and hollow, standing in clusters that look almost deliberate. They rattle faintly in what little wind there is. Where the cliffs seep, long hanging filaments of a dark green fibrous plant trail down the rock face like wet hair. These are the only tall things in the basin.

Nothing grows on the upper plates. The salt keeps them clean.

**Fauna**

For such an apparently barren place, the animal life is abundant, though nearly all of it is small. Along the water's edge, a dense population of shelled creatures the size of a fingernail moves in slow, spiralling aggregations. They graze the filmy biofilm on the mud and leave behind faint silvery trails that catch the light. In the deeper pool, something larger and translucent drifts near the surface, pulsing gently, its body filled with the same coloured crust that grows on the shrubs. It seems to feed on the crust, and in turn the crust may be nourished by its waste.

Above the water, insects are everywhere in the mornings: a thin, shimmering population that dances over the pools and vanishes into the cracks of the plates when the heat peaks. Several species of small, many-legged, armoured runners skitter across the crust at dawn and dusk, their shells banded to match the cliff strata. Lizard-like forms with wide, flat bodies bask on the warm rock and flatten themselves into the plate-gaps when anything large approaches. Birds are rare, but once in a while a single long-winged form circles the rim at great height, then drops into the basin with a sudden, unexpected stillness.

The animals seem to organise themselves around the water's cycle. When the pockets dry, the shelled grazers aggregate into dense, crusted clusters on the last wet mud. When rain comes, the deeper pool swells with activity and every creature in the basin moves toward it.

**The strange parts**

The basin holds several things that seem to belong to a different order of time.

The cliffs are riddled with hollow concretions: rounded nodules embedded in the strata, some as large as a person, that have weathered out and left smooth-walled cavities. Many of them are connected by narrow, winding channels that run through the rock. Wind moves through these channels and produces a low, intermittent whistle that is almost inaudible in ordinary conditions but noticeable in the stillness before rain.

Along the central pool's margin, the mud has formed low, concentric ridges, like the growth rings of a stump, each one a few centimetres high. The ridges are arranged around the pool's edge in a stepped series, and the outermost are older and more eroded. Someone who wanted to date the basin's history might count them, but a lifetime of observation would show only a single ring added every few years, so slow is the process that builds them.

Scattered across the lower terraces, half-buried in the silt, are a few dozen flaked stones. They are dark, fine-grained, and worked along one edge with a few deliberate blows. They are not arranged in any pattern and do not appear to have been used for anything recent. Each is weathered at the surface as though it has lain for a very long time, and the crust has grown over several. Whoever left them left no other trace: no hearth, no refuse, no path. They are simply there, the way a fallen leaf is there, and the basin has been quietly absorbing them.

**Sound**

The basin is mostly quiet. Wind here is weak and intermittent, rising for a few minutes at a time, then vanishing entirely. In the still hours you hear the tiny sounds of the place: the crackle of plates settling, the faint scrape of grazers on mud, the rattle of reeds, a distant insect's whirr. Water in the pools makes almost no sound except when something breaks the surface. The cliffs give back echoes that come back softened and late.

When rain falls, the sound changes entirely. A roar comes from the rim, the channels fill with rushing water, and the cliffs hum with the air forced through their hollow channels. Then, within a day or two, the silence returns, deeper than before.

**Atmosphere and time**

The air is warm and dry except at the bottom of the pans, where the water holds a faint, sharp, mineral damp. Dust does not settle here; the salt crust keeps the air clean. You can smell rain before it comes, a cool iron-and-stone scent that rises off the cliffs an hour before the first drops fall.

Nothing here is fast. The plates rise over decades. The pool's rings accrue over years. The cliffs are retreating at a rate measured in fingernails per century. The shelled grazers complete a full cycle with the seasons, and the shrubs live longer than any animal that feeds on them. The basin seems to have been in roughly this state for a very long time, and it will probably remain so for longer than anyone can imagine, rising and falling with the rain, slowly rearranging its own floor, and quietly keeping every stone that is set down upon it.
