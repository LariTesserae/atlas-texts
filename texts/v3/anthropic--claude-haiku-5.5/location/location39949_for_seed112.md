**The Upland Roar**

You arrive on a plateau so high the air is thin and bright, with a clarity that makes distant things look close and close things look wrong. The sky is pale enamel, the sun hard and white, and the light lies over everything without shadow-softening, so every edge is sharp. It is cool, cold enough that breath clouds at dawn, and the cold has a metallic taste.

**Terrain**

The land rises in long, scarred shelves, each a few hundred meters high and running for kilometers. Their faces are stratified, banded in ochre, rust, and a grey-green that might be mineral or might be dried plant matter fused into rock. Where the shelves meet, they fall into canyons whose floors are lost in motion. Nothing here is flat. Even the high meadows tilt, tipping toward the lower country like a table set on a slope.

Water is almost absent. There are dry channels, polished smooth and lined with glassy crust, that once carried floods. Occasionally a pocket of brine lies at the bottom of a sink, too thick to move, its surface crusted in white plates that crack and reform as the temperature swings. Rain is rare, but when it comes it comes all at once, and the ground is built to receive it.

**Vegetation as Substrate**

Plant life covers nearly everything, but it is not a forest or a meadow in the usual sense. It is a single enormous fabric. The ground is a dense, layered mat, a woven crust of root, stem, and fibrous leaf, several meters deep in places. Where the mat is cut open, you see its structure: pale vascular cords thick as your arm, branching and rejoining, a living lattice that holds the shelves together and feeds the upper growth.

From the mat rise towers and columns, plant-architecture that has grown up over centuries. Some are slender and fluted, like organ pipes of wood gone to stone. Others are broad, bulbous, with ribbed sides and open crowns where pale fronds catch the wind. The towers are sparse, separated by kilometers of mat or bare rock, so the land feels emptier than its vegetation would suggest. Between them, the mat itself is the landscape, sprawling and undulating, pocked with sinkholes and blowouts where the lattice has torn open.

**Industry Without Makers**

Here the "engines" are living. Along the canyon walls, giant pressure-organs swell and collapse. They are like lungs or bellows made of tissue, each hundreds of meters across, sealed in a membrane that stretches taut and then releases. When they vent, the air shudders. Sap is pumped through the cords of the mat under enormous pressure, and wherever the pressure fails, a fountain erupts, a brief geyser of viscous fluid that hangs in the cold air and falls in glittering sheets.

There are no roads, no walls, no buildings, no hands. But the machinery of this place runs constantly: pumps, valves, sluices, pressure chambers, all grown rather than built, all working. It is industry with no industrialists, a factory with no door.

**Fauna**

The fauna is abundant and loud. Insect-analogues move in clouds, their wing-beats forming a continuous drone, a buzzing so dense it becomes texture rather than sound. Larger creatures, many-legged and segmented, graze along the mat, tearing sections free with a crack like splitting timber. Above, flyers circle the towers in wide spirals, their calls sharp and piercing, cutting through the constant background.

Animals and plants are entangled. Some beasts carry seeds in their guts and deposit them in the cracks of the shelves. Others burrow into the mat and open new channels for sap to flow. A few seem to tend the pressure-organs, nudging them, feeding them, keeping the rhythm.

**Sound**

The sound is deafening. The wind is never gentle. It funnels through canyons and across shelf-edges with a roar that drowns speech. Dry pods rattle on the towers like thousands of castanets. Pressure-organs release with a bass boom that you feel in your chest and teeth. Sap-geysers hiss and splash. The insect drone never stops. Beneath all of it is a deep, slow groan, the sound of the mat shifting and settling, enormous and patient.

Silence exists only in the moments between storms, and it is brief.

**Dynamics and Storms**

The place is turbulent. Storms come and go across the plateau, their fronts sweeping over the mat in waves of dust, spores, and grit. Lightning is common, and where it strikes the mat it can ignite pockets of dry fibrous growth, sending plumes of smoke that burn for days. Growth is fast and violent: towers can rise in weeks and collapse in a single gale, their fronds torn free and sent tumbling across the shelves. The mat re-knits itself over the scars, but the process is chaotic, and the shape of the land changes constantly.

Erosion and creation proceed together. A canyon wall may crumble while a new tower shoots up beside it. Sinkholes open and fill. The brine-pans expand and contract with each rain. Nothing settles for long, yet the overall form endures, as though the whole plateau is a single organism, always in motion, always rebuilding itself, always in turmoil.

**Light and Atmosphere**

The light is bright, almost painful at midday, and it lies flat and white across everything. Dust hangs in the air and catches the sun, turning the sky into a haze of silver. Mornings are cold and clear, and the air sharpens. Afternoons bring storm-clouds piling over the western shelves, dark and fast. Dusk comes quickly, and the sky turns violet and then bruised green before full dark, when the towers stand as black silhouettes against stars and the drone of insects fades only slightly.

**The Rules Here**

Nothing is made by hands, yet everything is made. Growth is the only construction. Pressure is the only power. Turbulence is the only stability. The plateau does not rest, and the sound of it never ends. Those who would cross it must move with the storms, listen for the booming of the pressure-organs, and watch the mat for the sudden cracks that signal a sinkhole is opening beneath their feet.
