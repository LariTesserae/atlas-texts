# The Drowned Plateau

The land rises to a broad, tilted tableland, perhaps two thousand meters above whatever sea once touched its edges. Nothing about it looks dry. Water covers all of it, but not as a simple surface. It stands in layers. Ground water seeps up through black soil and pools in shallow basins, and above those basins a dense, cold mist hangs at knee height, thickening toward the horizon until it becomes a grey wall that swallows the far ridges. Higher still, the air itself is heavy with moisture, so the whole world feels like the bottom of a lake that has forgotten the surface.

The light is poor. The sky is a low ceiling of slate cloud that never quite breaks, and what daylight reaches the ground arrives diffused and bruised, mostly the color of wet ash. Without the sun, the plateau runs on a dim, cold glow. Patches of fungal matting along the stream banks give off a faint blue-green phosphorescence, and where the lush vegetation grows densest, it seems to hold the light the way deep water does, so the eye keeps finding brief pale gleams in the shadows.

That vegetation is the strangest thing here, and it is abundant. Stalks rise from the flooded ground like giant reeds or kelp, pale and ribbed, some taller than a house. Their tops split into fans of translucent leaves that trail down through the mist. Thick green mats of moss and lichen blanket every rock and fallen trunk, glossy with constant wetness. Vines hang from the stalks in curtains, dripping steadily. Nothing here is still. The stalks sway in currents of cold air, and the mats swell and contract as they drink. Pollen-like spores drift upward through the fog and gather in slow spirals that catch what little light there is.

There is no animal life at all. No insects buzz, no birds call, nothing scuttles through the reeds. The absence is noticeable, almost pressing. The plants do the work of living in its place, and the process that might be called ecology runs on growth, decay, and water alone. Fallen stalks sink into the basins and dissolve into dark, nutrient-rich silt within weeks. Spore clouds settle into new mats. Roots lift and fall as the ground's water table rises and drops over days. The whole system pulses on its own rhythm.

The sound is constant and heavy. A waterfall roars somewhere in the grey distance, and its echo rolls across the plateau in waves. Closer streams gurgle through stone channels, and where water pours into deep fissures, it makes a hollow, booming thud that travels through the ground. The stalks creak and knock together in the wind. Every so often a vent of trapped gas breaks the surface of a basin with a sharp hiss and a splash that can be heard for kilometers. Between these sounds lies a steady drone of dripping, a sound so pervasive that it becomes the background of thought.

Human-made things appear here only sparingly, scattered across the basins and ridges like dropped objects. Low cairns of flat stone rise above the waterline, their tops green with moss. Some have been stacked into tall, narrow columns and bound with fiber cord that has gone black and brittle. A few stilted platforms made of lashed reeds and split timber stand in the shallows. They are weathered grey, sagging, and have clearly been abandoned for a long time, though not so long that they've vanished. Occasionally one finds a broken tool: a flint blade wedged in silt, a carved bone handle, a sharpened wooden stake leaning against a boulder. The craft is simple and hand-made, and each artifact seems to have been left in place deliberately, as if marking a spot where something once happened.

The land's rhythm is slow and cyclical. Over a span of days, the water table rises and falls in a rough tide. At high water, the basins merge into shallow lakes, the reed stalks stand waist-deep, and the mist thickens. At low water, the silt is exposed in grey, cracked plates, the mats dry and curl at their edges, and the stream channels scour deeper. Weather on the plateau moves in long fronts that roll in from the east, bringing sudden downpours that swell every stream within an hour. Afterward the air hangs wet and still for days. This push and pull keeps the place constantly in motion, reshaping the boundaries between land and water, and with them the stalks, mats, and stone markers that rise and sink with the tide.

Beneath all of this is a sense of something older than the abandoned structures. The cairns and platforms suggest that people once lived here and organized their lives around the rising and falling water, but the plateau has absorbed them. Its ecology does not notice them. The stalks grow through the stilts. The mats cover the cairns. The water finds its way into every crack, and the land keeps breathing its slow, wet, dark breath.
