The first thing you notice is that the ground isn't ground. Step off a causeway and your foot meets a surface that was made on purpose: a crust of fired clay, pressed in tiles the size of a hand, their edges still sharp, as if they were laid last week. Beneath them lies packed ash, crushed shell, and reed-fiber, layered in bands you can see where the edges have split. Nothing here was ever bedrock. The whole low country is a fabrication laid over a flood plain, and the flood plain is still trying to be a marsh.

The land is flat, a few hand-spans above the water table. Terraces step down to the margins in gentle sets, each edged with a lip of stacked stone and woven withy. Canals run between them in long straight lines that bend only where something forced them to, and the water in them lies so close to the top of the banks that a dropped leaf sits almost level with the path. Everything is damp. The air is thick and warm and carries the sweetness of rot and flowering at once. Dew never quite leaves the lower surfaces, and the brick walls sweat in the mornings, leaving dark stains that dry into pale rings.

Vegetation is lush and grows through the made things rather than around them. Reed beds stand in ranks, planted in rows that still follow the surveyor's grid. Vines climb the fired-clay posts of old markers and have bent the posts slightly out of true. Broad-leafed plants the color of wet slate grow from cracks in the terraces, and their roots run horizontally just beneath the tiles, lifting them in neat ridges. Trees are few and tall, mostly planted, their trunks wrapped in cord and bark-cloth, their canopies clipped flat at the height of a low roof.

The fauna is abundant and quietly unafraid. Herons stand on the rooftops of squat storage houses. Frogs sit in rows along the canal edges, all facing the same direction. Long-necked birds pick through the reed-beds with the patience of tools. Insects hang in the warm air in dense, slow columns. Fish turn in the shallows, but the water never breaks with their rising, so you see the ripples only after they've already passed.

Sound is thin. The rare calls of birds arrive muffled, as if from behind cloth, and the water makes almost no noise against the banks. You hear your own footfalls on the tiles, a dry clicking, and the soft lap of canal water on stone, which seems to come a moment late. People are seldom visible, but their tools lie where they were set down: flat paddles leaning against walls, baskets half-woven, clay vessels stacked to dry.

Light is harsh and even. The sun sits high and white, and shadows fall short and crisp beneath everything, though some shadows lie slightly the wrong way, pointing toward a sun that is not quite where the sky says it is. Reflections in the canals show a sky a little darker, a little more gold, as though the water is a few minutes behind.

Here the reality of the place begins to fray. A canal runs uphill for a dozen paces, then resumes its level course without a visible join. A reed-bed's rows turn back on themselves, so that a row you walked past early in the morning reappears at your back. A wall of fired brick carries, in its inner faces, the impressions of roots that never grew through it, and the impressions are fresher than the brick. Certain pale rings on the walls match exactly the rings of dew from the day before, and when you look again they match yesterday's rings instead.

Nothing changes, though nothing is quite still. The heron on the roof keeps its angle; the frogs keep their faces toward the same bend in the water; the clouds stand over the terraces in the same lace of white. Yet the surfaces hold a faint shimmer, as if the whole country were a painting being breathed on very slowly, and the breath never settles. The place seems to be in equilibrium so complete that it has forgotten how to become anything else, and it persists in its made, wet, bright, humming-without-sound stillness, waiting for a change that the makers, long gone, once meant to bring.
