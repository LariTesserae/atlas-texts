**A Laid Basin**

The first thing you notice is that the ground is not ground. It is a floor of set stone, fitted and re-fitted, rising in shallow terraces toward a rim about the height of a standing man. Nothing here was ever soil. Someone, or many someones, laid every course by hand, bedding flat slabs in clay, packing the joints with crushed shell and charcoal, cutting channels where the water was meant to go. The work is so old that it has stopped looking like work. It looks like sediment. The courses sit in bands of grey, ochre, and a dark greenish stone, and they read the way cliff strata read, as if the basin had been deposited over a long span of time rather than built in one.

The whole thing is perhaps a few hundred paces across, a single bounded hollow with a clear edge. Beyond the rim the air goes flat and grey, and you cannot tell whether there is more land or only haze. Inside, the space is open and sparse. Things stand far apart: a tall post of fired clay, a row of upright stones with holes bored through them, a squat cistern with a lid of overlapping tiles. Between them is a lot of nothing, and the nothing is wet.

Water is everywhere, and it is not standing so much as seeping. Every joint in the stonework weeps. Thin sheets run down the terrace faces and gather in the lower channels, which are narrow and deep and carry a slow, brown-clear flow toward a central sump. The sump is round, lined with dressed blocks, and its surface is never still; it boils gently from below where the groundwater pushes up. Mist sits low over the channels in the cool mornings and does not burn off until well after noon, if it burns off at all. Condensation beads on everything that stands still: the posts, the tile lids, the undersides of the stone arches that span one channel where a walkway once crossed. The air tastes of mineral and cold iron.

The plants are few and stubborn. Moss fills the joints and the north faces of the upright stones, grey-green and thick as felt. Low rushes grow in the lower channels where the flow slackens, and a pale, fleshy creeper crawls over the rims of the cisterns like a spill that never dried. There are no trees. The vegetation seems to be there mostly to hold the water in place, and it does this with the patience of something that has been doing it for a very long time.

Light is dim and comes from everywhere at once. There is a sun somewhere behind the overcast, a bright smear that lights the rim and leaves the basin floor in a steady dusk. Where the water pools, it catches what light there is and returns it upward, so the channels seem to glow faintly from within, a dull silver. In the deeper joints and the shadow of the arches, the light is almost gone, and small pale shapes move there.

And the sound is the loudest thing in the place. Frogs, or things that fill the frogs' role, call from every channel and joint in overlapping layers: a wet, rhythmic knocking from the sump, a thin rising whistle from the rushes, a deep resonant bellow from the cistern mouths where the sound comes back doubled. Insects hum in a continuous drone that rises whenever the wind moves across the rim. Water talks constantly, drips ticking on tile, the trickle of the channels, the bubbling of the sump. Now and then a larger call comes from far off past the rim, something with a voice like a hinge being forced, and every creature in the basin goes briefly silent before the chorus resumes.

The fauna is abundant, and it is active. Long segmented things with too many legs move in the seams of the stonework, flicking in and out of the moss. Pale, eel-like bodies slide through the deeper channels, rising to the surface in slow spirals, and the surface bulges when they pass beneath. Flat-bodied creatures cling to the upright stones in colonies and open their gills in unison when the mist thickens. Birds, if they are birds, come down into the basin in the cool hours, skim the sump, and rise again with something glinting in their beaks. Predators and prey are never far apart here. Every few minutes something is caught, something else is swallowed, and the chorus shifts in pitch to mark the change.

The strangeness is in the relation between the made and the grown. The stonework has been colonised so thoroughly that it behaves like a living substrate: moss thickens in certain joints each season, channels silt and reopen, a cistern lid cracks along a line that is clearly following old water. The builders are not here. Their tools, if any survive, are buried under centuries of sediment and seepage. What remains is their order, which the water keeps trying to undo and which the creatures keep reinforcing, each in its own way, so that the basin is now a made thing that is also slowly becoming a natural one, and nobody can say where the one stops.

If you stand near the sump at dusk, with the frogs knocking and the mist rising off the channels, you may notice that the laid courses in the lower terraces are not quite level anymore. They have settled, a fraction of a finger's width over a hundred years, tilting toward the water. The basin is still draining into itself. It has always been draining into itself, and it will go on doing so after anyone who could read its plan has gone.
