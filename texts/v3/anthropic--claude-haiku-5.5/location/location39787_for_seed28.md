# The Basin of Hollow Stone

This is a pocket world, a basin roughly twelve kilometers across, sunk into a plateau and sealed by sheer walls of banded rust-colored rock. A single breach in the rim, a fissure perhaps forty meters wide, lets in a column of hard white light. From the floor, the sky is a bright disc that never quite looks like sky. The basin is an enclosed room in a much larger geology, and it feels like one.

## The Ground

There is no water here, and there has not been for a very long time. The floor is a dry mineral pan, cracked into plates the size of tables, their edges curled upward like old paper. Between the plates runs a fine grey silt that puffs underfoot and clings to skin and cloth. Where the silt is thickest it has been packed hard by generations of feet and carts into roads, and the roads are pale and smooth as bone.

The erosion here is old and settled. The walls have worn into terraces and rounded shelves, and the talus at their feet has long since stopped moving. Nothing collapses now. The basin has reached a kind of geological patience, and the only changes come from the things living on its floor.

## The Growth

The vegetation is enormous and almost violently green, and it has no business thriving in a place without water. The plants are thick-stemmed and waxy, with broad leaves coated in a faintly iridescent film. That film is the key to the whole basin. It is not water but a heavy, non-aqueous secretion, something like a sap that condenses from the vents below and runs down the leaves in slow, oily rivers. The plants drink from this, and their roots reach down into a porous layer of resinous stone where a slow seep of hydrocarbon-rich fluid collects.

The trees are tall and lean, with bark that peels in long translucent sheets, and their crowns shiver constantly. Between them climb vines that are almost woody, and beneath them spreads a dense understory of low, fleshy plants that clench and open their leaves when touched. Everything is oily to the touch and smells faintly of resin and warm metal.

## The Sound

The basin is loud, and most of the noise comes from the plants. Seed pods on the taller trees split open with dry cracks like gunshots, and the sound rolls around the walls for seconds afterward. Reed-stands sway and clatter, their hollow stalks knocking together in long rattling waves. When wind comes down the fissure it finds a hundred resonant openings and produces a low, sustained drone, something between a horn and a chant, that you feel in your ribs before you hear it. The vents below the plateau hiss continuously, a white noise underneath everything else.

Animal sound is almost absent. Fauna is rare, and the few creatures here are mostly small, slow, and quiet: a grey burrowing thing that surfaces at dusk, a bird with a hooked beak that perches on the highest pods and screams once before flying away. The loudness of the basin belongs to the plants, the wind, and the people.

## The Built Environment

The people here have built a city across the basin floor and up the lower terraces of the walls. It is dense and layered. Stone houses with flat roofs are stacked on each other, connected by ramps, ladders, and rope bridges strung between the taller trees. The oldest structures are carved directly into the rock, with doorways shaped like the mouths of bells. Newer buildings are timber and woven resin-mat, lashed together with fibrous cord.

The streets are crowded. Carts pulled by long-legged beasts move through the silt-roads, and markets spill out of courtyards where the plants have been cut back to make room. The city is primitive in its tools: chipped stone blades, bronze-colored hand-hammered fittings, pottery fired in open pits, looms of wood and bone. Light and heat come from the vents, which have been capped with clay chimneys and fitted with hand-pumped bellows. Some of the vent-chimneys glow orange at night.

Everything is built in a loud, crowded, layered way. Doors bang, cart wheels grind on stone, pods crack overhead, and the city's own bells ring from the terraces at irregular intervals. The sound is constant and dense, a wall of noise that residents seem to live inside without much notice.

## The Light

The light is strong and white, coming down the fissure in a shaft that moves across the basin as the day passes. Where it falls, the oily leaves flare with color, and the silt glows almost like snow. In the shadows under the trees, the light is soft, green-gold, and filtered through the sheeting bark. Around noon the basin is painfully bright, and the people move through the shade. In the evening the shaft fades to amber, and the vent-chimneys become the main source of light. The walls turn the color of dried blood, and the whole city glows faintly from within.

## The Strange

Some things here do not fit together as they should. The resin-fog that feeds the plants also seems to pool in low hollows and form slow, drifting shapes that look almost like animals before they dissolve. Certain stones in the walls hum when touched, at a pitch that changes with the temperature. The people have a word for the basin that translates roughly as "the room that breathes," and they explain that the vents rise and fall in long cycles of about forty days, that the plants open or close their leaves in response, and that the sound of the city changes with each cycle. Outsiders who stay long enough report that the basin's noise seems to come from somewhere just behind the ears, and that after a few weeks, silence feels like a missing organ.

## Rhythm and Motion

Nothing here is still. The vents pulse and shift. The shaft of light crawls across the floor. The plants open, close, crack their pods, and drop their oil. The people trade, build, repair, and argue. Every process is slow in its own way, but together they make a constant, churning motion, a balance of growth and wear in a sealed space that has been running for a very long time.
