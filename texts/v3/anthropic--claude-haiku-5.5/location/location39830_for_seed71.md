# The Planed Highlands

The land stands very high, on a plateau so old that it has stopped trying to be a mountain range. Its ridges have been worn down over unimaginable spans until they are long, low swells, rounded like the knuckles of a sleeping hand. Nothing here is sharp. Every edge has been breathed on and softened. The rock underfoot is a grey-violet granite, polished in places to a faint sheen, and where it has cracked, the fractures have filled with peat and moss until the stone reads as a suggestion beneath the green.

## Water and Air

The air is cold and wet in a way that never quite becomes rain for long. A cloud-sea sits at the lower edges of the plateau and rises in the afternoons, spilling over the rims in slow, grey curtains. Rain comes in intermittent bursts, a few minutes of steady fall that leaves every surface beaded, and then the sky clears to a pale, washed blue for an hour before the next front arrives. Condensation is constant. Stones sweat. Leaves hold drops that never quite fall. The ground is spongy, and where it dips, shallow tarns lie flat and mirror-still, edged with reeds that never bend because there is no wind to bend them.

Rivers here do not cut so much as wander. They braid across the flats in shining threads, slow and brown with tannin, and they pool in long oxbow lakes where the water is so still it holds reflections for minutes after a stone breaks its surface. Some pools are very deep and very clear. Some are shallow enough to walk across, and in these, faint patterns of old channels show through the silt, as if the land is remembering rivers that have since moved on.

## Vegetation

Growth is lush, close, and layered. Mosses carpet everything: grey-green cushions on the rocks, rust-colored mats on the peat, pale silver lichens that climb the standing stones in slow spirals. Ferns grow waist-high in the hollows, with fronds the size of umbrellas and stems thick as wrists. Low shrubs with dark, glossy leaves crowd the hillsides, and above them rise broad, slow-growing trees with bark like cracked plate armor and canopies so dense that the forest floor is a cool green dusk even at midday.

There are no flowers to speak of, or only rare ones: pale, waxy blooms the color of old bone, open for a single day and then folded into their own stems. Fruit is bitter and gourd-shaped, hanging in clusters under the trees. Much of the plant life seems to grow toward the stone ruins, and roots lace through the mortar of old walls until the walls and the roots are indistinguishable.

## The Built Layer

Settlements dot the plateau at wide intervals, and they are large, far larger than their inhabitants could plausibly have needed. These are cities in the sense of extent: grids of stone foundations, terraced plazas, long processional avenues running arrow-straight across the land until they disappear into mist. The construction is primitive. Every block was shaped by hand, with stone hammers and abrasive sand, and the joins are visible. But the scale is staggering, and the layers are deep. One city sits atop another, which sits atop a third, each generation building over the last, so that doorways open onto stairs leading down to older doorways, and some walls show three or four different systems of orientation, rotated against one another like a stack of maps.

Nothing moves in these ruins except water and plant growth. Gutters run with rainwater. Courtyards hold small pools, and in the pools grow thick green mats of algae that glow faintly at dusk. Hearths survive in some rooms, black-stained and cold. Tools are scattered where they were set down: stone adzes, bone awls, flat clay tablets scored with lines that repeat in patterns almost meaningful. There are no bodies, no bones, and no sign of how the people left.

## The Weirdness

Here reality begins to fray, and it does so in quiet, specific ways.

Some streets are longer on the way out than on the way back. A person walking an avenue from one end will arrive at a plaza that should be a mile distant, and when they turn to retrace their steps, the avenue is the correct length again. The mist holds shapes for a while: outlines of towers, figures of people standing still, then dissolves them. Mirror-pools reflect a sky that is not the sky above them. In one, the stars are arranged in an unfamiliar pattern, and in another, the reflected plateau is green where the real one is grey.

The rain sometimes falls against the wind, a few drops rising in a column from the stone. Shadows lag behind their objects by a half-second. Old carved inscriptions, when read at dusk, seem to have shifted letters, and in the morning they have shifted back. Some of the terraces are slightly tilted in ways that make standing on them uneasy, not steep enough to slide but enough that the body insists on correcting for a slope that isn't there.

Time moves oddly in the lower ruins. Moss grows visibly over a few hours and then stops, as if it has reached a decision. Ferns unfurl in the space of a breath. Water in the courtyard pools occasionally runs backward through its channels for an instant before correcting itself.

## Sound and Silence

This place is mostly quiet. Without animal life there is no birdsong, no insect hum, no rustle of small creatures in the undergrowth. The sounds that do exist are sparse and have room to echo: water dripping from leaves into standing pools, the hiss of distant rain, the low creak of old trees shifting in air too still to call wind. When the mist comes down, sound dies entirely for minutes at a time. Footsteps sink into moss. Voices seem to stop a few feet from the speaker's mouth.

Occasionally, from very far away, there is a deep, slow sound like a stone dragged across another stone, or a bell struck underwater. No one has found its source. It may be the plateau settling, or the rain finishing its long work on the ridges, or something else that the land has grown accustomed to doing.

## Rhythm

The place is active in its slow way. Weather turns over every few hours. Plants grow and die on cycles measured in days. The rivers shift their braids by inches each season. The mist rises and falls with the light. Erosion continues, invisible but unceasing, grinding the last edges off the stones. Nothing here is still for long, even when it looks still. The land is finishing a sentence it began a very long time ago, and it is in no hurry to end it.
