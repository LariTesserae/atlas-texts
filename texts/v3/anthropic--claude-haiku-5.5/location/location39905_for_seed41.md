**The Sinking Room**

You descend into the ground by a path that was once a stair. The first steps are still square-cut, their edges rounded by centuries of rain and root, and each one is a little greener than the last. The air thickens as you go down. It is warm and wet, like breath held in a closed mouth, and it smells of rot, resin, and cold stone under the green.

The land here has fallen in on itself. Long ago the limestone roof of a cavern gave way, and what is left is a basin about the size of a village square, sunk some forty meters below the surrounding plain. Its walls are pale and fluted, stained with orange and black where water has run for ages. Overhead, a ragged ring of sky shows through a tangle of hanging vines. Light falls through it in slanting columns, and the columns are full of drifting pollen and gnats. Below the ring the light goes to amber, then to a green dimness, and the floor is nearly dark.

**The Floor**

The floor is a grove. Trees grow out of the broken stone at impossible angles, their trunks leaning toward the light. Strangler figs have wrapped older trees so completely that the host has rotted away inside, leaving a hollow lattice of living wood shaped like a tree. Ferns the height of a person crowd between them, and beneath those a carpet of moss and fungus glows faintly where it is wet. Everything is wet. Water beads on every leaf and runs in the grooves of the bark, and the moss gives underfoot with a soft, cool give.

Through the grove runs a shallow, black pool, fed by a seep from the north wall. Its surface is still except where something moves beneath it. Lily pads cover half of it, and their undersides are crowded with eggs and small, dark, busy creatures. In the middle, a stone slab sits partly submerged. It may once have been an altar or a step or a boundary marker. Its carving is worn to suggestion: a curve, a notch, a line that could be a path or a river or a hand. Roots have entered its cracks and split it in places, and a fig has grown up through its center. The tree now supports the slab, and the slab holds up the tree.

**The Walls**

The walls are alive with small, layered ecologies. Water seeps from the stone in curtains and sheets. The rock is pocked with holes, and each hole is occupied: a spider, a snail, a colony of dark and patient insects. Ferns root in the cracks. Vines with broad, ribbed leaves climb in ropes thick as a wrist. Above the lowest tier, bats hang in clusters, folded and twitching, and every so often a few of them drop from the ceiling and vanish into the grove.

Near the top of the north wall there is a ledge that holds a row of square-cut stones. They look like the footings of something: a wall, a room, a shelter. Nothing is left above them but moss. Their joints are filled with living root, and a line of small, bright-backed beetles crosses them in single file, always in the same direction, always following a route that has been worn into the stone.

**Atmosphere and Sound**

The air is heavy and very still. Heat sits on the skin. You can hear almost nothing: a soft drip from somewhere above, a rustle when a lizard shifts in the leaf litter, the faint scrape of a snail on wet rock, the whisper of wings. The quiet is not empty. It is the quiet of many small things moving carefully, and when one of them stops, the silence comes down heavier.

The light changes slowly. Above, the sky-ring brightens and dims with the passing of clouds, and the shafts of light move across the grove like the hands of a clock. The pool reflects the ring in fragments. When it rains above, a fine sheet of water comes down through the opening and the whole grove seems to exhale. The walls darken, the moss brightens, the pool's level climbs an inch over an hour and then falls back as the water drains through the stone.

**Rhythms**

Nothing here holds still. The fig-wrapped trees are slowly strangling their hosts, and the hosts are slowly losing. Fallen leaves and insect bodies are carried down into the pool and disappear. Frogs call only at dusk, when the bats are out and the shadows are long. Fish in the pool rise to take the midges that gather in the columns of light, and a long, pale shape sometimes moves below the surface, stays a while, and goes back into the dark under the altar. The spiders rebuild their webs every morning, and by evening most of them are torn, and the spiders eat the moths caught in them. The beetles walk their line along the ledge. The seep runs. The roots push. The rock weathers a grain at a time.

**The Odd Part**

At certain hours, when the light has turned amber and the air is at its stillest, the moss along the walls gives off a faint, cool glow. It is not bright enough to read by, only enough to see the outlines of things. The pool then reflects something that is not quite the sky, a deeper shade of blue with a pattern in it, like the ghost of a much larger room above. The beetles stop moving. The bats return to their ceiling in silence. For a while the whole grove seems to be listening. Then the light fades, the bats come back out, and the frogs begin.

You stand at the bottom of the stair, wet to the knee, and realize you have been holding your breath. The air is too warm to be pleasant and too alive to be frightening. Somewhere in the ferns something small and quick moves away from you, and the leaves close behind it.
