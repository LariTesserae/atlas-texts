**The Sunken Terraces**

You are standing at the bottom of a hollow that has no visible roof. The darkness is total, not the dimness of a cave mouth or a moonless night but the absence of any photon that could reach you. You know the place only by touch, by sound, and by the faint chemical bite in the air. The air is cool and still, but not quite still. Somewhere far above, a slow draft moves through the void and stirs the fibers around you.

The ground beneath your feet is salt. It is a grey-white crust, hard and faintly ridged where it has dried in layers over unknown ages, and it crunches softly under weight. There is no water here. There was once, long ago, and the salt is its memory. Where the crust cracks you can see the mineral strata beneath, banded in ochre, pale green, and bone-white, each band a different epoch of drying. The basin dips gently toward its center, so that everything here is settling inward, as if the whole place were slowly exhaling into its own low point.

The built structures rise from the salt in tiers. They were made by hands with simple tools, stone shaped by stone and wood pegged with wood, and they are enormous. Terraces step up the basin walls in concentric rings, each one a level street or plaza, each one lined with low walls and the bases of pillars that have lost their tops. Erosion has done most of the work. Edges have been rounded into soft curves. Stairways have worn into ramps. Doorways have sagged into arches that look more like the mouths of animals than the work of builders. Nothing here is sharp. Everything has been sanded by ages of salt-laden air until it has the smoothness of river stone.

What makes the place strange is the plant. It covers everything. It is not scattered across the ruins but woven through them, so that the terraces, walls, and columns have become indistinguishable from the fibrous lattice that has grown over them. It is pale, the color of bleached bone or old paper, and it has no need of light. It draws what it needs from the mineral seep in the stone and from the faint chemistry of the air. Its tissue hangs in curtains from the upper ledges, lies in mats over the floors, and climbs the pillars in spirals thick as a man's torso. Where the lattice is thinnest, you can see through it to the stone beneath, like frost on glass. Where it is thickest, it forms a membrane that moves when touched and holds a faint warmth.

Here the density of the place reveals itself. Although the plant covers every surface, it is only a skin. It has very little mass per unit of space. Its fibers are fine and branching, and between them lie enormous volumes of nothing. You can stand in a plaza that is entirely draped in pale growth and still see, through the gaps, clear black air extending upward and outward for what might be a hundred meters or a thousand. The structure is less a forest than a web laid over a vast emptiness. Your footsteps echo off into that emptiness, and you do not hear them come back for a long time.

The sound of this place is the most startling thing about it. It is loud, in a way that a place this still ought not to be. The lattice ticks and creaks as it shifts in the draft, a dry continuous clicking like a thousand small insects counting. Every so often a seed pod somewhere along the walls splits with a sharp report, a crack that rolls through the terraces and dies slowly into the dark. Far off, a section of ancient masonry gives way, and the sound of stone falling into stone takes long seconds to reach you and longer to fade. The wind through the upper voids produces a deep, sustained hum, almost a chord, that you feel more in your sternum than your ears. Underneath all of it is a faint, constant hiss, the sound of the salt settling.

The people who built here used tools that were only a step beyond the stone hand-axe. You can see their work in the tool marks on the stone blocks, in the regular pattern of their drill holes, and in the carved channels that once carried water across the terraces. The channels are dry now, and filled with the same pale growth as everything else. Occasionally you find a small object caught in the lattice: a fired clay bead, a flake of obsidian, a bone needle, worn so smooth that its origin is hard to tell. These are the only things here that seem to have been made recently, although they are older than any count you could make.

Nothing moves quickly in the Sunken Terraces. The lattice grows over centuries, consuming a new ledge with the patience of a glacier. The salt deposits a new layer every few decades. The stone erodes by a grain's thickness per generation. Even the seed pods split on their own slow schedule, so that the sudden cracks feel less like events than like the slow heartbeat of something that has nearly finished its life. The place is not dying, exactly. It has simply arrived at a shape it intends to keep, and it is in no hurry to change it.

If you wait here long enough, you begin to hear the rhythm underneath the noise. The creaks gather into long waves, rising and falling with the draft. The seed pods crack in clusters, then fall silent for an age. The hum of the wind swells and sinks in a cycle you cannot quite count. You are standing inside a very large, very old instrument, and it is playing a piece that will not finish while you are alive to hear it.
