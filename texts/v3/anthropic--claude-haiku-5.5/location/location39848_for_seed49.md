# The Sump Plain

The land is nearly flat and nearly water. From any rise you can see the whole thing at once: a grey-silver expanse running to a horizon that never quite sharpens, because the air above it is thick with vapor and the vapor carries a faint shimmer. Standing water covers most of the ground in sheets a few centimeters deep, and the sheets are never still. Wind combs them into ridges, rain pocks them, and something beneath keeps pushing up through them in slow, rhythmic swells. The plain breathes.

Nothing here is green. There are no plants, no leaves, no grasses, not even the soft mats of moss you'd expect in a place this damp. The ground is mineral: pale sinter that has crusted over the mud in plates, rust-colored iron bloom in the channels, and glassy black fused material in long seams where something once burned very hot and the heat was never quite cleared away. Where the sinter has grown thick it forms terraces and rimstone pools in the manner of a limestone cave, but the rims are sharp and geometric, edges pulled straight by some process that prefers right angles.

## The Architecture

Everything that rises above the sheet water is built.

The settlement, if that is the word for it, does not begin at a single place. It starts at the water and climbs. Low causeways of pale fused ceramic run out across the plain like the spokes of a broken wheel, and at their junctions stand the first structures: squat, many-chambered pumping halls with intake mouths that gape at the sheet water and pull it inward through grates the height of a man. The water goes into the halls and comes out through vents high on their walls, misted, and the mist drifts back down across the plain as a permanent drizzle that feeds the sinter.

Beyond the halls the structures grow taller. Towers rise in clusters, lattice-spined, their outer skins built of shingled plates that overlap like scales. The plates are not fixed. Over hours a tower can shift its face, opening a gap here and closing one there, and the gaps breathe air in and out with a hollow sigh. Bridges connect the towers at every level, and many of the bridges are not finished. They reach out into empty air and stop, and then days later are found extended a few meters further, or retracted, or rerouted into a loop that wasn't there before. Some of the older bridges have sagged into the water and been built over again, so that the ruins are layered beneath the living structure like the strata of a cliff.

There are no streets in the human sense. There are corridors of standing water between the towers, and along them move barges, floating platforms, and the long segmented haulers that carry sinter and bulk material from the outer quarries inward. Everything here is in motion, and most of the motion is building.

## Sound

The first thing a visitor notices, before anything else, is the noise, and it does not let up. It is a layered roar. At the base is the water: the rushing intakes, the hiss of vents, the constant slap and chuckle of sheet water against piling and plate. Above that is the machinery: pumps knocking on their cycles, shingle plates clacking open and shut, lattice joints ticking as they expand in the heat. Above that, the fauna.

The fauna is abundant to the point of being a weather system. Flocks of pale, long-winged things wheel over the towers in clouds that dim the sun, and their calls are sharp, ratcheting, almost mechanical, a rattling like dry seed pods thrown in a bucket. Beneath the water, larger bodies move through the channels, surfacing with a boom and sinking again, and the sound of them travels for kilometers. Along the causeways, crawling things the size of dogs, armored in overlapping iron-colored plates, scrape at the sinter and grind their mouths together with a noise like gears. Insects in dense swarms hum through the mist. Nothing is quiet, and after a day you stop hearing the noise as noise and begin to hear it as the place's pulse.

## Weather and Change

The air is warm and close, always near saturation, and it rains often but not all the time. Rain here arrives in pulses, each one changing the plain. Water backs up into the channels and the towers flex their skins to shed it. Sheet water rises and some quarters flood, and then the pumping halls work for a day or two and the water drops, and the sinter shows new patterns in the mud. Sometimes a whole district shifts: a stretch of causeway lifts on hydraulic legs and walks a hundred meters sideways over the course of a night, and a line of towers realigns itself to face it, and by morning the map is different.

Nothing here is ever finished. The plain is in a state of constant creation and dissolution, and the constructions seem to respond to each other, to the water, to the fauna, to whatever process it is that runs the place. Some structures are self-repairing: a crack in a shingle plate closes over within an hour, the fused material flowing back into shape like wax. Others unmake themselves on a slow cycle, and their components are carried off by the haulers and fed into new growth elsewhere. The city is, in a sense, a single organism that has no interior and no edge, only an ever-shifting boundary against the sheet water.

## Light and Ecology

The light is ordinary daylight, bright through the haze, and the sky is a pale, even grey-white with the sun a smeared disc behind the cloud. Shadows are soft. At dusk the towers light from within, pale blue-green in their lattice joints, and the flocks come home to them in great spiraling columns that look like smoke rising the wrong way.

The ecology runs on what the place has and lacks. With no plants to produce sugar, the fauna feed on the mineral and the water: the crawlers graze the sinter and the rust, the swimmers filter the channels, the flying things take the insects, and the insects feed on the mist and the film of dissolved material that coats every surface. Dead fauna sink into the sheet water and are taken apart by the pumping halls and returned to the lattice. Death here is not an ending. It is an input.

The people, if there are people, are not visible at first. You see the work of hands in the joints of the towers, in the hatch-doors and handrails and the bright hooks where barges are moored, but the hands themselves move at a distance, in enclosed galleries and sealed hauler-cabs, and they do not come out much into the mist. The place seems built for something to live in it, and the something is probably not only human, and the building has been going on for longer than anyone has kept count.

## The Edge

At the far limit of the plain, where the sheet water thins into a grey mudflat and the mudflat meets the sky, the towers stop. The last structures are not grand. They are low, half-sunk, and they are the oldest. Their surfaces are crusted with sinter a meter thick, their lattices fused solid, their shingle plates fixed in a single shape that never moves. Nothing swims near them. The flocks do not roost on them. Standing among them, the roar of the city drops to a distant surge, and the sound that replaces it is the drip of water from a hundred cracked spouts, slow and steady, like the place remembering how it used to be before it started building.
