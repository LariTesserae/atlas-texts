**The Stillhigh**

You stand on a plateau that was once a mountain range. Its peaks wore down so long ago that they became a tableland, then a shelf, and now a surface so level it looks drawn with a rule. The rock is pale grey-ochre, fine as flour where the wind has worked it, and it rings faintly underfoot like a struck bowl. Nothing here is young. The stone has been broken and reformed so many times that it no longer holds fractures. It has settled into a final form and will not be shaped further.

**Air and light**

The sky is a clear, pale blue that fades to white at the horizon, and the light is ordinary daylight, neither harsh nor dim. The air is cool and thin. Breath comes a little quicker than it should, and distant edges have a faint crispness, as if you could count the grains on a rock three miles off. Dust hangs in the air without falling. It forms slow, glittering veils that catch the sun and never settle, as though the atmosphere has forgotten gravity for these particles.

**Ground and minerals**

The mineral crust is cracked into polygons a few hands wide, each edge rimmed with pale salt-like crystal, though there is no salt and no sea. The crystals are a dry sediment left by water that left so long ago that the memory exists only in the chemistry: gypsum roses, banded chert, iron-stained nodules that break open to reveal concentric rings like the cross-section of a tree. Where the crust has lifted, you find hollows lined with glassy stone, smooth as if polished by a tide that never came back.

**Vegetation**

Plants persist, sparse and very patient. Low grey cushions cling to the cracks, dense as felt and hard as bone, growing perhaps a finger's width in a generation. Tall thin stalks rise out of the crust at wide intervals, each with a crown of narrow, folded leaves that track the sun and fold again at dusk. They are old, some of them clearly older than any animal in sight. Their trunks are dry, fibrous, and ring-marked, and where one has fallen its outline remains in the stone like a fossil, though it is only a few decades down.

**Fauna**

This is the crowded part of the place. Creatures are everywhere, in numbers that feel impossible for so barren a land. They move across the crust in dense, shifting bands: small armored things like beetles the size of a thumbnail, pale six-legged runners that flow over the stone like poured sand, long-necked grazers that step through the stalks in herds that never seem to thin. Above them, dense clouds of winged creatures hang in the air at every height, forming layered strata of motion. The density is so high that it feels like the land itself is breathing.

The animals are nearly silent. They click, rustle, and tap against the rock, but there is no call, no cry, no roar. The herds move together in perfect quiet. Predators, if there are any, pass like shadows.

**Traces of makers**

Human-scale traces are scattered and faint, far apart. Stone circles of uncertain age ring a few shallow hollows, their stones worn down to knee height. A line of upright slabs runs across a rise, and each one has been cut with notches at regular intervals, like a count or a calendar that stopped long ago. Once in a while you find a tool: a hand-sized flake of dark chert with a sharpened edge, or a bone awl polished from long use. Some of these artifacts are placed, aligned to the sun or the stars. Others seem to have been dropped in the middle of a task and never picked up. Nothing is recent enough to show wear from weather.

**Breakdown**

This is the strangest part, and it resists description. The horizon sometimes doubles, with a second, fainter skyline sitting just above or below the first. Shadows fall in directions that don't match the sun's position, or lag behind the objects that cast them by a few seconds. A herd of grazers that has passed out of sight may reappear in the same spot, moving the same way, as though the land has folded back on itself. The same beetle can appear on two different rocks at once. Distances behave badly: a stone a hundred paces away can look close enough to touch, and the next rise can seem to fall away forever. Sometimes the silence deepens into a pressure that feels like the moment before a sound, and then nothing comes.

**Stillness**

Nothing here changes in any way that counts. The sun stands at the same angle through the whole day. The dust hangs in the same veils. The herds move in the same loops, and the stalks fold and unfold at the same hour each day, forever. The erosion stopped long ago, and the plateau is in equilibrium with a sky that no longer has weather. It is a place that has finished becoming.

Walking across it, you have the sense that you have arrived somewhere the world already ended, and that the animals, the stones, and the quiet all know it and have agreed to keep going anyway.
