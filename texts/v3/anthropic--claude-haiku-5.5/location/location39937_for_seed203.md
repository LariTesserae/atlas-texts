**The Sunken Reach**

You stand at the bottom of a basin so wide that its far wall dissolves into haze, and so deep that the sky is a bright coin far overhead. The floor is not ground. It is plant, all the way down. A continuous, living mat covers everything, layered in pale green, rust, and bone-white, and it rises into stalks, shelves, and arches. Nothing under the mat is visible. You walk on it, and it gives a little, like wet felt over something that breathes.

**The light.** It comes from above, down a shaft or rift in the rim, and it is harsh, nearly white, the kind that bleaches color and makes shadows hard-edged. It should be warm, but the air is cold, so the light and the cold argue with each other all day. Frost forms on the upper leaves in the morning, and by noon it has become a field of small mirrors that throw glare back up into the shaft. Dew appears on the undersides of things and then sublimes. You squint constantly.

**The plants.** Most of the mass is a cold-adapted genus that builds itself upward in pale, fibrous columns, hollow and flexible, something like reed crossed with coral. These columns are substrate for everything else. Mosses drape them, lichen-like sheets crust them, and climbing vines braid between them into hanging nets that sag with water. Some columns have swollen bases full of stored moisture. When you press one, it weeps cold sap. In places the columns have collapsed in long, sprawling heaps, and new growth is already sprouting from the rot, so the dead and the living are indistinguishable at a glance.

**The weather.** The basin makes its own climate. Transpiration from the mat rises into the cooling upper air, condenses against the rim, and comes back down as rain that falls only over certain stretches, so you can watch a storm form, drench a slope, and move on across the floor like a slow animal. Mist pools in the low places in the early hours and lifts by midday. Thunderheads can build inside the basin itself, lit from above, and their lightning strikes the tallest columns, which smolder for days. The air smells of sap, wet stone, and something sharp and mineral, like struck flint.

**Erosion and newness.** The land is young and has not yet settled. The rim is raw, with sharp shelves and fresh fractures where it broke away recently. Meltwater channels cut through the mat in clean, angular gullies, and the walls of those gullies show pale, unweathered stone. Boulders lie where they landed, unrounded. A slope that was solid last season may have slumped into a new fan of gravel and torn vegetation. Nothing here has had time to become old.

**Change.** The whole place is restless. Vegetation rafts, large islands of mat torn loose by a storm, drift slowly across the lower pools and collide with the shores. Columns grow visibly over days, and columns also fall with a sound like a distant tree-crack, followed by a rustle as the mat reorganizes around the gap. Fungal blooms appear overnight on the rotting heaps and are gone within a week. Paths you memorized are gone by the next visit. Survival here means keeping up with the reshuffling.

**The built.** People have been here, sparsely and not recently. There are cairns of flat stones stacked to head height, marked with scratches that might be counts or directions. Lean-tos made from lashed columns sag into the mat, half swallowed. A hearth ring of blackened stone sits in a clearing, its ash long since rained into the moss. Scattered flint flakes and a few hand axes lie in the gravel near the gullies, sharp enough to still cut. Someone once carved a series of notches into a standing column, and the new growth has pushed the marks upward, so they now sit well above reach.

**Fauna and sound.** Life moves through the mat but rarely shows itself. Tracks appear in soft patches: three-toed, heavy, leading to a burrow whose entrance is ringed with wet pale soil. You might glimpse something slow and large, a grazer or scavenger with a long neck, that lifts its head, regards you, and sinks back into the columns. Small insects hover in the mist in clouds that are never quite visible. The sound is mostly quiet. You hear dripping, the hiss of condensation running down stalks, the creak of columns settling, and every so often a distant crack and rustle, the place reorganizing itself.

**The rules.** The basin is a closed loop. Water rises from the mat, falls on the mat, and runs back into the mat. Growth feeds on collapse, and collapse makes room for growth. Anything that stays still too long gets overgrown, and anything that moves too quickly tears itself apart. Visitors learn to move at the pace of the weather: slowly, pausing often, watching the sky for the shaft's bright edge clouding over, and never stepping onto a mat that feels too spongy, because beneath it the basin is always ready to drop you into the water it holds.
