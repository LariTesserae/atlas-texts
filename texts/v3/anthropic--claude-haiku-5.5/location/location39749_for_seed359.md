**The Low Terraces**

You come down into a shallow bowl, perhaps a kilometer across, with no river at its bottom and no lake. The ground falls so gently that you notice only when you look back and see how far you have descended. The air is warm and still, and it smells of cut stone, crushed green, and something sweet and resinous, like sap left too long in the sun.

Nothing here is natural. Every surface has been shaped by hands. The bowl is stepped in concentric terraces, each about the height of a seated person, faced with fitted blocks of dark fired clay. The edges are still sharp. Chisel marks show in the faces, and in places the mortar is pale and not yet weathered. The whole place is young. Its gullies look freshly cut, its spoil heaps still hold their angles, and the channels that run down the terrace steps have not yet softened into curves. It looks like something finished a season ago, or perhaps still being finished.

**Water**

Water is the scarcity that organizes everything. There is no stream. The builders have worked around this with a system that is easy to miss at first: long low walls of coarse stone that run along the upper rim and catch the mist that rolls off the ridge at dawn. Beneath them are sunken cisterns, lined with clay, roofed with slabs and covered in soil. Narrow clay pipes carry the condensate down to the terraces. Every few hundred meters you pass a stone trough, and each one holds a thin, still layer of water with a film of pollen on it.

The vegetation is lush in a way that seems to contradict this dryness. Vines climb the terrace walls in thick green sheets. Broad-leaved shrubs spill over the edges, and the fields between the terraces are dense with something like millet, its heads heavy and nodding. Everything is green, growing close together, almost touching. You can't walk a straight line through any of it without pushing stems aside. The soil between the terraces is dark and rich, and you suspect it was carried here, one basket at a time, from somewhere else.

**The living things**

Fauna is abundant. Small birds nest in the wall gaps, and their calls stack up in the morning. Lizards with pale bellies run along the top courses and freeze when you look at them. Insects hang over the troughs in clouds, and at dusk something with long, soft wings moves through the vines in silence. The sound of the place is mostly leaves and the drip of condensate into the troughs, with occasional footsteps and, very far off, the regular knock of a stone being shaped. The quiet is deep and warm, the kind that makes you lower your voice without deciding to.

Some of the animals look slightly wrong. A bird perches on a wall and its shadow lies a little behind it, as if it is still arriving. A lizard stops halfway across a stone and you lose track of which of its legs is which. You can't tell whether these things are rare or whether your eye keeps failing to hold them.

**The weirdness**

This is where the place stops behaving like a place. The terraces do not quite agree on how many steps there are. If you count them going down you get one number, and going up you get another, and the difference is never the same twice. A path you walked this morning may now run to a different wall. The shadows of the clay blocks fall toward the bowl's center regardless of where the sun is. Sometimes the mist sits in the terraces in flat layers, like floors, and you can walk on them for a few steps before they give way.

Doorways open onto the terraces they face, but the terraces beyond them are sometimes one step higher or lower than they were. The builders seem to have accepted this. There are marks on the walls, small carved sequences of notches and dots, that look like tallies, and the tallies do not agree either. Someone has been counting for a long time and has not reached a conclusion.

**Tools and people**

The tools are simple: stone axes, clay mallets, bone chisels, baskets slung from poles. No metal anywhere. The work is slow and everything shows it. A wall is built a course at a time, and new courses are added only when the older ones have set, so a single section may take a generation to finish. You may find a worker resting against an unfinished face, drinking from a gourd and watching the mist come up over the rim. They will nod to you and go back to work, and you will be unsure whether you have seen them before.

**The slowness**

Nothing here moves quickly. The vines lengthen by a finger's width each season. The terraces settle a little each year, and the builders fill the cracks. The condensation walls gather water so slowly that the troughs seem to refill in the time it takes to lose track of a thought. Even the strange shifts in the terrace count seem to happen over long stretches, appearing only after a long absence. The place seems to be going somewhere very gradually, or perhaps being built toward a shape it has not yet reached and may never fully take.

If you stay until evening, the mist rolls in from the rim, the vines close around the lower steps, and the birds go silent one by one. The clay blocks hold the day's warmth, and the troughs give off a faint sweet smell. Somewhere below, one of the terraces is probably a step higher than it was this morning.
