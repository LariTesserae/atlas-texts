The basin is shallow and wide, a saucer of grey silt that lies only a few metres above the sea it once drained into. Its floor is a flat, cracked pan of clay and fine mineral sand, pale where the damp has dried it and almost black where it still holds water. Nothing grows here. The ground is covered in a mineral crust, glassy and dimpled like dried mud, that breaks into plates underfoot and rings faintly when struck. Where the crust has lifted at the edges, it shows fine laminations, layer on layer, laid down so long ago that the bands have hardened into something like slate.

Water is everywhere but never in quantity. The air is warm and wet, and a fine condensation settles on every surface in the early hours, beading on the crust and running into shallow channels that feed a few sluggish, brown-green pools. Rain comes in brief, heavy bursts, perhaps once a week, and then the pan steams for hours. The pools are thick with suspended silt. Their surfaces show slow rings where something beneath is always moving.

The machines are the strangest part of the landscape. They are industrial in kind: pumps, drums, flywheels, long cast-iron pistons, and hoppers the size of houses, all of it heavy and mechanical. But there are no buildings, no roofs, no fences, no walkways. Each machine stands alone or in a loose cluster, bolted to bare footings that have sunk into the silt and cracked the crust in rings around them. Their housings are corroded to a uniform rust-brown. Some have been partly swallowed by the crust, which has crept up their bases and sealed their seams in glassy flows. Their function is not obvious. A few drive belts that run into the ground. Several drums turn slowly, lifting a dark slurry from the pools into troughs and spilling it back. Others simply turn, endlessly.

The sound dominates everything. The machines produce a constant low drone, a rhythmic clanking, and a hiss of steam from valves that open and close on fixed intervals. Heard from a distance, the drone is steady enough to become a kind of silence, but close to a machine it is loud and physical, felt in the chest and teeth. The sound carries across the flat basin for kilometres. Nothing answers it. No birds, no insects in chorus, no wind in anything, only the machinery and the occasional sharp crack of crust settling.

Fauna is rare. The few living things are small and mostly seen at the edges of the pools: pale, many-legged crustaceans that move in slow, wary arcs across the silt, and a scatter of dark beetles that cling to the warm metal of the pumps in the morning and scatter when the drone changes pitch. There are no large animals. There are no tracks but theirs.

Light is ordinary: a pale sun through a thin, steady haze, shadows short and soft at midday and long and cool by late afternoon. The haze never fully clears. It holds the warmth close and gives the basin a damp, muffled quality, as if the whole place were breathing through cloth.

The unusual thing about this landscape is that it has stopped. The machines run on fixed cycles. The drums lift the same load and spill it in the same place. The steam valves open at the same interval, season after season. The condensation forms on the same plates and runs into the same channels. The crust has grown to its present thickness and no further. The beetles return to the same warm pumps each morning. Nothing is being built, nothing is being abandoned, and nothing is being worn away faster than it is being laid down. It is a balanced loop of water, metal and silt, running at full strength and going nowhere, in a basin that has already become what it is going to be.
