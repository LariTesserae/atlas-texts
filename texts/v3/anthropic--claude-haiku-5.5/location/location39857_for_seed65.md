# The Singing Shelf

The land rises in steps to a high plateau where the air is thin and the sky looks nearly black at the zenith. The ground here is pale tuff, laid down in ash beds long ago and cut by water and wind into a forest of fluted pillars. Some stand as tall as a three-storey house. Others have fallen to rubble that has been worn smooth, and many are only knee-high stumps. Nothing up here has been built, and nothing has been placed. Everything you see was shaped by weather over a span of time that no one has measured.

## The Stone

The tuff is cream and grey, banded with rust where iron seeps through. Its surface is pocked with honeycomb weathering, and in the lee of each pillar a fine white dust has piled into drifts that never quite move. The pillars are hollow. Years of rain and freeze-thaw have opened tubes through their cores, some as narrow as a finger, some wide enough to crawl into, and these tubes run up through the stone and open at the top like the mouths of organ pipes. The rock is in equilibrium. Erosion takes a grain here and a flake there, but the shelf has the look of something settled: the broken pillars have rounded edges, the gullies have stopped deepening, and the talus at the base of each column has the same angle everywhere.

## Water

Water is present but never plentiful. Meltwater seeps from fractures in the cliffs and runs in shallow threads across the plateau, pooling in basins of bare rock where it turns the color of weak tea. Each afternoon, moisture climbs out of the gorges on the eastern edge and wraps the pillar forest in drizzle and drifting mist. The rain is light and steady, almost a mist that beads on the stone, and by evening it has gone. Dew condenses on the tuff before dawn, so every surface is slick and dark for an hour or two before the sun dries it to pale again.

Along the eastern edge the plateau falls away into a gorge with a river at its bottom. Its waterfall is the largest sound on the shelf, a continuous roar that rises out of the chasm and fills the air from every side.

## Vegetation

Plant life is sparse, and it grows where it can hold on. Cushion plants form dense, low domes of grey-green leaves, tight as a clenched fist, sheltered in the cracks between stones. Tough tussock grasses grow in the sheltered hollows around the pillars, and dwarf shrubs with silver leaves crouch in the lee of the larger columns where snow lies longest. Lichen covers most of the rock, in rings and rosettes of orange, black, and yellow, and it grows on the shady north faces where the dew lingers. The plateau is not green. It is mostly the color of stone, with the plants showing as spots and seams of muted color.

## Light

The light is hard and white. The air is thin enough that the sun seems closer than it should, and shadows are sharp, black, and short. The pale tuff reflects almost as much light as it receives, so the whole shelf glows, and anyone standing in the open has to squint or wear dark lenses. Shade is cool and deep. Walking from sun to shadow means stepping from glare into near-darkness, and the eyes take a while to adjust. At dusk the light turns amber, and the pillars glow from within like lanterns before going dark.

## Sound

The shelf is loud, and the loudness is constant.

The wind moves across the plateau from the west and enters the pillars, and every hollow column sounds a note. The pillars don't whistle as a single note does. Each one resonates at its own pitch, depending on the length and width of its tubes, and across the plateau hundreds of them sound together in a dense chord that shifts as the wind changes. In a strong wind the chord is overwhelming, a pressure more than a sound, and it does not stop. Some pillars hum low enough to feel in the chest. Others keen high and thin. Taken together they form something close to a cathedral organ played by weather.

Over this come the animals. Herds of horned grazers move across the plateau in long lines, and their bellowing carries for kilometers across the stone. Flocks of broad-winged birds descend into the gorge in clouds, and the sound of their wingbeats and cries rolls up over the rim. Along the cliff faces, colonies of small, stout-billed birds crack shells against the rock all day, a sharp, repeated clatter that never stops.

The waterfall is the deepest sound, and the wind carries it everywhere. Standing near the edge, you can feel it through your boots.

## Fauna

Animal life is abundant, though it is spread thin across a vast space. Grazers appear as specks on the far horizon, and from a distance a herd looks like dust moving slowly over the stone. Groups gather in the lee of the largest pillars, where wind is lighter and grass grows a little thicker. The grazers are heavy-bodied and shaggy-coated, with ridged horns that curl back over their heads, and they move in the same paths year after year, worn into shallow grooves between the pillars.

The birds are the most numerous creatures on the shelf. The cliff colonies run for kilometers along the gorge rim, and every ledge is crowded. Gulls and wading birds work the edges of the meltwater pools. Smaller things live among the cushion plants and in the holes of the honeycomb rock: lizards the color of lichen, beetles that graze on the algae in the dew, and a few species of burrowing rodent that dig through the white dust drifts.

Predators are fewer and harder to see. Long, low shapes sometimes cross the grassland at the edge of the herds, and the grazers watch them closely and then go back to grazing.

## Tools Without Builders

The shelf has no built structures and no made artifacts, but there is tool use here. Along the base of the cliffs, in sheltered spots, you find heaps of smooth, flat stones piled up over generations. Each heap has a hollow at its center where the stones have been worn by repeated blows. The birds of the gorge carry shellfish and cracked eggs up from the river and drop them on these anvil stones until the shells break. The heaps are pale with bird droppings and rubbed bright at the edges, and they grow slowly, a little higher each year.

Nothing on the shelf was made by a hand that planned it. The birds have learned to use the rock, and the rock has been worn by their use, but no mind out here is building a world. The tools are the result of repetition, not design.

## Weirdness

The strangest thing about the shelf is that it does not change. The pillars sing the same chord under the same wind. The mist rises from the same gorges at the same hour, and the drizzle falls for about the same length of time before the stone dries. The grazers follow their grooved paths, and the cliff birds return to their ledges each morning. Seasons come and go, but the shelf seems to be running a long cycle with no beginning or end. Standing in it, you get the sense that the place is holding its breath and that the breath is a sound that never runs out.

At night the pillars do not fall silent. The wind keeps moving, the chord continues, and the waterfall roars on under the stars, which are sharp and close and so many that the sky looks like a bowl of frost. The animals sleep in their lees, and the only sound that changes is the one that the wind makes, and even that stays the same.
