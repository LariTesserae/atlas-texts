Beneath the surface, in a hollow of pale limestone so old that its walls have been polished smooth by seepage, lies a settlement that has not visibly changed in a very long time. The people who live there have no name for it beyond the sound of the water.

The ground falls in terraces, a stepped bowl about the width of a long valley, and each step is floored with sediment packed hard by feet and time. Water comes from everywhere. It beads along every ceiling seam, slides down the walls in sheets, and drips in steady counts from stalactites that have grown into thick, glassy columns. The air is always near saturation. Every surface wears a film of mineral bloom, white at the edges and glossy in the middle, so that nothing is ever fully dry and nothing reflects light cleanly. Cut channels carry the runoff from terrace to terrace through a grid of stone sluices, and each sluice has a gate of slotted slate that someone opens and closes on a schedule older than anyone's memory.

The lowest terrace holds a mere, a flat dark pool maybe a few hundred paces across, its surface so still it doubles the ceiling. Knee-deep puddles fill the margins. Nothing grows in or around it, because nothing grows here at all. There are no roots, no moss, no fungus, no green or brown anywhere, only the white crust, the black of wet stone, the grey of cut blocks, and the faint sheen of the water. The mere holds a few things that move: blind, pale, shrimp-like crawlers that feed on the bloom, and fish with no eyes, drifting in the cold clarity near the bottom. They are rare enough that the people hunt them with ceremony and do not eat them all.

The architecture is dense and layered. Houses are hewn from the cavern's own rock, or built from blocks cut out of its lower galleries and fitted without mortar, each stone seated in a socket that has been recut again and again over generations. Buildings lean against one another for support. Roofs of one level are the floors of the next. Ladders, ramps, and rope bridges knit the terraces together, and the whole arrangement feels less like a city laid out on the ground than like a honeycomb grown out of a cliff face. Arches are everywhere, because arches are what the stone wants to be. There are cisterns cut into the floors, a market on the second terrace where bundles of cord and bowls of rendered fat are traded, and a set of stone pillars at the center of the bowl where the older people sit.

The tools are simple: flint and antler chisels, bone awls, hammerstones, stone axes, cord of sinew and of a fibrous material pulled from some pale animal. There is no wood to burn, and fat is precious, so the lamps burn tar. Seeps of thick black bitumen rise through cracks in the lower galleries, and the people dip reeds into them and light them in clay cups. The flame is orange, smoky, and low. It leaves soot on the ceilings, and that soot is scraped and kept for pigment. The lamps are set in niches at regular intervals along every passage, and the light they make is the main source of illumination in the city, pooling in gold on the wet stone and fading into a dim, uncertain murk everywhere else.

The sound is the first thing a visitor notices, and it never stops. Water is everywhere, and the cavern takes every drip and trickle and amplifies it, folds it back on itself, and rolls it out across the bowl. Sluice gates clack open and shut. Chisels ring against stone in the quarry galleries, a steady percussion that runs through the city like a heartbeat. Voices carry and double and blur. The people have hollowed stones that they strike to mark the hours, and their deep, hollow tones hang in the air for a long time before they dissolve into the constant patter of the water. Nobody here knows silence, and nobody seems to want it.

A few strange things sit in the middle of all this ordinary labor. Certain wall-faces give off a faint pale luminescence, a cold blue-white glow from mineral salts that seems to brighten when the water runs over them and dim when it stops. The people use these faces as reference points to measure time, though they do not measure it in hours but in the cycles of the glow. Water here is never truly still, yet the levels of the mere, the cisterns, and the channels never rise or fall. The inflow and outflow are balanced so precisely that the city's waterline is a fixed mark carved at the same height on every wall, and the people renew the carvings whenever they weather, cutting them again in exactly the same place.

And nothing here is permitted to change. A block that cracks is replaced with an identical block cut from the same seam. A carved sign is recut in the same strokes. A household that dies out is replaced by one that occupies the same rooms, repeats the same rites, and keeps the same schedule of gates and bells. The city is full of people, full of noise and motion, full of labor, but it has the quality of a held breath, a stable equilibrium in which everything moves and nothing alters. The water keeps dripping, the chisels keep ringing, the tar lamps keep burning low, and the faces of the walls keep glowing and dimming in the same rhythm they have always kept.
