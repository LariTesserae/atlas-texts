**The Weeping Shelf**

You stand on a plateau so high that the clouds sit below you like a gray sea, and their tops are lit gold by a sun that seems closer than it should. The air is thin and cold, but the light is fierce. It bounces off every surface, so nothing here is shadowed for long, and the glare makes your eyes water until you can't tell whether the wetness on your face is condensation or tears.

Water is everywhere, and it is the one thing this place does without rest. The rock is never dry. Every fracture bleeds a thin silver film, and the surfaces are sheathed in it, so the stone gleams like wet slate even at noon. Where the film gathers, it runs in braided channels across the flats, shallow enough to step over, cold enough to ache in your ankles. The ground is a mosaic of pale, salt-crusted terraces, each one rimmed by a lip of mineral crust where water has evaporated and left its ledger. The terraces step down in tiers toward a distant drop that you can hear but cannot see past the cloud.

There are no plants. Not a moss, not a lichen, not a stain of green. The palette is mineral and absolute: bone-white salt, black glass-sheened basalt, rust-orange iron seeps, and the pale blue-grey of rock that has been wet for longer than anything has been alive. Where the water pools, the surface holds a faint iridescence like oil on a puddle, and the colors shift as you move your head, though you have not seen anything that could be producing them.

The dominant forms are spires. They crowd the plateau in their hundreds and thousands, columns of dark stone rising from the terraces to heights of ten or twenty meters, slender at the tips and swollen at the base where their roots spread into the crust. They are hollow. Wind moves through them and they sing, a low resonant tone that rises and falls as the air shifts, and because there are so many, the sound never fully stops. It layers into a chord that you feel in your sternum before you consciously hear it. Between the spires, drips fall from overhangs in irregular rhythm, each one striking a pool with a clear, bright note, so the drip-chorus and the spire-chorus interlock and fight for space. The place is loud in the way a crowded hall is loud: not one sound, but a dense weave of many, all of them close.

Rockfall is frequent. Spires shed their outer shells in slabs when the water freezes in their fractures, and the crack and slide echo off the cloud-hidden walls below with a delay that sometimes seems too long. Once, you hear a fall that begins before the rock above you has moved.

That delay is the first of several things that do not agree with one another. The horizon is close in one direction and recedes in another; a spire that looked a hundred meters off turns out to be a few steps away, and when you walk toward it the terrace you were on bends beneath you so that you are walking uphill without climbing. Shadows point in different directions, and sometimes two shadows fall from one spire. Water in the braided channels sometimes flows backward for a few heartbeats, pooling toward its source before reversing. The clouds below occasionally hold still while the light on them keeps moving. None of this is violent. It is simply the place declining, in small ways, to behave like one place.

Among the spires, there is evidence that someone was here. Cairns of flat stones are stacked in precise, leaning towers, their tops worn smooth by rain. Walls of dry-laid stone run partway across some terraces and then stop mid-course, as if the builders had been interrupted or had simply given up on the idea of a wall. Half-buried in salt crust you find flakes of black glass, chipped by hand into blades and scrapers, their edges still sharp, their working surfaces dulled only by time. Some rocks have been struck or scratched with marks that look like tally strokes, or like diagrams of the spires themselves, though the lines run in directions that do not match the spires as they stand now. There are rings of stones that once held fires, but the centers are clean. No ash, no charcoal. The hearths were cold before anything burned in them.

The weather is an active system. Fog surges up from the cloud-sea in pulses every few minutes, swallowing the spires one by one until the whole plateau is a white room of humming stone, then tearing open again to reveal the terraces shining wet and the sun hard overhead. Between surges, the wind drops and the spires fall silent for a breath, and the silence is so sudden it feels like a held hand. Then the next gust finds the first hollow column and the chord rebuilds itself from the edges inward.

You can walk here, slowly, on crusted salt that holds your weight and cracks under your heel. You are never quite dry, never quite warm, and the light never lets you rest. The place is old, settled into the shape it has held for a very long time, and it is also restless, constantly reworking itself in small and large ways. Whatever built the cairns and struck the glass is gone, or has become one of the forms among the spires, or has never been fully here at all. The hearths are cold, the walls are unfinished, and the sound of the spires goes on, as if still waiting for someone to answer it.
