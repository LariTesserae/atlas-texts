# The Stepped Highlands

You come up through cloud. There is no ground at first, only a gray-green luminance that thickens as you climb, and then the air clears in a rush and you are standing on a terrace at the edge of a shelf of land higher than any mountain should be. Warm air moves over it. The sun is close here and hangs in a thin, bright haze, but the light that reaches the floor has passed through a canopy so dense it is less light than a memory of light, a dim amber murk that makes every surface look slightly underwater.

## Terrain and Structure

Nothing here is simply ground. The high plateau has been shaped into terraces, some a few meters wide, some stretching for kilometers, stepping down in long curves toward a lower country you cannot see because cloud fills the gap like milk in a bowl. Each terrace edge is a wall. Each wall is made. Stones are fitted in courses so tight that no mortar shows, and where the joints have loosened, roots have pushed through them and gripped, so the wall and the plant that holds it are now one thing.

The ground between walls is paved with flagstones laid in patterns that are only visible from altitude, spirals and branching lines that run across the whole shelf. Nowhere is there bare soil. Moss has filled the seams and lichen has crusted the stone, and above all of it, grown up through the paving, are the trees.

## Vegetation as Architecture

The trees are the buildings. Their trunks have been trained, over what must be generations, into halls: a single great tree bent into an arch, its branches woven into a roof, its roots cut and guided into stairs that wind up its own flanks. Some are thick as towers and hollow inside, with doorways grown into the bark and windows where the wood has been pruned to let in the dim light. Lashings of fiber are still visible on some limbs, though the bark has long since swallowed most of them. Other structures are woven entirely from vine and cane, lattice-domes sixty meters across that sag and breathe with the wind, their interiors hung with pale fruits that give off a faint phosphorescence after dusk.

There are no bare trunks, no dead wood. Every surface carries something living: fern, orchid, fungus shelves, a felt of epiphytes that holds its own small water. The canopy above is so continuous it reads as a ceiling, and the upper branches of the great trees are themselves platforms where other plants have settled and built gardens of their own.

## Water

Water is everywhere and nowhere in particular. The air is saturated. Each morning the terraces are slick with dew that does not dry until the sun has been up for hours, and each afternoon the cloud comes up from the lowlands and settles over the shelf for hours more. The rock weeps. Seams in the stone bleed steady threads of water that run down the paving into channels cut to carry them, and the channels feed cisterns sunk into the terraces, and the cisterns overflow into rills that run down the walls in glassy sheets.

Some of the water behaves oddly. Mist sometimes drifts *up* a stairway, pulled by currents that do not match the wind on the open air. A cistern on a higher terrace fills when rain falls on the terrace below it. None of this disturbs anyone who lives with it. It is simply how the water moves here.

## Weather and Motion

The shelf has its own weather, a system large enough to be felt as a climate. Fog banks roll across the terraces in long slow waves, each rising and falling over a few hours, and when they thin you can see that the walls and trees have shifted their posture: branches turned toward the light, vines drawn back from the damp. Once a season the great trees flower together, a rolling tide of pale color passing from one terrace to the next across a week, and the air fills with pollen that hangs in the light like dust in a cathedral.

Growth is visible if you watch for it. A root that was in a joint yesterday has pushed a stone a finger's width out of line. A stair has been partly overgrown and partly cleared by something that is not quite a gardener. Things here move at a biological pace, slowly enough that you have to stand still to see it, steadily enough that the landscape is never the same twice.

## Fauna

Animals are abundant, but they are not loud. Insects rise from the mist in clouds at dusk, and they glitter when they cross the light. Birds nest in the architecture, in the hollows of the halls and under the lattice-domes, and their nests are woven into the plant structures so that a bird's home becomes part of the building and the building grows around it. Larger creatures move slowly along the terrace walls, grazing on the lattice and the epiphyte felts. They are heavy-bodied and patient, and they leave the paths clear without seeming to know there are paths.

Predators are rarer and quieter. You see them as shapes in the canopy, not as hunters on the open ground. There is not much open ground.

## Sound

It is quiet here, and the quiet is layered. There is the drip of water from leaves and the steady patter of runoff in channels. There is the creak of living wood as the halls shift in the wind, a long low groan like a ship's timbers. There is the hush of mist moving over stone, and once in a while a bird call that carries across two terraces and fades into the cloud. The big silences are between these sounds, where the air is so thick and the space so wide that sound seems to die before it reaches anything.

## Emptiness

The density here is low, and that is perhaps the strangest thing about the place once you understand it. The built-and-grown terraces are real and continuous, but they are not crowded. Between one hall and the next there may be a kilometer of open air, misty and bright, with nothing in it but cloud and the far-off shapes of other structures appearing and vanishing in the gray. You can walk for days across the shelf and see no one, no animal larger than a bird, nothing but walls and trees and water and the slow pale movement of fog. Presence is sparse. Everything that is present is very present.

## The Breaks

The place is not wholly coherent, and it does not try to be. Some stairs climb and descend the same flight, so that you arrive where you started and feel you have gone somewhere new. Some doorways open onto a different altitude: a hall on the upper shelf might have a door that looks out over a plain of cloud in another season, or onto a terrace that you could have sworn was on the far side of the mountain. Lichen on the walls grows in patterns that are almost text, arranged in lines that repeat with small variations, and nobody here can say whether it is writing or merely growing. The architecture seems to maintain itself: a broken lintel is quietly bridged by a root that was not there before, a collapsed wall is re-laid by the vines that fell with it.

No one built this in the sense of having finished it. It is still being built, by the plants and the water and the slow hands of a process that uses stone and wood and fiber as its tools, and the builders, if there were any, have become part of the walls.

You stand on the highest terrace at the end of the day and the cloud comes up around you. The light goes amber and then gray, the insects begin to rise, a wall somewhere below you creaks, and you realize that you have been standing still for a long time, and that the hall behind you has moved slightly closer, or perhaps you have moved.
