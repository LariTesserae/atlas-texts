You learn this place with your ears first. Your eyes give you nothing, because there is no light here at all, not a glimmer or a stain of dawn. What reaches you is sound, and a great deal of it. Water runs somewhere below in dozens of voices at once, from thin tickling drips to a deep, steady pour. Insects whine in a register that seems to come from inside your teeth. Something large knocks against wood, rhythmically, a long way off. And underneath everything is a machine's hum, so low you feel it in your sternum before you can name it.

The ground is bedrock, but old bedrock, worn into soft rounded shapes by ten thousand years of rain. Its surface is slick and cool, and it weeps. Every stone sweats a thin film of water that beads, gathers, and runs into the channels between plates of rock. The erosion here is finished. Nothing is raw. The valleys have settled into their own curves, the ridges have been smoothed into long humps, and the streams have cut their beds to the depth they will keep. The plateau has been in balance for so long that the balance itself has become a kind of stillness, even as everything moves.

You are high up. The air is thin enough to feel in the lungs, but it is warm and saturated, so thick with moisture that it sits on the skin like a damp cloth. Condensation forms on everything. It drips from the undersides of leaves, from the lips of metal pipes, from the hair on your arms, and from the ceiling of cloud that hangs so low over the upland that it seems you could reach up and wring it out. Rain falls here in an ongoing, unhurried way, the drizzle of a place that never quite dries out. Puddles form, merge, and vanish. The water table rises near the surface and gives the whole country a spongy feel underfoot, as though the land is always half-drowned and half-alive.

The vegetation is lush beyond sense. Ferns as tall as houses lean on each other in arches. Vines knot between trunks in braided ropes thick as a man's arm, and their leaves are broad and fleshy, dripping constantly. Moss covers everything not still moving, so soft and deep you lose your footing in it. The growth has no clear hierarchy. Fungi shelve up the trunks in pale tiers, and some of them are pulsing slightly, breathing in a rhythm you can feel if you press a palm to them. The forest is dense, layered, nearly impossible to push through, and every surface is occupied.

Among the growth stand the machines, and they are the strangest part. They are industrial in scale and purpose: pumps the size of cathedrals, iron towers strung with cable and pipe, long conveyor belts running up the slopes and disappearing into the cloud. Some are rusted into the vines so completely that the plants have become part of the frame, holding up gantries and sealing gaps in boilers. Others are still running. Their pistons rise and fall with a slow, deliberate weight. Gears turn behind grilles. Valves hiss. These engines are what lift the water from the lowlands and push it up onto the plateau, and perhaps this is why the land is so wet, because the machines have been feeding it for so long that the wetness is now a settled condition, not a weather. The built presence is scattered, though, not dense. You find a small hut of corrugated iron every few hundred meters, with a cistern on its roof and a window with no light behind it. You find a gauge bolted to a stump, its needle pinned at a stop, and a rusted sign in a script you cannot read. These are the traces of people who once tended the place, or perhaps still do, somewhere deeper in the dark.

The fauna is abundant and loud. Frogs, or creatures that fill the frog's role, call from the pools in choruses that rise and fall like tides. Things with wings move through the canopy, and when they pass overhead you hear the thrum of them, several at once, then a sudden silence when they drop out of range. Larger animals move through the undergrowth: heavy, deliberate steps, the rustle of something pushing aside a fern wall, a long low call that seems to answer the pumps. Predators and prey are everywhere, and you hear the chase as much as you see it, the scream cut short, the scuttle of something running. Nothing here is still for long. The ecosystem is in full motion, feeding and being fed, with the same urgency as the machines.

This is what makes the place strange: sound is its only true map, and it is a deceptive one. The echo off a cliff tells you the cliff is near, but the same echo might come from a pump three valleys away. The drip that sounds like it is at your shoulder might be fifty meters off. The machines' rhythm can be mistaken for a heartbeat, and the heartbeat for a machine. Some of the sounds repeat in patterns too regular to be natural, and some of the animal calls answer the engines with a precise timing that suggests they have learned them. You cannot tell, here, where the living world ends and the built one begins.

Above it all the cloud sits low and dense, and it seems to breathe with the pumps. It condenses, releases, condenses again. You can feel the pressure change when the big engines cycle. There are weather systems up there, slow moving banks that roll across the plateau in the dark, bringing heavier rain, then thinning, then gathering again. The place has its own climate, and its own rules, and they are not written in any language you know. They are written in the sounds, in the water, in the slow, enormous machinery grinding away beneath the clouds, keeping the wet world wet, and keeping the dark dark.
