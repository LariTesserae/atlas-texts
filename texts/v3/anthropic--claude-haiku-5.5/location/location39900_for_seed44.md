**The Lowest Ward**

You arrive by the levee road, which is the last dry thing for a long way. Below it, the district lies in a shallow bowl, and the bowl is green in a way that doesn't look like countryside. It looks like a building that has stopped being maintained and has been adopted instead.

The ground is water first. Where the streets used to be, a skin of black silt holds a few centimeters of standing water in the low places, and the air above it is thick enough to wet a shirt without rain. Everything drips. Gutters sag with moss. Window frames sweat rust-colored beads. Under the sun, the whole bowl steams faintly in the mornings, and the steam lifts off the vegetation like breath off a sleeping animal.

The plants are not scattered over the district. They are the district. Vines the color of old bottle glass climb every surface and have thickened over the decades into ropes, then into trunks. A fig, or something like one, has taken the substation at the district's center. Its roots went down through the concrete footing and split the transformer housing, and its aerial roots now hang in curtains over the dead copper, drinking condensate. Stacked mats of fern grow in the gutters of four-story offices, and their fronds hang out over the sidewalks like awnings. Where a street has collapsed into a sinkhole, reed-like grasses stand chest-high and rattle. Rooftops have become thin meadows, with shrubs rooted in pooled sediment that has been building up for years. Nothing here is bare. Even the asphalt is only visible in the cracks where it still lies open to the light, and those cracks are full of fine green threads.

Architecture persists in the shape of things. You can read the grid of the old streets by the lines of trees that followed the sewer trenches. You can read the factory floor by the way the trunks stand in rows where the machinery bolts once sat. A line of cooling towers rises at the far edge of the bowl, concrete hyperboloids now furred with creepers, and their shadows fall across the reed beds like sundials. Overhead, a gantry crane runs on a rail along a dead dock. It is still powered. A single yellow light blinks at its top, and every few minutes it creaks forward a meter and stops, forever carrying nothing.

The machinery is the other half of the place. Two pumping stations hold the water back. They are the reason the bowl is wet but not a lake. Their diesel engines are older than anyone who remembers them as new, and they run on a schedule maintained by people who live on the far side of the levee and never walk down into the bowl. The pumps thud and roar in alternating cycles. You can feel them in your sternum before you hear them, a low drumming that comes up through the mud. Their discharge pipes spill into a canal that runs along the edge of the bowl, and the canal is loud in its own right, a hard continuous rush over concrete weirs. Rain on the corrugated roofs adds a metallic hiss. Wind moves through the fig canopy with the sound of surf. Somewhere a freight train crosses a trestle, and every window in the district hums in sympathy for the length of its passing.

Under all this noise, the wildlife is almost absent. A few herons stand motionless in the reed beds, and once in a while a kingfisher cuts across the canal. Insects are there, but in the way of an afterthought, a thin whine at the edge of hearing that the pumps cover completely. No large animals. No nests in the old trees, or none that you can see. The bowl belongs to its plants and its machines, and the two have reached a working arrangement: the pumps keep the water from rising, the plants keep the ground from sliding, and both slowly take the district apart and reassemble it into something that might one day be called a forest.

Nothing here is changing quickly. A root that cracked a wall last decade is still cracking it. A sinkhole widens by a hand's breadth each year. The silt rises a few millimeters with each flood season, and the fig's canopy thickens by one more branch. If you come back in twenty years, the cooling towers will likely be only half visible, the gantry still blinking, the pumps still thudding, and the levee road will be a little more worn at its edges. The place does not end. It keeps its slow, wet, loud, green equilibrium, and waits for someone to decide whether to finally turn the pumps off.
