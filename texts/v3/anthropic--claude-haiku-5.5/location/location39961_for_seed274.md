The hollow lies low in the land, a basin about the width of a longhouse, sunk into a shelf of pale stone. Its rim is a ring of rounded boulders, and the floor is a shallow sheet of water so still it reads as a mirror until something disturbs it. Nothing grows here. There are no stems, no leaves, no roots. What covers the rock is a skin of grey-white mineral crust, fine-grained, pocked like dried porridge, and in places it has built up into low rounded knobs the colour of old bone.

**Water**

Water is the element of this place. It seeps from the seams in the rock wall on every side, so the stone is always darkened and glistening, and a thin film runs down the faces in slow sheets that never quite drip. The air is saturated. Droplets bead on the crust, on the rims of the clay vessels, on the backs of your hands, and the fabric of anything you carry stays cold and clammy. Where the seepage meets the basin, the water is clear but holds a faint milky bloom of suspended lime, so the bottom is visible only as a pale blur. Every surface that is not stone is slick, and footing is treacherous along the margins where the crust gives way to ooze.

The water does not rise or fall. The marks on the rim, faint scored lines cut into the stone at the same height all around, show a level that has held for longer than anyone counted. Whatever feeds the basin and whatever drains it are in perfect balance.

**Mineral forms**

The crust is not uniform. Concentric rings spread from the centre of each knob, as though something once pulsed outward and then stopped. Some knobs have split into columns no thicker than a finger, clustered like reeds, though they are stone through and through and ring faintly if struck. Along the wall, the seepage has laid down long hanging curtains, banded in ochre, rust and cream, and their tips have frozen mid-drip, each bead hardened into a small pale lens. Nothing here is new. The forms are complete, weathered at their edges, and they have not grown in a long time.

Crowding is the defining quality of the place. The floor is packed: knobs jostle against columns, columns lean on curtains, boulders sit wedged against one another with no gap between. There is no clear ground to walk on, only a route picked stone by stone across the crust, and every step meets another presence. The effect is less like a garden than a settled crowd, each piece pressed against its neighbours and holding its place.

**Built remains**

Human or near-human hands have been here, but not often, and not recently in any sense that matters. Three or four dry-stone cairns stand on the higher rim, each about the height of a sitting child, built from flat stones laid without mortar. Some have fallen and been partly rebuilt, and some have collapsed back into heaps that the crust has begun to cement. Among the stones lie shards of coarse, hand-formed clay, thick-walled and grit-tempered, with fingerprints still visible in the fired surface. A few pieces of worked flint or chert lie half-buried in the crust, their edges chipped by blows from a stone held in the hand. On the rock face near the largest cairn, someone has scratched a row of short parallel strokes, then a set of crossing lines, then a circle. The lines are weathered soft, and the seepage has run through them so often that they look more like seams than marks.

The tools are simple: hammerstone, scraper, a wooden paddle long rotted to a dark stain. Nothing suggests metal. Nothing suggests haste.

**Light and sound**

The light is dim and even. It comes from a high slot in the rock to the north, where a band of grey sky shows through, and it falls onto the basin without sharp shadows. The crust gives back a soft pale glow, and the water holds it like pewter. Late in the day it gets no darker, only greyer, and it never brightens into anything you could call sun.

The sound is low. Water makes almost all of it: the slow tick of seepage striking stone, a faint hush where thin sheets slide over the curtains, and occasionally a single drop falling into the basin with a short, round note that dies quickly. The crust does not crack, the stone does not shift, and nothing moves in the air. A few small insects, if any, make no sound that carries. The silence between drops is heavy and complete.

**Fauna and ecology**

Animal life is rare to the point of absence. Occasionally a small pale creature, something like a woodlouse or a blind pale grub, moves slowly across the wet crust and vanishes into a seam. There are no birds, no fish, no visible prey. The ecology is the water, the mineral, and the slow chemistry of the seepage, which works through the stone without anyone to witness it.

**Rules of the place**

Time has stopped here in a particular way. The water holds its level, the forms hold their shapes, the cairns hold their lean, the scratched lines hold their weathering, and nothing is becoming anything else. Change is not absent so much as finished. Anyone who comes here finds it as the last visitor left it, and will leave it as it is.
