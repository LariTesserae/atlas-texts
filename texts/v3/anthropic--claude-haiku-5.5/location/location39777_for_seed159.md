# The Sunken Forge of Ansk Hollow

The land falls away in a wide bowl, roughly the width of a river valley, and the bottom lies well below the surrounding plain. Its rim is a ragged lip of pale, layered stone that looks freshly broken, as if the ground had split only recently and the edges haven't weathered yet. Light comes down into the basin without hindrance. The sky is open and bright, and midday sun reaches almost the whole floor, so the depression feels more like a pit in a sunlit plain than a cave. Nothing here is hidden. Everything is being made in plain view.

## The Floor

The basin floor is not finished. In places it is still soft, a grey-brown slurry that sucks at the feet and steams where the sun heats it. In others it has set into a glassy crust, black and ribbed, with bubbles frozen in mid-rise. Mineral water seeps up through hairline fractures across the whole bottom, and where it meets the air it deposits a skin of white and rust-orange sediment. These skins thicken overnight. By morning a shallow flat that was pools and mud the day before may be a pale terrace with a rim, and by the next dusk the rim has cracked and drained into a new channel.

The ground is always being laid down. Salt crusts form in cupped hollows and split into polygons that curl at the edges like drying paper. Calcite and silica build tiered ledges along the seeps, each step a little taller and more fluted than the last, until they resemble stacked plates or pale, fused leaves. There are no roots anywhere. Where a rooted plant would be, there is only mineral: stalks of banded travertine that rise from vents, grow for a few hours, and collapse under their own weight with a crack that echoes around the bowl.

## Water and Weather

The air is warm and wet, and the humidity is thick enough to bead on skin. Rain comes in brief, violent bursts from clouds that gather over the rim and drop straight into the basin. Each downpour turns the floor into a sheet of running brown water for an hour or so, which carries sediment, dissolves the soft crusts, and then sinks away into fissures or pools in the lowest hollows. Between storms the sun steams everything dry again, and mist rises in columns from a hundred vents.

Pools are everywhere but never stable. Some are hot and clouded green-white with dissolved minerals. Some are cold and clear enough to show the layered walls beneath them. A few are pitch-black with oily sheens and smell of sulfur. The water levels rise and fall through the day, sometimes by an arm's length, as the fractures open and seal. Rivulets that run for a season will vanish overnight, and new ones appear where the crust tore.

## Fauna

This is the most crowded part of the place. The floor is alive with creatures that do not look like they belong to any settled ecology, because nothing here has had time to settle. There are no plants to graze, so the animals eat what the ground gives them: mineral films, bacterial mats (grey, leathery, clinging to the wet undersides of ledges), and each other.

**Crust-grazers** move in low, dense herds across the terraces. They are roughly the size of goats, with flattened, armored bodies and long, scraping mouthparts. They lick the fresh crust off the flats and leave shallow, glossy trails behind them. Where a herd has passed, the surface is scoured clean and wet, and a few hours later it is already beginning to set again.

**Shellbacks** are smaller, many-legged, and carry domed, mineral-hardened shells on their backs, which they build by secreting layers of calcite and gluing grit to them. They travel in loose clouds and swarm onto whatever is newly exposed. When a shelled colony dies, its shells remain, and the floor is littered with them: pale, spiral, and piled into loose drifts that make walking treacherous.

**Mud-diggers** burrow through the soft slurry in long, branching tunnels that collapse and reopen constantly. Their heads break the surface in clusters, nosing about, and they make a wet, sucking chatter as they dig. They are not dangerous, but they make the ground heave and ripple, so the flat looks as if something vast is breathing underneath.

**Vent-hangers** cling to the steam-vents in great hanging masses, like curtains of wet hide, and drop when disturbed. Their bodies seem to draw minerals from the vapor, because their skins are crusted with sparkling salts that flake off when they move. In the early morning they hang in dense sheets, and when the sun hits them they glitter.

Above it all, **wing-spinners** circle the rim and dive into the basin on the updrafts from the vents. They are narrow, translucent, and lightweight, and they feed on the swarms of small insect-like things that rise from the pools in clouds at dusk. The air is never still. There is constant movement, and nearly every surface is occupied.

## Built Traces

Something has been here before, or is here now at a low level. Tools and signs of tool use are scattered across the lower terraces, but they are rare and rough. There are cairns of loose stone piled to waist height and cracked, probably by heat or water. Some stones show chipped edges where they were struck to make a sharp face. A few flat slabs lie in lines across the flats, perhaps marking old crossings, but they have sunk and tilted, half-buried by new crust. There are shallow pits dug into the slurry, lined with hammered rock and filled with pale sediment, as if someone were trying to hold back the water. Most have already been overrun. Old fire-scars blacken the lee sides of some ledges, but the ash is long gone, washed into the mud.

No dwelling survives whole. Whatever built here is absorbed quickly: the ground that holds it is always moving and always burying. The traces suggest a people, or a people's ancestors, who came down into the basin to work the minerals or find shelter, and who learned that anything built on this floor would be silted over within a season. The marks are small, practical, and provisional. Nobody here builds for the long term.

## Sound

The basin is loud. The sounds are layered and constant. Steam hisses from a hundred vents at once. Water chuckles in the channels and rushes when a rain-flood starts. The crust cracks and pops as it cools, and each crack goes off across the bowl like a distant shot. Mud-diggers gurgle and smack. Crust-grazers scrape. Shellbacks click their many legs on loose stone, a dry rattle that rises and falls as clouds of them move. The vent-hangers make a soft, wet slap when they drop. And over everything, the wing-spinners whine on the thermals, a high, thin note that cuts through the rest.

When a stalagmite-like travertine stalk collapses, the sound is like a tree coming down in a forest, followed by a rolling clatter of fragments down the slope and a long, quivering silence before the animals resume.

## Atmosphere and Rules

The air smells of sulfur, wet stone, and something faintly sweet, like crushed mineral dust. The heat is steady, and the humidity sits heavy in the lower bowl, so the air feels thick. Rain can start at any hour, and when it does the whole basin changes within minutes. Light is strong and clear, bright enough that shadows are sharp, but the steam and mist soften distant things, so the far rim often looks like a wall of pale haze.

A few rules govern how the place behaves. Nothing stays where it is put. Anything built, dropped, or left out is covered, dissolved, or carried off within days. Stone that reaches the air hardens fast, and stone that lies in water dissolves slowly. Animals that die here are absorbed into the crust within a week, and their mineral shells become the foundation for the next layer. The surface is never the same twice. Walking across the basin is a matter of reading the ground each morning and trusting that it will be different by evening.

Above the rim, the open plain goes on in every direction, and from the lip you can see the bowl's features spread out below like a map being redrawn in real time: new channels cutting, old terraces collapsing, herds moving in long, slow lines across the pale flats. The whole basin is an unfinished forge where the land is still deciding what it will become.
