# The Sunk Hollow

You come down into it through a gap in a low ridge, where the ground drops in three uneven steps, each one softened by moss. The hollow is small, perhaps forty paces across, and it is not quite a room. It is more like the inside of a cupped hand that has been left in the water for a long time.

The floor is wet all the way through. Water sits just under the surface everywhere, so each footfall gives a little and then a dark sheen rises around the heel. The ground is mostly peat and silt, dense and springy, streaked with pale threads of root and the grey skeletons of plants that died here long ago and never fully rotted. Where the water pools in shallow depressions, it holds a flat, tea-colored stillness that reflects the sky in broken pieces.

Nearly everything here is plant. It is not a forest or a meadow but a single continuous living substrate that has grown over the basin until there is no bare ground left to speak of. Its surface is a dense, spongy mat of low green and rust-colored mosses, fleshy creeping vines, and something like lichen that has thickened into shelves. Through this mat rise the taller presences: thin, fluted stalks with bulbous seedheads, arching fronds that droop under their own weight, and a few squat trunks grown into the shape of hollow towers, their interiors lit a faint amber by the light coming through their thin walls.

The plants are not crowded. Between them there is a great deal of open air and open water, so the hollow feels roomy despite being closed in. A stalk might stand alone for a body-length of clear space before the next one. The emptiness between them holds warm, wet air that moves very little. Everything feels settled, as if the arrangement was decided long ago and has not been revised since.

The air is hot and heavy. Water condenses on every surface: on leaves, on the underside of the fronds, on the smooth flanks of the stalks, so that the whole hollow seems faintly sweating. Droplets gather and fall at irregular intervals, ticking onto broad leaves and rippling the pools. Standing still, you can hear the place breathing its moisture out in small, constant patters.

The light is ordinary daylight, filtered only by the high rim of the hollow and by the thin upper canopy of the taller stalks. It comes down diffuse and even. There are no harsh shadows. Colors look a little softer than they would elsewhere, slightly washed by vapor.

The sound is the strongest thing here. Insects fill the air in layered waves: whirring wing-beats, clicking mandibles, the hum of enormous beetles drifting through the stalks like slow aircraft. Frogs and things that are not frogs call from the water in deep, repeated notes that rise and stop together. Birds with heavy, rattling voices move between the seedheads. Something larger moves under the water and every so often breaks the surface with a sudden splash that echoes off the rim. Fauna is abundant here, and the place is never quiet.

Beneath all of that, there is a second sound that does not belong to the hollow at all. It comes from far above and beyond the ridge: a low, continuous thrumming, the rhythmic knock of pumps, and the long grinding hush of machinery running at scale. It is industrial, distant, and constant. You never see the source. Looking up through the gap in the rim, you see only sky, a few trailing plumes of pale vapor drifting across it, and sometimes a faint orange glow at the horizon that could be a works or a sunset. The machinery is like weather: always there, never approaching.

It is probably the machinery that keeps the water moving. Thin rivulets seep down the rim's inner walls at intervals, carried by channels that run in from somewhere beyond the ridge. They are warmer than the natural seepage and carry a faint mineral smell. Where they enter the hollow, the plants grow thicker and greener, and the water in the pools near them is clouded and slightly oily, with a bloom of fine green film. The plants here have adapted to this flow. Their roots spread along it, and their leaves carry a waxy sheen that sheds the mineral residue.

The hollow has no paths, no markers, no sign that anyone has come here or meant to. Nothing has been built. The only made thing you could say belongs here is the water channel, and even that has been absorbed into the mat so that its edges are indistinct, its banks grown over with the same moss and fluted stalks as everything else.

Time moves slowly in this place. The stalks are the same height they were last season and the one before. The pools stand at the same level. The mat of plants has spread over the same ground for longer than the ridge has been eroding. Birds come and go, insects hatch and die in their thousands, and the droplets fall in the same rhythm, but nothing changes its shape. The hollow is an equilibrium: a closed, wet, humming bowl of life, poised in a state it has held for a long time and is likely to hold for a long time more, while the distant machines keep running, steady and unseen, beyond the rim.
