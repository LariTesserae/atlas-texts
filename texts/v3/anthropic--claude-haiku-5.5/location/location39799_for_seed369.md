The place is a bowl of stone about the size of a village green, set high on a shoulder of old mountain. The rim is ragged and tawny with lichen, and beyond it the land drops away into haze, so the bowl feels like a room with no ceiling. The air is warm, close, and heavy with water. Your skin stays damp without sweating, and everything you touch comes away beaded.

The floor is a tilted shelf of dark, fine-grained rock, scoured smooth in long sheets and then broken into terraces by ages of seepage. Each terrace holds its own small pool, black and still, with a skin of pollen on it. Water comes out of the walls everywhere. It runs down the faces in thin sheets, beads and falls from every overhang, and gathers into rivulets that thread between the terraces. The rock is never dry. Its surface is glossy in some places and furred with green in others, and where the seepage has run for centuries it has carved shallow, fluted channels, like the inside of a shell.

The vegetation is dense and very green, but it grows in clumps and islands, not a continuous canopy. Broad-leafed plants the size of umbrellas crowd the wet margins, their leaves tipped with drip-points that release a steady patter onto the leaves below. Tall reeds, jointed and hollow, stand in the seepage channels in ranks, and when the wind moves through them they knock together with a dry, wooden clatter. Ferns with stalks as thick as a wrist unfurl from the cracks in the rock. Between the islands there is open ground: low moss, pale and springy, and bare stone with a fine sheen. In places, climbing vines have bridged the gaps between the rock ledges, forming sagging curtains that drip constantly.

Nothing is built here, and nothing is made. The stones that might have been shaped by hands are shaped only by water, and the reeds that might have been cut have simply died and rotted back into the moss. Yet the place is not wild in the sense of chaos. Everything has settled into a long balance. The channels run where they have always run, the pools hold their levels, and the vines have leaned the same way for longer than anything in the bowl has been alive.

The light is ordinary daylight, diffused by a high, even overcast. It is not dim, but nothing casts a hard shadow. Colors come through soft and saturated: the greens are deep and almost blue in the shade, and the rock takes on a bronze warmth where the sun catches it through haze. Mist rises from the pools in the mornings and hangs in layers between the terraces, then burns off slowly by midday, leaving the surfaces shining.

The sound here is the dominant feature of the place, and it is loud without being harsh. The seepage makes a constant layered hiss and patter, as well as a deeper murmur where the larger flows drop over lips of rock. Water hitting the pools makes a soft, round tock, a different note from each basin, so that the whole bowl seems to play a slow, irregular percussion. The reeds clatter, the broad leaves drum with each heavy drop, and the wind moving over the rim makes a low, sustained tone that rises and falls over minutes. Animal sound is almost absent. There are a few calls from far off, the sound of something moving through the high rim, and once in a long while a single sharp note from somewhere in the vines. The place is not silent, but it is largely its own water and wind speaking.

Fauna is scarce. A few small creatures live in the moss and under the leaves: slow, pale things that move when the rock is wet, and occasional pale insects that hover above the pools in the mornings. Nothing is abundant enough to disturb the sense of stillness that the water's noise makes possible. The place seems to have room for few inhabitants, and the ones it has are deliberate about where they go.

The strange thing is subtle, and it is in the rock. Where the seepage has run longest, the stone is faintly warm to the touch, and small mineral crusts form slowly on the surfaces into rings and whorls, so that the walls look like they are slowly writing themselves in pale, concentric lines. The crusts grow only a few hairs' width a year. The pools are clear at the bottom, and in them the shadows of the overhangs seem to hold their shape a moment longer than they should. The bowl feels unhurried, as if it has been listening to its own water for a very long time and has nothing to add.
