**The Sinkwater Basin**

You stand on a floor that is not quite ground. It gives under the foot like wet peat, then firms, then gives again, and every step leaves a dark print that slowly fills with seep. Nothing here is dry for long. The whole basin is a low, warm lens of marsh, and the darkness is total. There is no sun, no moon, no glow from anywhere. You know where you are by touch, by the smell of sulfur and iron and rotting sweetness, and by the slow pull of the water toward the middle.

**The ground and the water**

The water sits just under the surface everywhere, so close that the ground seems to be made of it. Pools lie in shallow, mirror-flat plates, and in the stillness they hold a reflection, though nothing above them casts one. Some of those reflections show a sky: a pale, low, unfamiliar ceiling with clusters of points that might be stars and might be eyes. When you lean in, the image shifts a fraction late, as if the water is remembering a sky it saw once.

The land is young and sharp. Fissures open across the flats, some a hand wide, some wide enough to swallow a cart, their edges still raw and angular, not yet softened by weather. Warm vapor rises from them in slow columns that condense on your skin. Between the fissures, plates of dark rock have tilted up like broken slates, their faces glassy and cold to the touch, slick with a film that beads and runs. The newest rock is still faintly warm, and where it cracks it ticks softly as it cools, an intermittent sound that never quite settles into rhythm.

Water drips from everything. It runs down the cut faces of the slates, collects in the hollows of their undersides, and falls in long, unhurried strings from overhangs you cannot see. The dripping is the most constant sound in the basin, and it is so spaced out that you can go minutes without hearing it, then catch three drops in a row and feel your pulse answer them.

**The works**

Scattered across the flats are the remains of something built to drain this place, and it failed. Iron pylons lean out of the marsh at angles that suggest they were once plumb. Their cross-arms hold no lines, only rust-lace and the stubs of insulators. Pipes as wide as a man rise from the water, bend, and dive again, seams weeping oily sheen. Some are still warm. A few still turn.

The machinery moves on a timescale that makes it nearly impossible to watch. A flywheel the size of a house sits half-sunk in a pool, its spokes lifting a finger's width per year, the water around it lapping in a slow, endless tide. Pumps somewhere under the marsh still run at a fraction of their rated throughput, and you feel them more than hear them: a pressure change in the ears, a faint tremor in the standing water, the sense that the basin is breathing very slowly through a machine that remembers how.

Built things are rare, and the fragments are oddly specific. A control cabinet, its doors open, holds gauges whose needles all point at the same dead value. A flight of concrete steps climbs out of the water and stops three treads short of the surface. A cable runs along the ground for a hundred meters, then rises to the height of a head and continues through the air, its end thrumming faintly, where no pole holds it.

**The fauna**

The basin is full of life, and almost all of it is hidden. Without light there is no visible animal, only movement and sign. Things brush past your legs in the shallows, long and slick, and leave a ripple that spreads and reflects off the flat pools. Something with many small feet walks the cut rock in a rhythm that matches the cooling ticks, as though it has learned to time its steps to the stone. Clusters of soft, hanging forms cling to the undersides of the slates and sway when the drips land. When you touch them they pulse once, slowly, warm as skin, and go still.

Everything here is patient. The creatures do not rush, and when they feed they do it over hours, drawing slowly into the marsh. At night, which is always, the basin hums with their collective presence: a density of bodies felt through the water, a sense of being one of many occupants in a crowded room where no one speaks.

**Weirdness and the rules of the place**

The basin does not hold together well. Sound often arrives before its source moves, as if the echo were leading the event. A dripping you hear to your left may be landing behind you, and when you turn, the drip is still falling where you first heard it. The fissures do not always stay where you left them; a crack you crossed an hour ago may have closed, or moved a meter east, and some people report walking a straight line for an hour and returning to a fissure they swear they had already passed.

The machines do not always obey their own physics. A gear can hang in mid-air at the height of a shoulder, its teeth stopped in the middle of a turn, while the shaft beneath it sinks into the marsh. Water pooled on a slate sometimes flows uphill for a few seconds before correcting itself. Reflections in the pools sometimes show you yourself, a half-beat late, standing a little farther off than you are, looking as if you had just decided to step away.

Some areas are simply wrong. Certain stretches of the flats are denser, heavier underfoot, the air thicker in the chest, and the marsh there seems to gather around you like a tide that has been waiting. Stepping out of them takes effort and a few seconds of vertigo.

**Dynamics**

Nothing here changes quickly. The fissures widen by centimeters over seasons. The flywheel turns a few degrees a year. The fauna migrate between pools on tides no one has measured, and the water level rises and falls over decades, with the old pylons gradually going under and new ones, or the same ones, surfacing from the flats. The basin is not dying or growing, only settling, the way a sediment slowly consolidates into a stone that will one day be called a landscape.

It is quiet, mostly. The drip, the tick, the slow pressure of the pumps, the occasional long wet slide of something through the reeds. Beneath all of it, if you stay still long enough, you can feel the place's oldest process: the water finding its way down into the cracks, the cracks finding their way toward the middle, and the middle, patient and warm and dark, waiting to receive it.
