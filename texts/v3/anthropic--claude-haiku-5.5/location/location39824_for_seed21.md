**The Steaming Bowl**

You stand on the lip of a shallow basin, perhaps four hundred meters across, sunk into rock that is still young. The black stone is sharp-edged and unweathered. Its ridges look like they were cracked open last season, and the fractures are still crisp, with no lichen in the cuts. Where the rock has been exposed longest, a thin green skin of moss is only beginning to form.

The air is hot, but not dry. At this altitude the sun should be thin, yet it presses down with the weight of a low latitude, and the heat rises off the stone in shimmering columns. These columns meet cooler air drifting over the rim and condense into a slow, permanent fog that hangs at the height of your shoulders. Everything is faintly beaded. Your sleeves darken. The rock weeps. Water runs in shallow threads down every slope, pooling in the fresh cracks and drying out in the afternoon, so the basin floor is a patchwork of mirror-bright puddles that come and go.

The vegetation is lush in a way that feels almost impolite. Broad, fleshy-leaved plants the color of wet slate grow in clumps among the boulders, their leaves wide enough to shelter a child from rain. Ferns with stems as thick as a wrist unfurl in slow spirals. Vines hang from the spires in curtains, and their tips twitch toward the nearest drip. Yet the plants stand far apart, with bare black ground between them, so the basin never feels crowded. It has the quality of a garden that someone planted and then forgot, leaving the plants to choose their own distances.

Fauna is scarce. Once in a while a small insect with iridescent wings, a blue that shifts toward violet when it turns, crosses the fog and vanishes. Something with many legs moves under a leaf and goes still when you look at it. There is a bird somewhere high on the rim, but you only ever hear its call, a single falling note that comes from several directions at once. Life here seems to be concentrated in the water and the plants. The animals are visitors.

Built things are scattered across the floor, and they are strange. Most are small: a waist-high pillar with a spiral groove cut into its face, a ring of pale stones half-swallowed by moss, a gate of white material with no hinges and no visible seams. The most striking structures are lattices. Hundreds of fine filaments, thinner than hair and shining like spider silk, stretch between the black spires in sagging nets. They are wet with condensation, and the fog collects on them and runs down their length in droplets that fall into shallow stone basins set at the base of each spire. The lattice tends to lean toward the warm vents. Some filaments have been overgrown by vines, and the vines seem to have tightened around them, as if the plant and the machine have reached a truce.

The technology does not quite behave like machinery. The lattices hum at a pitch you feel more than hear, a pressure behind the sternum that rises and falls with the heat of the day. At dawn they shift position by a few degrees, slowly, so slowly you only notice by comparing against a mark you scratched in the stone yesterday. Several of the pale stones have cracks that are filling with a clear, glossy material, as if the structures were repairing themselves. Nothing here seems to have been built to last. It seems to have been built to keep working.

The basin has its own rhythm. Each afternoon the vents surge, the fog thickens into a warm white ceiling, and the lattices shiver with a faint tone. Then, as the sun slants toward the western rim, the fog pulls back toward the vents and the pools fall to half their size. The plants fold their leaves inward against the cooling air. The bird calls once, the insects go quiet, and for a few minutes the basin is almost silent except for the tick of water sliding off stone. Then the next cycle begins, and the water returns, and the basin breathes.

Sound is mostly absent. There is the drip, the hiss of the vents, the occasional fall of a leaf-weighted drop. Your own footsteps on the wet stone sound loud. When the lattices hum, the sound is so low and steady you might mistake it for your pulse. It is a quiet place that is never truly still, a small, wet, strange room at the top of the world, where something is slowly growing back.
