# The Sweating Plateau

You stand on a high tableland, roughly two thousand meters above whatever sea or lowland once mattered. The air is hot, thick, and wet, like the inside of a greenhouse that has been shut for a century. The sun is a pale coin behind a permanent veil of haze, so the light is dim and gold-grey, and it comes from everywhere at once. Shadows are soft and never quite reach black.

**Terrain**

The ground is dark, fractured basalt, scored into shallow terraces by ancient flows and cut again by later human grading. The terraces step down toward a distant rim that you can't see through the haze. Between them run old drainage channels, now lined with a pale mineral crust, and they fill slowly after each night's condensation. Hollows hold standing water the color of weak tea, clear at the edges, with a skin of iridescent film. This is not a marsh in the usual sense. It is a plateau that has learned to hold its own sweat.

**The City**

The settlement sprawls across the plateau in a grid that has long since stopped meaning anything. It was built for extraction and refining: smelter stacks, cooling towers, settling tanks the size of cathedrals, conveyor gantries that cross the streets at head height. Everything is layered. A factory floor has been built over, then roofed over, then refitted with smaller, meaner machinery, and the newest layers are all rust-red sheet metal and cable runs stapled to the older concrete.

Nothing here is crowded. Blocks stand a hundred meters apart, with long empty plazas between them where the pavement has lifted into slow, heaved ridges. Most windows are dark. Some are lit faintly by bioluminescent film growing on the inside of the glass, a cold blue-green that pulses on a slow cycle. You can see maybe five or six active structures from any point, and the rest exist only as silhouettes in the haze.

**The Sweat**

The defining feature is condensation. The night air cools fast on this height, and the day's heat-soaked stone and steel release moisture as the temperature drops. Every surface that faces the sky is beaded and running by dawn. Water sheets down the outer walls of the towers, collects in gutters carved by decades of seepage, and drains into the old cisterns. Pipes that were once industrial feedlines now channel this water through the city in a slow, complicated circulation. Some pipes still run pumps. The pumps cycle on schedules nobody remembers setting, and their rhythm is the closest thing the plateau has to a heartbeat.

The water has built things. Along the drip lines, white and rust-coloured mineral deposits have grown into delicate tiers, like coral or stalactites, and some of them have sealed old valves shut. In places the city has partly reabsorbed itself: a conveyor belt is slowly becoming a ribbed white spine, and a gate has welded itself open under a salt bloom.

**Ecology**

Plant life is almost absent. In its place are mineral films and crusts. Violet and ochre mats coat the north faces of walls and the undersides of gantries, thin as paint and tough as leather, and they gather the moisture and dissolve metal a few microns at a time. Pale fungal sheets spread through the shaded interiors of old pump halls, forming soft grey lace that glows faintly at the edges. Nothing grows upward. Everything grows along surfaces.

Animal life is rare. In the standing pools you may see slow, pale, segmented things, about the size of a hand, that feed on the film and move with a stiff, clicking gait. Once in a while, something winged and enormous turns in a thermal over the rim, riding the updraft from the warm stone. It never lands within sight of the city. It may be the only large creature on the plateau.

**Atmosphere and Light**

The air is heavy enough to feel. Each breath is damp, and fine mist hangs at knee height in the mornings, lifting by midday into a high, grey overcast. The haze diffuses everything. Distant structures seem to float. In the evening the light goes amber and then bruise-coloured before the bioluminescent windows bring up their cold glow. The city is not dark at night, only dim, and the sky never quite clears.

**Sound**

The plateau is mostly quiet. The sounds that do exist are small and steady: water dripping into metal troughs, a thin hiss from the vent stacks, the knock of a distant pump, the wind moving through lattices of pipe and producing low, uneven tones. Occasionally a sheet of condensation releases from a high ledge and crashes onto the pavement with a sound like a dropped sheet of glass. Then the quiet closes back over the city.

**Dynamics**

Nothing here is static, but everything moves slowly. The day runs through a cycle: heat builds, the haze thickens, thermals rise from the stone and carry the great winged shapes up along the rim. By afternoon the vent stacks open, releasing pressure from underground cisterns in long, warm exhalations of fog. At dusk, condensation begins again on every cold face, and the city's water starts its long descent through the channels. The mineral deposits grow a few millimetres a year. Some valves close, some open. The city keeps rearranging itself, one drip at a time.

**Rules of the Place**

- Water is the organizing force. Every structure is judged by how it sheds, holds, or channels it.
- Heat drives the weather, and the weather drives the water, so the city's processes run on a daily clock set by the sun and the stone.
- Nothing grows upward. Life, and the slow chemistry that stands in for it, stays on surfaces.
- The machinery still works, partly, and it keeps running on rhythms its builders set and nobody now maintains.
- The plateau does not forgive stillness. A pipe left unclear for a season will seize with mineral; a gate left open will be sealed in a decade.

If you walked here, you would feel the dampness on your skin before you saw anything, and you would hear water before you found the city. You would see the blue glow of a window before the building that held it. The place would seem abandoned and yet attentive, as though the machines were still listening for a command that stopped coming long ago and kept running anyway.
