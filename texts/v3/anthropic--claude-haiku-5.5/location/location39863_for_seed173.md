# The Lowmere Sump

The basin is a bowl of poured concrete about four hundred meters across, ringed by a wall that rises out of the water like a cliff. Once it was a reservoir for a city that has since moved away. Now the whole floor is under water, and the water is made. Nothing down here was ever soil or bedrock. Every surface, from the paved floor to the rock-look of the banks, was poured, cast, riveted, or bolted by people who are long gone.

**The water**

The light is dim green-gray, like the inside of a bottle. The water is cool, about the temperature of a cellar, and it moves constantly. Four large intake pumps on the north wall still run, drawing water through a grate and pushing it out through a spillway on the south side. The current is slow, but it is always there, and it carries silt in long braided plumes that catch the light from above. The pumps keep the water level steady. If they stopped, the basin would slowly rise and drown the towers that now break the surface, but they have not stopped in the decades since the last crew left. Nobody knows what turns them, only that they turn.

**The structures**

The tallest things are the old pump housings, squat cylinders of riveted iron that stand about twelve meters tall and show above the surface as black islands crowded with gulls. Lower down, the submerged structures are more intricate. A grid of gantries and catwalks runs across the basin at different depths, forming a lattice that divides the water into layers. Thick iron pipes arc between towers like the ribs of a sunken ship. Where the pipes have rusted through, the water pours in and out with hollow, booming sounds.

Erosion has worked on all of it for a very long time. Concrete surfaces have worn into smooth, rounded forms, like sea-stones or the lee side of a dune. Iron has bloomed into lace, with paper-thin flanges and bulbs of rust that look like coral. Nothing is sharp anymore. The buildings have settled into shapes that look natural, the way a tidewater cliff looks natural, even though every line in them was drawn by someone. The tile floor of the old settling tanks has been scoured into ripples, and in some places these have grown fine green algae that move in the current like hair.

**Life**

The basin is full of fauna, and it is loud with it. Eels coil in the iron pipes. Pale crustaceans, roughly the size of a hand, cling to every vertical surface and sweep the concrete with their fans. Shoals of small silver fish move through the gantries in tight clouds, turning all at once when a larger shape passes. Those larger shapes are pike-like hunters that wait in the lee of the intake grates, where the current brings food to them. A slower, heavier animal, something like a catfish with a mouth full of ground teeth, feeds on the crustacean colonies and leaves pale scars across the concrete.

Above the surface the gulls and herons come and go in noisy shifts. They nest on the pump housings and dive for the fish that the current pushes toward the spillway. Nothing grows on land here. The banks are bare concrete, and only a thin rind of lichen grows on the wall's upper edge. Life is almost entirely aquatic and machine-adjacent. Every ecosystem depends on the pumps' flow, the grates, and the pipes.

**Sound**

The loudest sounds come from the machinery. The pumps make a deep, steady thrumming that you feel in your chest before you hear it. Over that, the intake grates chatter as debris hits them. Water rushes through broken pipes and gurgles in long, hollow bursts. Every so often a valve closes somewhere in the lattice with a bang that echoes around the walls for several seconds, and the fish scatter. Layered over the mechanical noise are the animal sounds: gull cries, the clicking of crustaceans, and the low knocking of catfish feeding on the concrete.

**Rules of the place**

The basin works as a system, and each part depends on the others. The pumps keep the water moving, the moving water feeds the animals, and the animals keep the algae in check so the grates do not clog. If one of these loops breaks, the rest slowly follow. A clogged intake grate can starve the pike and the gulls, and a surge of silt can smother the crustacean colonies and collapse the lattice. The place has been running like this for a very long time, and it has settled into a balance that is neither natural nor fixed. It is just what a sealed, made, flooded machine does when nobody is around to stop it.
