# The Drowned Terraces

The town sits at the bottom of a bowl of air so full of water that the sky has stopped being a sky. Above the rim of the bowl there is light, pale and diffuse, but down here the fog is a medium more than an atmosphere. It is thick enough to feel on the skin as pressure, not moisture. Things are seen at a distance of perhaps forty paces, and beyond that the world dissolves into layers of grey-green, each a little paler than the last, until the eye gives up. You do not walk through this air so much as wade through it.

The elevation is high, the air thin and cold, and the cool keeps the water suspended rather than falling. When it does fall, it falls as a wall. Rain comes in slow, roaring sheets that move up the valley like tide, and the whole hamlet goes dark and loud for an hour, then leaves behind a lacquer of runoff on every surface. Then the mist returns and the water hangs again, waiting.

## The ground

The land was once a lake bed. It is now a series of terraces stepping down the valley wall, each one a shelf of old black stone that was laid by hand, maybe several hundred years ago, and has since been slowly reclaimed. The stone is dense, fine-grained, and wears smooth where feet have walked it. Between the blocks, roots have worked in and cracked the mortar, and where the mortar has gone, moss has taken over. The terraces are not flat. They have settled and tilted, and the water runs across them in shallow sheets that never fully drain, so the lowest step of every terrace is a long, tea-coloured puddle with a bottom of green silt.

Erosion here is an old story, mostly finished. The valley walls are rounded, the gullies are broad and graded, the scree at the base of the cliffs has become soil and then forest. Nothing is sharp. Even the cut-faces of rock are softened by a skin of lichen so that they look like upholstery.

## The plants

Vegetation is the material of the place. There is almost no bare surface. Everything is covered: stone in moss, moss in liverwort, liverwort in fine grey lichen that hangs from the eaves of the houses in long beards. The trees are tall, slender, and pale-barked, and they grow in loose stands with their crowns lost in the fog. Their trunks are wrapped in epiphytes, ferns and orchids and a hundred kinds of creeping vine, so that each trunk is a column of living green from the roots to where it disappears into grey.

The architecture is grown as much as built. Houses rest on platforms that were laid across the crooks of trees, and the trees have grown around the supports until the joints are sealed in living wood. Bridges between the terraces are a mixture of rope, plank, and root: some are woven from aerial roots that were trained across a gap and thickened over decades; others are planks lashed to trunks that have since swollen and locked the lashings in place. Walking on them is slightly giving, as if the floor breathes.

Gardens are not planted so much as tended. Sweet tubers and a dark leafy green that tastes of iron grow in terraced beds where the water is shallowest. Between the beds the moss is cut back in bands, and the cut moss is laid as mulch. Nothing in the town is cleared; everything is negotiated.

## The animals

The fauna is abundant and close. Frogs are everywhere, in every puddle and every cupped leaf, and at dusk the valley fills with their calls: a deep rolling chorus from the lower terraces, sharp clicks from the trees, and a thin, high whistle from something in the moss that repeats until it becomes part of the silence. Salamanders, pale and blue-spotted, move slowly across the stone, and they are the only creatures that seem unbothered by the people. Birds are present but seldom seen; you hear them as sudden clatters through the fog, wings beating on water, a call that comes from six directions at once.

Insects hang in the mist, drifting in thick clouds over the still water. Long-legged water striders move across the puddles in a continuous geometry of dimples. At night a pale, cold-looking light rises from the moss in some places, not bright, just a faint green pulse like breath.

There are larger animals too, though fewer, and they keep to the upper slopes where the trees thin: something heavy that snorts and crashes at night, and once in a while, a slow long-necked shape moving through the mist below the rim, visible for only a moment.

## The sound

The sound of this place is never quiet. Water is always moving somewhere: dripping from leaves, trickling down stone channels that the people have cut to carry runoff from one terrace to the next, rushing in the gullies, slapping against the stilts of houses. The frogs and birds fill the rest. Even the fog carries sound strangely, so that a voice from two houses away can seem close, and a dropped stone can seem to land under your feet when it is actually across the valley.

People here speak loudly, partly to be heard over the water and partly from habit. Work-songs rise from the gardens. Bells, made from hollow timber and tuned to the pitch of the valley, hang from the bridges and ring when the wind moves through them, which it does at irregular intervals, usually near dawn.

## The people

The settlement is small, a few dozen houses spread across perhaps three hundred paces of vertical terrace. Each house sits alone on its platform, with a long stretch of moss and water between it and its nearest neighbour. Density is low; the place is built to hold space open between presences. Houses are linked by bridges and channels, but nobody lives close enough to touch their neighbours' walls. Privacy is a function of distance, and distance is measured in the time it takes to cross a bridge when it is slick with rain.

They are craftsmen of the simplest kind. They make tools from stone and bone and hardwood. They cut channels with adzes, weave with fibre, lash with root and gut. There is no metal, and no sign that there was ever a need for it. Their knowledge is of water, growth, and weight: how a root will lock around a beam over ten years, how much stone a terrace will hold before it slumps, when the valley will flood and when it will drain. They think in cycles of decades. The rains come, the trees grow, the terraces shift, and the town moves with them by inches.

Life is slow here. Change happens across seasons and generations, and the people treat it as a conversation with the land rather than a project. A house that has grown too crooked is left to lean; a channel that has silted is dug out once a year and its spoil laid as mulch. Nothing is rebuilt all at once. The town is always in the middle of being repaired, and has been for longer than anyone remembers.

## The strange part

There is something slightly off about the place, though it is hard to name. The fog sometimes holds shapes: a pale vertical column that seems to rise from the lowest pool and hang in the air like a column of smoke, though there is no fire. The frogs go silent in a patch of the valley, and the silence moves, slowly, the way a tide does. The trees along the eastern wall grow in a curve that does not match the wind. People here do not discuss this much. They step around certain spots and leave certain channels unfinished, as if the water has its own opinions about where it should go.

It is a place where the land is slowly winning. The people are not defeated by that. They are patient with it, the way one is patient with an old animal that has decided to share a room. The terraces are going back to lake. The town is becoming part of the valley's own slow, wet, green argument with itself, and it will go on arguing long after the last stone has sunk into the moss.
