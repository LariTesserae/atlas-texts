**The Shelf Under the Throat**

You come up through a crack in a glass plateau at an altitude where the air is thin and cold enough to sting the teeth. The crack is barely a metre wide. It opens into a pocket, a cavity perhaps two hundred metres across, roofed by a lip of black volcanic glass and floored, far below, by a city.

The light is dim and brownish, the color of weak tea held up to a lamp. It comes through the roof in shafts where the glass is thin enough to pass it, and it reaches the floor already diffused by a haze of fine ash that never fully settles. Nothing here is lit by a sun. The city lights itself: sodium-orange lamps hung on cables, blue arc-furnaces that flicker when their feeds surge, and the cold red pinpricks of warning beacons on the stacks.

The ground is glass, but not the smooth kind. The plateau cracked and sheared as it cooled, and everything is still sharp. Edges catch the light like shards in a broken window. Ridges of obsidian rise in ranks, black and striated, some already eroded to soft curves by dust-blasting winds, others as fresh as a knife's edge. The sharpness is young. The whole pocket looks like it was split open last season.

Nothing grows here in any familiar way. Along the cracks and in the sheltered hollows between buildings, there are crusts of pale grey-green film, a mat-forming fungus or something like it, thin as lichen, which thickens into bristled tufts where the warmth from the furnaces leaks out. It feeds on the mineral dust and on the sweat of the machines. It smells faintly of struck flint. Where it grows thickest, the lower walls of the factories are furred with it, and its tufts are cropped and dragged away by something.

The fauna is abundant, and this is the first thing that presses on you. Small segmented creatures the colour of wet slate move in dense streams along every ledge and gantry. Each is about the size of a hand, shelled in overlapping plates that clatter faintly when they cross glass. They have no visible eyes, only ridged sensory fans that sweep back and forth as they walk. They work in colonies, hauling grit and fragments of spent alloy into the lower galleries, where they seem to be building something. Larger grazers the height of a man, with long jointed legs and kiln-blackened carapaces, browse the fungal mats along the walls, and their dragging feet leave scored tracks across the terraces. Above, on the cable runs, flying things like dry leaves flicker in and out of the lamp light, each one bearing a faint blue spark at its tip. They are the only things here that seem to want to be seen.

The city itself is dense, layered, and always changing. Industrial architecture rises in tiers from the floor to the roof, built from salvaged plate, cast black ceramic, and the sheared glass itself. The lowest tier is a bustling foundry district: rows of squat furnaces venting grey plumes that rise in slow columns and spread across the ceiling. Above it stand the production towers, tall stacks wrapped in cable and pipework, and at the top of each, a rotating array of mirrors that funnel what little light reaches the floor into the workshops below.

What unsettles you is that the city is not finished, and does not seem to want to be. A gantry that stood yesterday has been pulled down and its girders are being carried off by a line of grazers. A new scaffold, still hot and glowing at the joints, has unfolded from a wall where a kiln used to be. Pipes sprout from one building and bridge across to another, then go slack and drop, leaving their couplings to rust. Bridges form in the lamplight and collapse into rubble. Half the towers have walls of glass where walls ought to be, and the other half are shells of cable with nothing hung between them. The city changes the way a colony of ants changes: no single movement is planned, but the whole shifts constantly, so that the map you made of it an hour ago is already wrong.

The sounds are subdued. The pocket absorbs noise like wool. Under the furnace hum and the clatter of the crawlers there is a deep, unbroken quiet that makes your own footsteps seem too loud. No wind reaches the floor. The only sounds that carry are the sharp snap of a cooling joint, a distant machine grinding its teeth, and the soft crackle of the blue sparks where they land. Now and then the whole pocket breathes out a long sigh of released heat from a vent, and every creature on the terraces stops for a moment and then continues.

Standing on the lip, you feel the cold coming up through your boots and the faint tremor of the place under your feet, the pulse of furnaces and the slow grind of thousands of small hands. It is a closed world, a single held breath under a glass roof, and it is still being built, unbuilt, and built again, with no end in sight.
