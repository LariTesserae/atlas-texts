The basin lies below the level of everything around it. You reach it by descending a long, slumping shoulder of pale sediment, and the ground gets wetter and softer with every step until the last firm footing is a shelf of cracked mudstone that still shows the ripple marks of water that left it. Below that the land has not finished deciding what it is.

The surface is a fresh geology. Layers of silt, clay, and fine sand lie in bands the colour of weak tea, ochre, and bone, and where the water has recently pulled back they are still curling at the edges like drying paper. Rock is coming into being here. Thin sheets of sandstone are forming in the pools. Some blocks have already set into concretions, round and hollow, their centres still damp and grey. Along the seams, mineral water seeps from the rock face and leaves white crusts of salt and calcite that grow a few millimetres a season. Everything is in construction, and the ground moves under your weight.

Water is everywhere but it is not in a single body. It stands in shallow sheets over the mud, gathers in long brackish channels, and rises through cracks in the pavement-like plates until the whole basin looks like a surface of beaten metal, reflecting the dim sky in broken pieces. The air is saturated. Droplets hang in it and condense on your skin, on the rusted surfaces of the old structures, on the wings of the insects. Nothing dries completely. Even the stones feel cool and sweating.

Vegetation is sparse, as if the place has barely begun to accept it. A few species have claimed the high edges: stiff grey reeds that grow in clumps like rough brushes, a low succulent with fleshy, salt-beaded leaves, and sedge that stands in thin lines along the channel banks. Where the mud is firmest there are bare stumps of something that once grew tall and has since been drowned and killed by brine. Between the clumps, the ground is raw.

The light is weak and seems to come from everywhere at once. The sky is a single pale ceiling of cloud, thick enough to hold the warmth in, and the sun, when it shows, is a smudged disk that throws no real shadows. Distances flatten. You can see a long way across the basin, but the far rim dissolves into haze, and the nearest details come forward sharply, as though the eye has been given too few clues to judge scale.

Built things are scattered, and they are old. On a low island of hardened sediment stands a pumping station: a brick housing with its roof half gone, a pipe as wide as a man rising from the muck and bending over the water, its flange crusted orange. Further out, the skeletons of gantry frames stand in lines that suggest a grid laid down and then abandoned. Some of the iron has been eaten away into lace. A conveyor belt runs from the shore to nowhere, its rollers seized, the rubber long gone. A few sheds still hold sealed drums, and a rail spur has been half-buried so that only the tops of its rails show, bright with rust and a little salt.

The machinery is not all dead. Somewhere out on the basin an engine turns. The pump works on a slow cycle, one stroke every few breaths, and you feel it through the ground before you hear it: a deep, uneven thud, then a long rushing sound as the water is hauled up and thrown across a channel. Other pumps may run in the distance, out of sight. Their rhythm is not synchronised, so the sound comes in layers, and the beat of one overlaps the pause of another. Nobody seems to be tending them. They were built to drain this place and they still try, though the sea keeps coming back and the basin keeps refilling.

The sound is the first thing that overwhelms you. It is not one sound but thousands, and it fills the whole space with almost no gap. Frogs call from every pool in deep, rattling, mechanical chorus. Cicadas or something like them saw through the reeds in long electric pulses. Birds shriek from the grey stumps and their calls ricochet off the concrete and metal until they are nearly unrecognisable. Water runs in the channels, drips from the gantries, slaps against the mud, and bubbles up through the cracks. Under all of it the pumps keep time. Standing still is difficult, because the noise pushes against the body, and after a while you stop hearing individual things and simply feel the whole basin pulsing.

It is full of animals. The shallows are thick with tadpoles, insects skate in the film of the surface, and mud-dwelling crustaceans push up little spires and burrows across every exposed patch. The air above the water is packed with midges and larger flying things, their bodies shimmering in the dim light. Lizards bask on the warm stones and scatter into the reeds at your approach. Waterbirds stand in rows along the banks, stiff and watchful, and do not move until you are close. None of the creatures is strange in itself. Their numbers are the strange part. Every surface has something on it, every gap has something in it, and nothing here is empty.

The basin changes very slowly. The water rises and falls over seasons, not days. Salt crusts thicken across a year and crack in the heat. Reeds creep a few centimetres outward from the banks, and the sediment shifts so gradually that you would need to return after a decade to see the difference. The pumps have run long enough to cut their own channels, and the sea has been returning long enough to fill them. Everything here is in a patient process of becoming: slow, wet, loud, and crowded, a land still deciding whether it will rise out of the water or be taken back by it.
