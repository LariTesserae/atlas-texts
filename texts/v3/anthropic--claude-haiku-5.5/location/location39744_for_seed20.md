# The Drowned Mesa of Vell Ossary

You stand on a plateau that was lifted out of the ground only recently. Its edges are still sharp. The rock breaks in clean planes, as if it had been split yesterday, and the fractures have not yet been softened by rain or time. The whole table of stone is perhaps four hundred paces across, a shelf hung high above a country you cannot see, because the world below is gone under a sea.

The sea is not beneath you in the ordinary sense. It is *around* you and *through* you, a submersion that stops short of drowning. The air is thick with water held in suspension, so every breath is wet and warm, like breathing inside a bath that someone has forgotten to drain. Mist hangs in layers, each at a different depth, and the layers slide past one another. Above, the sky is a low ceiling of bruised slate. Light comes down from it dimly, a permanent late dusk that never quite becomes night. Everything is lit from no particular direction, so shadows are soft, pooled, and sometimes seem to rise instead of fall.

**The Water**

Water is the governing material here. It stands in the hollows of the rock as pools, and the pools are not still. They tremble constantly, pulsing with a slow circulation that comes up through the stone itself, as if the plateau were breathing. Where the rock is cracked, water wells upward in thin columns that break into fans of spray and fall back. Where the rock is smooth, sheets of water sheet across it, perpetually moving in one direction and then, without warning, reversing.

There is also water that falls. Rain does not come as drops but as a sustained wall that descends in slabs, so that you can see a curtain of it approaching across the plateau like a moving cliff. When it reaches you, the world goes white and roaring for a count of perhaps forty heartbeats, and then it simply withdraws, leaving the stone glistening and the air still full of mist.

**The Stone**

The plateau is pale grey, veined with black and rust-coloured seams where mineral water has stained the fractures. The surfaces are raw. Some blocks are still settling; you can see them shift by a finger's width when the water pulses beneath them, and occasionally a slab breaks off with a crack and tumbles into the sea below, which you never see land.

The edges of the table have no clear margin. The rock simply ends in a ragged lip, and past it there is only mist and the far-off sound of more water. You do not know whether there is ground under the mist, or whether the plateau floats.

**Vegetation and Fauna**

Plants are few and strange. Thin grey filaments cling to the wet stone in patches, moving gently in currents of water as if they were underwater even in air. Where a pool is deepest, a pale flat growth spreads across the surface like lichen on a lake, keeping its shape even as the water beneath it boils. There are no trees and no grasses. Life here is sparse and tenacious, and it seems to have adapted to the constant immersion by forgetting that it was ever meant to stand upright.

Animals are rare, and you will only glimpse them. Something moves in a pool, leaving rings that spread too fast. A bird-shaped silhouette passes through the mist with wings that never seem to beat. Once you may catch a sound of something large turning over far below the lip of the plateau, and then nothing.

**Built Remains**

Scattered across the stone are the traces of builders who are gone. These are small and partial. A staircase of seven steps climbs out of a pool and ends at nothing, its top step still dry while the rest are submerged to the ankle. A doorway stands alone on a rise, its frame carved with spirals worn almost smooth, and when you walk around it you find that the wall it once belonged to is not there. Nearby, a basin of carved stone is filled with water that tastes faintly of salt and iron, and its rim is worn into a groove where hands have rubbed it for a very long time.

None of these structures are complete. They read like sentences cut off mid-word. There are no tools, no devices, no mechanisms. Whoever made them worked with stone and water alone, and they seem to have understood water as a kind of architecture.

**Sound**

The sound of this place is overwhelming. It is not one noise but a dense weave of many: the roar of falling water, the hiss of mist, the heavy knocking of stone shifting against stone, the bubbling of springs bursting from the rock, and beneath it all a low, continuous drone that you feel in your sternum more than hear. Every sound is amplified by the wet air and the stone walls of the basins, so that even a dripping pool becomes a clanging bell. Silence is never available. Even when the rain withdraws, the plateau keeps up a relentless percussion of settling and flowing, and it is hard to tell where one sound ends and another begins.

This noise has a strange property. Sounds seem to arrive a little before their sources. You hear a slab collapse, then see the dust of it rise from the far side of the table, as if the plateau were slightly out of step with itself.

**Reality Breakdown**

Here the rules of the world begin to fail, and the failures are consistent enough to be called laws.

The pools reflect the wrong sky. Look into one and you see not the low ceiling above you but a clear blue expanse, and in it the outline of a much larger plateau, many times the size of this one, which is not visible from where you stand. Step to the edge of a pool and the reflection moves a half-second ahead of you.

Stairs lead nowhere but sometimes lead elsewhere. If you climb the seven steps out of the pool, you may find yourself at the top, or you may find yourself at the bottom, dripping, with the staircase now descending. Doorways open onto the same mist but at different depths.

Stone sometimes softens, briefly, under pressure. A hand pressed against a wall may sink into it as if into wet clay, then harden again.

Gravity is uncertain near the lip. Things dropped over the edge do not always fall. Some drift upward into the mist and vanish. The far country beyond the table is never visible for long, and when it is, it appears inverted, with its own plateaus hanging downward from a grey sky.

**Dynamism**

Nothing here stays still for long. The plateau is in the middle of becoming. Springs open and close within minutes. Pools deepen and drain. Blocks of stone shift and rearrange, so that the path you walked a moment ago is now a crevice filled with white water. Fresh cracks run across the surface with a sound like tearing cloth, and steam rises from them. The water itself is never settled, circulating in great slow gyres and then breaking into chaotic turbulence where two currents meet.

Creation and destruction are happening together. A new shelf of rock lifts from the sea while an old one slides away. The built remains are being reclaimed as the stone around them shifts, so the staircase you saw yesterday may be half-buried tomorrow, or may have grown a new step overnight.

**Atmosphere**

The overall feeling is of standing at the edge of a world that has only just finished forming and has not yet decided what it will be. There is awe in it, and dread, and a strange tenderness toward the water and stone, which are so clearly in motion, so clearly alive in their own indifferent way. The sea does not care that you are here. The plateau does not remember whatever built the steps. Everything is in the process of forgetting and remembering at once.

If you stay long enough, the roar begins to feel like speech. You cannot understand it, but you feel that it is addressing something, perhaps the stone itself, perhaps the sky that has not yet decided whether to open, perhaps you.
