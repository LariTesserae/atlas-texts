# The Hollow Under the Mat

You come down into it by a root stair. The light goes first, then the sky, and the air changes. It thickens and cools, and it carries the smell of wet bark, cold stone, and something sweet and fermenting, like fruit left too long under leaves.

This is a pocket. It sits in a shallow basin, perhaps a few hundred paces across, roofed by a canopy of pale, twisted stalks that lean together overhead and never quite close. Little light gets through: a grey dimness at midday, and at night nothing at all except the faint blue-green glow of fungal threads woven into the walls, which gives just enough to tell wall from open air.

## The Ground Is Alive

The floor is not soil. It is a mat, a single continuous body of moss, liverwort, and fine rootlace, thick as a mattress and springy underfoot. It rises and falls in slow swells. Where you press your boot, it sighs and seeps water. There is no bedrock visible anywhere. The mat simply goes down, saturated, into black peat and then into the cold seep beneath, which runs in threads through the whole basin.

Water is everywhere, and it is always moving slowly. Condensation gathers on the underside of the canopy and drips in steady, uneven rhythms that drum on the mat and on the roofs of the town. Shallow pools collect in the hollows of the mat, black and still, with a skin of pale algae. Thin rills run between the clumps and cut channels through the peat. Everything is faintly cold. Your breath hangs in the air.

## The Town Grows Into It

The settlement sits on the mat rather than on the ground. Its houses are low and partly sunken, built of flat grey stones stacked without mortar, roofed with bark sheets and rafters of split, bleached wood. Mat has grown over the foundations, down the walls, and across the roofs. In places the buildings are almost invisible under green. Doorways are curtained with hanging moss and open through thick sphagnum-like padding that was cut back to make a path and has since grown back.

The architecture is practical. The builders learned early that the mat will swallow anything that sits still for too long, so they build with it. Walkways are laid as lattices of woven stalk on raised frames, and they sag and break and are rewoven every season. Raised wooden platforms hold stores of dried fungus, rendered fat, and bundled bark. Drainage channels are dug and reopened constantly, because the water always returns.

There are few people. The town is sparse and inward-looking, its houses spaced wide apart with dark wet gaps between them, the spaces that the mat and its fauna claim. A few lamps burn in the windows, made from fat-soaked wicks in clay pots. They throw warm, unsteady light on the nearest curtains of moss and leave the rest of the basin black.

## Tools and Making

The tools are simple and carefully kept. Knives are split flint or fired clay, lashed to bone handles. Needles are bone or thorn. Ropes are plaited from the tough rootlace. Pots are coiled clay, fired in shallow pits. Metal is not seen here, though some people wear small bright bits of it, traded or found, as ornament or charm. The town works with what grows and what lies in the stone.

The most skilled craft is the handling of the mat itself. Crafters cut blocks of living moss and set them in patterns to make insulation, mats, and walls that keep the cold out. They know which cuts will heal and which will rot, and they rotate the cutting grounds in a slow cycle, so the basin is always partly harvested and partly regrowing.

## What Lives Here

Here the fauna is overwhelming, and nearly everything that moves is small. The mat is never still. It crawls with pale, segmented millipedes, beetles with glassy shells, and things with too many legs that scatter when a lamp passes. Eyeless blind-white crawlers thread through the peat channels. Under the moss, something soft and numerous presses and shifts, and the whole surface seems to breathe with it.

Above, in the canopy's shadow, insects gather in clouds that drift and thicken and thin, and at dusk the bats come out of the stalks in long streams. The bats are small and pale, with fine pink membranes, and they catch the insects in the dark with high clicking calls. The clicks are the loudest sound in the basin, besides the dripping, and most people sleep through them.

In the seep pools live small fish with no eyes, translucent and flushed faintly pink, which feed on the algae and the drifting larvae. The town keeps them in woven traps and eats them. There are also pale frogs that sit in the moss and do not call, and a larger grey creature that burrows along the seep channels and surfaces only at the thaw, when the mat loosens and the basin fills with the sound of thousands of things moving at once.

The predator-prey cycle here is fast and visible. The beetles eat the moss, the fungus eats the beetles, the bats eat the insects, and the mat eats whatever dies. People take a share at the edges, but they do not try to take much.

## The Turning of Seasons

The basin is always changing. Cold and wet are constant, but the pace of growth and decay has a rhythm. In the long cold months the mat contracts, its surface firms, the seep slows, and the drip from the canopy freezes into fine grey rime that crackles underfoot. Insects fall into dormancy in the peat. The bats are gone and the town is quieter than ever.

At the thaw, everything wakes at once. The mat swells and floods. The seep rises and runs through the channels, and the town must lift its walkways and reset its foundations. Fungus fruits in pale clusters on every wall. Insects boil up out of the peat. The bats return, and the basin fills with their clicking and the drumming of water and the wet, endless movement of small bodies across the ground. For a few weeks the place is almost loud.

Then the growth slows, the rains thin, the pools shrink to black mirrors, and the town settles back into its long grey patience.

## Odd Things

Some things here do not fit neatly. The mat seems to lean toward warmth and sound. Where a lamp has burned for years, the moss grows thick and pale in a ring, and where people have talked and sung in one place for generations, the curtains of hanging moss have grown heavier and curl toward the voices. The town's elders say the mat listens, and the younger people, who have seen the rings grow, do not disagree.

There are also the seep-fish, which are not quite like other fish. In the deepest pools they are said to rise and drift toward anyone who sings below the surface, but this is hard to confirm, and the townspeople do not sing near the deep water.

And there is the canopy itself. Nobody has been up to see its top. The stalks that roof the basin are too pale, too far away, and too many. Sometimes at dusk a faint sound comes down through them, a long low note like a stalk bending, and then the bats come out, and the drip begins again.
