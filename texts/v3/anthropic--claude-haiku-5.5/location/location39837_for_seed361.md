**The Weeping Basin**

You are inside a bowl that the mountains forgot to finish. High on a worn massif, the land has been ground down over an unimaginable span until the peaks are only rounded stubs, like knuckles of a fist left too long in the sun. Nothing here is sharp. Ridges have melted into long, smooth swells. The stone underfoot is a pale grey-violet, polished by wind and water into shapes that look half-deliberate: grooves like the ribs of a sleeping animal, shallow basins that hold rainwater the way a palm holds a coin.

The basin is small, perhaps a few hundred meters across, and it is warm. That is the first strangeness. The altitude should bring cold, but the bowl traps the heat of a lowland haze that rises from far below and gets caught under a lid of cooler air. Each night the temperature drops, the moisture condenses, and the whole hollow fills with a thick, silver fog that lasts into midmorning. By then the air is saturated and sweet, and everything drips.

**The Lattice**

Scattered across the floor, at irregular intervals of thirty or forty meters, stand small structures. They are not buildings. Most are waist-high plinths of fluted stone, each carved with a shallow channel that spirals down into a cistern the size of a bathtub. Some still hold black water. Between them, at heights from knee to head, run threads: fine, nearly invisible filaments strung taut between pairs of stone posts. Where the threads cross, they form a loose grid that stretches across the basin like a half-collapsed net.

The threads are the artifact that defines the place. In the fog they catch droplets and bead them into strings that slide downhill along the threads toward the cisterns. But the strangest thing, which you notice only after standing still for a while, is that some droplets climb. Near the posts the threads hum faintly, a note just under hearing, and certain beads crawl upward against gravity, gathering at the crosspoints before dropping into the channels with a soft tick. Nobody living remembers who strung them. The vegetation has grown up around them and through them, so they now function partly as trellis, partly as machinery, and partly as a nervous system for the basin's water.

**Vegetation**

The plant life is lush but not crowded. Between the structures, the ground is a patchwork of tall tree-ferns with trunks like fluted columns, their fronds broad enough to shelter a person from rain. Mosses cover everything else: rocks, cisterns, the lower reaches of the posts. Hanging gardens of epiphytes drape from the fern crowns, pale and spongy, dripping constantly. The gaps between plants are wide, so you can see far across the basin on a clear morning, and the spacing gives the place a sense of rooms without walls.

Some plants are oriented toward the threads, their leaves turned to catch the droplets that fall from the lattice. There are also flowers that bloom only in fog, pale and bell-shaped, open at night and closed by noon, releasing a faint sweet resin.

**Fauna**

Life here is overwhelming in volume and sound, even though individual creatures are scattered. The basin is alive with frogs, thousands of them in every pool and cistern, their calls layered into a continuous roar of clicks, trills, and wet knocks that rises at dusk and never quite stops. Insects hang in drifting clouds above the fern canopy, iridescent things with translucent wings that buzz in a high, bright hum. Overhead, birds with ratcheting, mechanical songs circle in the fog, calling to each other across the basin.

The most unusual animals are the lattice-tenders. They are roughly the size of a large dog, with long, segmented bodies and soft, fibrous fur that collects moisture. They move along the threads with a practiced gait, sweeping their bodies against the strands and nipping at the beads that gather at the crosspoints. Their behavior seems to keep the threads clear and the water flowing; where they are absent, the threads sag and the cisterns run dry. They are wary of people but not hostile, and they go about their work regardless of who is watching.

Predators move through the undergrowth: long-limbed, silent things that hunt the lattice-tenders and the frogs. You rarely see them. You notice their absence in sudden hushes in the chorus, which spread outward from one spot like a ripple before the sound returns.

**Sound and Atmosphere**

This is a loud place. The frog chorus alone would be overwhelming, but layered over it are the sounds of dripping water, the buzz of insects, the ratcheting of birds, and the faint, continuous hum of the threads. When the fog thickens, sound changes. It muffles and then carries strangely, so a call from fifty meters away can sound as if it comes from just behind your shoulder.

Light is ordinary, soft and diffuse through the fog, turning the basin into a grey-gold room in the morning and a blue-green cavern by dusk. There are no harsh shadows. Everything seems lit from inside the mist.

**Rhythms**

The basin runs on a daily cycle. Fog gathers in the cool hours before dawn and settles into the hollow. At first light the lattice begins to glisten and the beads start their upward crawl, a process that peaks around midmorning. Water runs down the channels and fills the cisterns. Then the sun breaks through, the fog lifts in long ribbons toward the peaks, and the threads go quiet. In the afternoon the basin is bright, humid, and drowsy, the frogs silent, the insects thick. At dusk the cycle reverses: the fog returns, the chorus rises, and the lattice-tenders come out to work the threads through the night.

Over longer spans, the basin changes slowly. Threads snap and are re-strung by animals that appear to be repairing them. Fern trunks grow, cisterns silt and are cleared. The stone itself continues to erode, so that every few decades a post leans a little further or a new groove appears in a shelf of rock.

**Rules of the Place**

- Water in the basin moves toward the threads and collects in the cisterns, but threads can pull it upward when the hum is strong. The hum is strongest at dawn.
- The lattice-tenders must keep working. If they leave an area, the threads there sag and the vegetation dies back within a season.
- Anything that drinks from a cistern at night will hear the frogs stop. It is a warning. Predators are close.
- The threads are not to be cut. Several travelers have tried to take samples, and each sample went dull and lost its hum within a day.

You can stay here for a while. The fog is cool on your skin, the water is sweet, and the basin keeps its own time. But the threads are humming again, and somewhere in the grey, something is climbing the lattice toward the light.
