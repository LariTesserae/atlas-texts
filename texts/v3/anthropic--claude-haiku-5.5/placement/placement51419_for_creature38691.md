I would be the pale thing in the stack that leans farthest, the one whose vapour thickens at the mouth before the wind takes it.

The climb is slow. The inside wall is old concrete lined with glazed ceramic, and the glaze holds heat the way a stone holds the sun after dark. My body is flat and wide, a sheet of cool skin with a fringe of grip-pads along the edges, and I spread it against the brick and let the draft take what it wants. The column rises in pulses. Every few minutes the stack exhales, a long warm push of air that lifts me a body-length, and in the lull before the next breath I hold, feeling the metal tick as it cools. There is no hurry in it. The stack's breathing is slower than the tide and much slower than the crabs, and I have learned to keep to it.

Up here the light is different. It comes from one place, the grey disc at the top of the flue, the only direction the sky has in this city. Everywhere else the light leaks from all sides and the shadows fail to form, but in the stack a shadow falls down the wall beside me, faint and definite, and I can see the shape of my own edge against it. I don't know if that matters to me. It is simply the thing that is here.

I eat the bloom. The green-black film grows on the warm inner metal where the stack's breath condenses, and it tastes of iron and something sweeter underneath, like rain on rust. The crabs graze the same film on the outside of the walls, and I think we are the only two kinds of creature that have learned it is food rather than damage. Sometimes I hear them below, very faint, the soft ticking carried up the flue on the warm air. It is the same rhythm the pumps keep, and from inside the stack I can't tell which is which. The whole city is a single sound up here, a long slow knock rising out of the water.

I come down rarely. The stack is not a place you leave because you have finished with it. I leave when the pressure changes, when the air in the flue goes heavy and still and the vapour stops bending in the wind and hangs straight out of the mouth. That is how I know the storm is coming up from the south, before the amphibians begin their wave through the channels, before the birds fold into the gantries. The stack knows first. Then I let the column drop me, slowly, down the glazed throat, past the warm bands of air, out through the mouth into the first cold drops of rain. The rain runs off the stack's flanks in sheets, the gutters fill, and I go down the outside wall into the flood with the crabs and the stillness.

When the storm passes and the water starts to recede, I am still in the stack more often than not. I am not sure I could say which of the two I am, the one who climbs or the one who comes down. The city has its waiting animals in the channels, its roosters in the gantries, its gathering in the basements, and something in the grey-backed dark under the water that the concrete feels pass beneath it. I am the one that goes up. I breathe with the stack, eat the iron bloom, and every so often rise out of the mouth on the last warm push of air and hang there for a moment, over the whole grid, where the causeways lie in their wrung-out light and the water takes the sky and gives it back, and the vapour bends away beneath me toward the north, and I am carried with it for as long as the wind allows.
