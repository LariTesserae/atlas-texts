I would be a late arrival, someone who came over the eastern rim in a dry season carrying only what the water could not take. I would not have been born to the knocking. I would have learned it the way the children do, by falling on wet stone until my feet learned the grip, and then by sitting in a household for years while the taps went back and forth around me like weather I could feel but not name.

I would live on the middle terrace of the north gully wall, third step up from the cistern. The house would have three rooms cut into the slope and a stone hearth that the household carries up and down with a patience I still find remarkable. My hood would hang by the door, woven reed, dark with the day's fog. My hands would be the wrong hands still: palms soft at the center, fingertips not yet dense enough to read the difference between a seam of quartz and a fracture about to slide. The household is kind about this. An aunt, broad as a doorway and quick as a grazer, once pressed my palm flat against a wall and held it there until I felt a rockfall three terraces down, a dull shiver, a sound more like a thought than a noise. I did not feel it for a long time afterward. Then I did.

Mornings I would wake to the chime of the filter-feeders closing in the pools below, a sound like someone tapping the rims of a dozen cups at once. The fog would still be lying in the lower quarters. From the step outside my door I could see the flood lines on the embankments, the rust-red bands stacked one over another like the rings of a cut tree. I would want to read them. I would stand there too long, trying to find the one with my name, though I have no name on any wall yet. The painters would be out already, kneeling beside a fresh line with a child holding the lamp, not speaking. I would not go down to them. Painting is for those who have proven they can read the water, and I have only begun to read it.

Through the day I would carry things. Water jars up the stairs. Nets down to the channels. Clay pots, when the rain comes and the household climbs to the upper rooms and we sleep pressed together for a week, listening to the roar below. I would learn the shape of that roar, how it changes when a buried channel opens, how the note drops when the sink is pulling hard. I would learn to tell when the water is lying to us.

The sink I would never approach. I would respect its lip the way the household does, without discussion. But I would feel its breath, the cold draft that rises before a storm carrying wet iron and deep stone, and I would be among the first to turn toward the upper rooms. Some days I would wonder whether the belief is true, whether the rain comes early because the sink breathes or because everyone has learned to listen for the breath. I would not say this aloud. I would knock it, perhaps, in a short pattern to my aunt, and she would knock back something I did not understand, and we would both laugh in the silent way of this place, with our shoulders rather than our voices.

At dusk I would push my hood back. The bedrock would begin its pale green glow, and the whole Hollow would seem to be storing the day in its stone and releasing it slowly. I would climb to the rail above my door and lay my palm flat against the wall, the way I was taught and still do badly. Somewhere above, a single knuckle would strike twice. Somewhere below, two taps would come back through the rock, faint and steady, the way a heartbeat sounds under a blanket.

I would not always understand what was said. Some nights I would hear only the rhythm of it, the household talking across three houses without raising a voice, and I would feel a kind of grief at being outside the sentence. But some nights, I think, I would catch a word. A name. A warning about the water. A line about a child born in the season of the high flood, and I would know, without quite knowing how, that the stone was telling me I was part of its record now, that someday a painter would kneel beside a fresh band of red and someone would have to decide what to call it.

Then the rain would come over the eastern rim, grey and early, and I would keep my palm where it was, feeling the first shiver of water move through the buried channels, listening to the stone say what it always says: that nothing here is permanent, that the water always returns, and that the people who stay are the ones who learn to hear it coming.
