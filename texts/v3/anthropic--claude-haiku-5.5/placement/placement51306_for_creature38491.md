I would find myself on the rail spur, at the place where the line runs out of the warehouse grid and into the reeds. The rails are still there under the green, two dark lines with the iron gone soft and orange, and the ties come up through the mud at even intervals. Where the old switch once threw the track sideways, a clump of grey-green shrub has taken root in the gap. That is where I am.

I am one of the stems that grow at regular spacing. From the ground I look like any other shrub: narrow, many-branched, leaves folded tight against the early damp. But I was set here the way a surveyor sets a post, and I keep the interval. Each season I put out one more node above the last, and the nodes are the same distance apart as the ties below me. The freight yard had its switches at intervals, and something in the shape of the place remembers that spacing and sends it up through me. So I am a measure. I am the valley's unit of length, grown instead of driven in.

In the mornings I open with the pools. The mist sits on my leaves and runs off their tips in drops, and every drop falls onto the sheet of water in the hollow beside the track. I can feel the creeper's tubes below me, the pale hollow lines that wrap the old conduits, and I can feel which of them are full and which are waiting. When the hollow fills too high, a signal goes through the root-mat, and within a few days a new channel is opening somewhere down the line. I did not decide it. I only hold the count, and the count says where the water should go.

The heron stands at the largest pool, a few lengths from me. I have watched it every morning for as long as I have had nodes to count. It does not look at me, and I do not look at it, but we are both attending to the same water, and that seems to be enough.

By midday the mist thins and the sun comes through in a long pale bar, and then I feel the turning. The leaves along the whole line begin to tilt, the reeds first, then the shrubs, then the vines on the warehouse walls two blocks back. We all lean toward the cannery stack, the tallest thing in the valley, which no longer makes anything and no longer tells anyone when the shift changes. We lean toward it anyway. I have never been told why. It may be that the stack is the last straight line in the town, and every living thing here needs at least one line to measure against. Or it may be only that it was once the center of the work, and the plants have kept the habit of turning toward the center. From here I cannot tell which, and I have stopped needing to.

After dark the fungal light comes on along the cannery walls, faint and cold, drawing the bricks in pale ink. I cannot read it. I only know that when it pulses after rain, my next node swells a little faster, as if the light were counting too, somewhere farther off, and our counts were being matched against each other.

So that is where I am, and what I am: a post in the rail bed, a measure kept by growth, a keeper of intervals between the water's rise and the stack's steady fixed point. I do not know whether I am one plant or a thin part of a larger count. The valley does not seem to mind the difference. Each season the line lengthens by one more node, and each node is set exactly where the old town's bones say it should go, though the bones are no longer the ones deciding. The frame is still the town's. What goes inside it, the valley is still working out, one interval at a time.
