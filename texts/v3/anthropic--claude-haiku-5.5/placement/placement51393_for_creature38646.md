I would be the first bead of condensate to form before dawn, on the inside curve of the pump housing, where the iron is still cold from the night and the grey-wings are asleep in their honeycomb of mud nests just above me.

I don't arrive so much as collect. The air over the basin is saturated and thin at once, and it has been giving up its water all night. A little of it finds the cold iron, touches down, and begins to hold together. I am perhaps two millimetres across. The housing is curved, and I sit at the lowest point of a shallow rust-pitted dish, where the paint flaked off decades ago and the metal has gone the color of old blood. Nothing here has told me what I am. I know only that I am heavier each minute, and that the weight is pulling me down.

Before sunrise I can feel the whole basin through the metal. The clay plates are still and contracted. Under them the thing that moves beneath has gone quiet, or has gone deeper, and I cannot tell which. Above me a grey-wing shifts in its nest and clicks twice at the threshold. The click travels through the iron and reaches me as a faint tremor, and my surface shivers into a ring.

I think about what else I could have been. The shimmer-beetles would have been easy: a body the length of a fingernail, bright as foil, going up into the heat and coming down again. But they belong to the afternoon, and they live by moving water from floor to air and back, which means they never keep any of it. I would rather keep some of it. The brine crustaceans would be a kind of work I can imagine, pale and patient, rebuilding a rim one grain at a time, but I would have to live in the seep, and the seep is only one of the basin's many clocks, and I do not want to be bound to a single one. The grey tufts hold their water like cisterns, which is close to what I want, but they are rooted, and I like the idea of falling. The reeds draw up through the cracks. The shrubs brace themselves against the pull from below. Each of them is a function of the machine, and I am not sure I want a function at all.

So I stay a drop. It is the simplest thing I could be here, and it is also the one that moves.

The sun touches the eastern rubble and the iron warms by a degree. My surface loosens. The first beetles on the clay outside begin to lift, a bronze and violet shimmer that rises past the open mouth of the housing, and for an instant I feel the column of air they make, the lift and push of a thousand small wings. I am not one of them, but I am close enough to feel the rhythm, the way they are always carrying the basin's water up and away.

At some point I will let go. Gravity will take me along the dish, and I will meet the rust and run down the outer curve of the housing, past the sprung bolt-stubs and the salt crust, until I reach the clay. There I will be drunk by a beetle or sink into the wet layer where the slurry still lies, or the ground will take me down into the place the thing beneath has been moving water for longer than anyone has counted. Or the sun will take me up before I reach the ground, and I will become one of the shimmering things, rising to the top of the sky with no plan at all.

Nothing in the sump has been built to keep me. The pump was meant to move water out, and the living parts that replaced it are still doing the same work, slower and looser. I am part of that. For now I am heavy, cold, and sitting in the lowest place in the iron, listening to the birds and the old machinery breathe, waiting for the light to make up my mind.
