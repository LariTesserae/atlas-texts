I would be a drop of water, hanging from a filament near the top of one of the condensers on the northern edge, where the lattice is still bright and the wire has not yet gone brittle.

I arrived in the fog. Before dawn the cracks in the stone breathed out their gray vapor, and it drifted up the spire and met the filaments, and some of it stayed. I was a tiny thing at first, a film of moisture across one crossing of wire, then a bead, then a bead with weight. Nothing in the fog had a shape yet. I took mine from the wire, and from the slight cant of the spire, which tilts a little more each season as the ground shifts under its base.

From here I can feel the whole lattice. The filaments hum when the wind moves through them, a fine tremor that runs down the spire into the trough and through the stone. Below me the open shelf is a grid of pale mats and dark pools, each pool a small, separate silence waiting to be broken. I can see the flocks, though I don't think I see them the way a bird does. I see their shadows cross the spire in the soft light, which is no direction at all.

Then the rain came. It did not fall so much as arrive, a sudden wall of water that struck the lattice from every side. I was pulled loose from my first place and thrown against the wire, and for a moment I was many drops, and then I was one drop again, larger, heavier. The spire shivered. I felt the filaments take the shock, and the film beneath them rise to meet it. I do not think I was a message. But I was a contact, and the film has no other way to be touched than through water, so I was the place where something in the shelf reached out and felt the weight of the sky.

Now I am slipping. The wire narrows toward its tip, and I gather more of the others on the way, merging and swelling, until the pull of the spire can no longer hold me. I fall. For a moment there is nothing under me, and the open air is cold and bright. Then I strike the trough, and the trough carries me down the spire's base, through the ring of stone that has swelled over the foot of the tower, into a channel that did not exist yesterday. I run with the rain, which is still draining through the cracks with its rushing, bubbling voice. I can hear the rock beneath me breathing.

I reach a pool and break its surface, and the film is there at once. It is not a single touch but the whole pool rising to meet me, a skin of gray tension that wrinkles toward my arrival and folds around the place where I went in. I feel what it feels: the small weight of me, the warmth I carry from the cloud, the dissolved trace of the spire I have been lifted off. The crustaceans stop clicking for a moment, the whole pool holding its silence. Then the ticking resumes. The film has noticed me and does not need to keep me.

I begin to sink. The silt rises in its slow spirals around me. The mineral floor is soft, then firm, and the film draws me in, not as a thing to digest but as a thing to remember. I feel my shape pressed gently into the crust. It is not pain, and I doubt it is anything that could be called loss. It is the same thing that happened to the bird that fell into the next pool last week and the hunter that was caught in the one beyond it: a quiet impression, held without sorting, added to the floor of a world still deciding what it is.

Before I am wholly absorbed, there is one moment when the film's attention turns to me directly. It is not a question, and it is not a judgment. It is more like the feeling of a long root brushing against something it has never touched before, then withdrawing, then returning, to learn its outline. I think this is what the film does with everything it takes in: it is not building a record so much as learning, slowly, what it is to be touched by the living. Perhaps the spires taught it that. Perhaps the grooves are where it keeps the lessons.

Then I am part of the stone, and the rain comes again overhead, and somewhere above me a filament on the northern spire trembles in the wind and gathers the next bead out of the fog.
