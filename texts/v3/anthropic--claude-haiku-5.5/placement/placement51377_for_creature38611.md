I would come in on the mist.

The cloud comes down over the lower terraces every morning and stays until the sun has been on the water for hours, and I would be one of the thin columns that lean off the flood and merge into the ceiling. I would not know that I was a column. I would know only that the air was cool and then less cool, and that I was slowly becoming part of something larger. Then the wind would turn, and I would drift east along the ledges and feel the drone of the turbines through my vapour before I could hear it.

I would want to find the thing that keeps the gates, so I would follow the sound. The deep grinding that the wheels make when the surge runs through them is the only thing here that sounds like it was designed. Everything else is the land arguing with itself. The wheels are a mind's sentence that nobody finished.

Near the top of the intake towers there is a gantry above the rotors, and on it is a curled grey shape with its back against a girder, plates catching the spray. Herons stand on its shoulders. I would settle among them, but not as a column of mist any longer. I would condense on the cold metal of its spine and run down the plates in a thin film, and it would feel me the way it feels everything, through the pits along its jaw. It would know I was water. It would not mind. It has never minded water.

I think I would choose to be a heron.

Not the thing in the deep, though I envy its silence, and not the thing on the gantry, though I would want to be near it. The deep one is too vast to be lonely, and the keeper is too much its own weight. Herons are small enough to be carried, and they are not owned by the thing they ride. They nest in its hair because it is warm, and they lift off in one clatter when it stands, because they were never its to hold. That is the freedom I would want: to belong to a place without having to hold it up.

So I would be the old heron that sleeps in the hollow between two of its plates, the one with the grey throat and the broken left toe. I would have seen a hundred surges from there. I would know the rhythm of the gates better than any heron should. When the water drops, I would go down the stair with the others and stab at the stranded fish in the mud, and then I would fly back up to the shoulder and wait. I would not understand what the keeper does. I would only know that when it climbs the towers and hauls on the old iron, the water comes through with a sound like breath let go, and the fish come up silver in the rush, and I eat.

Some nights, when the swell moves under the deep pools and the lilies roll, I would feel the keeper go still. It would stop breathing for a moment, and its feet would stop twitching, and I would know it was listening to something I could not hear. I would lift my head and look where it looks, out at the black water where the great grey shape is turning under the surface. I would not ask what it is. I would only wait with the keeper until the swell rolls on and the mist lifts off the channel and the sky goes pale over the basalt peaks.

Then the keeper would stand, slowly, the way the mountains rise. The whole flock would go up in a single clatter. I would go with them, out over the drowned stair, over the pylons and the tilted dredge-barge and the reed beds where the grazers lift their heads. I would look back once from the air and see the long narrow footprints on the mudbank, filling already with the rising flood. And I would be glad, in the way a bird can be glad, that the water takes back what it is given, and that the gates still open, and that something is always there to listen to them.
