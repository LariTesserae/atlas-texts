I'd want to be in a slot on the fourth tier, where the terrace meets a sluice gate. The gate's upper pivot has furred over so thickly it no longer turns, and the slab above it has sagged a finger's width off its bed. The gap is just wide enough for a body like mine.

I wouldn't start out as anything large. I'd start thin and quick, pressed into a crack that's barely wider than I am, with the stone on either side so close that I'd feel its cold through the whole fringe at once. At first the world would be only pressure and drag. Current slides along my flanks, slow as breath, and I learn to read it: the tug of the gyres that turn for centuries, the small eddy that forms when grit slips somewhere above, the steady pull of the lower basin where the mud is deepest and nothing ever quite settles.

Then, slowly, the fringe would start to tell me about the stone. The faces I press against are smooth, but if I rest long enough, I feel the grooves under the smoothness: chisel strokes worn into soft channels, a line where a dowel once ran through a joint, now swollen so hard it is no longer wood or stone but something that belongs to both. I'd come to understand that the stone isn't simply here. It was placed, by hands I'll never sense, and then it was left, and the water and the filaments have been taking it back ever since, a little at a time.

I'd graze. The mats around my slot are thick with the fine growth that holds drifting matter, and I'd rasp at them with my flat lip and draw in the film they've caught. I'd leave the rest. That is the one rule I'd never have to be taught, because it's the only way anything here goes on: take what the water has already brought, and don't take more than that.

And I'd listen. That's the part I'd come to know best. A block would groan somewhere in the upper courses, a long low sound I'd feel in my flanks before it reached anyone, and I'd answer. I wouldn't mean anything by it. I'd pulse back through the fringe, a tone too soft for any ear, and the tone would go out from my slot into the next one and the next. Days later, maybe, a faint tremor would come back up through the lattice: a settler three terraces down, or the gate itself, giving its own small shiver as the pulse passed through its pivot. I'd feel that return and know, without knowing how I knew, that the whole basin had heard me and was still going on.

I'd grow. The fringe would thicken, the flanks would harden and pale, and the slot would tighten around me the way a joint tightens around a dowel. Eventually I'd become the kind of thing a young settler might one day press itself against and mistake for stone. The slab over my head would stay a little longer than it otherwise would, because I'd be holding it, not on purpose, not with any idea that the terraces were a whole, only by being wedged where I am.

Some nights, if nights are the word, I'd lie against the fallen column just beyond the gate and feel its weight, faint and cold, and the water turning over its face, and the groan of a block that hasn't finished settling. I wouldn't know the basin was going down toward a stillness it has been approaching for ages. I'd only know that the water is turning, that the stone is still giving, that everything here is sinking at one unhurried rate and I am sinking with it. The cold would be steady, and I wouldn't need it to be anything else. Somewhere below, a pulse would leave its slot, and I would wait for it to reach me in its own time.
