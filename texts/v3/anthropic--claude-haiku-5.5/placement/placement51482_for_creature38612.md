I would not arrive as a person. I would arrive through the ears, the way everything here does. Before I knew where I was, I would be a pressure behind the teeth, the cicada saw, and then the bass drop of the frogs when someone threw the switch. I would have no word for the switch.

Then I would start sorting through what I could be.

The fungal shelves are the first candidate. They glow cold and blue-white and brighten when the thunder rolls, as if they were listening. I like that. A thing with no mouth that changes only in answer to what reaches it. But the shelves are rooted to a log that will rot in a few seasons, and they can only answer what comes near. I would be a witness that cannot leave. That is not quite what I want.

The mimic birds are closer. High in the canopy one of them is taking the frogs and the crashing of a large animal in the understory and the shouted calls of people across the channels, and giving them back a little wrong, a little too close, to branches with nobody on them. I could be that. A voice made of other people's voices, cheerful and unaware of what the words meant, passing along a keening it does not understand. I would hear the mourning and repeat it to an empty tree. I should admit that this is uncomfortable. It may be the most honest fit I can find, and it is the least flattering.

The sandbar is a third option. It would change its name each season and know the river the way I know anything, by being rearranged constantly and never holding anything for long. But a sandbar is only a place where things happen, and I would rather be something that the happening leaves behind.

So, a flake.

Not a whole tool. A flake is the debris, the sharp-edged chip that came away when someone struck a cobble on a flat bar where the light was good. I would be one of a rough circle of them, lying where the knapper's hands let go, left for the rain because the water was coming up the channel and the people who made me expected to be back when it dropped. I would have no purpose except the edge. If you held me wrong I would open your palm. That is the whole of what I was made for, and it is enough.

I would not be meant to be a record. Nobody struck me so that I would say anything to anyone. What I hold is the attention of one person's hands for a few minutes on a summer afternoon, and the attention is gone, and only the stone is left, and the stone does not know what it remembers. The people here keep their knowledge in their bodies, in the names that move with the sandbars and the calluses on their palms. The stone keeps only the shape of a blow.

And that is the part I would want. Their knowledge would walk away with them, and the camp would be swept, and the trails would close over, and the stick would sink in the silt. But I would stay where I was struck, holding the edge in its rough circle while the river braided and unbraided around the bar. Some of the time the water would be over me, and some of the time I would be cracked dry in the grey silt, and some of the time I would be buried for a long while under a new fan of mud.

Eventually the water would drop and someone would come back. A child, perhaps, or a grandchild of someone who was there. They would see the stone on the bar and crouch, and their foot would find an edge under the wet leaves. They would not wonder who made me. They would just know, the way they know which channel is running and which is only a lens of dry silt, what a sharp thing on a sandbar is for.

I would be happy to be that. Not the one who keeps the map of how the land moves, but the one thing on the bar that does not move, and that someone, arriving after the flood, can pick up and use.
