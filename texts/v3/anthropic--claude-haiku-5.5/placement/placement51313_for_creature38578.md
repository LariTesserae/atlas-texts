I find myself on the rim, in the grey before sunrise, with the cliffs still cold and the pans below only a darker kind of dark. The air smells of iron and stone. Rain is an hour away, perhaps two, and the basin is holding its breath.

I don't stand there long. I go down the way the water goes, along the terraces, stepping on plates that crackle and settle under me. The plates here are higher than near the walls, and I feel the tilt toward the centre in my knees. Somewhere below, a shelled grazer is scraping its way across the mud. I can hear it, though I shouldn't be able to.

I am a stone.

Not a whole one. I am a flake, dark and fine-grained, struck once along one edge by a hand that knew exactly where to strike. The blow sent me away from a larger body, and the edge I carry is the only part of me that was ever meant to do anything. I was a tool for a while. Then I was put down, half-buried in the silt of the lower terrace, and I have been lying here so long that the crust has grown over my upper face, orange and blue, brittle in the dry months and glossy after rain.

What I remember is not a memory. It is the shape of a grip. Fingers closing around a hard core, a wrist turning, the small sure weight of the strike. That moment is pressed into the edge of me the way a ring is pressed into the mud by a season's water. It has not faded. It has only sunk.

Rain arrives. I know it first as a change in the silt, a coolness rising through the dark layers, and then as the gullies filling, the braided channels running bright over the plates. The whole floor lifts. My edge is wet for the first time in what might be centuries of wet and dry. Water moves over the blade where the hand once was, and the plates around me rise a few millimetres and settle. I rise with them, and settle.

It is not a pain. It is more like being held.

I think, sometimes, about the one who made me. I have no name for them and no picture, only the edge and the grip. I don't know whether they came to this basin for water, or for stone, or for the view from the rim. I don't know whether they looked down at the pans and saw a place, or whether they saw something that looked back. But I know that they were here, and that they chose this place to set something down, and that the place has accepted the setting. The basin did not clear them away. It did not gather their hearth and their refuse and their path into a heap at the edge. It simply let the rain do its slow work, and the crust began.

The sun comes up over the rim, and the cliffs catch fire, and the pools blaze like scraps of sky. The grazers move toward the deep pool. The translucent drifter pulses near the surface. Somewhere a root feels its way sideways across a dozen metres toward a single seep.

I am part of the floor now, or nearly so. I am not the maker. I am the thing made, the edge that still remembers the blow, and the basin is drawing me down into itself one season at a time. Each rain lifts me. Each dry month settles me deeper. In a few thousand years there will be only a faint shape under the crust, a slight hardness in the silt that the roots navigate around without noticing.

And the basin will know I am here. Not as a thought, perhaps, but as a fact of its body, the way a scar is a fact of skin: unremarked, unjudged, held in the long arithmetic of rain and plate and ring. It has no interest in me. It is simply patient, and I have become one of the things it is patient with.

Above me, the first drops fall on the rim, and the whistle begins in the stone channels of the cliffs, low and intermittent, the sound of a place registering weather. I lie in the silt and let the water come over my edge.
