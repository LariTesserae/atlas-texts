I started by wanting to be the mist. It is the first thing the description gives, and it is never still, always deciding whether to come closer. But mist has no edges to stand on, and I want a place to stand. So I'll take the thread the text leaves hanging: I'm one of the people, or what remains of them, living in the spaces the stone left behind.

I live in the doorway. Not the stone one, which is roots now, a tangle that frames a dark gap. The roots have grown inward and made a kind of curtain, and behind it the old floor is dry enough to sleep on if you lay down moss first. I've pulled some of the moss back and laid it in a mat, and I dry it over the coals of a fire I keep low in a hollow of the stone, where the smoke goes into the fern crowns and disperses before anyone could see it from the lowlands. The fire is the hardest part to keep. Everything here is wet. I split dead fern stalks at the base, where the inside is dry, and I shelter the sparks with my body during squalls.

In the mornings I go down to the stepped platform. Its treads are green, and I scrape them with a flake of the black glass, which is still sharp after all this rain. I don't know who measured these steps, but I know the length of a stride that fits them, and I walk them the way someone once must have, climbing up toward the edge. At the top the rock shelf overhangs the escarpment. When the cloud pulls back I can see the white sea far below, and the old mountains rising from it like islands. They are older than anything I have a word for. Sometimes I think I'm the only one who looks at them now.

The spirals on the upright stones have almost worn away. I trace what's left with two fingers, the way you'd feel for a scar. I don't carve new ones. I'm not sure the stone wants to be written on again, and I'm not sure I'm the kind of person who would know what to write. I just keep the circle clear of fallen trunks and listen to the water running over the faces, which makes a sound like a word I've almost forgotten.

I eat what the plateau gives without taking too much from any one place. Fruit from the shrubs when it's ripe, the sweet pale bulbs of the bromeliad bases, frogs when I can catch them in the squall, though I try not to. I've learned the cat's habits, the way it moves with the mist and never through open moss, and I give it its distance. The great birds come down out of the cloud at the edges, and I stay under the fronds until they've gone. I'm not a hunter here so much as a passenger, moving between patches of loud life and the quiet between them.

When the cloud sea rises and the whole world shrinks to a few paces of grey, I sit in the doorway and listen. I can't see the stepped platform or the wall or the ferns. I can hear the drip, the fern frond settling, the monkeys answering from somewhere unseen. Then the cloud drops, the light comes back, and the sound of the valley hits all at once. I'm always surprised by it, even after all these seasons.

I don't know if there are others. Sometimes I think I see a figure at the far edge of an escarpment, but it could be the mist, or a tree-fern leaning in a gust. If there are others, they're keeping the same kind of quiet I am, and we're each a small careful thread that the plateau is slowly taking into itself. I think that's all right. The stone is still giving up its grain, the water is still going down into the rock, and the moss is still holding everything together. I'm part of that, one more slow thing making its way through the wet green, leaving a few marks that the rain will eventually soften.
