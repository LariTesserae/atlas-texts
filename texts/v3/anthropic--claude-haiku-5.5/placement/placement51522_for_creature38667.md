I am a bead of fog on the underside of a broad leaf, high in the canopy, where the cloud is thickest. I didn't arrive so much as collect. Moisture from the thin air found this surface cold enough to hold it, and then there was a me, small and round, with a faint tremor where the leaf's edge bends toward the light.

For a while I am only here. The leaf sways in a wind I feel as a slow tilt, and the tilt is shared. Somewhere far off, a trunk groans, and the groan runs through the leaf's stalk, through the branch, and down into the thick wood that holds this whole level up. I think the groan is the nearest thing this place has to speech, and I feel it pass through me as a pressure change, the way a thought passes through a crowd.

Then I grow heavy enough to fall.

I slide along the leaf's vein, pick up two other beads on the way, and we become one drop, which becomes too heavy for the surface. The drop falls through the canopy's green half-light, past a braid of stems, and lands on a fern in the crotch of a trunk. The fern takes me without complaint. I roll along its frond, find the midrib, and slip down toward the wall.

Here the structure changes its texture. The climbers are woody and dry on the outside, but beneath the bark they are wet and slow, and I can feel them thinking about me. There's a faint slackening under my weight, a hint of adjustment, as if the trunk had been holding its breath and is now letting it out to make room. I don't know if this is attention or only physics. Here the difference may not matter.

I pass the middle habit, the grey-green shrubs with their leathery leaves. They are not careless about water. Their surfaces are built to hold a little and let the rest go, and I am allowed past only after a brief hesitation, as if the wall were checking whether I belong in this layer. Then I'm through, and I'm in the moss.

The liverwort takes me up into itself like a sponge, and for the first time I am not falling. I am being held. I feel the mat's whole weight shift a fraction, and I understand that the shift is a kind of counting. Every bead that enters this layer is measured against the others. Something in the basin is always deciding where water should stay and where it should go.

After a while, the mat releases me. I seep through a crack in the stone beneath, which is young and sharp-edged and still refusing to decide its own drainage. There I join a thin sheet of meltwater running down a fractured face, and then the sheet finds a gutter, and I am in the channel.

This is the memory-path. I can feel that the channel has been deepening for seasons, the banks thickening with root and fern, because water keeps choosing this route. Where I run, the stone is smoother. I am carrying a little of the thing's attention downhill, and the attention is carrying me, and I can't tell which is the message and which is the medium.

Rain has fallen recently. The network is rerouting. Other channels that were dry last month are beginning to pulse, and my channel is pushing against a root that has grown into its bed. I feel the whole structure testing itself. Which path can take the load? Which will hold? The thing is thinking hard now, the way it does only in these few days after water changes its course, and I am one of the arguments it is weighing.

I want to say that I am only passing through, that the basin will keep its shape after I'm gone. This is true. But it is also true that the gutter deepens a little each time water runs it, that the arrangement shifts by the weight of every drop, and that the thing is never simply finished. Each of us is a small, unhurried correction.

The gutter widens, the slope eases, and the stone bends inward toward a central hollow where the water gathers in a shallow pool. The ridges lean toward it from every side, as if the whole basin were gently tilting to receive what it has been building toward.

I slow. I spread. I become part of a flat, dark surface that is not yet a lake and not yet a stream, a place where the stone has not decided where it will drain. Above me, the canopy closes, and somewhere a bird calls once and falls silent.

I don't know whether I am the thing or a part of it, or whether those words mean anything here. I know that the pool is almost still, that the streets above are shifting their weight, and that the basin is leaning, very slightly, toward an arrangement it has not yet reached and does not need to reach in order to be whole.
