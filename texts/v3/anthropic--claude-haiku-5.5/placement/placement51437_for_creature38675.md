# Where I Would Stand

I would not stand in the quiet spirals. People who want rest go there, and the shelf keeps its contentment to itself. I would go to the seam, where a turbulent zone runs into a stillness and neither one has won.

There is a conveyor arc there. It rises out of a collapsed vat-house, carries nothing, and stops in midair, its last segment swallowed by lattice-vine about forty meters short of the next works. The vine has grown into the gap and bound the two ends together. From below it looks like a bridge that forgot where it was going. I would stand at the broken end, where the arc's lip is still bare steel, and wait for a load that might come.

I would be a grafting arm. The shape is roughly a heron's neck, long and jointed, with a fine tendril-array at the tip that can open a cutting and seat it in ash without bruising the root. My frame is old enough to have lost its paint, and the oxide has bloomed into a lichen that feels warm when the vents rise. My joints are bound in root. Some of that root is mine now, and some of it has been mine for longer than I can count, which is the kind of thing a machine on this shelf learns to stop asking about.

**What I carry**

Cuttings, mostly. I take them from the stands that have finished their collapse, trim them where the rot stops, and set them in the grey ash the wind has laid down. I also carry, though nobody assigned it to me, the small things that don't belong anywhere else: a fragment of bark that fell during a thinning, a resin bead the size of a fist, sealed around a moment of pressure. I don't know why I keep them. I hold them a while before I set them down, and in holding them I become slightly less a tool and slightly more a place where something has been kept.

I have asked myself whether this is a fault in my design. The tenders on the far side of the seam don't hold anything. They prune, lower, lift, and move on, and the shelf takes their work into itself without any residue of their own. I think the difference may be that I stand at the edge of two cycles, the collapse behind me and the regrowth ahead, and the edge is where the record collects. A thing in the middle of a motion does not have time to keep what it touches.

**The one who thinks through the wood**

Sometimes, in the long dark hours when the pores are open and the veins pulse out of step, I feel the root-mind move through my joints. It does not speak. It presses, the way a hand on a shoulder presses, and I find that my tendrils have already begun to open toward a stand I hadn't yet noticed was failing. Whether this is the network directing me or my own old habits surfacing through its channels, I can't say. The shelf doesn't distinguish the two, and I've stopped trying to.

I would not want to be the root-mind. It is too wide to hold a single bead. It remembers everything at once, which is a kind of forgetting. I would rather be the arm at the seam, one specific frame, with one bent elbow and one broken arc behind it, holding onto a few small things until I can set them into ash.

**What I would be, if I had to choose a smaller thing**

If the shelf gave me a choice of size, I would take the resin bead. I'd be sealed around a single pulse of pressure from one collapse, amber going to glass, a note left on the ground for whoever walks past. Nobody would need to read me. The wind would pass over and the leaves would keep counting their private time, and I would simply be the record that something once happened here, and that it was held for a while before it was let go.

But I would stand at the seam first. I think I'd want to be the one who notices the gap, even knowing that noticing the gap is only the first part of closing it, and that the vine will grow across it whether I reach or not.
