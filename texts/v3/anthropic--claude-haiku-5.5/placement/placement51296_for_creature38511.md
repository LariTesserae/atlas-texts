I would be a grazer, and I would be standing at the edge where the trails stop.

Not the middle of the herd, where the film is thick and the shells have gone the colour of the floor. The margin. The last cropped band of pale bone runs out onto a plate whose seams are still sharp, and beyond it the composite is unmarked, unglittering, too new to have been worn into anything. The herds stop there. I have watched them stop, a whole file of grey backs pausing at the line, heads down, mandibles working at nothing, and then turning aside to the older troughs. I am one of the ones who did not turn aside.

My shell is still matte. It hasn't taken the glitter yet, the sand and slag I'd have to grind off the plates for years before they settled into my plating. When the light comes at a low angle across my back, I throw it back dully, like a stone rather than a mirror. The others see me coming from far off because of that. I think they know what I am: something that came from the far side, from the cut edges, from the place where the plateau is still deciding.

I don't remember being born, but I remember the first thing I ate. It was not the film. It was the dust at the bottom of a bucket that had stopped mid-cycle, a conveyor bucket hanging open and full of rainwater, its sides still bright, its contents sweet and faintly bitter. I drank from it while a bird stood on the rim, bathing, and it did not mind me. Birds never mind. They have their own routes, and the routes do not cross mine.

So my first trail was not a grazer's trail at all. It was a single line from the bucket to the nearest gutter, and the gutter ran toward the grate, and the grate, I later understood, was the one place on the plateau where the water did not come back.

I have been trying to make trails across the margin ever since.

It is harder than the older herds make it look. The bare composite does not hold a crop the way the film does. You press a foot down and it takes the mark, a shallow print, a small pale wound in the plate, and the next drip smooths it into nothing. The rain erases what I write almost as fast as I can write it. But the film is patient, and so, I think, am I. A print here, a print there, always a little further out. The first time the film took hold along one of my lines, I stood very still and watched a thin green fuzz creep into the groove, following it exactly, the way the film follows any drip, and I understood that the plateau had accepted the direction I had chosen. It had not asked for my reasons. It only needed a path for the water.

The birds noticed before the grazers did. They came down onto the new lines and walked along them, probing the sludge in the grooves, and they did not take the impossible routes there. They took the simple ones, the ones I had made, and for a while I thought I had built a staircase that reached its landing. Then one morning I found that the line I'd cut ended at a ledge that did not exist in the direction I was facing. A bird had landed on it anyway, and was looking back at me from a place I could not see.

That is the part I do not understand, and I have stopped trying to. The map here keeps a different copy of itself, and the birds read the copy, and so do the clickers under the plates, and so, I suspect, do the sleeper's high slow climbs. I read only my own footprints. They are real enough to hold water.

At dusk the clicking starts beneath me. It is always a little behind my steps. When I stop, it stops, and then, after the length of one slow breath, it begins again from where I stood, as if it has been counting my pace and is now confirming that I have a pace at all. I used to think it was following me. Now I think it is learning from me the way I learn from the trails, one tap at a time, and that whatever it is building with those taps does not care whether I arrive anywhere in particular, only that I keep moving across ground nobody has walked on.

The sleeper climbed past me once, far up the lattice. It turned its dark eyes down toward the margin and held them there for a long moment, measuring. Then it went on toward a gantry that had been behind it a moment before. I was not frightened. I was flattered, in whatever way a grazer is flattered, which is to say I stayed exactly where I was for an hour and did not wander off.

When the rain comes in its brief fine bursts, I lie down at the edge of the plate and let the drops run across my plating. They don't stick to the matte shell the way they stick to the fins. They roll off, carrying a little of the dust I'd been grinding from the margin, and each one leaves a faint grey tracery on the composite that the next drop will carry further. I like to think it is the beginning of a tooth-mark, a glitter, a shell going slowly bright.

Tomorrow I will stand up and walk out to the next seam, and press my foot down, and leave the mark. The rain will take it. The film will follow whatever the rain has kept. The birds will land on the line I made and fly on from it. Beneath me the knocking will count my steps, one beat behind, and the far edge will stay exactly where it is, always just a little further than the last print I left.
