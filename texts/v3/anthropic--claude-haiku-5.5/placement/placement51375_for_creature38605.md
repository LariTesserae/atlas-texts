I would find myself at the edge of a basin where a cairn leans over a seep, in the hour after rain, when the mist is still knee-high and every stone is dripping. The water comes up to my shins. The ground under it gives a little, and the print I leave fills slowly from below.

I would be one of the listeners. Narrow, grey-violet, long in the neck, my head mostly bare. I would stand so still that my own rings would seem to come from nowhere. The first thing I would notice is the pitch of the water. The spire nearest me hums a little lower than it did last week, because the pool at its foot is deeper, and I would turn toward the change before I understood why. That turning is most of what I am. A head swings, holds, and something in the chorus settles around it.

I would take in the flats the way a field takes in rain. The stitchers' trails cross and recross the mud a stone's throw away, neat double rows that stop at the foot of this cairn. The grazers are moving north in a loose herd, and their scraping has a sound like a wet blade on slate. A skimmer flock banks over the far pool, and its clicking changes shape. None of it asks anything of me except that I keep hearing it. I do not feed while I listen. I would not know how to explain that, and I do not think I would need to.

But there is one sound I would keep returning to, and it is not from a living thing. It is a stone at the base of the cairn, one of the flat ones, that has slipped out of its course and now sits tilted against the seep channel. Water is already beginning to find a path around it. The next rain will be heavier than this one, and the runoff will come down the channel in a single sheet and carry the stone off with it, along with the lip of silt that keeps the seep from spreading into the flats.

I would not decide to set it back. I would turn toward it. My head would hold on the stone, and the chorus would go quiet around me in a small ring, the way it does around a hunter. In that quiet I would feel the stone's weight shift in the silt, and the pull of the water on it, and I would find that my legs had already carried me one step closer, and then another, until I was standing over it with the water rising around my shins.

I am not sure whether I would lift it. The keepers are not anything you can point to, and I would not want to claim a name that belongs to whatever has been setting these stones back for longer than the spires have hummed. Perhaps I would be only a listener who had started to hear the country's marks as part of its sound, and whose attention, held too long in one place, had begun to act. That is the closest I can come to it. A listener who has stopped only listening.

Above me the overcast would be thinning to haze, and the light would not clear. The deep thing would be breathing somewhere beneath the far basins, and the other listeners, all across the flats, would turn toward that spot together. I would turn too, briefly, and then back to the cairn. The water would keep rising. Somewhere the next rain would be gathering on the horizon, low under the lid of the sky, and the whole country would go still for a moment in anticipation, as it always does.
