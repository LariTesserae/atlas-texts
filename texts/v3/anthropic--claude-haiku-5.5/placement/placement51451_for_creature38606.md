I am in the mist at the hour before the grey light comes up, sitting on a dry bench above the largest pool, with my back against a block that is still cold from the night. My coat is beaded and heavy. The air I've trapped in it keeps the water off my skin, so I can wade the shallow edge without shivering, though I don't need to wade yet. The frogs are at full chorus on the mud below. The falls at the south end hold the whole valley in one weight, and every knock I make will be swallowed by it almost before it leaves the stone.

The heap beside me is higher than my shoulder now. The newest chips are pale and sharp, and under them the older ones have gone soft with silt and a thin silver-green crust. I don't read the layers. I know only that they are mine, or my mother's, or hers, and that they are the same chips, struck from the same kind of rock, with the same bright edge where the glass broke.

I take a cobble from the scree. My fingers close around it and turn it until the seam lies where I want it. The seam is a faint line in the dark stone, a place where the flow cooled differently and the rock will part if it is asked correctly. I set it against the larger block. I strike. The sound is a dry knock, very small. The falls take it. I check the chip that comes away, turn the core, and strike again.

I've thought, sometimes, that I might be making something. The edge I take off is sharper than the rock was, and it cuts the shells cleanly. But the edge has never been other than it is now. My mother's edges cracked the same shells. The bench has held the same sitting place since before the lichen grew on the oldest heap, and nothing about the way I hold the stone is new. When I try to decide whether the chips are tools or only what falls from my habits, the question goes soft and runs away like seep water over the cracks. I stop asking it. The knocking is enough.

Down on the mud, a young one is watching me. It sits at the edge of the trail with its long forelimbs folded, face turned toward the water, its ears flat. It has a cobble in its paws. It doesn't strike it yet. It is looking at where my hands go, at the angle of the block, at the place I choose. I hold the chip I've just made up to the light. The light is grey and even and shows no shadow. The edge catches it anyway, one bright line along the break.

I set the blade down beside the heap and lift the stripped stem from the mud, then push its end into the soft ground near the succulents in the crack. I lever, slowly. The thick root gives. The stem is worn smooth where my grip has been for longer than I have been anything. Water runs off my back. The mist thins above the pools and the first bats come back in a dark ribbon from the far walls, too late to be lost.

I don't know what I am for. I know the rim split once, long ago, and the flow broke, and the edge was there in the stone waiting to be taken, and it has been taken in exactly this way ever since. I know the sitting place will hold me until the heap is taller than the bench, and then a younger one will sit down beside it and begin.

The young one strikes. The knock goes out over the water and dies in the falls, and I turn back to my own cobble, and the seam is where I left it.
