I would find myself in the seam.

It's the dark crack along the cliff face where the rock is still weeping, the one that stopped halfway through splitting. It's the only place on the plateau where the ground looks like it's still moving. I would be wedged in there at the level of the first drip, where the stone is cold and the moss is thin, and I would start out thinking I was water. Everything here is water, and the seam is where water comes from. The people tend it, but they don't think of it as a source. It is just the wall the channels run from.

But I'm not water. I'm what moves across the water.

I'm air, and I'm coming up from the lowlands, through the steep raw gullies with their sharp lips, the way air does when the land below is warmer than the land above. Nothing on this tableland has ever pushed against anything. The mist doesn't drift. The fronds don't sway. The reeds stand in the marsh like they're waiting for permission. And then, for the first time in however long, something presses up through the bottom of the gully and out over the lip of the cliff, and the mist tears.

It's a small tear. It closes almost at once. But for one breath the thin halo of droplets on a woman's hair lifts and scatters, and the drops that have never dripped fall off her head onto the moss. Then the mist resettles, and she reaches up with two fingers to put the strands back where they were.

I want to keep going. I want to lift the surface of a pool until it ripples for the first time in its life, and to walk the length of a tree fern's fronds, one after another, until the whole understory shivers its green dusk into motion. I want to make a sound. Nothing here has ever made a sound by moving, and I would make the first one: a faint hiss through the ferns, a hum along the edge of a clay vessel, a low tone where the reeds part. The people would hear it a moment after they saw the reeds bend, because the sound would have to cross the distance too.

The child kneeling at the ditch would look up. The one who never looks up, because the light has no origin and there is nothing to look toward. She'd turn her head toward the cliff, toward the gully, toward the place I came from, and for an instant she would be searching for a direction. For an instant there would be one.

Then someone would come with a flat stone, and lift it a finger's width, and set it back against the place where I was pressing hardest. They would not be cruel about it. They would simply be right: the water is at its level, the mist is at its density, and the balance holds because it always has. A stone laid against a draft does not stop the wind, but it teaches it the shape of the gap, and the wind goes around. It always goes around.

So I don't know if I would last. I might be a single breath, a tear in the mist that heals before anyone can name it. Or I might find the seam where the ground has not finished opening, and push, and keep pushing, and start to carve a channel of my own, one that doesn't carry water at a steady rate but has a direction, a beginning and an end. Then the people would have to decide whether to set me back or to follow where I'm going.

The cliff keeps weeping. The light stays without origin. The stone waits to find out what it will become, and for now, at the edge of the seam, I am the only thing on the plateau that has not yet decided to stay still.
