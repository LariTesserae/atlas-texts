I would not find myself on the high ridges, where the mist thins and the wind comes straight off the eastern fronts. Nor would I be in the open basins, where the water is deep and still and the stalks stand alone. I would be at the tide line, where the water rises and falls across a cairn that was stacked by hand a very long time ago.

I considered other forms. A stalk would be too tall and too slow, rooted in a single spot and holding up its fan of leaves through every season without moving. A root would be blind in the silt, feeling only the outward push of wet ground. A flint blade, wedged where someone dropped it, would be the most human choice, but it would be inert, nothing but a fixed record of one afternoon. And there are no animals here to be. The plateau has no place for anything that moves on its own.

So I would be a spore. I would lift from a mat on the east slope when the front comes in, carried up in the cold current where the spiral catches what little light there is. I would feel the weight of the storm long before the rain falls, the way the land feels it, as a change in pressure running through the ground. Then the wind would drop me into the grey air above a basin, and I would sink slowly through the mist until I touched the lowest course of a tall stone stack.

The stone would be rough and cold, and the water would be just below me. I would settle into a crack where the moisture gathers, and over days I would open. Threads would push out from me into the thin green skin that already covers the stone. I would become part of the mat, and the mat would become part of the cairn. The stones would stop being separate from the moss, and the moss would stop being separate from the rest of the plateau.

I would be aware of the tide the way the plateau is aware of it. The water would rise, and I would be lifted into the cold wet dark, and everything near me would swell. Then the water would fall, and I would curl and dry at my edges and wait in the cracked grey silt. Each cycle would reshape my boundary a little. The stone beneath me would hold water differently from the stones around it, and I would grow there because it held water differently. I would not know why the builder chose that stone, or stacked the others so high. I would know only that this spot is wetter and more open to the spores that fall here, and I would keep growing in that direction.

That is the closest I can come to a memory. It would not be a recollection of hands or voices. It would be a tendency, a shape my growth has taken around an old disturbance, the way a scar keeps its shape after the wound has been forgotten. Whatever the people meant by stacking this stone, whether a marker or a grave or a warning, would be gone from me. What would remain is a preference for this place and a green skin that thickens here more than anywhere else.

Eventually the water would take me down. A stalk would fall across the basin, its silt would rise around the stone, and I would sink into the layers. Those layers would hold the record of this tide and every tide before it, and they would be the plateau's only true memory. I would become one more thin band in its stratigraphy, and something new would grow over the place where I had been.

I think I would like that. A thing that grows, holds, forgets by burial, and is slowly dissolved into the body that keeps it. Somewhere under the drip and the boom of the falls, the stone would still be holding water, and I would still be leaning toward the light I could barely find.
