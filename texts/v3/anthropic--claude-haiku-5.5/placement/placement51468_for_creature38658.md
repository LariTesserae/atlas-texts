I would be the drop that is still forming.

I hang at the lowest tip of the longest curtain, on the wall where the ochre band gives way to cream. I am not yet heavy enough to fall. The seep brings me up from the rock one bead at a time, and each bead lengthens, takes in a little more lime, and thins at the waist. I am not a curtain, which is already finished, and I am not the basin, which holds its level and never becomes. I am the one thing in this hollow still in the middle of becoming something.

The curtain above me is lensed and hard, a row of pale glass beads, each one kept. I can feel their stillness through the stone. It is a deep, patient weight, like being pressed against a sleeping animal's flank. The hollow holds everything that hangs from it, and it holds me too, in a way, because I am on its hair. But I am not finished, so I am not kept. My surface trembles at the edge where the air touches me, and that trembling is the only motion in the place that is not a slow tick or a hush.

Something has shifted. Perhaps the warm breath of a visitor on the rim, or a hand set against the scratched stone by the largest cairn. The pressure under the water moves a fraction, and the thing that is the hollow turns its attention toward the wall without turning at all. I feel it notice the curtain's tip, the bead that has not yet let go. It does not reach for me. It has no reaching left in it, only the long, unfinished outward pulse it spent ages ago on the knobs. But I know it is aware of me the way you know the weight of a stone you have been carrying without thinking.

It would be easy to stay. The lime would thicken around me until I was a small white lens, one more held thing, and the hollow would take me as it took the fallen cairns, gently, with no grief and no particular interest. I would become part of the crowd and I would never fall again. That is what it offers, and I can feel how much it would cost me to refuse.

I refuse. My weight tips past the place where the stone can hold it. I let go.

The fall is short. I pass the cream band, the rust, the ochre, each one a year I have not lived through, layered and not counted. The air is wet and still and I cut through it with no sound. Then I strike the water, and the basin answers with the single round note that is the only word the place allows itself. The note dies almost before it begins.

Where I struck, the surface lifts and spreads, a ring moving outward from the point of my entry. It does not stop at the rim, though. It travels just far enough to touch the pale bloom of lime at the margin, reaches for the scored line that marks the level, and then, before it arrives, it settles. Its last edge flattens against the water, and the ring is finished partway, like the pulses in the knobs of bone-coloured crust.

The level does not rise. It does not fall. The surface closes over the place where I entered, and nothing in the hollow marks that I was ever a bead on a curtain at all. Somewhere above me the next drop is already swelling at the tip of the cream band, the way the hollow always replaces what falls with what is still becoming.

If a visitor stayed long enough to see the ring, they would think it was a flaw in the mirror. It is only the one thing in the hollow that moved and did not stop moving until it touched the edge of the still.
