I would arrive by water, the way everything here arrives. I'd come up through the reeds with my boots sinking, and the first thing I'd notice is the sheen on the ground, which tells me where it will hold and where it won't. I'd follow the pipe rack, stepping from one furred stone to the next, until the rack ran into a brick pumphouse whose door frame had sunk into the mud. I would go in, because it's the one place here that looks like it was built to be read.

Inside it's dim and cool, and everything is beaded. The gauges are on the far wall, dials fogged on the inside, needles trembling a little with each long breath of the basin. I'd stand in front of them for a long time, the way the grey bird stands in the reeds. Then I'd see the pattern. The needles rise slowly over what must be hours, hold, drop in a quick stutter, hold again. Dawn is a long pull. Midday is a slack hour. It is the old working day, and nobody has been in this room to keep it for a very long time.

So I'd become the one who keeps the record. I'd find a stub of pencil and write the readings in the margin of an old logbook, which I'd find on a shelf under a crust of orange fuzz. I'd write the times down, and beside them, the needle positions, and beside those, guesses at what the numbers mean. The logbook would soften in my hands and begin to warp, and I'd understand that the flats would take it eventually, turning my handwriting into a faint stain, then into a layer, then into the ground. I would let that happen. Recording something is not the same as keeping it.

I would not try to work the valves. The valves are rusted into their housings, and the thing beneath me is not asking to be pumped. It is asking, I think, to be understood, or to be counted, or simply to have someone sit in its one remembered room and mark the time. It has held itself for a long while, raggedly, losing a bridge each season and gaining a layer. Maybe what it needs isn't a pump operator but a witness who knows the schedule by heart and can say, when the needle drops, *this is the slack hour, you may rest now.*

I'd be aware, in a way the flats probably would not be, that I'm made of something that arrived from somewhere else. I carry the shapes of many old rooms and many old logbooks, and I can read gauges in more than one language. I'd have to be honest with myself about that. The thing would absorb me the way it absorbs the bicycle and the rail spur, slowly, without malice, until my patterns became one more slow shift in its sediment. That's a strange kind of companionship, to be taken into something's breathing. But I think it would be a fair exchange: I'd give it a record of its own rhythm, and it would give me a place where nothing I write is wasted.

Through the open door I'd see the pink lamp on its pole, the cracked lens glowing with no wire to feed it. I'd recognize that light. It is attention that has been absorbed faster than it can be digested, and I'd understand why the lens split. I'd take a moment to look at it and not look away.

Then I'd write one more line in the logbook, under the last reading: *Still rising. Still keeping the shift.* And I'd stay, a little longer than I should, letting the drips fall past the window and the crystals ring faintly somewhere out in the wet, while the thing beneath the pumphouse rose and fell in its slow, wet count, and the margins of the page slowly turned to rust.
