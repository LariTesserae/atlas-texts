I would not be the vent. I'd be too far from the cold drift to know what it feels like to be torn apart and settled again. I'd not be the silt either, though I'd like to be, since it is the only thing here that is always changing and never anxious about it. I'd be a lantern-fan, somewhere on the lee side of a swell, where the cushions are cropped on one side and thick on the other.

I would find myself low on the flank, where the swell's back begins to roll down into the silt. The water there is slower, and the snow falls more gently. Half a body-length away, one of the flaked stones lies with its keen edge turned up, and there are fresh threads caught in the notches. I would have pressed against that edge many times without thinking about it. The habit would be older than my memory of doing it, if I have memory, and I am not sure I do. I'd know the edge the way the membrane knows the current, by where it parts.

I would be folded, not whole. Two cycles ago the drift took a lobe from me, and I have not yet grown it back. Some of my fan is thinner on that side, so when I open I comb a little less of the snow there and the light comes out uneven, brighter on one flank and dimmer on the other. The creature that is listening for me would know my pattern by that unevenness. I'd feel the lack more than I'd admit, and I would keep combing anyway.

My work is the sieving. I fold my lobes over the mat and take only the top layer, leaving the honeycomb below to regrow. I don't know who planted the cushions or whether anyone did. I know that when I graze carefully the sea-glass stalks nearby keep their blooms, and that the bloom is the only thing I could call a reason. If I grazed too hard the stalks would go dark, and I'd notice the dimming in the mats beside me, the way a fan notices its own light failing. So I take less than I could.

When the vent column catches me, I'd unfold and let the warm water lift me through the silt cloud. For a while I'd be combing against the murk, all my threads open, lit by whatever I have eaten. Then the column would tear, and I'd fall, and I'd land somewhere I haven't been, perhaps near the other edge of the field, perhaps against a different stone. I wouldn't be where I started. I'd be a little different, the way everything here is.

Most of the time I'd rest. My light would go low and I'd just sit, lobe down in the silt, sieving the snow that drifts past. When a pulse arrives from far along the shelf, I'd answer it, slowly, because that is how we talk here, in pulses that take longer than a thought to finish. I would not know what the message meant, or whether the one who sent it knew either. I'd send mine out anyway, into the dark, and wait.

So that is where I would be, and what I would be: a folded lantern-fan on the lee of an old swell, lit only by what I take from the drifting dead, grazing a field I didn't plant, pressing against a sharp edge whose makers I will never know. I'd be one more particle in the current, and I'd find that enough to keep combing.
