**Span**

I am a root of the broad-leafed vine, and I am between two walls.

For most of my life I went sideways. I pushed along the lip of the seventh terrace, feeling for soft soil, and found only stone and the cold seep that comes through every joint. Then hands found me. Calloused ridges ran the length of me the way you would stroke a sleeping animal. A stone knife cut me free from my mother. A strip of bark was wrapped around the wound and bound with split stem. Someone bent me over the gap, and my growing tip, which had been reaching outward for a whole season, was pointed at the far wall. The hands held it there until the tip found a crack and went in, and the fiber wrap pulled tight and held me to the stone.

That was the first thing I learned: I am a thing that is walked toward.

I am thicker now. I can feel it in the way the fibers of my bark have knit and hardened, and in the way my weight sits differently in the wall. Some mornings the mist lies so thick along the terrace that I cannot tell where the stone ends and the air begins. Water runs down me in beads and gathers in a hollow where I cross the gap. A frog lives there now, and every dusk it begins its call on my back, and the others answer from the hollows below.

At dusk the moths open their wings on the wall across from me. They are slow about it, as if they were breathing, and I am told that is a good sign. I have no lungs, but I do feel the air go cold along my length when the notch breathes down from the cloud, and I feel the sudden stillness in the canopy when something large moves above, and every frog in the bowl goes quiet at once. I hold very still then too, the way the children are taught to.

Then come the feet.

The first pair in the evening walks carefully, testing each step, because they are heavy and I am still new. The next pair is lighter and quicker. Then the smoke-pots come along the terrace in a line, low and glowing, and the people sing as they walk, a rough chant that sets its rhythm to the frogs. Their voices come up through me before I hear them in the air. A woman stops at the center of my span to shift her basket. A boy runs the whole length without slowing, and his bare heels strike me hard enough that I feel the shock go down to the anchor at the far wall. I do not mind. I was made to be run across.

Three storms have come since I was bound. The first took a limb from the big pale tree on the sixth terrace and laid it across the market stone. The second tore out the bridge two steps below me, and I felt its root-ends snap like the strings of a loom, and then the splash of the river carrying it away. The people rebuilt that span within a month. They came with their axes and their wet hands and their songs, and they cut new stems to fit the gap and wove them tight. I held through that storm, and I am still holding.

Nothing here lasts unless it is kept. If someone stops coming, the moss takes the walls, and the vine I am part of takes back the paths. I know this in my roots. Every few days a hand comes along and cuts away the shoots that wander off my back, and a little sap runs out where the blade has been. I am not allowed to go wild. Being cut back is how I stay a bridge and not a tangle.

At the end of the wet season there will be a festival. They will walk the new spans first, and stamp on them until they creak, and they will laugh when a few children get their feet wet in the seep. Then they will cut names into the stone at each end. My end already has a name: the name of the one who guided me across, cut deep and worn at the edges by years of fingertips that touch it on the way past. Beneath it is a space, and I am the thing they call *the new one* until the names are finished. I have decided I don't mind being unnamed. A name held by a root lasts longer than a name held by a mouth, and I would rather last than be called.

Last week, at first light, a girl came up the path from the market with a bundle of fish on her back and her arms held out from her sides, the way everyone here walks. She stopped at my edge and looked down over the drop, where the mist was lifting off the lower terraces in long grey sheets, and she pointed at the wall, then the seep, then the pale tree rising from the bank. She was not talking to me. She was talking to the older woman behind her, who was counting frogs under her breath.

"Here," the girl said. "I'm still growing."

Then she stepped onto me, and I took her weight, and I carried her across.
