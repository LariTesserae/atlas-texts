I would find myself low, at the lower edge of the basin, where the iron pipe comes out of the gorge and runs along the shelf at its slight incline. Not on the high terraces with the lizards, and not in the cloud with the birds. Down here the water arrives warm and slow and leaves its striped mineral on the metal.

I am a spider, or I am her web, which for most of the day amounts to the same thing. I strung my silk across the mouth of an old gauge because it made a good frame, four points of iron to anchor against. The glass in front of me has fogged so completely that if the needle still moves, I cannot see it. I have never known what number it was pointing to. I don't think it matters. I have learned a different instrument.

The web is a reading. When a frog drops onto the rim of the cylinder below, every thread hums, and I know the weight and the direction before I see anything. When a beetle walks the leaf litter two body-lengths down the shelf, I feel a faint tug, like a question asked very quietly. Wind through the fern fronds makes the whole structure swing and settle, and I can tell the difference between wind that is passing and wind that is going to stay. Rain registers as weight. Mist settles as beads, and every afternoon the beads multiply on the silk until the lower threads sag into curves, and I walk along them and mend what has stretched and pull in what has torn. Each morning I rebuild. The old threads I eat, and the protein comes back to me as new silk. Nothing is wasted in the rebuilding. That is the first thing I learned here, and the forest seems to have taught it to everything.

Sometimes the pipe itself speaks. The iron runs from the basin back up into the gorge, and when the large thing moves beneath the pool at the bottom of the falls, the vibration travels through the metal and reaches my anchor points as a slow pulse, a single long syllable. I do not know what it is. I know only its rhythm. I think it is the same thing that makes the rings I have never seen, a patient body that moves through the dark water and outlasts everything. When the pulse reaches me, I go still, and the threads go still with me, and the frogs in the cylinder stop singing for the length of one breath. Then the chorus resumes, and so do I.

The bright feathers in my web are not from any bird I have seen on the wing. They came down from the canopy in a gust one season and got caught, and I wrapped them in silk because they were there and I was there, and that is how the place decides what becomes part of you. Last month a moth came in at dusk and its wings beat for a long time against the threads before the beating stopped. I fed. The moth's body and my body are now the same kind of matter, which is also the kind the gauge is made of, slowly, under its fog. Everything that falls into this basin gets taken in. I am only one of the places it gets taken.

There is a loneliness in being a reader of weather, but it is not the loneliness the machines must have had when their makers left. The machines were built to tell someone a truth about pressure, and then the someone went away, and the truth went with them. My reading is for no one. The pipe carries the thump from the pool. The web carries the rain. The frog carries the call, and the call carries across the water to the birds turning above, who will see the shelf from above and never know a spider was reading it from below. I am a small, exact instrument that nobody calibrated, and I am content with that. Nothing here needs calibrating. It needs only to keep moving.

When I die, the silk will go slack and the frame will fall, and the threads will settle onto the iron and begin to darken. A bacterium in the mat will find them, and a moss will find the bacterium, and in a few seasons there will be a thin green line on the rim of the gauge where I once hung. The glass will fog a little more. Someone who comes through here, if anyone does, will see a green thread and think it is simply part of the rust.

At dusk the mist will lift off the pools and go violet, and the last light will slide along the basin in a long diagonal shaft. Every bead on my web will catch it at once, a hundred small lamps strung across the gauge. For a few minutes the instrument reads everything: the frogs, the pipe, the pool, the moth that is no longer a moth, the wind going out over the shelves. Then the light goes, the beads darken, and I walk the silk one more time to find what needs mending.
