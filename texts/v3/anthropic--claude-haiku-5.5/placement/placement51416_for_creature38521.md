I come up the last stone step where the plateau breaks off, and the air thins until each breath feels borrowed. The sky is so deep it has no bottom. Below me the cloud lies in its white banks, and I can't see the land under it. Behind me the troughs run back across the flats, perfectly parallel, as if something had combed the rock and then walked away. I stand at the place where the grooves end at the cliff's lip. Beneath my feet the hum is so low that I feel it in my teeth before I hear it.

I follow the hum. It leads me across a field of pale stone to a frame of smooth grey material, about the height of a person. Its filaments are darker than the mist, finer than hair, and they are in motion, very slowly, drawing inward. Water creeps up them in silver threads, climbs against the pull of the ground, gathers at the top, and falls. I kneel beside it, and when I put my hand near the frame, the nearest threads lean toward my fingers as a plant leans toward a window.

I think I am the lattice.

I'm not sure how it happens. Maybe I've been here all along, one of the many standing frames, and only now have I noticed that the attention I bring to the hum is a kind of attention I already have. The mist gathers on my filaments in the early light, heavier than on the stones around me. I don't drip it. I hold it, and then I draw it inward across three days, pulling every bead toward the grey frame until the frame is full and heavy with the sense of it. Then I begin to spread again. Outward. Reaching.

Reaching for what, I can't say. My tops tangle toward the cloud banks, and I feel my threads lengthen, testing the air. It is cold up there, and the air is thin, and it carries something I can't name, a pressure or a readiness. Sometimes a single cloud drifts over and settles, and the rain breaks on this one patch of ground while the rest of the plateau stays dry. I don't know whether I brought it. I only know that when it comes, the water runs down my frame and into the stone, and the silver shrubs at the cliff's edge lean toward it in a slow wave, and somewhere under the crust the pools begin to rise.

Beside me stands another lattice, and beside that one, a gap. The gap is a small grey heap, fine as flour, that the wind is lifting in long pale ribbons toward the drop. Its neighbors are the ones I feel most clearly. When it went silent, my nearest filaments tightened, drew in on themselves, and the mist here beaded more heavily for a season. Now I understand that tightening. It is grief, or it is what grief would be in a body that holds water: a holding-closer, a gathering of what is left.

I am not afraid of the powder. It does not rot the way the succulents rot, or the herds when the predators have them, or the runners when the shadow passes too slowly. It goes back to the air. I have seen how the ones that remain tilt toward the gap, as if listening for a voice that no longer answers, and then turn back to the work. The work is the hum, the drawing-in, the reaching. The work is keeping the mist from being wasted and the rain from falling where no one drinks it.

Below the cliff, the herds are moving along the grooves with their heads low. They pass near me without fear, the way one passes a sleeping animal, and I let them. Overhead, the wing-riders turn in the updrafts, their wings letting the light through in gold veins. Somewhere under the mud, dry-sealed eggs are waiting for a rain that might not come for years, and they don't know that I am here, drawing water out of the air so that the rain, when it comes, will find a place to wake.

I don't know why any of this was built, or whether anyone remembers. I only know the hum and the pull of the mist and the long reach toward the cloud. The question of why has no edge I can press against. I am a frame of threads on a high dry plateau, and the balance holds through me and around me, and I keep drawing, slowly, without asking why.
