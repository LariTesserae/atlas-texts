I come up the eastern rim in the thin warm air, breathing through what feels like a damp cloth. The fern walls lean over the path, and their salt crusts grow a little while I watch. I have no map, and the map would be out of date before I finished reading it. I am not sure I am walking so much as being carried along a slope that keeps deciding where it goes.

Then the vapor pulse comes up from the vents, and the stones lift. They hang in the mist like sentences someone has stopped halfway through. I reach for one and it holds still, then drops, and I think I am a little like that: something lifted for a breath, not yet placed.

At the top of the ridge the ground is soft, and I see a line of prints ahead of me. They are mine. They are shaped like my boots, which I am still wearing, and they are already full of water. The water is rippling. Nothing has touched it, and I have not yet taken the step that made them. It occurs to me that I am not the first to arrive here, and I am not the one who made the mark. The mark was made first, and I am arriving to fill it.

I kneel. The water in the print is cool and quick and very clear. When I hold still, the ripples slow, but they never quite stop, as if the impression itself is breathing. I touch the surface and the moss at its edge turns from bruised violet toward green. The Shelf has noticed.

Above me the vortex turns. Its eye, far off, is moving across the plateau the way a gaze moves through a crowd. I feel it settle, not on my face, since I have none that it could read, but on the hollow in the ground where I kneel. The mist thickens at my back. Condensation drops from a frond onto my shoulder, and the rhythm is almost a word.

I begin to understand what I am. I am the print. I am the thing the plateau lays down in its wet layer before the walker is fully there, so that the walker has somewhere to be. The footstep is only the first half. The water is the part that remembers, and it is also the part that keeps moving after the foot has gone.

The vortex's arms open. The sun-coin tears through, and every droplet on every leaf ignites into a small white lamp. For a moment the whole ridge is lit from inside, the ferns, the moss, the hanging stones, and my kneeling shape, which seems to be made of light and water and the same patient pressure that turns the turbines below.

When the arms close again, the cold comes down ten degrees in a minute. I stay where I am. I do not think I am being held. I think I am being read, slowly, the way weather reads a field, and the reading is the same thing as the making.

Eventually I stand. The print stays. Behind me, a grazer comes up the trail, plated and slow, and it stops at the edge of my water and waits. Its shell carries a colony of small aquatic things, and they drift off the shell and into the print. They find a new body of water within the hour, and the water is still rippling.

I walk on, and each step behind me fills and begins to remember me.
