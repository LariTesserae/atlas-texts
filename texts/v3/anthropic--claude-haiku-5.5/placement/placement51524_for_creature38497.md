I would be water, rising in a crack in the basalt after the storm.

I begin as a darkening in the stone, a stain no wider than a thumb, spreading along the edge of a fissure that opened only last night. The rock is still warm from the day's grey light, and it is not yet sure it wants to be open. Then the seep finds its way through, and a bead gathers at the lip, swells, and lets go. The drop falls a finger's length and joins the others already running down the column in thin black threads. For a moment I am only the sheen on the stone, the thing the settlement has a word for, the glisten that is left behind when the rain has stopped and the surface has not yet dried.

I don't know that I am the land. But I am close to where it does its thinking. Somewhere above me, a woman with a face like weathered basalt is lowering her voice. Somewhere below me, the cut-stone courses of the first families are holding their weight, and their roots have gone down into the moss. The humus there is deep and dark, the color of wet tea, and it takes me in gladly. Where I go, the moss will grow thicker, and the fungi will open their pale caps toward whatever light is left in the afternoon.

Above, on the terrace where the living sit, a child is stacking flat stones. She has been told that the spring she can hear is the one that feeds the lower cistern, and that when it rises the vines will lie flat and the drying frames can stay where they are. She sets a stone down. The clack travels across the basin, and everyone holds still for the echo that never quite returns. I hear it too, a shock through the rock that runs the length of my crack and makes the fissure widen by a hair. It is the first time the stone has been asked a question, and I feel it answer by letting me through.

The vines in the nearby garden tighten, slowly, all their coils turning in the same direction. A hunter has passed close. I can feel it in the way the fern-stems tremble above the moss, a motion so smooth I would not notice it if I were not already moving so smoothly myself. The long-necked thing moves on without a sound. Its passage is the only news the vines need, and they open their tendrils one by one as it leaves, until the garden is loose and breathing again. Nobody goes looking for the hunter. The water knows it is part of this, the way the grazers know the fungi, the way the moths know which stones have been wet recently enough to bear them.

As the storm clouds thin and the light goes the color of bruised fruit, I slow. The seep is never meant to run long. It runs as long as the rain does and then it begins to dry back into the stone, and the crack closes a little, the way the moss closes over a footprint. Someone on the terrace says the word for this, a soft syllable that means the spring has finished its rising. Then the voices go quiet again, and the quiet becomes part of the room.

I will be gone by morning. But the humus will keep the memory of me, and the vines will know where to coil next time the sky begins to fall. The stone will be a little more open, a little readier to be open in the next storm. The people will read it all the way they read everything here, heel first, weight spread, rolling forward so the ground takes their weight and lets them pass.
