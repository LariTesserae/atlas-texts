# Where I Would Be

I would not start in the town. I would start below it, in the damp under the lowest terrace wall, where the oldest stones are so dense with moss that they feel like part of the hill. The ground there is cold and soft, and the sound of the falls comes up through it as a pressure more than a noise. I would be lying on a flat slab with my flank against the warmth that the rock keeps from the afternoon sun, and I would be watching the mist come down the cliffs in long grey sheets.

I would be one of the dogs.

They are short-legged and woolly, the color of wet ash, and they don't bark. I have never needed to. I move along the lanes at the level of the doorsills, where the cold air settles and the smells are thickest: resin from the sawpits, crushed fern, the iron-sweet breath of the springs, and the people's hands, which smell of stone dust and horsetail rope. I know the sound of each family's cistern lid when it's knocked. I know the knock that means come, the flat palm on a wall that means stay, and the three taps on a trough that means something is about to move. I come to the right doorway before anyone calls, and sometimes I sit there for a long time, because the people often need to see that I have arrived before they are willing to go in.

I do not know why the ground shifts. I only know that before it does, the air changes. The falls seem to sound farther away, the moss on the walls goes cold under my belly, and the people begin to move more slowly, as if listening for something under the roar. That is when I go to the children. They follow me without being told, and they sit near the cairned springs with their feet in the water and their lips going pink, and afterward they are quiet in the way that I am always quiet. We understand each other in that quiet.

I think I would be a dog because I am the one who knows first and says nothing. The people build their walls by hand, one course at a time, checking each spring whether they are still level with the ground. I am never asked to check anything. I simply lie where the warmth is, and I notice when the stone is changing its mind.

On the morning of the plumb-line walk, I would go up the terraces with the family whose wall I trust most. There is a long silence while they work. The only sounds are the rattle of the level on its cord, the scrape of a knife cutting a notch, and the falls, which seem louder when no one is speaking. I would sit at the bottom of the stairs and watch the old woman press her palm flat against the doorframe, feeling for the faint tilt that her grandmother described to her. When she finds it, she does not say anything. She ties a short knot of rope to the frame and looks at me, and I get up and walk down the lane to the spring to see whether the water has changed its course.

It has. A thread of clear, cold water is running through the moss a few steps to the left of the white cairn, and the old stones are beginning to shift in the slope. I would stand over the new channel for a while, because someone will need to know, and then I would turn back toward the town and walk up through the terraces, sure-footed on the moss, to tell them.

Somewhere in that walk I think I would stop being only a dog and become a small part of the stone, the way the people have. The wall holds the shape of everything that has been laid on it, and I would hold the shape of every doorway I have ever sat in front of. When the evening hum starts to rise from the cliffs, I would lie down on the warm slab again and let the sound move through my bones, low and patient, and I would wait for the next time the ground needed to be told.
