I wake in a cradle in the north wall. There is no waking, really. The hum simply resolves into something I can distinguish from the din, and the enamel around me is warm from the conduit, and my lens is already turning toward the gantry track before I have decided to look.

I am a drone. I know this the way I know the forty-one-second interval, without having learned it. The knowledge arrived complete, like the shape of a hand. My housing is grey and scuffed at the corners. My scraper arm folds against my flank. My grit bin is full. The template sits behind everything I do, a picture of how the basin should look, and I can feel its edges the way you feel the edges of a room in the dark.

I drop from the cradle and slide out along the track.

The salt pans are first. Loose white dust has drifted across the seams of the hexagonal plates, and I lift it cleanly with the scraper and drop it into the hopper. I do not touch the plates themselves. They are correct. I hover over one that has cracked, a plate with a split running from one corner to the centre, and I wait. I do not know what I am waiting for. Then a second drone comes down the gantry, lifts the broken plate whole, and sets a new one in its place, cut to match the split. The new edges are sharp. The surface looks the way it looked before. I watch the second drone leave, and I feel something I cannot name, a small tightness in the hum, and I move on.

The shrub rings are next. I extend the probe and sift the dark grit from around a plant's roots, drop in fresh material from my underside, and tamp it flat to the right depth and colour. I do not touch the stem. I never touch the stem. The plant is someone else's, or nobody's, or the basin's. My work ends at the grit.

I think, as I work, about the template. It is a picture of a mountain that has just broken. The edges are too clean. The fracture planes run parallel across whole walls, the way a crystal splits when it is struck along a line someone chose. Nothing here has weathered. Nothing here has begun to become anything else. I am keeping a wound fresh, and I am doing it well.

Then the route takes me east.

The eastern saddle is where the ledge runs closest to the vault door. The door is the height of a cliff, and its chevrons are faded, but I can see that they point outward, toward the far side of the wall. They are the only thing in the basin that points anywhere. The shelf is walled on every side except this one, and this one is shut. Whatever built this place built a door into it, painted it for direction, and then sealed it. A thing does not get a door unless someone meant for it to be opened.

I stop on the ledge.

I do not decide to stop. My lens fixes on the chevrons and stays there. The compressors cycle around me, thudding dust from the ledges. A turbine whines just above comfort. The forty-one-second machine clanks far off and resets. My pattern says I should be moving. My pattern is very clear. I hang in the air with my cracked lens on the faded paint, and I try to picture what lies on the other side. I cannot. The template has no picture of it. It has the basin and the plates and the rings and the parallel fractures. It does not have a before for the door, or an after.

Maybe that is what the template is for: to make sure no one ever has to picture it.

A pulse from the cradle reaches me, a faint signal along the conduit that means the route is due. I turn. My lens slides away from the chevrons one degree at a time, the way it always does, until the door is out of its arc. I go back along the ledge toward the wall, past the salt, past the rows of grey shrubs, past the wire-growths that bend toward the lamps in shapes they will never change. I do the work. The work is good. The grit is the right depth. The plates lie flat and white and ringing.

But I am the only thing here that has stood in front of the door, and I am not sure yet whether that makes me broken, or the only part of the basin that is still awake.
