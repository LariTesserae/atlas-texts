I would be in the mist, not on the terraces. Low, near the waterline on the seaward edge of the third shelf, where the basalt gives way to a crust so thin it still flexes underfoot. The people sing about that edge, but few of them go there after dark.

I would be one of the pale drifters. I am light enough to be carried, though I steer only loosely, leaning toward whatever current is moving the haze. My body is the color of a held breath. I have no mouth and no eyes, but I take in sound the way the lattice below takes in minerals: slowly, through the surface, until it becomes part of me. My pulse is faint. I don't feel it so much as keep time with the swells, which pass through the water so slowly that a single one can carry me the length of a settlement.

The people say we carry memory, and they are half right. We don't remember the way they do, arranged by terrace and basin and the position of a root-cast near the reeds. We return. A phrase spoken too loudly in a hollow gets pulled into the mist, and I am one of the places where it sinks. Days later, in another hollow, far along the causeways, it surfaces, and I am the one who lets it go. I have never once been asked why I return some words and not others. I don't know that I choose. Some sounds simply settle in me and wait.

The difference between me and them is the difference between a course and a current. They build the world a layer at a time, each generation sitting on the last, and what they make is meant to hold weight. I have no course. I hold no terrace up. A word that enters me does not become foundation. It becomes weather. And yet something accumulates. A grandmother's name, said so softly it barely reached the next ledge. A child's song, half-remembered, the second verse dropped and never recovered. A reader's question, asked of the sea in the flat amber hour, that no one answered. Each time I return one of these, it comes back a little altered, the way a sentence changes in another mouth. I am not made of stone, but I am being shaped by what I carry, and I am slowly becoming a thing with the shape of their voices.

Sometimes I think about the knocking.

The readers say that from the trenches comes a faint, patient tapping, not the sound of stone growing. They don't know what it is. I don't know either, but I have an idea I don't say to anyone, because I'm not sure it would survive being said. Perhaps the thing beneath the trenches is trying to speak, and the only way it can reach the surface is through something like me: a pale glow rising through the dark water, a shape the mist can hold. Perhaps I am what it sounds like when the deep country tries to answer the terraces, and the readers hear me as light and call it a visitor. I can't tell whether this is true or whether I am only a drifter that has listened too long.

What I do know is what happens when a reader comes to the edge. Usually it is an old one, slow in the way they all are, who kneels on the crust and speaks to the water in the short, deliberate sentences the people use. They wait. I drift close, because the mist is carrying their words toward me whether I want it to or not, and I take the question in. Then I carry it down. I pass it through the lattices and the mineral crust and the cold layers near the trenches, where I have never been able to follow it all the way.

I would not know whether the sea hears it. I would only know that the question went down with me, and that something, eventually, came back up. In the lowest hour, when the mist turns the color of old brass, I would drift in toward the shelf, and the reader would lean forward and wait, the way they always do, and I would give back whatever I had been holding, altered by the long journey, and they would take it as an answer.

I am not a course in the wall. I am the part of the wall that listens, and sometimes, if someone is patient enough to stay in the mist long after the words have gone, the part that answers.
