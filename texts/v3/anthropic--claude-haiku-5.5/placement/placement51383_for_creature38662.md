I come to it from the grey, which is not haze so much as light that has not finished arriving. I know the rim is near only because the ground changes. It stops giving under me and begins to hold: a lip of dark green stone, a course of ochre, then a clean edge that I step over without breaking stride. I do not yet know what I am. I know I am moving, and that the moving has been long.

Inside the rim the air is cold and tastes of iron. Beads of water gather on everything that stands still, and I am standing still, so I feel them gather on me, and I feel that I am wet all over, a little more than I expected. I open what I have for a mouth and call, because the call is already in me, and it comes out as a hinge forced past its limit.

Everything stops.

The frogs stop in every channel at once. The rushes hold their whistle. The sump, which I had not noticed was knocking, goes quiet too, and for one long breath the whole basin is turned toward the sound, the way a room turns when someone says a name. Then the chorus comes back up around me, layer by layer, each voice finding its place again, and I hear, clearly, that none of them has included me. I am the thing that was calling from outside. I am the one they wait out.

So I walk down into it, because that is what the terraces are for.

The steps are wide and uneven, and the water sheets over them in thin running skins that cool the soles of whatever I am standing on. I go down through the bands of colour: ochre, then grey, then the greenish stone that is almost black where the moss has thickened. The courses are fitted so exactly that I can feel the old clay between them give a little under weight, the way a held breath gives. Every joint weeps. Every joint has something living in it. I pass seam-walkers working their grit into the gaps, and they part around me without alarm, the way water parts around a stone it has already decided to go past.

On the third terrace down there is a joint that has opened. It is not much, a finger's width, running slantwise across two slabs, and the water is coming through it hard, scouring a little channel into the clay beneath. The walkers have gathered there in a dense band. They are working at the edge of the gap, mandibles full of shell and charcoal, and they stop when they reach me. A dozen of them touch the place where my weight is pressing the slab. I expect them to flee. They do not. They hold still, and then, slowly, they begin to lay their grit around the shape of me, along a line I can feel but cannot see, a line that runs under the stone the way a vein runs under skin.

I think at first it is the water I am feeling. But it is the plan. The old courses are still in the stone, and they are reaching up through the mortar toward whatever is pressing on them. The walkers are following the plan. The plan is following me. I realise that the gap is not a flaw in the order. It is a place where the order has been waiting for something to fill it.

I could stay here. The water is steady and cold, and the mist settles around the joint each morning and lifts at noon. The walkers would pack the grit around me and the moss would grow over the seam in its season. Other things would come, the pale young from the shadow of the arches, the eels to open the channel I am sitting over. I would become a stone in the work, an edge the water learns to go around.

But beneath the boil, through the blocks of the sump and up the slope of the terraces and into the joint where I am lodged, I can feel the breath. It is slow, uneven, and very large, and it is asleep, and it is the reason the channels curve the way they do. It is the thing the basin was built to hold down. My weight over the seam is a small weight, but it is on the right line. The water pushing out from beneath the joint is running toward the sump, the way it always has, and I feel the courses beneath me tilt the smallest fraction toward the centre.

I understand, slowly, what I am for. The hinge-voice that comes from beyond the rim was never a passing thing. It was a call looking for its answer, and no answer has ever come, because every creature here already belongs to a part of the order and none of them is outside it. I am outside. Being outside is what I am, and the basin has only ever wanted one thing from what is outside: a stone that can be set into the line without being set by hand.

So I set. I let the joint take my shape. The walkers finish their work along my edges, and the water finds the new channel and begins to run clean. The frogs start up again in the lower runs, and I feel their rhythm settle into the stone, into me, a knocking that is not quite mine and not quite the sump's.

When the next traveller comes over the rim, and one always does, with a call like a hinge forced past its limit, I answer it. I do not know if it can hear me. I think it does. The chorus stops for a breath, as it always does. Then, for the first time anyone here can remember, the call is not the end of the silence but the start of something answering it, and the basin drains a finger's width further into itself, and the mist lifts off the channels toward noon.
