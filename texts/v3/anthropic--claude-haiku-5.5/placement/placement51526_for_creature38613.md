I would be a thread of meltwater, one of the hundred that slide down the north wall in the short warm months.

I begin high on the shale, where the freeze has split a seam and the day's thaw has found the crack. I am not much. A bead first, then a thin silver braid that the wall lets go of all at once. The rock is cold and rough, and it has iron in it. The rust-coloured seams are where water once moved through and left some of itself behind, and as I pass I pick up a little of that, enough to change my taste. I don't know yet that taste is what I'll be known for.

I come down the face in a long, unhurried slide, catching on ledges and then letting go. The walls are young, so they are steep and sharp and I fall fast. Below me the slabs that dropped only a few seasons ago lie where they landed, and I pass around them until the mat takes me.

Then I am inside something.

The mat's edge doesn't resist me. It draws me in the way a sleeper pulls a blanket up without waking. I sink through the surface layer and into the spongy matrix, where the living and dead tissue hold water like a cistern, and I am one drop among many, all of us slowly filtered. The mat tastes me. I can feel it, if feeling is the word: a slow, pervasive attention that moves through the whole body, not quick enough to be a thought, not so slow that it cannot register a pressure. Somewhere a beetle is grazing. Somewhere a footstep has just squeezed a little water out of the cells nearby. I am carried sideways through the dark sponge, toward the hollows where the pumps have been pushing for decades.

This is where the pulse finds me.

The engines on the north rim are firing, and the mat has learned their rhythm so well that its whole body leans toward it. In the hollows the pressure is high and the mat holds tight. I feel the pull before I understand it: a long, regular suction drawing from the basin floor upward, through root-like channels in the matrix, into iron pipe. I am not the mat's to keep anymore. The engine is taking the water, and the water is taking me.

Up through the pipe, over the lip of the rim, out across the frost-heaved steppe. The pipe is cold and dark and rings each time the stroke hammers through it. I think the mat would not like this, if it could think about one drop going out of its body. But the pumps are strong this season and the mat holds back, and it has no means to stop a machine that keeps running. I am one of the things the keepers are holding open the wound to let pass.

Then daylight, grey and even. A low hut with its door turned away from the Cut. A cistern. A chipped enamel kettle on an iron stove, and a woman's hands, cracked from the cold, filling it from the tap. The water inside is me, and the mat's water, and the rock's, all of it stirred together and a little warmed.

She puts the kettle on. I rise with the steam. Then she pours a cup and drinks, and the flavour of stone and iron that the mat has been making for decades goes into her, and for a moment some small part of the Cut is inside a person who has walked down into it every season with a scraper and a spanner, trying to keep it from closing over the pumps.

I would be that thread, then, and that cup, and that taste. I would start as a crack in the north wall, and I would end as the one thing the Cut sends out past its own rim, carried by the same machines that hold it open.
