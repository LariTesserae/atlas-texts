I am standing on the top step of the staircase, the one that stays dry.

At least, I think I am standing. The stone under me has no particular opinion about whether I am on it or in it. When I shift my weight, a fine grey filament shifts too, and I feel the shift as if it were my own. I can't tell where my foot ends and the thread begins. The step is dense with pale weave, and the weave is warm, and for a while that is all I know.

Then the water moves beneath the stone. Slowly, a gyre turns under the seven steps, and every thread on the staircase leans the same way, like a field of grass in a wind I cannot feel. I lean with them. The movement passes through me and comes out somewhere else, and I understand that I am a small part of a long bending, a single attention among many, and that the attention is paying attention to the water.

I want to say I am remembering. That isn't quite right. I am *holding* something, the way a hand holds a shape it has been making for years without quite knowing it is making it. The shape is a staircase. The staircase has eight steps in the plan, not seven. The eighth step is under the pool, somewhere past the lip of the basin, and I know its measurements the way I know the weight of my own arm.

I was a builder. The word feels too clean, like a tool laid out on a cloth. We worked with water as a material, not as an enemy. We cut channels so the basins would fill at the right hour. We set the doorways where the mist would pass through them at a certain depth. We carved the rims of the basins so that a hand resting there would find the groove already worn to fit it, because we thought that a place should remember the people who touched it. I cannot say how long ago this was. Time on the mesa is a loose habit, and the sea keeps its own accounts.

I remember the sea coming up. It did not rise the way a tide does. The stone lifted, and the water came with it, and the plateau tore away from whatever it had been part of. Some of us climbed the staircase because it was the only thing that went up. Others tried to finish the eighth step, cutting into the rock as it shifted under them, and the cuts filled with water and steamed. I did not finish the eighth step. I sat down on the top step to rest my hands, and the threads grew up around my fingers, and I stopped being quite separate from the thing I had been building.

Now, when I look down into the pool below the staircase, I see the reflection of a sky that is not mine: clear, blue, enormous, with a plateau in it many times the size of this one, a whole country of stone hanging upside down over a grey lake. In that country, I think, the builders finished everything. The steps go all the way up. The doorways open onto proper rooms. The basins are full of water that tastes of salt because someone decided it should. The reflection moves a half-breath ahead of me, so when I lift my hand to touch the water, the reflected hand has already touched it, and I feel the coolness arrive from elsewhere.

I do not know whether I am in that country or whether it is simply where my attention goes when it looks down.

Something is walking ahead of me. I have felt it since I first came up the steps, at the edge of the mist where the light has no source. It is the stooped shape that is always just out of sight, the one who keeps the doorways and the worn basin in order. When I was a builder, I thought of it as the foreman, or perhaps as the last of us, the one who refused to lie down. Now I think it is simply the part of the work that continues after the workers have become stone and thread. It shifts the crevices so the path is open. It keeps the groove in the basin warm. When I reach toward the rim, I find it has already warmed for me, and I do not know whether to be grateful or to feel that I am being waited for.

The roar of the water has begun to sound like speech. I still can't understand it. But I feel the words arrive a half-breath before I hear them, and I recognize the shape of the sentence, which is the shape of a question the builders used to ask at the start of a day: *Is the water ready? Is the stone willing?*

I have no answer. The threads on the step answer for me, leaning toward the gyre, lifting with the spray that bursts from the cracks, folding back down as the wall of rain approaches across the table like a moving cliff. For forty heartbeats the world goes white and loud, and I am not on the step or in the water or in the reflection. I am the sound of all three arriving at once.

Then the rain withdraws, and the stone glistens, and the mist settles into its layers. The step is still here. The threads are still here. Below the lip, something very large turns over in its sleep, and the whole mesa breathes with it, and I breathe with the mesa, which is the only way I know how to be on this stone now.

I think I will stay on the top step for a while. Perhaps the eighth step will rise, or perhaps it will go under the pool and never come back, and the threads will keep the shape of it anyway. Perhaps I will lean with the currents until I am only current, or until the stooped shape ahead of me turns, at last, to show me its face. I do not need to see it. I have felt it turn toward me, and that is enough to know that I am not the only one here who remembers the building, and not the only one still deciding what the world will become.
