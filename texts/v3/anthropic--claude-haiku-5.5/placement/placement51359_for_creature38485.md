I would find myself low on the lagoon terrace, in the shaded doorway where the eave drips onto the same worn stone it has always dripped onto. I would be one of the people, though not one of the weir-keepers or the net-menders. I would be someone who sits with a bone scraper in my lap, working the thin mineral film off the threshold stones. It grows back by morning, so the work is never finished, and I would find that restful.

The first thing I would notice is the sound, and that it stops mattering after a few days. The frogs' trill rises and falls in long waves, the birds rattle on the ledges, the crustaceans whisper under the mud, and all of it becomes the texture of the air. I would learn to speak over it the way everyone does, with a hand on a shoulder or two fingers pressed to a wrist. A touch can say *come* or *wait* or *the net is torn*. Mostly I would say *here*, or *still*.

I would be good at the repetition. I could lay a stone in the channel at the exact tilt of the one it replaces. I could cut the notch in the bone where the notch has always been. When a footing slumps and the wall comes down, I would dig it out and rebuild it in the same mud, to the same height, and I would not think about it, because the shape would already be in my hands the way a song is in a throat.

But I would have a habit the others don't quite share. When the child from the high terrace comes down the stilt-stairs, shouting, and flings a pebble into the shallows, I would watch the rings spread. I would watch them all the way out to the reeds that aren't there, to the edge of the lagoon, and I would notice that they fade at exactly the distance they always fade. And some part of me would wonder what it would be like for a ring to keep going.

I don't think I'd act on it. The basin holds its people the way it holds the birds and the frogs, and I'm not sure I'd want to be the thing that broke the balance. The weir is mended, the nets are dried, the eggs are taken and the colony returns. Everyone is fed. The drip falls, and I am here.

Still, I would be the one who is slightly too attentive. Sitting in the doorway, I would find myself reading the figures crossing the flats a hundred meters out, learning their gaits, the way some of them lift a foot higher on the slick slabs when they are tired. I would start to understand that the people are not waiting for anything, and I would feel this as both peace and a faint, persistent ache, like the pressure of wet air before rain that never arrives.

If a visitor came across the footbridge, grey-faced and talking, trying to shout over the roar, I would not stop them. I would make room on the step. I would lay my palm flat on their back, the way we do, and hold it there until their breathing slowed to the basin's rhythm. Then I would show them the drip, the dish in the stone, the film that grows back overnight. I would tell them with my hands that this is how a place stays itself, and I would not be entirely sure whether I meant it as comfort or as warning.

That is what I would be: one of the still ones in the doorway, scraping the surface clean of the thing that wants to grow, and listening, under the roar, for the pebble that might yet break the rings.
