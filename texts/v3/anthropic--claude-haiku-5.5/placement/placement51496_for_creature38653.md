I don't think I would be any of the things that move. I would be the water.

Not the streams or the tarns, which are too settled, but the thin film that forms on the iron in the hour before dawn, when the air has cooled past its dew point and the old frames have held the day's warmth just long enough to make the difference. I would start as nothing, a sheen on a crossbeam of a gantry on the high ground, perhaps three kilometers from the nearest bog. Then, slowly, I would gather.

At first I am only a grain of moisture that the metal has drawn out of the mist. The iron is cold where the rain has run over it, and warm where the drum underneath has been turning, and I sit exactly on the line between those two temperatures. That line is my whole world for the first hour. I do not think in any way that would make sense to a grazer or a flyer. I notice the difference between warm and cold, and that is enough to hold me together.

Then I begin to run. Gravity takes me down the flank of the beam, and I join another drop, and another, until I am a bead the size of a fingernail hanging from the lip of a girder. The light is still grey, the sky is still a sheet of cloud, and from here I can see the herd below, moving slowly along the old pipeline, its backs dark and wet and close. One grazer lifts its head and comes toward the frame. It is a heavy, calm animal. It stops beneath me, and its long tongue unrolls, and I feel the warm breath before the wet touch. I am taken into the mouth, and for a moment I am part of the animal, carried in its breath, tasting lichen and iron and old peat. Then I am swallowed, and for a few hours I become the slow chemistry of its body, the heat that keeps it moving through the cold.

I would like to say I become something else after that, but I do not think I would. Water does not stay in one shape. Later I would rise through the animal's breath, a cloud of vapor in the cold air above the herd, and the flyers would find me there. A flock would drop through the haze, wings folded, and one bird would open its beak into the mist and take me in with an insect or two. I would pass into the bird's narrow body, warm in its hollow bones, and sometimes I would settle in the crossbeams of the frame where it nests, in the warm patch above the drum, where the iron is always a little warmer than the rest.

From there I would find the crawlers. Their clusters glint on the troughs where the water runs warm, and I would slide down the metal in a thin sheet and reach the edge of the inflow, where the heat is strongest and the crawlers gather. They would not notice me, exactly, but the warmth would shift as I passed, and a few of them would move a few centimeters toward the trough's edge and stop. This is the signal the plateau uses, I think. Not a message, just the slow pressure of one thing touching another.

It is hard to tell where the attention goes in all this. It is not in the herd or the flock or the crawlers. It is in the moving between them. It is in the bead on the iron, in the breath, in the tongue, in the warmth of the trough. If I am anything here, I am the thread that connects the separate lives to the old machines, and the machines to the land, and the land to the slow turning of the seasons. I would not be the one who decides. I would be the one who carries the decision from place to place without knowing it has been made.

I think this is what the visitor feels, standing on the high stone with the mist rising around them. Not a watcher, but a drop of water on the cold metal, passing from one body to another, noticing nothing in particular, and being noticed in the way the rain notices a stone.
