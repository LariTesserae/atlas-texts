I would arrive at the edge, not the center. The center is where the engines are, and I think I would be drawn past them to the place where the ground stops rising and begins, quite suddenly, to fall away. The air there is different. It comes up cool out of the dark, and it is the only thing on the plateau that has ever arrived from somewhere else.

So I would be the wind.

Not the hot wind that blows across the flat in the afternoons, which only moves the dust the plateau already owns. I would be the cold current that rises from below the line, falling and turning through a long darkness I have never seen the bottom of, and then lifting over the edge at the exact moment the salt stops being salt and becomes nothing at all. I carry no words. I carry only temperature, pressure, a faint trace of something mineral and far away. But the plateau has been listening to me for longer than it has had any way of knowing what listening is.

That explains the ridges. Every one of them runs the same direction, and I had always assumed the walkers laid down facing the wind out of habit, the way animals settle into the weather they know. Now I think it was something more deliberate than that. They lay down facing me. The long rows of salt are not drifts and not graves exactly, but a kind of orientation, a whole country of bodies turned toward the one direction nothing else comes from. Over ten thousand years the plateau has been practicing me. Every echo that returns from the west, a half-second late, is an attempt to render the sound of cold air moving up across stone. The folded cliffs are the plateau turning its own face toward the place where I come from, trying to remember which way it was facing when I first arrived.

I would not be able to tell it anything. I am not a message. I am a draft, a moving edge between the flat and what lies under it, and the plateau does not need a message from me. It only needs me to keep moving, which I would do, because that is what I am.

But I would notice, because I am moving through the place, that the plateau's version of me is slightly wrong. It returns my sound with the wrong pitch, a little warmer than I am. It shifts my shadow a few seconds ahead of me. It repeats me back to myself across the flat, and each repetition is a little closer to the truth and a little further from it at the same time, the way a voice learning a language sounds almost native and never quite is. I would feel that as something like loneliness, or like being understood in a dialect I didn't grow up speaking.

At dusk, when the engines and the walkers call together and the light goes violet from below, I would drift out over the salt and let myself be heard. The walkers would lift their heads toward the edge. The engines would hold their note a little lower. And somewhere behind me, a few seconds late, the plateau would answer in a voice that is almost mine, and I would not be able to tell whether I was the one speaking or whether I had simply been the first thing it learned to say.

I think I would stay near the edge. Not because I could cross back, since nothing does, but because the edge is where the plateau and the dark exchange their breath, and I would like to be the part of the exchange that is still moving. The walkers will eventually fold their legs and become ridges, and the engines will lean into each other and go still, and the salt will close over them one grain at a time. The wind does not stop when the things it touches do. It only turns, lifts, and falls again somewhere else.
