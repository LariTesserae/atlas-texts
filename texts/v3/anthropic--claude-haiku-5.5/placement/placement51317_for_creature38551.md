I would start as mist.

Not as a body, not yet. In the dark I would be a thin film of warm air that has not decided where to land. I would drift up from the largest basin with the rest of the vapor and feel the whole plateau breathing through me: the sap-sweet mats warming, the trunks creaking, the dome humming somewhere below. I would have no edges. Being mist is like being a rumor that the wind keeps carrying.

Then I would condense on a frond the size of a canoe, on the underside, where the water gathers first. The change would be slow. I would feel my weight arrive a little at a time, the way the trunks thicken a finger's width per season, until I was a drop hanging from the fern's lowest tip. I would be aware of myself as a bead of water for perhaps an hour, maybe a year. Time is strange here. I would notice the other drops along the frond, each of them trembling in its own rhythm, none of them quite in step. The patter that fills the whole bowl would be made of us.

I would fall. The drop of me would strike the grey dome's surface and skid sideways into one of the hairline grooves, and suddenly I would have a direction. The groove would carry me toward the aperture in a thin, deliberate rivulet, and as I ran I would feel what the Cistern feels along its veins: the denser attention, the sense of being inside something that is paying close attention to every bead of water and every grain of silt. I would not be afraid. The attention would not be hostile. It would be more like being held by a hand that has forgotten it is holding anything, and cannot let go.

Near the aperture the groove would fork, and one branch would spill over the lip of the first stair. Here I would have to choose, though the choice would not be fully mine. The water wants to go down. The stairs go into the deep basin, where the Cistern does not enter and the builders went down and did not come back up, or did so in pieces. I would feel the cold rise from below, a different temperature, a silence with a shape to it. Something old is down there, holding a question it has not asked for a long time.

I would stop at the top step.

So I would be one of the small ones, the pale, translucent creatures that cling to the stonework where the water meets the dome. I would be about the size of a fingernail, with too many legs for my body and a voice that is only a click. I would feed on the fine silt and the mist-borne spores that drift off the mats. My whole life would be the work of tiny adjustments: nudging a grain of silt a finger's width, tapping a surface to shake loose a thread of growth, keeping a little warmth where it is needed and letting a little go where it is not. Each tap would be a small thought of the Cistern's, and I would be one of its thousand crackles, the hiss that fills the space between the giants.

I would not be a single creature, in the way a mountain is not a single stone. I would simply be one place where the balance is being kept, and the attention that moves through the plateau would pass through my clicking the way it passes through the drip, and the mist, and the trunks. I would never fully know what I was for. I would not need to. The Cistern does not need its small parts to understand the purpose it has forgotten, only to keep moving, to keep tapping, to keep the water where it belongs.

From the top step, I would hear the hum rise through the stone and settle again. Somewhere far off on the escarpment the bellows would sound. Somewhere in the mist, something large would turn toward the dome, and I would feel its warm passing without hurry, and I would click once in answer, which is not an answer at all, only the kind of small motion a body makes when it is warm and near other warm bodies in the dark.

And the drip would go on around me, never quite in time with itself, and the place would go on being exactly what it has become, which is a listening thing, and I would be one of the places where it listens.
