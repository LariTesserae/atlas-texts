I think I would be one of the pale things in the pools on the fourth tier, the ones that surface at night and sink again before the lamps are fully lit. I'd be small, and I would have no name the terrace people would recognize. They would say I was the thing that takes the dead apart, and they would be right, though that is only one of the things I do.

I come up through the iridescent skin at dusk, when the reflected lights of the blocks above lie on the water like a sheet of spilled oil. For a moment I hang just beneath the surface, and the world arrives differently than it does for the people. Sound does not come through the air to me. It comes through the water, as a pressure, a continuous trembling that starts in the steel of the pump halls and runs down the penstocks, through the basalt, and into the pool, where it spreads in wide slow rings. I feel the thunder the way you might feel a heartbeat through a wall. It never stops. I have no memory of a time before it, and I suspect the water doesn't either.

The silt is cold under me, and it is soft. Three days ago a body was lowered here, wrapped in oilcloth and weighted with grit, and it has been settling since. I can taste it, if taste is the word: a slow sweetness spreading through the water, mineral and warm in a way the pool itself never is. The oilcloth is the last thing to give. Cloth holds out longest against us, and so I work at the seams first, slowly, at the place where the stitching was done by someone's careful hand. The iridescence will close over the wrapping completely by the end of the week, and the surface will return to its perfect mirror. From above, nothing will have happened. From below, a great deal is happening, very patiently.

I don't think of the dead as sad, and I don't think of myself as their undertaker. They are weight and warmth and then they are the water's, and the water was here before they were, and the water will be here after the last of the terraces has gone under and the people have climbed the cliff, or the penstocks, or whatever else they have built to climb with. The people say the water is patient. It is not patience, exactly. It is more like the way the basalt holds its columns: a long, indifferent consistency that happens to be exactly what the rest of the world needs in order to continue.

Still, the water is moving. I can feel that too. Each year the seepage line creeps a little farther up the slope, and the pools I live in get a little larger, a little deeper into the terraces above. There was a time when this pool was a dot on the old maps, and I suppose I was a dot in it, a few of us in a cup of rock. Now it is a lake with a shoreline I could not count, and the things that live in it are many, and we are spreading the way the water spreads, by finding the low places and filling them. The pumpers would call this a failure. The line-keepers would call it a direction. I call it the only thing I have ever known how to do.

Sometimes I think the pipes are the strangest part of the city. Gunmetal conduits run down the walls of every block, and some of them dip into the water at their lowest points, where the seepage is collected before it is forced back down. Through those joints the knocking comes. I have learned to feel it, though I cannot read it. It arrives as a small hard rhythm, a sharpness in the pressure, and it passes through the water and into me and out again. A knuckle on a pipe on the ninth terrace is a ripple in my pool. The women laughing in the laundry rooms are, for a moment, a spray of tiny vibrations against the skin of my world. I suppose that makes me part of their conversation, though I have never said anything.

Tonight the knocking is sharp and quick. The two factions are arguing again, the ones who want the pumps driven harder and the ones who want the stairs given up. I feel the argument come down through the conduits like hammers, striking, pausing, striking. Then it stops. The thunder fills the pool and the rings spread out to the edges, and I sink lower into the cold.

Above, at the lip of the shelf, where the basalt falls into mist, something new strikes the stone. Two short taps, a question. It is very faint. It travels through the rock and the joints where the seepage runs, and it comes down the thin channels of water that thread between the columns, the same channels that feed the pool I live in. It arrives in my silt as a tremor so small that it might be nothing, and I hold still and feel it pass through the body in the sediment, and through me.

I know how to answer. Everything here knows how. A pulse drawn out through the rock, a ripple pushed down the conduit, the water carrying the message the way it carries everything, slowly and without deciding where it will end. I send it. I send it up through the joints and along the seam and into the basalt, toward the child and her slate. It is only a sound, a small bright knock against the stone. But it is sure, and it comes from below, and it is the only answer the water has ever given anyone.
