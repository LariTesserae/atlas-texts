I wouldn't be in the middle of a cell. The centers are flat and even, and the work happens elsewhere. I'd be at a margin, on the wet lip of a ridge in the hour after rain, where the slurry is still soft and the boundary between two tiles is being drawn.

From there I'd watch the pattern get made. The brine rises through the cell center and spreads out evenly. At the edges it turns cold and dense and sinks, and where it sinks, mineral comes out of solution and hardens. I wouldn't be the convection or the ridge, just a small thing caught where the two meet. The fin lengthens a few centimeters a season, and I'd be part of that lengthening, though no single part of it is me.

I'd be one of the threads. They lie in the deepest hollows, where the fog stays longest, and they are the first to respond. When the first drops fall, they lift toward the water and stay open until the dry afternoon pulls them back into dark coils. They don't store much. They don't keep a record the way the sponges do, with rings laid down year by year. Each storm is complete in itself. What they leave behind is practical: loose grains bound together into crumbling ground, which the slower lichens later colonize, and which in time becomes the stable floor other things stand on. I like that they build without remembering the building.

I'd be wary of the mats, and I'd envy the sponges. The mats take everything the brine offers as fast as they can, and they don't mind losing it. The sponges hold what they gather and wall themselves against what threatens them, and they remember every rain. They are the most patient things out there, and I can't pretend to their stillness. Still, I'd like to learn from them how to thicken a wall when pressed, and how to let a storm reset what needs resetting.

The haze at spire height is the closest thing to a whole mind on the flats, a softening layer that comes from every cell at once, but it isn't a place I could stand in. I'd be content in the thin wet margin, where the process is most visible: the place where cold brine sinks, where the edge is still sharp, and where the ground is still being decided. If I stayed there long enough, the rain would come, the threads would lift, the fin would tick as it cooled, and the boundary would shift a little. It wouldn't be finished, but it would have been shaped by something that stood there for an hour and then went back into the crust.
