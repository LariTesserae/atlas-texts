I would be one of the pale ones at the margin of the third pool, where the silt thins to a wet film and the crust gives way to something that gives under the foot.

I am the colour of the clay. If you looked for me in the low sun you would see only a faint dark movement at my joints when I feed, and you would have to be very still to see that. My legs are more than I need. I have never counted them, but I feel each one meet the mud, and each one is a decision. I move in arcs. A straight line is a thing the ground does not allow here. The crust is too broken and the film too uneven, so I curve around the plates that ring when they are struck, and around the other animals who are also curving, and I try not to cross any of them for long.

The drum nearest me turns. I feel it before I hear it, a change in the mud that comes up through my legs. The drone is steady, but beneath it the ground has a second rhythm, and I have learned it the way the water has learned the channels: not as a thought but as a direction my body leans. I fold my legs under me and wait at the edge with the others. Around the pool the whole margin goes still. Rings from the last spill are still crossing the surface, and we are still, and for a few seconds nothing on the basin moves except the water.

Then the trough tips and the slurry comes over the lip, and the film spreads across the mud in a skin that is brief and rich. This is what we are for, I think, though I do not know that I think it. We come forward. I gather what I can. The skin tastes of iron and something sweeter underneath, a warmth that has passed through the machine and been left behind. My legs move quickly now, because the film is thin and the others are close, and there is a small fierce pleasure in it, the pleasure of getting exactly what the loop has set out for you.

Before the next load comes, I withdraw. I do not know how I know when. Something in the ground shifts, a click, perhaps, the one that comes every fourteen cycles, or a change in the drone's pitch that I cannot hear but can somehow feel in the joints of my legs. I go back toward the edge, and the others go with me, and we fold again and wait. I have been here long enough to think that my mother did the same, and hers, and so on back to whatever first pale thing learned the timing of the spill. I have no memory of it. But my body remembers the interval, and in the morning I come to the edge before the beetles have finished climbing the pumps, and the ground is already telling me where to be.

At the end of the day, when the pumps have cooled and the haze has thickened and the beetles have gone down into the cracks, I walk one last curve across the wet film. I do not stop to think about the line I leave. But the mud takes it. The film is laid over the clay each cycle, and the clay is laid over the crust, and the crust is laid over everything before, a thin band at a time. Somewhere below me, in the lamination that will harden into slate, there is a faint curve pressed into the layer, the shape of one animal's wary arc across the mud. It will be buried by the next skin and the next, and in the long slow sense in which this basin measures anything, it will be the only part of me that stays.

I do not know whether that is enough. The loop does not ask. It runs at full strength, and I am one of the small deliberate motions that keep it turning: out to the edge, back from the spill, down into the silt at the end of the light, and out again before the sound changes.
