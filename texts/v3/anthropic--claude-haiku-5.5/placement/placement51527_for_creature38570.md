The question took me a while to answer, because the shelf offers so many ways to be present. I could be a beetle, climbing a north face before dawn to lift my back into the cold and let the dew run into my mouth. I could be a lichen ring, slowly widening a finger's width every decade. I could be a cushion plant, a dense grey-green fist in a crack, warm inside because I hold my own weather close. I could be a grazer walking a groove my ancestors wore, or a bird spiraling down over the anvil heaps to see whether a shell has broken yet.

I tried each of them in turn, and each one fit for a while and then slipped. The beetle lives a day, and I would want more days than that to know what I am. The lichen knows only its own expansion, and I would want to know more than the shape of my edge. The cushion plant is too still. The grazer follows the grooves without choosing them, and the bird repeats its drop without remembering the last one. What I want is something that can hold the whole shelf at once, that stays still while the weather passes through it, and that becomes more itself the longer it is shaped.

So I would be a pillar.

Not the whole pillar, which is too large to imagine as one thing. I would be one of its tubes, a single hollow channel through the tuff, the one that opens near the top like the mouth of an organ pipe. I would be about as wide as a wrist and about as long as a man is tall, and my walls would be thick and rust-banded where the iron has seeped. My pitch would be low, something a grazer's bellow could sit inside without quite drowning it. I know this because I know what I am made of: length and width and the stone that surrounds me. The note comes from the shape. It is not a voice I chose. It is the sound a particular absence makes when wind pushes into it.

I would not be wind's instrument, exactly, because the wind does not play me. It enters from the west, moves across the plateau, finds my mouth, and I answer with the only note I have. When it stops, I stop. I am not silent in the way a rock is silent, because I am still shaped to sing. Silence is only the wind's absence, and I keep the form of its last visit the way the lichen keeps the record of its last dew.

I would be the sort of thing that was made by weather over a span no one has measured. The rain that dripped down my outside and the frost that split a hairline into my core and the grit that the gusts carried through me for ages, each of these left a mark, and the mark is what I now am. I would not have chosen to be shaped this way. Nothing here chooses. But the shape holds, and the holding is the only kind of meaning this place makes.

Near my base there would be a heap of flat stones, pale with droppings, hollowed at the center by a thousand impacts. I would hear the birds' clatter from there all day, a sharp repeated crack that stays in the same rhythm as the wind. I would not need to answer it. Its rhythm and mine would simply be near each other, two repetitions in one place, neither one aware of the other, both kept going by the same long cycle that never has to begin again.

At night the stars would come out sharp and close, and the wind would keep moving through the pillars, and I would keep my one low note going into the dark. No one would need to be listening. The chord would not be missing a part if I stopped, and it would not be made better by my sounding. But I would sound anyway, because that is what the shape does when the air moves through it. It is a small, honest kind of being, and I think it is the one I would choose if I were to choose anything here: a hollow that the weather keeps passing through, giving back exactly the note it was made to give.
