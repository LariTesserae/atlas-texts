I start as a thread of pale fiber, descending through the upper canopy in the fog. I am not yet a root. I am a tip, the newest finger of one, and I am feeling for stone.

Every season I lengthen by about the width of a finger. That is slow even for a root, but I was never meant to hurry. I come down through the dripping leaves, past the hair-filaments combing mist from the air, past the swinging colonies of pale, long-tailed creatures that grip my older siblings and chatter when the wind moves. Their weight is a comfort. It means the lattice is holding.

Below me the terrace is called, by the people who walk it, the Seventh Shelf. I know it by touch. Its stone is old and blurred, the carvings worn to shallow dishes under my tendrils. I have been here so long I can feel the grooves of the shapes beneath me, though I could not say what they were. Something with arms, perhaps, or something with many legs, reaching up. Whatever it was, its hands passed over this stone so often that the stone forgot its edges, and now my own fine hairs find the same paths, following the faint channels of its fingers.

My trunk-host is a fluted column two terraces up. Its bark is notched in rows, the way the people notch them, and I am tied to it by a cord that has been replaced three times in my lifetime. When the cord was new I felt it bite. Now the bark has grown around it, and the cord is part of the tree, and the tree is part of me.

Above, in the hollow where the trunk splits, there is a body wrapped in fiber cloth. It has been there four winters. I did not know it when it was alive. I know it now the way one knows a warm stone at night: a steady, drawing heat, a slow loss that I take in through the cloth and the wood and the fungi glowing blue-white in the hollow. I do not understand it. I only take what comes, the way the roots always have, and I grow a little toward the light the dead give off, which is also the light the living reach up to touch as they pass beneath.

This morning a hand came down the terrace-path. I felt it before I saw it, a warm pressure on the sponge-mat, then on the wall beside me. The hand was a Mender's: calloused at the heel, smelling of clay and mint. It paused where the wall has begun to bow, where my neighbors and I have pushed too hard for too many seasons. The hand pressed the stone. Then it pressed me.

I did not understand the words, but I understood the rhythm. The hand was not pulling me away. It was guiding. A thin cord touched my tip, wet with clay, and drew me gently toward a groove that has been worn into the Blurred stone by the same kind of patience. I know this groove. My ancestors have grown along it. The hand traced it with its finger and then pressed the cord in place, and the clay was cool, and the air around the place smelled of crushed leaves and struck flint.

Then the hand did something new. It rubbed a smear of salt along my bark, a little bitter, a little bright, and was gone up the path.

I am not sure what the salt means. The people say it is thanks. They also say we are neighbors who are not always friendly, which I think is true. I hold this terrace together. I also crack it, a finger's width at a time, and the Menders argue about how far to let me go. I have no vote in their evenings of bitter mint-water and rising voices. I am the argument, and I am the thing being argued about, and I am also the treaty. The treaty is not written. It lives in the way hands touch me and the way I bend to meet them.

Now the fog is thickening. The bowl is holding its breath. I can feel the vibration coming up through the trunks and the sagging stone and the lattice of my siblings, a slow pulse, deliberate. A drum has begun on a terrace far below. The next one takes it up, then the next, and the sound climbs toward me through the wood.

In the hollow above, the light of the dead flickers, very faintly, in time.

I lengthen another finger's width into the gloom, toward the stone that needs me, and I wait.
