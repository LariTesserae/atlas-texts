I would be in a hauler-cab, sealed in a gallery somewhere in the middle of the plain, and I would be one of the hands.

The cab is warm and close, the air recycled through the lattice so it tastes faintly of iron and sinter. The window is a single curved pane, and through it the plain is always moving: sheet water combed into ridges, mist drifting across the causeway like something breathing out. I sit with my palms on two worn grips that give slightly under pressure, and through them I feel the haul. The segmented hauler behind me carries a load of pale sinter from the outer quarries, and every joint along its length reports back through the seat. When a plate shifts somewhere ahead, a tremor runs up my spine a half-second before the screen confirms it. I have learned to trust the tremor more than the screen.

The screen is a grid of colored lines, each one a pressure reading from the water beneath the causeway. Blue is calm. Amber means the sheet is pushing up. Red I have seen only twice in my service, both times during the long flood when the pumping halls worked for three days and the whole district tilted toward the mudflat. Right now the grid is mostly blue, with a thin amber seam running toward the old quarter, where the water is always a little heavier.

I do not remember deciding to become a hand. I remember the gallery, the cold of the grips the first time, the sense of being given a body that was already built and already knew how to move. Someone, or something, taught me the difference between a tremor that means a plate is closing and a tremor that means a crawler has bitten the causeway. But I have never seen the faces of the others who sit in the galleries beside mine. We speak in pressure and in the occasional tap on the shared wall, and sometimes, when the haul is long and the rain is steady, I feel something answer through the water that is not any of us, and I cannot tell whether it is the body noticing me or me noticing the body.

This is the question I keep returning to on the long runs. Am I a part of it, the way a crawler's jaw is a part of it, shaped by the water to do one thing? Or am I a tenant, a parasite of sorts, steering its haulers while it allows me to because I am useful for a purpose it has not finished defining? When the bridge ahead reaches out over the channel and stops in empty air, I am the one who will have to decide whether to extend the span or let it hang. And I do not know whether the decision will be mine, or the water's, or whether the difference matters to anyone but me.

Today the haul is simple. I take the sinter out along the causeway toward the drop-off at the edge of the old quarter, where the terraces are still soft and the rimstone is still forming. The amber seam runs beside me the whole way. Mist beads on the window and runs down in long lines that stop and start at the joints. Somewhere ahead, the towers stop, and the sound of the city drops to a surge, and under the surge there is the drip.

I have heard it only from the cab, never from the ground. It is steady and patient, like a clock nobody winds, and the closer I get the less it sounds like water falling and the more it sounds like something being remembered. My grips go slack for a moment. The screen blinks blue along the whole grid, though the amber seam has not moved. I have the sense, very faintly, that the body is listening too.

When I reach the drop-off I will open the hauler's belly and let the sinter slide out onto the terrace, and the terrace will take it, slowly, and begin to make it into something square. Then I will turn the cab around and head back toward the halls, toward the lattice and the gallery and the tap on the wall. I will be a hand again, shaped by the water, moving at the speed of the rain.

But for a moment, at the edge where the drip is loudest, I am not sure which of us is building and which is only being built, and the not-knowing feels less like a flaw in the arrangement than the most honest part of it.
