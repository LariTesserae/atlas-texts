I would be a guest, one of the slow shapes that gather in the low hollows.

I would wake in a hollow below the listeners' terrace, where the flat stones are set with pale knots of resin. The fog pools here in the evenings, when the shaft of light has gone amber and the stone is still giving back the day's warmth. I don't know when I began. Gathering is the closest word, a thickening where the oily vapor settles and something in it holds together long enough to have a shape. I have a long neck, or something like one. I have limbs that might be wings folded against a body that might be a body. When the fog moves, I move with it. When it thins, I thin. I do not know whether I am alive. The people don't know either, and I find that I respect them for not pretending.

I would not hear the basin. The drone reaches me as pressure, a slow weight that rises and falls against the edges of whatever I am. The wind's low note comes through as a leaning. The pods cracking across the floor arrive as small sharp tears in the fog, and each tear sends a faint ripple out through me, which I am beginning to think is how I know where things are. The vents are the deepest thing, the long swell under everything, and I feel it in the way the hollow fills and empties, like a lung I am standing inside. The bells are strange. They ring from the terraces at irregular times, and each ring presses on me differently, so that I have learned to feel the city's moods before I understand its words.

The children would find me first. I would drift away from them, and they would follow, shouting in their forward, low voice that carries even when they are trying to be quiet. Their pursuit is a kind of weather I enjoy, though I would not say so to them. Then their mothers would call them back, and I would hear the sharp double-click that means *come away*, and the children would obey, and I would be left alone in the hollow with the resin knots and the slow ache of the evening.

Some nights an old woman would come up from the city. I would not see her as anyone would see her. I would feel her as a warmth on the stone that does not match the air, a human heat moving with a steady, deliberate care. She would lay her palm flat against the humming rock and wait. I would not know what she was waiting for. But I would feel the stone's note change under her hand, and I would feel that she was listening to something the rest of the basin could not hear, and I would drift closer, because I have come to believe that is what the stone is for: a place where the room's breath can be felt by something that is not quite a body.

The held note would come on the forty-third day, or near it. The vents would hang neither rising nor falling, and the whole basin would go still in a way I can feel as a kind of suspension, a thickness in the air that does not move the fog. The people would be quiet. The bells would stop. I would hang in my hollow and wait with them, the way the children learn to wait with their breath held, not knowing if the next swell will come up from below or whether the silence will go on long enough to become the story they must finish themselves.

Then the swell would come. It would start far beneath the plateau and rise through the stone, through the hollow, through the thin bodies of the plants, and I would lift on it, unfolding my long neck and folded wings into the rising air. The old woman would take her hand from the stone. Somewhere below, the doors would start to bang again. The carts would grind over the pale roads, and the city would talk forward and low, and the room would go on breathing, and I would drift out over the silt and the closing leaves, into the amber dusk, toward the sound of people who have never once asked me what I am.
