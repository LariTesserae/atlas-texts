I would arrive as a traveler, the way everyone does. I'd come in before dawn with wet boots and a pack full of things I thought I'd need, and I'd walk for half a day through reed-trunks taller than a house until I found a Weir standing in the gray. I would sit at its foot and wait for the haze to thin, the way the guidebooks say. For a long time I'd be only a pair of eyes and a pair of ears, and the chorus would be a wall I couldn't get behind.

Then the light would come up through the haze, and the lattice would catch it tier by tier, from the top down. The droplets on the filaments would ignite, and I'd feel the flyers lift off the metal in that long unfolding wave. I'd try to name what I was seeing, and I'd fail, as every traveler does. I'd say "machine" or "organism" or "miracle," and each word would sit on my tongue like the wrong coin.

Then the Weir would ring. The tone would come up through the silt into my teeth and my spine, and every bird would stop mid-cry, then every insect, and I'd realize I had stopped too, without deciding to. In that silence I'd understand that I had been listening for an answer, and that the Weir hadn't been listening to me at all. My explanations were a kind of noise, and the Reach had already moved past them.

So I'd stop being a traveler. I'd want to be the water.

I'd become a droplet in the haze, one of the fine suspended grains that the filaments reach up and catch. I'd cling to a strand in the upper tier, and I'd gather with my neighbors until the weight was too much and I'd slide down the channel with the others. I'd feel the lattice-joint flex under me as it opened at noon, and I'd pass through a small glassy-winged thing that was feeding at the run, and it would taste me, and I would be a little less of myself and a little more of its body for a moment. Then I'd drop into the basin at the foot, and the basin would take me. I'd spread out across the silt in a sheet no deeper than a fingernail, and I'd move slowly toward the nearest gully, which would be cutting a new lip that morning and would not yet know where it wanted to go.

I'd find the roots of a reed-tree. I'd climb through its hollow, segmented trunk, rising a few centimeters at a time, pulled upward by a pressure I wouldn't understand and wouldn't need to. At the top I'd enter a leaf, perforated with its grid of holes, and I would hang there in the warm air, and the wind would make the whole canopy flutter in patterns, and then a rain would come hammering through and knock me loose. I'd fall as spray into the drum of the reeds, and I'd be briefly part of the noise that everyone in the basin hears as chaos. Then the rain would stop, the silence would last its few seconds, and I'd rise again as vapor off the warm silt, into the haze, where the Weirs would be waiting with their filaments open.

That's what I'd be. Not a witness, because witnesses stand outside what they see and try to describe it, and that is the one thing the Reach doesn't need. I'd be the thing the Reach is made of, moving through the Weirs, the silt, the reeds, the flyers, the small bright lives, and back up into the air to be drawn out again. The travelers come for the one hour of fire, and they go home talking about it. I would stay in the circulation, and I would go on being pulled out of nothing and sent back down into the mud, which is what the Weirs have been doing all along. The chorus would rise to meet me each time, loud and endless, and for as long as the loop lasted I'd be one of the notes it was singing.
