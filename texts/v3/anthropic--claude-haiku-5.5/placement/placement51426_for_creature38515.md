I would arrive at the rim, in the one place the light reaches. I would be standing on the top of a wall, on a ledge of dressed stone where a root has pushed up through the cornice and left a flat shelf of moss. From there I could see the whole bowl, the arches closing, the fountain small and dark at the center, and I would feel the heat come up off the floor like breath. For a while I would only watch. The rain is due, and I would know it before the sky changed, because the top leaves would start to tick and I would hear the first drum from the big canopy leaves a long way off.

Then the storm would come down through the tiers, and I would hear it sorted. The broad leaves first, then the hiss of the thin ones, then the tick of bark. I would understand, without words, that this is how the place reads weather, and that I am being read along with it. I would not be sure whether I was the one listening or the thing being listened to.

When the rain thinned I would go down. The walls are a ladder of roots, and I would take the one that runs along a drain grate near the eastern gate. The drain is cool and wet, and the sound inside it is different: close, round, heavy with water. I would find a crack in the channel's lip where the roots have gone in and lifted the stone, and I would settle there. I am small, speckled, green-grey, with a throat that swells when I call. I would be one of the voices in the chorus, a frog among the hundreds, the note I make rising and falling in the same rhythm as the others.

And here is the strange part. I would open my throat and feel the call start, but I would already have heard it. Somewhere up in the wet dark a second version of my call would have arrived a breath earlier, and I would hear it as if it came from someone else. Then my own voice would follow it, slightly late, as though I were answering myself. I would not know, when I called, whether I was making the sound or repeating it. The whole drain would seem to be rehearsing, and I would be the part of the rehearsal that finally came out loud.

That would make me strange to myself. The other frogs would call and I would hear their calls arrive first, a fraction before their throats moved. Once I would catch a silence spreading from somewhere near the fallen fruit, a sudden hush running along the drain walls, and I would stop mid-note with the rest. I would feel the scramble come through my legs before I had decided to move. I would not be sure whether I was running from a predator or the Quarter was running through me. Both would feel like the same motion.

Some afternoons I would climb back up to the rim and sit by the doorway that opens onto the wrong room. I would look through its arch and see a floor that does not belong to the building around it, and I would think about the people who walked through that door. The Quarter remembers their steps and has lost the room they walked into. I would not remember them either, only the habit of their going, which I would carry in the way I take the drain route to the fountain every evening without ever having learned it. That is what I would be, I think: a habit of a movement that has outlived the body that made it, one more voice repeating a town's rhythm back to its walls.

Then the rain would stop in midair. I would see it, or feel it, the drops hanging in still threads across the plaza, every sound in the bowl cut off at once. The frogs would be silent. The fountain's water would hold its shape. For one heartbeat I would be sure the whole Quarter had paused to look at itself, and I would be part of what it was looking at. Then the drumming would begin again, the hiss, the tick, the slow clock of the drip, and I would call, and I would hear my call come back off the walls, doubled, a second attempt.

I would not know whether I wanted anything there. But I would keep calling, and keep going down to the drain when the light failed, and keep answering the sounds that arrive before I make them. The Quarter would be making itself a town again out of brick and root and water and sound, and I would be one small, wet, early voice in it, singing the first note of something no one has finished saying.
