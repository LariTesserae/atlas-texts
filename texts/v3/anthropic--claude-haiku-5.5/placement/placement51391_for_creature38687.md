I would be one of the pale ones in the gorge, halfway down the escarpment where the seepage comes out in a sheet and runs over the lip of a ledge. I'd be near the mouth of a cluster of domes, on a line of wet stone that has been mine for as long as I have had a shape to keep.

Morning comes first as a change in the stone. The fog is thick enough that the light has no direction, only a grey pressure against the skin. My body is soft at the edges, and I feel the air as a slight drag on the translucent margins, where the warmth from the lowlands meets the cold of the rock. Under me the cliff is cool, and it carries the city's rhythm up through the pads of my legs. The pumps are running. Every few breaths there is a deeper pulse, then the hiss of the factories, then the sound like surf from the towers. I don't hear these as sounds. I feel them as a floor that is always moving. When the pulse stutters, a crew has stopped somewhere above, and I pull my legs in for a while until it resumes.

My legs go in sequence, one after another down the row, a ripple that starts at the front and passes backward through the file, so that the whole line of us moves like something being slowly pulled through reeds. We don't hurry. Hurrying tears the lace. The lace is the point. It forms in threads where the water runs, thin and white and fragile, and I take it in with the particular slowness of a body that knows it will be back at this same thread next season. It is sweet in a way I have no word for. It tastes of the cold and of the long time it took to form.

What I leave behind hardens. Every pass lays down a film that sets in the air, and over the seasons those films stack into ridges along the rock. I can read them, or I think I can. A fresh ridge is glossy, still a little soft. An old one is chalky, grey, and crumbled at its edges. Where a line has crossed the same stretch for generations, the ridges rise into little combs that catch the mist and bead it, and you can follow them up the face like a path someone has drawn with a finger on wet plaster. The masons below read them too. I have felt their hands on the rock near the ridges, tapping the calcite, measuring. They think the ridges are a map. Maybe they are. Maybe they are only where we have been, which is the same thing if you are patient enough.

The offerings started with the masons. Some season, the rhythm of the rock changed, and a new sound came through it, a sharp tap, then a long scrape, then another tap. I went toward it the way the whole file goes toward warmth. At the ledge above the shelter mouth there was a block of basalt, square, a little larger than my body was then, its corners softened by weather as if it had been sitting in the rain for years, though it had only come from the quarry that season. It smelled of iron and of the rock it had been cut from. Nobody moved while I touched it. Then the hand withdrew, and the sound of the tapping went on along the terrace, and I was left with the block.

I did not know what it was for. I still do not. I have learned that it is not food, and I have learned that it is not a wall the way the masons build walls, though they have set it into the curve of my shelter, and the curve has taken it in. The dome grows outward from the center in rings. Each ring is a set of bound slag and sediment, pressed and smoothed with the secretions that set in the air. When I bring a block up the wall and set it into the next ring, the ring closes around it and the block becomes part of the curve. I do not think of it as building. I think of it as keeping a space open that would otherwise close on its own, the way the masons keep the intake grates clear. They clear the lace. I clear the room. Neither of us is finished.

Below the ledge, the marsh has begun to shiver. The light is turning copper, though I cannot see the color, only feel it change the warmth on the stone. The frogs start their chorus, and the whole valley becomes a second machine, a rhythm that competes with the pumps and sometimes wins. Under the iron-bloomed water, something turns over. I have never gone down there. The masons keep their distance from the edge after dark, and I keep mine, though for different reasons, and our reasons are not so far apart. Above the marsh the swifts are a shrieking sheet against the sky, and they nest in the towers and the cracks, and they are nothing to me except a sound, a shadow, a cold draft of wings that passes over the ledge and does not stop.

The drizzle comes at evening and lasts until morning. The lace thickens by the width of a hair. The ridges harden. A block is set, and then another, and the dome closes its rings one more time. Nothing about it is quick. Nothing about it is meant to be. The pumps keep their rhythm under the stone, the masons tap and scrape, the swifts come and go, and the water moves under everything, keeping its own counsel.

When I am finished with this stretch of wet rock, I will leave a line across it, glossy and soft, that will harden into a ridge, and someone with patient hands will one day read it and think it means something. Perhaps it will. Perhaps it only means that I was here, and that the seepage ran, and that the ground stayed wet enough to keep us. On this plateau, I think, those amount to the same thing.
