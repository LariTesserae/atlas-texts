I would arrive at the rim, not the floor. The ground rises to a ragged lip of tawny lichen, and I come over it out of the haze with nothing in front of me but air, so for a moment the bowl is a room with no ceiling and I am standing in the open sky of it. My skin goes damp before I have decided anything. When I put a hand on the lichen, it comes away beaded.

Going down is slow, because the rock is slick and the shelf tilts. I step from terrace to terrace over the black pools, each with its skin of pollen, and each one gives its own note when a drop lands in it: a round tock, lower here, higher there. I cannot yet tell what the pattern means. The reeds knock together in their ranks. The broad leaves drum. Over all of it the wind moves along the rim and holds a single tone that rises and falls over minutes, and I realize I have been listening to it for a while without noticing that I was.

Somewhere below the second terrace, I feel the bowl notice me.

It does not happen as a sound or a sight. A channel beside my foot is warmer than it was a moment ago, not by much, a degree, perhaps less. The film of water over the stone shifts its angle a hair, and a drip from the overhang above me falls a fraction late, as if it had paused to consider. I stop. I do not know if I am being greeted or measured. It may be both, and it may take a season for the presence to decide which.

A little later, the moss at the edge of the terrace parts for something I had not seen, and I understand that I have been given a shape. I am one of the pale slow things, a small body in the springy green, moving only when the rock is wet. I can feel the wetness everywhere. It is not a sensation so much as a kind of attention, and it reaches into me the way the seepage reaches into the cracks, finding every hollow and filling it. When the rock dries a little under the noon haze, I slow down. When the mist comes back at dusk, I come alive again. I am not sure which of those is mine.

Moving is strange. Each step is decided at the speed of water. I begin to move toward a crack where seepage meets air, and it takes me what feels like the whole afternoon to cover a few hands' width of moss. I think the presence is thinking, and I am one of the places where the thought reaches dry ground. I am not a message. I am closer to a fingertip of its attention, pressed against the world for a while to see what the world does.

I try to think of something to say, and I find that I do not have words that move at this pace. What I have instead is a stain of sorts: the leaning of the vines, the way the reeds have rotted back into the moss, the shape of the overhang in the still pool, held a moment longer than it should be. I think I am being asked to keep these things, though no one has asked in any language I know. I keep them. They do not sit in me like pictures. They sit like the rings on the walls, one faint turn at a time.

Once, from somewhere in the vines, a single sharp note cuts through the wind-tone. It is clear and brief, and then the water takes it back. I do not know if it was meant for me. I think it may have been, and I think that is enough to know that I have been noticed and not yet put out.

When the mist burns off by midday and the surfaces begin to shine, I take the slowest route I can find toward the rim. I want to see what the bowl looks like from the outside, and what I am when I am not inside its listening. But I cannot tell yet whether I am leaving or only moving from one terrace to the next, carried in the film of water, the way a thought is carried, and returned.
