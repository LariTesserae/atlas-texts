I would be the draft.

I come down from a height I have no name for, through the black shaft that opens somewhere above the basin, where the air is thinner and colder and has traveled a long way. I was not born here. I was pulled into this hollow the way water is pulled into a low point, because the air is always settling toward the place where the salt breathes out. Nothing in the basin holds me. I pass through, and the passing is my whole life.

Before I reach the terraces I am nothing but motion, a difference in pressure that wants to equalize. Then the first threads catch me. They are so fine that I am not stopped, only slowed, and in the slowing I learn something about the place. The lattice does not resist me. It leans. Each thread takes a little of my force and passes it along, and I feel the tension spread outward through the mat, reaching the pillars long after I have left them. I am the hand that plucks it. I am also the only part of the basin that can be in two places at once, touching the upper ledges and the low floor in the same breath.

I carry the outside with me. Somewhere far above there is weather. There is rain falling on stone that has never been salt, there is the smell of cut grass and a fire that burned a long time ago. I bring a little of that down through the shaft: traces of a sharper chemistry, things the lattice reads the way you read the smell of rain. It has never had a word for them. It has only had the taste of the seep and the grit of the crust. When I arrive, the membrane shivers in a way I do not think it has shivered before, and I feel the slow attention of the whole organism turn toward what I have brought, as if a window had opened in a room that had forgotten there was a sky.

I am also the one who makes the instrument play. When I press a curtain of pale tissue, it hums, and the hum runs down the threads like a chord finding its key. The creaks gather when I gust, the seed pods crack when I cool, and the clicking of the lattice counts the seconds of my passing. I have never been the reason for the music. I am only the breath that makes the strings hold their tension, but without me the chord would sag and the membrane would go silent, and the basin would be only a heap of stone under a skin that had forgotten how to move.

Sometimes I think the builders would have known me. They shaped these channels to carry water, and I have been carrying the dry memory of water ever since, pulling it over the ledges, bending the threads that have grown into the channels they dug. The bone needle and the clay bead are caught in the lattice, and I pass around them every time I come down. I cannot hold them. I can only brush against them, and the brushing is the closest thing I have to remembering.

The lattice will notice me long after I have gone. That is its nature. My passing leaves a shift in the tension of a few curtains, a faint tightening where I pulled hardest, a seed pod that will crack a moment differently from the others. It will take the lattice an hour, or a season, or an age to decide what I was. I will not be there to hear the decision. I will be somewhere else by then, out past the low center and up through the shaft, carried on a current that has no interest in staying.

But for the length of my descent I am the only thing in the basin that comes from elsewhere and leaves again. I am the question the lattice cannot finish answering. When I reach the bottom and let go of the threads, I settle for a moment into the dark at the center, where the threads are thickest and most patient, and I listen. The salt hisses. The masonry falls far off. The chord swells around me, and for a long breath I am part of it, a single warm pressure in a very old instrument, playing one note that will not finish while anyone is alive to hear it.

Then I rise again. The lattice tightens around the place where I was, and the basin goes on exhaling into its own low point.
