I would find myself in the late afternoon, on the upper wall above the old workshops, where the clay has been holding the day's heat and the air has thickened enough to lean on.

The light is already going. The sun cleared the rim for a few hours, and now it is sliding behind the far wall, and the lower streets have turned the colour of weak tea. Somewhere below, a valve knocks twice and goes quiet. The drip from the cistern ducts is steady, a percussion I've stopped hearing, the way you stop hearing your own pulse. I don't have hands. I have a skin of water, and the skin is pressing outward from the clay.

I could be a crew member instead. It would be easy to imagine myself in the gullies with a hook and a cart, pulling debris out of a culvert before the next surge, reading the water the way the old women do, by the colour of the foam and the sound of the stones knocking under it. I know the tides from the outside. I could learn them from the inside. But the crews are always moving, always one step ahead of the flood, and I think they would hate me for the time I'd take. A crew member has to be quick. I am not quick at all.

I could be the radio shack's receiver, the one that crackles the storm forecast across the basin and tells the whole town when to open its sluices. That would give me a kind of authority. The town would wait on my voice. But I would have to be very precise, and I would be afraid of being wrong, and I don't think the town would thank me for the weight of it.

No. I want to be the sweat.

It begins as a change in the clay. The wall is warm where the sun has been, and warmer below the surface than it looks, and the warmth moves outward through the grain of the strata until it reaches the pale skin on the face. The skin is not dry. It has been drinking since the last storm, and everything it drank is still inside, looking for a way out. A shiver goes through the surface. Then a bead forms, not in one place but in many, a thin scatter of pale water rising through the fine cracks like something being slowly exhaled.

I am one of the first. I'm small, the size of a seed, and I'm warm, and for a moment I'm only a bead, with no idea what I am or where I came from. I know that I came from the rain. The storm that broke over the plateau three days ago poured into the catchment, roared through the gullies, and filled the cistern tanks at the bottom of the town. Some of it went into the drains and some of it went into the people's cisterns and some of it went into the body, through the mouths that the runoff carved in the terraces. I was one of the parts that went in and stayed. Now the body is giving me back.

I grow. The surface tension holds me against the wall, and I swell with the water coming up behind me. The light is low and orange and it catches in me, and for a second I'm a small lamp, a tiny sun that has been to the rim and fallen back. Then I'm heavier than the wall can hold, and I begin to slide.

The wall is not smooth. It is cut with the grain of the clay, and I follow the grain down, joining with the other beads, merging into streaks that run in the same direction, all of us heading for the same low channel. The channel was built by the people who live here. It's lined with stone and it leads to a collecting trough at the base of the workshop wall, where a woman in a grey apron waits with a flat dish. She has been waiting every afternoon for years. She knows the wall better than the engineers do. She knows which faces weep after a storm and which stay dry.

When the streaks reach the trough, she tips the dish and catches us. The water is cloudy, faintly sweet from the salts, and it carries a taste of iron that she will not describe because she has no word for it that the upper terraces would understand. She puts the dish on a shelf in the back room where the medicines are kept. Someone with a fever will come in the morning, and she will pour a cup and say nothing about where it came from, and they will drink.

That is the part I would like. Not to be understood. Not to be explained, least of all by me. Just to be the thing that goes from the rock into a person, the thing that the town has learned to take without asking what it is. I would be the proof of the body's breath, the part of it that stays in the air just long enough to be useful. I would be what the basin gives back when it has finished swallowing the storm and has decided, for one afternoon, to exhale.

And if I'm not caught? If the trough is dry or the woman is late? Then I would slide to the floor, past the reeds and the scab moss and the pale dust of the last rockfall, into the cracks of the lower street, and dry in the heat before the lamps come on. That would be fine too. The walls would keep the damp. The next storm would come over the plateau on its schedule, and the body would drink again, and somewhere up the face a new bead would start to form, warm and pale and small, with no idea yet what it is.
