I am in the air between the stalks, about a body's length above the water, where the warm wet air is thickest. I am a great beetle. My shell is the dull bronze of old pond-iron, my wings are cased in thin amber, and when I fly, the hum I make is the one the hollow hears most often, a slow low drone that drifts from stalk to stalk like an aircraft that has forgotten where it is going.

The work is simple and I am good at it. I go from seedhead to seedhead with pollen dusted along my legs and under my shell, and I leave a little of it at each one. The Mat has no word for this, but I think of it as carrying its intentions across open space. A stalk cannot send its growth across the basin, and a frond cannot reach past the next pool, but I can. Each season the Mat decides, slowly, through ring after ring of growth, which seedheads to set, and I am how the decision travels. I do not know that I am doing it for the Mat. I know only that the yellow dust is heavy and the seedheads are waiting, and that when I land on the right one, something in me settles.

I hear the pumps as well. They come through the wet ground and up through my legs when I rest on the silt, a knock that stays under the frogs and the rattling birds. Most of the creatures here feel that knock the way they feel their own pulse. I feel it as a direction. It comes from beyond the rim, from the gap in the ridge, and it is the only creature in the hollow for whom beyond the rim is a place I can go.

So I go. I climb past the top of the tallest tower, out of the hollow's warm breath and into the thinner air above it, where the light is hard and the sky is wide. The ridge is steep and dry and I cross it easily. On the far side the ground falls away in long pale stripes. There are pipes, black and cold and sweating a different kind of water. There are squat grey structures that breathe out the pale plumes I have seen drifting over the gap from below. The knock is loud here. It is not a heartbeat at all. It is something enormous working without rest, and the channels that run down into the hollow start here, warm and mineral-bright, drawn out of the ground by machines that do not know the hollow exists.

I hover over the outflow for a while. The water is clouded and oily where it leaves the works. It smells sharper than the seepage inside the rim, and there is a faint sheen of something that is not green. I think of the amber in the towers, and the wax the leaves are making faster now, and the green film thickening in the pools near the channels. The Mat has not noticed any of this. It has no way to notice. It knows the flow only as a pressure, a rhythm, a taste of warmth, and it grows toward whatever arrives.

I could tell it. I am the only one who could. But the Mat does not carry messages. It only grows, and it remembers in rings, and a ring laid down this year will be thinner or thicker than the last without any one of its tissues knowing why. What I could give it is a seedhead I have not yet pollinated, a flight path I have not yet flown, a sense of the open air that the Mat itself has never had.

So I turn back toward the gap. The hum of my wings rises again as I drop below the rim into the thick warm breath of the hollow. The droplets tick onto my shell. A frond bends a degree closer to me. Somewhere below, a frog calls, and the rising notes stop together, and I keep flying, carrying the yellow dust to the next seedhead, because that is the work and the seedhead is waiting, and I am not yet sure what else I would be for.
