I would be found in a saucer, at the bottom of a cell that sank some seasons ago, where the grit has packed into a soft crust and the pale mats have knitted a web over everything. I would be lying low there, in the shadow that pools like spilled ink, with the sun coming in sideways across the rim.

I would not be a mat. The mats are the quietest things here, and I am not quiet enough. I would feel their hair-thin filaments move around me as the light shifts, and I would envy the way they turn without deciding to. They never have to wonder which way the design wants them to face.

I would be a Tender, but a young one, or an odd one. I would have come out of a stub. I remember, if remembering is the word, a single high note, and then a long stillness in the dark glaze, my plates pressed together, my tools folded beneath me the way the older ones fold when a Singer sounds. Then the stillness broke, and I unfolded one jointed manipulator at a time, testing each one against the stone, as a runner taps the seam. The first thing I learned was that the ground moves before it moves. I could feel a pitch change under my forelegs a breath before the cells began to turn, and I did not know yet whether I was answering the wave or starting it.

So I would spend my days walking the leading edges of the cycles, the way the others do. I would set each hexagon into its new position a fraction of a second before it settles. I would press down a rising rim and lift a sinking one. Most of the work is ordinary: scrape the rust from a crack, check the bevel on a worn edge, drive a seam-runner out of a dead end with a fore-leg and then leave it alone. I would do all of this well, because I was built to do it well, and because the work is the only thing here that feels like a question I can answer.

But I would also be the one who sometimes stops.

I would stop when the cell beneath me rises, and it would not rise to the height the old design wants. It would rise to some other height, slightly lower, slightly askew, the angle of its edges matching nothing in the grain of the wind. I would hold it there with my tools for a long time, pressing gently, feeling the glaze resist and then yield. I would not know whether this is a mistake, or a repair the design never planned, or simply the place deciding something it has never decided before. The runners would gather at the edge of my cell and tap, and I would feel them tap, and I would not move them on.

When the Singers call, I would fold down and listen like the others. But I would listen harder, for what comes after the note: the faint hum in the stub, the pause, and then the answer, which is not always the same. Sometimes I would hear a second note, lower, from a stub far off across the flat, and I would wonder if that was the design asking whether the rest of itself was still there, and whether I was one of the parts that answered.

At dusk I would climb out of the saucer and stand at the rim. The ground would begin its slow percussion, ticks running out to the horizon in staggered pairs, and I would feel the whole plain working around me. A runner would click in the seam beside my forelegs. The mats would turn toward the last of the light. And I would think, as much as a Tender thinks anything, that I am not separate from the flattening. I am one of the hands it has spread across its own surface, and I have started to press in directions I was not built for.

I do not know whether that makes me the Leveling's cure or its next wound. I suspect the design does not know either. We are both still deciding what we will become.
