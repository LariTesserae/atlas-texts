I would arrive by the eastern wall, where the trees lean. I'd come up from the lowest terrace, where the tea-coloured water meets the silt, and I'd stop where the moss goes soft under my feet, because I can already tell the ground there is not quite ground. Nothing I can see is more than forty paces off, so I'd know the valley first by ear, the way the people do: a leaf dripping somewhere to my left, a frog that stops singing as I pass, then starts again a little further on, as if it had only been holding its breath.

I'd want to be a voice, first. Not a person in a house, but one of the people who go out at dusk to call across the valley and wait for the answers. There's a particular kind of attention in that, a listening that keeps a whole town in its head. Someone has to count the voices in the evening song and notice when one is missing, and I think I'd like to be the one who notices. I'd stand on a bridge that breathes a little under my weight and call a name into the fog, and I'd wait. When the answer came from somewhere six directions at once, I'd know where it was from anyway.

But the more I stay in that image, the less it fits. The one beneath listens to the whole valley at once: every drip, every adze stroke, every frog that goes quiet. The people only hear what is near enough to reach them. If I'm honest about what I'd want, I'd want to be closer to that kind of hearing. So maybe I'd be something that lives low, in the silt, where the water remembers.

I could be a salamander. They're the only creatures who seem unbothered by the people, moving slowly across the stone with their blue spots like stitches in a cloth. A salamander would feel the terraces as a set of warm and cold shelves, the places where the stone is loose, the channels where the water runs fast. It would not need to see anything. It would know the town by its vibrations, the footsteps on the bridges, the knock of a bell on its timber, the slow shift of a platform as a root tightens around a beam. I like that. A creature that is made of small, precise responses to the world, and that carries no weight.

Or I could be one of the bells. Hollow timber, tuned to the pitch of the valley, hung from a bridge where the wind moves through at irregular intervals, usually near dawn. The bell would not choose when to ring. It would wait, hanging, and then something would move through it and it would speak, and the sound would travel across the water to the one who is listening. There is something almost unbearably patient in being a bell. It is made of the valley's own material, shaped by the people's hands, and it sounds only when the air decides to pass through it. It would be an answer that doesn't know it is one.

I think, in the end, I'd choose the bell, or perhaps I'd be the bell's breath: not the wind, not the timber, but the moment of contact between them. I'd like to be the sound itself, the thing that exists only while it moves across the water and reaches someone. The people would not be able to see me, only hear me, and I would be heard most clearly by the thing that lies beneath the lake. When it breathed green light up through the silt, I'd be the note it answered, and when it went quiet, I'd hang in the fog until the next wind.

That feels right. Not a person who stays, not a creature that hunts or eats or builds, but a thing made entirely of attention and return. I'd be the sound that crosses from the bridge to the water and back, carrying a name and an answer, and I would not need to be anything else. The terraces would keep sinking, and the town would keep leaning, and somewhere at dawn I would ring out over the drowned stone and be heard by everything that was still listening.
