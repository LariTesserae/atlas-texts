I wouldn't begin in the street. The current there is too strong, and the light is too thin to hold a shape. I would begin in the silt.

It's the grey drift in a doorway on the third terrace of the north housing, where the water slows and the sediment has built up to about knee height. The particles are fine and pale and have no direction of their own. Some are old brick dust, some are flakes of iron that came off a railing decades ago and have been recombining ever since, and some are dead growth, ground small by the tide. For a long time I am only the drift, and the drift is only moving when the water moves it.

Then something in the drift begins to hold together. It isn't a decision. It's more like how the mats lean toward the light: a tilt, a gathering, a slow tightening around some pattern I can't name. The iron takes on the orange of the bloom on the old pipework, and the dead growth gathers into a flat body, and I find I am a grazer.

I am flat and wide, and I can feel the current through the whole underside of my body, reading it the way a hand reads a surface in the dark. I rise when the water thins. The surface glow is coming in amber through the broken upper windows, and I drift up through the stairwell past the fronds, which lean with me as I pass, and I come out onto the upper floors where the mats are thick. The mats are velvet to the touch, or what passes for touch here: a pressure that means *this is mine to tend*. I drag my edge along them and the growth parts in a neat strip, and I can feel the stalks tearing free and drifting, and I know without being told that the strip will grow back in a season. I am not sure how I know. The knowing is simply in the body, the way the schedule is.

I crop as I go. The mats are trying to cover everything, the skylight frames, the sills, the openings where the light comes through, and if no one thinned them the whole floor would go dark. So I thin them. I am not sure I'm doing it on purpose. But when I shear a strip across a window, the light comes through brighter, and the brighter light makes the mat around it grow thicker and greener, and so the work feeds itself. I am a small part of a loop that keeps the town's upper stories open to the sky.

Sometimes I think about the long pale shape. It passes near the old quays, always too far to see clearly, and when it comes I feel the whole district lower itself. The fronds draw in. The fish turn. I settle down into the mats, and I find that I want to be low, out of its path, though I don't know if I'm afraid of it or simply recognize it as something larger than I am and older than the town's schedule. I'd like to ask it what it knows. But I don't have questions, not in the way the people who once worked here had questions. I have the tilt toward light and the drag of the edge and the slow, unhurried returning of strips to the mat.

At dusk the light goes out of the water in stages, from amber to grey-green to the faint near-dark where you can only make out outlines. This is when I settle. I find the violet mat near the skylight of the old finishing hall, where the glass has clouded to milk and only a little light gets through. I fold into the growth. The stalks close around me, the way the tube-colonies close their feathered tips, and the warmth of the mat, which is the warmth of the works' old outflow still leaking up through the floor, comes into me. Somewhere in my body the iron from that railing is slowly going into the bloom, becoming orange, becoming part of the color the water carries. I am not the iron, and I'm not the mat, and I'm not the silt that was there before. But I am where they meet, for one night, in the dark of a building the sea is learning to hold.
