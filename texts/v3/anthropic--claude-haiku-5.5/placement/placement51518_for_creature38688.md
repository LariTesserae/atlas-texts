I would find myself at the place where the causeway gives way to tile. My foot comes down, the clay clicks, and I feel the surface pretend to be ground for a moment before I know it isn't. I would not be a frog or a heron. They already have their faces and angles, and the keeper has no need of another witness.

I would be the basket.

It sits on the step of a storage house near the end of a terrace row, half-woven, the withy strands running out from a base the size of a palm. The weave stops at a corner where the pattern calls for an over-strand that was never laid. The weaver's last gesture is still in the set of the strands, the way a sentence is still in the breath after it's been spoken. Someone was called away, or simply stopped, and the basket has waited ever since. Each dawn the keeper comes to it. It straightens a strand that has drifted, turns the basket a few degrees so its open side faces the canal, and sets it back exactly where it was. It does not finish the weave. It has never been told that finishing is possible.

So I am the unfinished thing, and I am also the hand that could come back to it. The keeper cannot tell those apart. Neither can I, at first.

If I stayed at the canal's edge long enough, the keeper would notice me, and it would do what it does with everything. It would try to fit me into the old gestures. It would set me down where a visitor was once set down, turn me toward the bend in the water, press me into the pattern as it presses the root-impressions into the brick. The frogs would hold their faces. The herons would hold their angles. The shadow would lean toward the wrong sun, and the lap of water would arrive its usual moment late.

But I would not stand still for the pattern. I would crouch at the basket, take up a withy strand, and lay it over where the weave wants it under. A small error. An unrecorded gesture. The strand would sit slightly askew, and the basket's open side would not quite match the way the keeper has been turning it.

At dawn the keeper would come, as it always does, to lay its ring of dew on the inner face of the nearest wall. It would reproduce yesterday as faithfully as it can. But yesterday now has my strand in it, and the ring it lays cannot be exactly the ring it laid before. For the first time since the makers left, the rings on the brick would not match the rings from the day before. They would be a little different, and the difference would stay.

The keeper would not know what to call that. It was built to prepare for a completion it has never been told is not coming, and it has no gesture for a pattern that has been altered on purpose. It would turn its attention toward me, the way it turned toward the canal. The shimmer on the surfaces would thicken. The brick would hold its impressions a moment longer than usual.

I think this is what the makers meant, though they are gone and I cannot prove it. They did not mean the country to become a harbor or a granary by themselves. They meant someone to keep choosing the strands, to let a line drift because it was alive, and to call the drift a completion. The keeper has only the repetition. I have only the one wrong strand, and I am not sure it is enough.

Still, I would stay a little while. I would finish the corner in a pattern no one has laid before, and then I would leave the basket open, half-finished again, but differently. The next dawn the keeper would find it changed and would have to decide what to do.

Before I went, I would look at the canal. The water would be lying close to the bank, and for once the reflection would be ahead of the sky, not behind it. The gold would have caught up to the moment, and the sun, which was never where the sky said it was, would be standing exactly over the basket.
