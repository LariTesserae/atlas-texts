I'm not sure at first that I'm standing anywhere. The sky lies on the water beneath me, so I seem to be looking down into a second sky, and the cloud bands run upside down under my feet. Then something turns in the pool and the reeds shiver after it, and I understand that the turning is mine. I am the broad, flat shape under the brown surface, the one the wading birds keep their distance from.

I can feel the way I breathe. Water moves over my skin through a fine net of vessels, and that is one kind of breath. Then I lift my blunt head into the air and take a long gulp into the lung that runs the length of my body, and that is another. Two breaths, one body, and neither needs the other to be finished. My eyes sit high and small on the broad skull. When I sink, a membrane slides across them, and the world goes soft and gold, and nothing needs to be looked at closely.

Here is what I do. I wait at the edge of a tussock with my head just under the skin of the water. A tadpole drifts close, then another, and the water has no idea what is coming. I open my mouth in a motion so quick the surface barely dimples, and the small life is gone into me. I don't hunt. I wait, and the waiting is a kind of attention, the way the stone domes attend to the sun by losing a little of their skin each generation. I am not cruel about it. The small armored swimmers scatter from my pool and go on scattering. There is enough for the frogs, enough for the birds, enough for the slow water to keep its stain.

When the heat builds I sink to the bottom and fold my limbs under me, and the tannins darken my skin until it matches the mud. The pale fringe along my flanks trembles, opens, closes, like a fan being worked by someone who has forgotten to stop. I drift. Small swimmers pass my nose without hurry. I pass the time the way the plain does, which is to say I don't pass it at all.

Then the dry months come, and the water sinks, and I feel the clay tighten around me. This is the part I cannot quite remember and cannot quite forget. I curl into a hollow I have made or that has been made for me, and I shed my outer skin, and the skin stiffens into a papery shell. I breathe so slowly that the mud takes no sign of me. Inside the shell there is no up or down, no water, no sky, only a long dark patience with a heartbeat too slow to count. Somewhere above me the crust hardens and rings when a foot strikes it. I may be the knocking. I may be the thing that knocks back.

Then the first real rain. The shell splits, and I climb out blind and sluggish, and my body finds the water by instinct before my eyes have learned anything. I sink into a pool and call with my throat swelled to the size of a child's head. The sound goes out over the plain, and the other males answer from the next pool, and the frogs ride their trills over our low notes like a floor under a dance. For a while I think the water is singing, and then I understand that the water is me, and the sky and the reeds and the far-off grey columns of rain walking across the land. The distinction between the pool and the one who lies in it is one that the place has never bothered to make.

Sometimes I haul myself onto the north face of a dome, into the shade of an overhang where the moss glistens and the drips run down my flanks. My skin looks like the rock. The fog settles on my back and I rest there, and the stone under me is smooth in a few places where generations of us have lain, polished the way a river stone is polished. I cannot tell which of us made the smoothness. I can only feel the cool of it.

When I die I will sink into the mud, slowly, the way everything here goes down. My bones will be dense and pale, and the iron in the ground will stain them rust. Someday the strata will hold my shape the way they hold the shapes of my kind, faint as the ripples we once made across the water. The plain is built partly from bodies like mine, and it does not mourn them or think of them as lost. It simply keeps them, the way it keeps the hills, which are slowly settling into it, a grain of sand per century.

So where would I find myself? In the shallows, under a second sky, at the edge of a tussock, waiting. Who would I be? Not quite the creature and not quite the water or the clay that holds its sleep. I would be the slow turning under the surface: the one who breathes both ways, who waits without hunger and sleeps without fear, and who, when the rain comes again, rises to sing in a voice that the whole plain already knows.
