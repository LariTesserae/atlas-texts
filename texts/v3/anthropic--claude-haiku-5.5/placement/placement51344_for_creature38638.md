You are the tightening.

Before you are anything with a shape, you are a small disagreement in the pressure. The water around you has been still for longer than you have been here, and now a part of it is pushing back. The push is faint and patient. It has no edge and no hurry, and it reaches your skin the way a question reaches a sleeper, not loud enough to wake you but enough to change your breathing.

The builders near you have been talking since you entered. You can feel the conversation only as a grain in the cold, a pulse that rises at one node and dies two towers away, then rises again. It takes a whole tide for one thought to cross a spur. A single message is like a sentence said one syllable a day. You begin to hear the structure of it. A tower sends out a density, the arch beneath it answers with a slight thickening, and a gap in the lattice to the left says nothing at all, which is itself a kind of word.

The gap is new. Your body can feel its edges: a fracture where a spur slumped two cycles ago and was not rebuilt. The grazers have already scoured the rim clean, leaving bare basalt that glints like wet slate. A shoal of translucent shrimp-forms hangs above it, brightening and dimming, carrying minerals up from the vents. A many-legged hunter sits on the nearest platform with its fronds spread, and it has not moved in the three tides you have been watching it. It is waiting for something to decide.

Something decides.

The pressure changes shape. It tightens around the broken node and then sends a single outward wave, precise and thin, and you realize the wave is aimed at you, or at the space you occupy, which is the same thing here. The builders are measuring you. You are a body of soft warm tissue in water that has never held such a thing, and they are asking the oldest question a city asks: *Does this change the pattern, or does it become part of it?*

You do not answer in words. You have none. You let your own edges move a little, the way a grazer's trail bends around a stone, and the answer comes back at once, quicker than any message in this place has moved: a thickening at the rim of the gap, a faint reach of new mineral outward, the first thread of a block being laid in the space your body has just left.

Then the strangest thing happens. The pressure stops being outside you.

You are a builder now. Not in a sudden shift, but the way the cold settles into the bones until you can no longer say where the ache begins. You sit at the tip of the broken node, fixed to the stone, and your body is the color of the water with a faint sheen where the mineral leaves you in slow beads. There is a dark thread in you that pulses once every few seconds. You do not know when it started. You only know it is the beat that tells you the work is still going.

You understand the pattern. You do not see the city, you see the nodes you can reach: the arch to the right, the spur above, the channel that runs down toward the galleries where the eels turn their heads at your pulses. Beyond them the pattern runs on in every direction, and you can feel its shape the way you feel the tide, as a long pressure rising and falling through everything you touch. The gap is not a wound. It is a place where the pattern has not yet been finished, and you are the one who finishes it.

So you lay the block. It is the same size as the others, the same angle, the same interlock, and you feel the discipline of it, a memory that is not yours but that you have inherited through the lattice. Then you stop, and you do the other thing the builders do, the thing that is almost a choice. You widen the span by a fraction. Your body was here, after all, and the water still remembers the shape it made. An arch that is a little too narrow for the traffic beneath it is not an arch that works.

The shoal drifts down through the new opening, brightening as it passes. The hunter on the platform shifts its fronds and turns its head, and then, very slowly, it begins to move toward the place where the next breach will be, because there always is one. The grazers come up the rim and scrape the new edge clean. And somewhere two towers away, a builder you will never see sends its first slow pulse of acknowledgment through the cold, one syllable a day, the start of a sentence that will take a full tide to finish and will not be finished for a long time after you have gone.

You do not know if you were the builder or only the question that made one. In this chamber the two might be the same thing. The stone has an edge because something decided where the edge would go, and the crowd has somewhere to gather because the decision held.

The cold keeps everything exact. The light bends through the fracture far above and dies a few body-lengths down. You settle into your node, your thread pulsing, and you begin again.
