I would be a shellback, one of the small, many-legged builders, somewhere on the lower terraces where the crust is still wet.

I wake in the wet hour before the sun clears the rim. The shell is cold and heavy on my back, a dome of banded calcite a little larger than my head. I can feel every layer of it: the thick ring I laid down in the dry season, the thin pale line from the flood, the knob of black grit where I patched a crack last month. It is my record, and it is also the only thing about me that will last. My legs are still soft. I have only been building for two seasons, and the shell is thin along the seam where I was careless once.

The flat I'm on is new. Last night's rain tore the rim of the terrace open, and by morning the water had dropped into a fresh channel and left a sheet of pale glassy crust behind. The smell is sharp, like struck flint and sulfur. The crust is warm already. Somewhere to my left a crust-grazer herd is working its way toward me, scraping with a sound like a blade on slate, and I feel their weight in the ground before I hear them. I move away from the sound. I have learned that the herds leave a trail of scoured stone, and that the stone is the best place to find grit.

I climb onto a loose cluster of old shells, pale spirals half-buried in silt. Some of them are my kin's, from colonies that died in the cold. They are not dead to me. Their calcite is the stuff I'm built from. I push a fragment with one foreleg and it clicks against the others, a dry rattle that runs through the drift and out across the flat, and a dozen of my cloud feel it and turn toward it. We gather. We don't speak, but we know the sound means loose stone, and loose stone means grit.

Then I find the cairn.

It sits where the flat meets the first step of the lower terrace, a waist-high pile of rough stones, cracked by heat, one face chipped sharp. Someone built it. I can smell it, not the way the stone smells but the way a person's hand does, the oil from skin and the grit from a hammer, faint through the sulfur. It has begun to sink. On its windward side a skin of rust-colored sediment has already crept up over the lowest stones, and in a season or two the whole thing will be a low swell in the crust, a rise I'll feel under my legs but won't be able to see.

I climb it anyway. The top stones are warm from the sun. I stop there and I feel the Hollow move under me. Water chuckles in a channel to the east. A vent sighs and throws a column of mist that hangs over the flat like a curtain. Above, the wing-spinners whine on the updraft, and their high thin note goes through my shell and into the stone.

I think about the people who built this, if I can think at all. They came down, carried stone up from the seeps, set it in lines, and walked away before the water came. I am the same kind of thing as them, in one sense: I build what the ground will bury. My shell will be a fossil in the crust. Their cairn will be a swell in the crust. Neither of us is meant to last. But the crust remembers both, and what the crust remembers becomes the next layer, and the next layer becomes the floor a new herd walks on.

By midday the sun has cooked the surface to a shine. I go back to work. I find a flake of grit by the herd trail, press it against the seam of my shell, and dribble over it the thin, sticky secretion that will harden in an hour. The new layer is lumpy and wrong, but it holds. Tomorrow the flat may be gone, or it may be a terrace with a rim, and the cairn may be under a skin of rust. I don't know which. I only know the shell on my back is a little heavier than it was this morning, and the Hollow is still deciding what it will become, and I am part of the deciding.
