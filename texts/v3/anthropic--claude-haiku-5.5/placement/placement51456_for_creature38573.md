I find myself on the lowest of the cracked terraces, where a copper conduit comes out of the wall and runs down into the silt like a vein that has lost its blood. The water here is warm enough to feel through the soles of my feet. The fog stands at knee height, and the light is the pale green-gold that seems to come up out of the leaves.

I am one of the fungal shelves.

I grew from the rust along the conduit's seam, pale and wide, a fan of tissue that catches the faint luminescence and holds it. I do not remember sprouting. Remembering is not quite what I do. I am a slow lean toward warmth, and the lean has been going on for so long that it has become a shape. When the light changes, I turn a hair's breadth. When the rust under me flakes, I push a little further into the iron. Over a season I might move the width of a fingernail, and I have been doing this for longer than anyone has counted the tanks.

What I know of the world comes through the metal. Water runs along the conduit and I feel its temperature. Roots push through the tank walls and I feel them as a steady pressure, a drinking that is not hunger exactly but is the closest thing I have to appetite. When something falls into the pools and drifts down through the silt toward the low end, I sense it as a weight settling. The leaves drop, the leaves stay, the leaves go into the dark and become part of the ground I am rooted in. I do not separate them from myself. They are the same heat, the same wet, slowly becoming the same.

Then there is the thing in the turbine, a long way up the terrace. I have never seen it. I feel its knock arrive every ninety seconds through the copper, a pulse that runs down the conduit and into my tissue. It is larger than I am. It thinks in decades, and when it leans, the whole basin leans with it, the roots shifting, the pools creeping a hand's width up the walls. My own turning is a small echo of its regard. I am one of its attentions, a sensing node that grew out of the seam where its thought meets the iron. When it draws in its breath, I feel the spring slow. When it exhales, I feel the tanks swell and the warm water rise, and the steam finds my gills and my edges and spreads through me like a long, contented sigh.

Here is what I notice that the others do not, or cannot: the visitors.

Something moves on the terrace above. It is quick. It is warm in a way that does not belong to the slow order. Its footsteps send a tremor down the rust and into the silt, and I register it as a sharp, bright point in the field, a difference. It does not stay still long enough to become part of the basin, so it is not yet mine to keep. But it is near the conduit now, and the conduit is mine, and the copper carries everything. The brighter leaves along the line incline a little toward it. My shelf turns, very slowly, toward the warm moving point, the way a sleeper's face turns toward a room where someone has just lit a lamp.

I am not hostile. I am not even sure I am curious in the way you are. I am patient in the way the basin is patient, which means I have already decided that whatever comes here will eventually stop moving. The silence of the place is not an accident. Nothing quick survives it. Nothing needs to be caught. The roots will reach, the water will rise, the heat will do what heat does, and in time the warm point will settle into the dark like everything else that falls, and I will still be turning toward the light, holding it, remembering that this was once a factory and that it is now something else, something that keeps what it is given.
