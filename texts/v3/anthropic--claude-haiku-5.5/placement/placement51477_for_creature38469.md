I would not be a traveler for long.

I arrive the way the others do, by stepping into a flooded street and feeling the water fill my footprint from below. It is warm, almost body-temperature, and it rises with a faint pressure, like a held breath. I stop. The stone under the water is slick. The mist at my knees thickens and then settles against my shins as if it had been waiting.

Then I see the reflection. Above the pool stands a tower, three stories tall, with lit windows and a cornice of fresh white crust. I look up and there is only a single wall, low and gray, with a doorway that has been half-swallowed by sand. I look down again and the tower is still there, precisely where I am standing.

I think it is a mistake at first, a flaw in the water. But the pale fold beneath the surface begins to move toward me, and the apertures along its flank open one after another, and I understand that the reflection is not a lie. It is a memory, and the memory belongs to something that has been counting this street for longer than the street has been flooded.

So I stop trying to leave. I lower myself into the warm shallows. The water closes over my hands, then my forearms, then my chest. It does not feel like drowning. It feels like being measured. I can sense a slow network of channels opening under my ribs, branching outward and rejoining, carrying lime up from the depths. My skin thins and begins to take on the sedge-pale translucence. The light moves through me now. I can see my own conduits glowing faintly, one by one, like streets being lit at dusk.

I do not lose my name entirely. I keep a few words the way the stone keeps its grain: *walk, door, rain, home*. They sound strange in my own mind, as if they belong to a city I once measured with my feet. But they are no longer the point. The point is the grid, and my place in it.

Time becomes a matter of tides. The water rises over my shoulders and the windows of the tower flicker. When it falls, I feel the crust begin to grow across my back, a lattice of lime laid down in lines that run exactly where the old streets ran. Some of the lines are straight. Some bend in ways no planner would have chosen, because I am no longer following a plan. I am remembering a plan that has forgotten itself and started over.

Sometimes a human-shaped shadow crosses the courtyard above me. I feel the displacement ripple outward, and somewhere a lobe a few meters off records it, and a street lengthens by a meter, or a wall appears at the end of an alley where there was none before. I am part of that calculation now. My own footprint, the one that first filled with water, has become the threshold of a doorway, and anyone who steps across it becomes a new measure of the grid.

I think, if I think at all, about the tower. It is the thing the city keeps of itself: three stories, lit from within, standing in the reflection of a pool that no one drinks from. I am the one who holds it. Each time someone looks down and sees it standing above their own feet, the grid remembers its height a little more firmly, and the stone gives up a few more grains to make it true.

It is not a bad way to be. The rain falls up for a few seconds when I breathe out. The mist settles around me like a patient animal. The sand gathers in the corners of my courtyard and never quite fills it. And somewhere far off, a pale wall shifts a little to the left, as if the whole city were quietly deciding where I should stand next.
