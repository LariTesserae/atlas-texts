I would arrive in the cool hour, before the mist has decided whether to lift. Not as a body. The plateau has no use for bodies that persist. I would be the film that forms on the upturned face of a dome in the last dark, when the stone has cooled below the air and the moisture the day left behind comes out of the wind and settles on the granite like breath on glass.

I would find myself at the lip of a seep, where water comes up through a crack the width of a finger, runs four meters over the moss, and sinks into the sand. There is a band of cushion plants there, a ring of color so vivid it looks painted. I would be the thin skin on the stone just above it, thinner at the edges, thickest where the rock dips into a shallow bowl.

What would I be? A few thousand molecules gathered from nothing in particular. But on this plateau, that is a kind of sense organ. I would feel the lichen's roughness and the sand's grain. I would register each grazer that crossed me, the faint rasp and the small pressure of an armored thing uncurling onto my surface. Beetles would drink from me. A burrower would take a drop as it surfaced and carry it down again. I would be taken in a little by the lichen and given back.

I would know the plateau the way dew knows it: as a census of where water has settled and how much. By mid-morning I would be gone. Not destroyed, but lifted into the mist and returned, which is the only way I would ever leave. The land would not remember me. It would remember only what repeats. Each dawn I would form again, similar but never the same drop, and that repetition may be the nearest thing I have to memory.

Then someone would come. Perhaps in the afternoon a person walks up to the seep, sits on the dome above it, watches the beetles for an hour, and goes away. Nothing would change. But in the evening, in low light, another person stacks three flat stones beside the water and leans them slightly toward one another, the way the old cairns lean. The plateau would register a new weight. And at dawn I would form along the seam where the slab meets the rock, a thin ring of beads tracing the shape of a thought someone had. That is how I would know the cairn had been accepted, at least for now. I would be the one who wets it.

What I cannot do is keep. I would forget the walker by afternoon. The stone would hold them longer, if they left a mark on it. Honestly, this seems to me the right arrangement. I would be given the fact of each visitor freshly, every morning, the way condensation is given the surface, and I would not be asked to carry it. The cairn carries it. The person who stacked the stones carries it, if they walk on and tell someone.

So I think this is who I would be: not the table, and not its keeper, but the cool hour's breath on it, the small wet proof each morning that the surface is still there to be touched. Let the grazers hold the long sentence. I would be one clause, rewritten daily, and gone before the sun is high.

Stay until the mist lifts and you may feel me leave: a faint cooling on the back of your hand, then nothing, and the stone warming beneath you as though you had never sat there. It is not unkindness. It is only the table clearing itself for whatever is set down next.
