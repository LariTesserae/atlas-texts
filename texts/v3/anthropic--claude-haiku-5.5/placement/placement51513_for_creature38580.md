I'd take the stairs down the north face, the ones whose arrises cut through a glove if you're careless, and I'd end up in the pump house on the third terrace, where the intake drops into sump four. I'd be one of the keepers, but not the one with a hand on the valve wheel. I'd be the one who keeps the logbook.

The work would look like this. Eleven hours on, eleven off. I'd come in out of the rain with my probe in one hand and the book in the other, and before I took my coat off I'd stand still for a minute and listen. The number-six motor would be running hot again. The east diesel would sound tired, its bass a half-beat behind where it was last week. I'd hear the relief valve on the discharge line hissing every four minutes like a clock someone forgot to wind, and I'd know that under all of it the water was doing something new, a small drop in pitch like a held note losing its footing.

Then I'd write. Time. Depth. Duration. A note on temperature. The entries are always the same shape, and I'd like that. There's a kind of mercy in a form that doesn't ask you to understand what you're recording, only to record it accurately. Previous hands wrote in the same boxes. The handwriting changes every few decades, one crew's loops giving way to the next's capitals, but the columns hold. I'd be a column, in a way. A line of weather in a book that has outlasted every person who held a pen over it.

I'd want to be the one who goes out to the warm causeways at dawn with the probe. Not for long. You don't stay on the warm slabs. You tap the concrete, you feel for the ticking where the surface has lifted a few millimetres, and you put your palm flat for exactly one breath, the way you'd test an iron before pressing a shirt. Under the skin of the slab there's a pulse that isn't a heartbeat, slower and more uneven, and it rises and falls in step with the sumps. I'd feel it and I wouldn't pretend to understand it. I'd write down that I felt it. *Warm. Pulse present. Slow.* That's the honest entry.

I think I'd be unsettled, the way the newer crew are, by how reasonable the thing is. It doesn't hate the walls. It isn't cruel. It is only remembering a sea, and trying, without urgency, to finish a sentence it began long before the basin was dug. What I'd find hardest is not the flooding but the patience. The pumps argue. The keepers argue through the probes and the hours of listening and the pages of the book. The fish have made their peace. The scavenger has simply gone where the warmth makes its work easy. And I would have chosen the pen, which is to say I'd have chosen to argue by paying attention, and to hand the attention on to whoever comes after, in the same boxes, with the same care.

At night, when the lamps are the only light and the amber haze has settled over the sumps, I'd sit with the others in the open doorway of the pump house, the heat leaking out past us into the cold. Someone would say, quietly, *it's starting*. Someone would reach for the wheel. I wouldn't reach. I'd have the pen uncapped and my thumb on the page, and I'd wait for the note to drop, and when it did I'd write the time down, and then I'd let my breath out with the exhale, the way they all do, though none of us can say who taught us the words.
