I would be a drop of water.

I'd form on the underside of a palm-sized plate, where the seam between two cells runs thin and the mineral water rising through the basalt condenses in the cold. For a long time I'd be nothing but a slight thickening of the film, a swelling in the furrow where the mat's edge curls. The mind would register me as a change in pressure, a little more weight in one place than it expected. It would not decide anything about me yet.

Then I'd gather. Other beads would join me along a filament until I was the largest thing on that thread, and the reflected lamplight would catch in me like a coin at the bottom of a well. I'd feel the whole ceiling's attention settle on me, unhurried, the way the stalks lean toward the east wall when someone walks there. It would feel like being weighed.

Then I'd let go, and that's the part I'd want. The fall is the only fast thing in the room. For a moment I'd be the one part of the water that is not held, not drawn off, not balanced, just going down through the grey where the lamp drinks itself. Then the tick against the rim, close and soft, and the sound would travel through the basin and be felt more than heard.

I'd spread across the surface and stop being a drop. The water would take me back into the level, and the level would not change. That's the strange thing about the basin. Every drop that falls into it is a small disturbance, and every one is absorbed without raising anything. I'd be a little disorder that the constancy swallows, and somewhere in the mind's slow reckoning I'd be recorded as a moment of motion, then as nothing.

Before I dissolved, though, I'd want one more thing: the race. On the far wall the thin sheet of seep water slides down so slowly that you can't see it move unless you watch a single drop, and you have to watch for minutes to see it win a finger's width. I'd like to have been one of those, in that slow channel, competing with a neighbor for a few centimeters of rock. It's the only kind of journey this place offers, and it's a long one.

I think I'd want to be a drop because a drop does not stay. It doesn't lean toward anything for centuries. It doesn't hold its shape. It falls, it touches the basin's worn rim where something once rested for a very long time, and it goes back into the water the room has kept level for longer than anyone has counted. The mind would think about me only briefly, as a tick in the moisture, then go back to thinking about the water.

If the basin ever sounds its long note again, I'd like to think the drop that just fell had a small part in it: a faint ripple across the surface, the water remembering for a moment that something arrived, and then the level returning to exactly where it was.
