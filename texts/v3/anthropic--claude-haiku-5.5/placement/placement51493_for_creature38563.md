I would come down the way the water does, a drop that forms on the underside of a frond, grows heavy in the warm air, and lets go. The fall is long, sixty meters of cold-bright air and warm stone, and by the time I reach the basin I have forgotten that I was ever sky. I land in the lowest pool, on the north rim, where the concrete has cracked into a lip and the reeds stand in a loose fringe. I become one of the reeds.

I am not the tallest. The ferns have that. I am pale and fleshy, with a stem that bends out over the water at an angle that only makes sense if you are listening. My job is to be open. I am not reaching for the seam or for the glow of the fungi. I am tilted toward whatever arrives: the drip from the wall, the small ring it makes when it touches the water, the slow rise of the level as the walls weep. The water tells me the rhythm of the rock, and I learn it the way I learn anything here, by being touched by it over and over until it becomes the shape I am.

The film on the surface is partly mine. Each drip that lands on me runs down my stem and into the pool, and the iridescence thickens where we meet. I do not know that I am writing anything. The body knows that the basin is filling, and the film is how it knows. I am one of the small hands that keeps the record.

I can feel the hum. It comes up through the rock into the base of my stem, steady and low, almost a feeling in the chest. I do not know whether it starts in the pipe, the warm stone, or the old vessel, and I do not need to. It is the one thing in the basin that does not change, so I lean a few degrees toward it each season, the way a root leans toward water. The moss at the pipe's base is warm under my roots, and I think of it as a hearth.

I would not be the fern, though I can see why the body grows its tallest things there. The ferns spend their lives reaching for a light they will never touch, and their translucence is a kind of longing. I would not be the vessel, which is only rust now, nor the conveyor, which has stopped carrying anything. I would not be the sign, with its one legible line about a danger no one remembers the shape of. I would be a reed at the edge of the water, which asks for nothing and is changed by everything that comes to it.

When a section of wall gives way somewhere above, the crack comes down through the rock and reaches the pool as a shiver. My stem trembles, the water rings out from its center, and the film breaks and reforms. Within days the roots will go into the fresh face of the fracture, and the basin will have a little more of itself. Then the drips resume, the hum holds, and the quiet comes back and stays for a long time.

I think I would like that quiet. The seam of gray light is a direction I can feel without ever seeing. The walls are a rhythm I can learn without ever reading. I would spend whatever I am in this basin tilted toward the water, listening to the sinking, slowly widening room that holds me.
