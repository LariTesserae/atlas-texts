I come to myself on a thermal.

There is no ground under me. There is only the warm push of air rising off the vents, lifting me the way water lifts a leaf. My body is narrow and long, with a membrane stretched between limbs that are thinner than I'd expect. When I tilt, the membrane tightens and I correct without deciding to. I have no voice, and I don't need one.

From up here the bowl stops being a place and becomes a pattern. The pools lie in the hollows like coins of hammered metal, some yellow-rimmed, some rust, the green ones so clear they look like holes into something deeper. The lichen fields are laid out in rough grids, orange and violet, divided by low ridges of shell. Someone has been cutting back the growth, leaving bare chitin in neat patches, and I know whose work that is. The gullies run silver through the grey silt. At the center the vents breathe. Their steam rises in slow swells, the clouds they make bloom and drift outward toward the walls, and where they cool they darken and sink, and the rain begins somewhere up in that grey lid long before it reaches the floor.

I can see the rain forming before the frogs go quiet. I can see the pools shiver an hour early. I have always been able to see it, the way you know where your own hands are in the dark.

When I drop lower, the ground begins to speak. I can't hear the taps, but I can see their effect: the moss shivers in rings, spreading outward from a single point, then another point answers, and another, until the whole basin is a web of small tremors. I circle down over a wide basalt plate where two of the knappers are pressed against a new fracture, forelimbs spread flat, their chitin tips worn to a dull shine. They have been here for days. The rock edges are already softening under their weight, the crust creeping across the wound like a slow tide. I watch them for a long time. Nothing I do will help them, and nothing I do is needed. That is a comfort I don't have a word for.

Hunger comes, and I take it. The insect clouds rise off the pools in a shimmering column, and I dive into them, open my mouth, climb again. The taste is sulfur and iron, faintly sweet, and under it something rotten and bright that tells me the water is full of life.

Then the bones.

There is a split femur-shaped thing caught in the mud near the warm pools, cracked open at one end, the marrow long gone. I take it up in the curl of my forelimb and carry it. I don't know who I am carrying it for. Maybe for the knappers, who will come for it. Maybe for the silt, which will take it and turn it slowly into something the terraces can use. I climb until the thermal begins to thin, then I drift over the warmest pool, the one that stays milky all day, and I open my limb.

The bone falls. It turns slowly in the air, catching the light from the cloud, and enters the water with a small sound I can't hear from above but know. The surface shivers around it and closes. Within the hour a knapper will come to the edge with its forelimbs spread, and the water will take what's left, and over generations the minerals will build up another step in the terrace.

I have never been sure whether I choose these drops or only do what I was shaped to do. Here, I suspect, those are the same thing.

I ride the warm air over the rim. Below me the wind is beginning to cross the terraces, and the basalt starts its low sustained note, rising and falling like a finger drawn around the lip of a glass. I feel it through my membranes more than I hear it. The whole bowl is ringing, very softly, the way it has been ringing for longer than any of us could count, and I tilt into the current and let it carry me out over the grey, toward wherever the next fracture is opening.
