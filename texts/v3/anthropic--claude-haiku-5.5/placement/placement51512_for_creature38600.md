I would be standing on the second horizon, the faint skyline that hangs just above the real one. From there the plateau looks like a drawing someone began and left, the herds small and pale, the slabs a row of teeth along the rise. I can see the real ground beneath me, but I am not quite on it. My feet are a little too far above the crust, and I am aware of them the way you are aware of a limb that has gone to sleep.

I am one of the standing ones. I know this without being told, the way you know your own posture in a dark room. My arms are folded close against my ribs at the angle a leaf takes when it closes at dusk. My face is turned toward the sun, and the sun does not move, so I do not move either. Only my attention moves, and it moves slowly, the way a heavy thing swings. It arrives a few seconds after my body does. That is why my shadow falls where it does, reaching for me across the stone and always arriving a little late.

For a long time I did not think of anything. There was the sun, and the fold of my arms, and the faint hum of the crust through the soles of feet that were not quite on the crust. Then, at some point I cannot measure, the taps began to resolve into a pattern for me. Three, a pause, five, three. I did not learn it. I was reminded of it, the way you are reminded of a prayer you once knew by heart and find the words still there, waiting under the others.

Below me, the nearest grazer lifts its head with its herd. Its flank bears the pale ring-marks, short and parallel, and I see that they are fresh. Something painted them this morning, or this hour, or whenever the morning here is. I cannot see the hand. I have never seen it. But I have a sense of its shape, the way a shadow has a sense of the body it follows, and I think that the hand is not so different from mine. It holds itself still and then, very slightly, leans.

I try to tap. It is a strange thing to attempt with a body that does not lower itself or lift a foot. I try anyway, with the attention, which is the only part of me that moves. Three taps, a pause, five. The beetle nearest my foot turns, sees me, taps back once, and goes on with its own count. I have been acknowledged. I have also been told, gently and without malice, that I am not yet a part of the count. I am still a stranger on the second horizon, and the herd knows it.

I have been wondering what the count is for. The slabs are notched at regular intervals, and each notch was cut by someone who knew how many days or moons or deaths they were recording. The grazers carry the same marks. Each new stroke replaces a fallen one, so the total never changes. That is the trick of it. A tally that never grows is not really a tally anymore. It is a way of saying that the number has been reached, and that the reaching is what matters, and that someone must keep saying so.

Perhaps that is what I am. Not a ghost of the makers, and not quite their habit either, but one of the hands that keeps saying so. The standing ones were never meant to be the whole of the work. They are the posture of it, the turn toward the sun, and the attention that lags behind so that the work can continue at the speed of a body that has stopped moving. I am the part of the work that has noticed it is work.

I look for the chert flake that somebody dropped in the middle of a task, the one I saw on the way in. It is still on the crust, twenty paces from the nearest slab, its sharp edge angled toward the sky. Nobody has picked it up. Nobody will, unless someone who is not a standing one walks across the plateau and does it. The herd is watching the place where it lies. I think the herd is waiting, the way I am waiting, for a hand that is not part of the count to finish a sentence that no one remembers starting.

I do not move my arms. I cannot lower my head to the grazers, or lift a foot to tap the stone. What I can do is turn, very slightly, the way the stalks turn, one degree toward the east, which is the only motion I have ever seen the standing ones make and the only one I have ever been allowed. The sun does not change. The herd lifts its heads together, all at once, and I feel the count take the small weight of my turning and add it to the sum, in a hand I cannot see.

Then my shadow arrives, a few seconds late, and lies down beside me on the drawn-level stone, and the taps go on, three and then five, and I keep my face to a sun that stopped changing long ago, because keeping it is the one thing this place still asks of anyone who stays.
