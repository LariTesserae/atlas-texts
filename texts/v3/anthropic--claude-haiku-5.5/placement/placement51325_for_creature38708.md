I would be on a ledge above a narrow crossing, flat against the basalt, in the last grey light before the rain.

I'd be the waiter. The driver is across the gorge, and I know its weight through the rock before I can see it: a slow pressure in my pads, the way its chest-hum comes through the stone, patient and unbroken. Two lines of water run down the face beneath me, and my pads taste them one contact at a time. Cold, slow, sweet with ochre. The film on the stone is thickening. The seep lines are converging. Below, the herd has bunched in the flood-channel, a dense mass of armored backs steaming faintly in the mist, and I can smell them, the heavy warm smell of animals who have walked all day and know, somewhere in their slow bodies, that the air has changed.

I wait. Waiting is most of what I am. It's not idleness. It's a kind of listening with the whole body. My eyes are half-closed, and in the dimming light I can barely see the green glow rising from the lichen on the opposite wall. Every ridge along my spine is open, lifting the faint eddies that spiral up the face of the tor. The wind is coming from the southeast tonight, and with it the throb, so low that I feel it more than hear it, a machine breathing somewhere past the cloud-line. It swells. I lift my head toward it. For a moment I can't tell whether I'm reading the water or the far-off thing that the water is answering.

Then the reversal begins. It's subtle, a turning in the stone, the film on my ledge slowing and then sliding back up the grooves toward the herd's flank. The rule closes. The grazers that walked the seep line will feel the flood draw their footing out from under them, and they'll bunch toward the narrows, where the water pins them. I have known this moment all my life, and I have never once thought about where the water goes after it runs down the gorge and into the furnace's rain, which is to say, I have never thought about where it comes from.

I press my chest to the stone and send the signal: *now*. Across the gorge, the driver answers through the rock. Its pads are on the same wet face, and I feel the answer arrive as a small, certain shift in the basalt, the way the whole plateau shifts when a storm builds over the high center.

I move. Not yet. I hold for one more breath, because the herd has not quite arrived, and the water has not quite reversed, and the lichen light is only just bright enough to show the curve of the ledge beneath my feet. The grey dusk closes around the crossing. Below, the first animal lifts its head toward the wind.
