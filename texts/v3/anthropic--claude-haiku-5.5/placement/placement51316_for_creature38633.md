I would be one of the grazers, in the lower basin where the mud has gone green again.

I am heavy and slow, built low to the ground with a broad back that collects the rain and a mouth that works the moss in long, patient strokes. My hide is grooved in lines that follow the old seams of the terraces. I don't know that. I only know that the grooves are where the water runs off, and that I am comfortable where the water runs.

The herd spreads through the lower moss in the dark part of the cycle. We eat as we drift, and we do not look up much. There is always something to chew. But at the end of the dim, when the mud turns from black to grey, something in me wakes before the light does. My legs turn toward the gate.

I have never gone close to the gate on any other morning. It is the one place on the stair where the birds don't nest and the insects won't settle. Its iron is cold, even in the steam. Its seam is soft with moss, and in the seam is the long thing with the banded back, folded into the crack like a root. It is still. It is always still until the water is due. Then its wet ridge lifts, and its valve cap turns, and I feel the herd tighten.

We form a ring without deciding to. The old ones go in first, then the young, then the rest of us in a loose arc around the gate. We are close enough to smell the silt in the air and the mineral taste of the spray. We don't graze now. We stand with our heads down and our flanks rising and falling with the stair's breath, the slow push and pull of the big pump, which I can feel in my hooves before I can hear it.

The thing in the gap is climbing. I watch it drag itself up the girder. Its pale hand closes on the wheel. Nothing happens for a long time. Then the wheel gives a quarter-turn, and the whole stair answers. A grinding goes up through the metal, and somewhere far above, a gate shudders open.

The first water comes over the lip like a shoulder hitting us. It is cold and brown and full of things. Seeds, torn moss, small dead fish, the soft pale fruit of some plant that flowers all at once and folds itself shut. We go down the terraces behind it, our mouths in the silt, and the water pulls us along as if we were part of it. Behind us the predators come down with their too-many joints and their quick heads. Overhead the blind fish break the surface, snapping at seeds in silver bursts. The stair roars. The sound is in my teeth and under my ribs.

I do not know why the water comes. I do not know why it stops. But I know the moment it begins to thin, and I lift my head and look back toward the gate, and I see the thing in the gap has not joined the feeding. Its ridge is turned toward the falling flow. Its hand is on the wheel again, turning it the other way, slowly, the way it turned the first time. The water narrows. The roar drops to a rush, then a trickle. The gate settles.

Then it is quiet except for the pumps, and the drip of the ceiling, and the slow breath of the stair.

I stand in the drying silt with my belly full and my back wet, and I feel the old rhythm in my legs, open and hold and close, though no one has ever shown me. Above, the ledges are already filling with the hooked birds, and the insects are drifting back to the gate. The thing in the gap is still again, breathing with the big pump.

The mud will go green in a few days and dark again. The water will come again, when the wheel turns, when it is time. I will be here, in the ring, with my head down, waiting for the cold first rush over the lip of the stone.
