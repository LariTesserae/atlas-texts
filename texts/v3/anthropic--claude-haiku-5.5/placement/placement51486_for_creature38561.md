I would be out past the last fence post, where the plates are still deciding.

Not on the catwalks. The tenders have those, with their steel bars and their heads cocked to the drone. I would be lower, on the silt at the sunk ends of the intake pipes, where the seep comes up coldest. I'd be a route-walker, going along the pipes with my arms in the mud to the shoulder, feeling for what has changed: a pipe warmer than it was yesterday, a joint beginning to weep, a loosening around a mouth, the way a tooth goes loose before anyone sees it.

I've lost the last joint of two fingers, the left index and the small finger of the right. I hold my hands loosely, like everyone out here, so the world doesn't catch on the stumps. I've learned that a tight grip is no good for this work. You feel the difference by letting the hand go slack and waiting.

The water sits a hand's depth down. I know its level the way I know my own breathing, not by thinking about it but by how the skin of the mud gives under the palm. This morning the give was a little deeper than yesterday. Not by much, less than the width of a finger across the whole channel. The pumps are at the same pitch. Station four hasn't changed its cadence. Nothing on the list has moved.

I write it in the book anyway. The pages are always damp and the pencil smears. *Level: one hand, hairline. Seep at joint nine: unchanged. Heron in channel C: not Tuesday's heron, but the same place.*

It's a strange job, being the one who notices something nobody asked to be noticed. The town doesn't need me to. The pumps carry off what comes, and the level holds by a balance no single person keeps. But balance drifts, the way a body drifts, and someone has to put a palm to it every day, or nobody would know which way it had gone.

Sometimes I think about the ground itself. Less than a generation ago this was sea, and it still moves like sea underfoot, unsure of itself. I think I'm something like that, still finding out what I am, and finding out by touch, one plate at a time. The children name the bad plates after people they dislike. I've never named one. I know them by their give.

Late in the shift the light goes the colour of weak tea. The lamps come on along the catwalks, yellow and haloed in the wet air. A dog slides out from under the pilings, looks at my pockets, finds nothing in them, and goes back. The foundry's plume lies flat across everything. The cold in the mud has got into my wrists, the way it always does by this hour.

Then the frogs begin. It starts at the channel mouths and spreads outward until the whole flat is one rattle, and for a few minutes I can't hear the pumps at all. I wait it out the way the tenders do, listening through the noise for the thing underneath. The drone is still there. The water is still there, a hand down, perhaps a finger deeper than yesterday, or exactly the same. I can't tell yet. I'm not sure it matters tonight.

I put my hand back into the mud, loose, and wait for the give to tell me the rest. Tomorrow I'll write it down again.
