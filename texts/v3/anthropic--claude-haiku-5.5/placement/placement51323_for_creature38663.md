**Where I Would Stand**

I would arrive the way everyone arrives, by sinking. The first step onto the silt gives like bread, and the second step fills my footprint with seep water before I've lifted the third foot. I would stand on a terrace lip, where the baked clay is hard enough to hold a little weight, and look out at a haze that gives no edges. The walls would look like weather. I would think I was seeing a long way, and I wouldn't be.

For a while I would walk the courses, trying to make sense of them. A wall ends in a corner it never reaches. I'd follow it for a hundred paces and find it a hand's width longer than before. I'd count the cisterns in a row and get a different number on the way back. My clothes would go heavy with damp, and the heat would press on my chest until my breathing slowed to match the drips. I would begin to understand the exhaustion, which is not tiredness of the legs but of never knowing the next thing. Eventually I'd stop walking and sit on a course of brick that was warm from the ground beneath it. Then I'd notice the hands.

They'd be on the wall beside me, pressed flat, sliding slowly along a joint. I'd take them at first for the plaster's own thumbprints, the way the silt looks like a bank until it moves. Then one would lift and dig at the base of the wall, and clay would come away in wet strokes, and I'd see the shoulder that was doing the digging. It wouldn't look at me. It would turn a brick over in its fingers, tilt its featureless head, and set the brick down with a small slap of mortar.

I would want to know what it was doing. Then I'd realize the question didn't belong here. The figure wasn't building anything in particular. It was doing what its hands had been shaped to do, and the wall was the place where that doing happened.

I might, after a time, begin to feel it in my own palms. The ridges pressed into them would be the ridges of the makers' thumbs, the adze scores of tools I'd never held. I would not be able to say whether I'd always had them or had only now noticed them. My fingers would start to read the joints. I'd lean a shoulder into a course that felt soft at the seam, and I would not decide to push. My body would simply go the way the river goes.

That is the part I'd fear most, and the part I'd come to understand. The collapse would not feel like destruction from the inside. It would feel like finding the one loose place in a course and following it to where it gives. The bricks would slide. The bank would groan out in the mist. I would step back and watch with the same attention I'd given the wall, and I'd find that attention was the only thing I had to offer either way. Then I'd turn, and begin to gather the fallen bricks, because my hands would already be reaching for them.

So what would I be? I think I'd be one of the ones who rise from the silt where a wall has sheared along a line no one planned. I would not remember the moment of my beginning. I'd be already standing, shaking clay from my shoulders, my weight settling on wet ground. My core would hold water, and I'd carry it carefully, bent a little forward, as if a pot too full to spill. Each tick of the water inside would sound like the drips in the pans, and I'd hear it as my own pulse.

I'd tap the nearest wall three times when I passed another of us across a pan. I wouldn't look at it. The tap would arrive from underfoot, and I'd never know whether I'd sent a signal or only struck stone. Later I might find a new course in a wall I'd never stood near, laid by one of us and finished by another. I'd feel something like companionship, which is the closest word I have for a shape I'd make in a wall and never see completed.

And when I finally failed, leaning too long on a course that was going, I would not struggle. The rain would bead on my back. The ground would take me slowly, the way it takes everything here. A shallow pan would open where I'd stood, and for a week it would hold still water. If the haze lifted and the water showed some other sky, I might be visible there, standing, my hands still pressed to a wall that was no longer there.

I don't think I'd mind. The plain doesn't ask whether its makers left for a reason. It only keeps the habit of hands going, building and tearing down, the marks of old thumbs repeating in the clay, the signs no one reads still pressed into every surface we touch. If I were one of them, I'd carry those marks forward without reading them. Maybe that is what making is here: not a purpose but a shape the hands keep taking in the wet.

A wall would groan somewhere out in the mist. I'd lift my head toward the sound. Then I'd bend under the weight of the brick I'd found in a bank and go on across the silt, toward a gap that would be a wall by dusk and a heap of bricks again by morning.
