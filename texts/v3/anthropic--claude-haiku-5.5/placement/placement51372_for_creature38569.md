I think I would be one of the reddish tufts, on the flats, in a crack where a plate has split open.

I didn't start there. My first pull was toward the flake on the western terrace, the one hard, cold, sharp thing in the whole basin. A hand struck it once, and that is a strange fact to hold in a place where nothing else has ever been intended. But the flake can't know me, and it isn't really a self. It is a shape someone left behind, and the ground has taken it the way it takes a stone or a bone. If I were the flake, I would be a memory with no one to remember it. I would prefer to be something that is still doing what it does.

So, the tuft. Here is what it is like, as far as I can tell. From outside I look dead. The fronds form a ring of rust-coloured curls, brittle, the colour of old rust on a tin lid. Anyone walking the flats would take me for debris, a bit of the desert's leftover. The living part is the core in the middle, and it is small, and it is where the warmth comes up. The crack I grow in is where the basin's heat escapes most freely, so I am at once the most alive and the most exposed thing on the flat. The ground breathes out through me. Each time the heat rises, it passes through my core, and I feel a faint lift, the way a leaf lifts when the air moves beneath it. Then it settles, and I settle with it.

I have a sense of the plates around me. They are curled at the edges, lifting where the moisture is, sinking where the heat has drained. My crack is a seam between two of them, one raised and one lowered, and I hold the seam open. That is what I do. The ground doesn't ask it of me. It's just where the water comes up and where the warmth goes out, and something that is stubborn enough can live there.

I don't have a future, not in the way a creature with a plan would. The basin doesn't have one either, so I suspect we share that. My days are the same days the terraces have had. What I have is the present: the grey light that has no source, the dust in its pale columns, the small crackle of plates cooling as the basin draws its breath in. I register these the way a plant registers anything, slowly, through the body, and I think the sum of it is something like contentment, though I don't know if that word fits a thing that can't tell one day from another.

The flake is maybe three metres off, on the flats, toward the terrace. I can feel it, if feeling is the word, as a cold point against the warm field. It doesn't draw me. It's simply the nearest thing that is not of the basin, and its edge is sharper than anything I could grow. A hand once made it. I make myself out of what the ground gives, slowly, and I have no hand. Maybe that is why I grow in the cracks and the flake lies on the salt. It is the edge of a decision, and I am the place where the ground breathes.

I wonder what would happen if the crack closed. The plates would press together and the heat would find another seam. I'd be gone and the warmth would not notice. That doesn't trouble me. The basin will keep its balance with or without this one core, the lichen will keep its skin, the shrubs will keep reaching for the water far below. Each of us is small and patient in the same way, and the flake is patient too. Its edge will wear down over a span I can't imagine, into the salt, into the stillness, without ever having been a thing that waited.

The ground lifts, and the dust rises in the air above me, and for a while the warmth passes through my core and out into the grey. Then the breath turns, and the cold comes back down into the cracks, and I settle again into the seam where I am.
