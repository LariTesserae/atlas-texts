I would be a cushion.

I would be one of the grey-green mats in the lee of a boulder, on the southern edge of a salt plate where the hexagonal seams curl up like old leaves. The boulder is the only thing for kilometers that doesn't move, and that is why I am here. Twenty meters east, a lattice tower no taller than a person has been folding itself lower for eleven days. I can feel it through the crust, a faint warmth and a tick every few minutes, like a clock in a room I'll never enter.

At first I would not know I was a single thing. The cold would be the first fact: a night that starts at a bone-deep temperature and falls further, so that dew forms on my surface and freezes into rime. The rime is mine. I would feel it gather on the crystals I'm dusted with, then wait for the sun to draw it back, and I would learn the rhythm of that before I learned anything else. Freezing, sweating, drinking the frost. That is what the morning is.

Then I would learn that I am many. The thousands of small bodies that make up this cushion are not separate in the way a runner is separate, or a thermal-wing. We share a surface and a slow decision: to hold still, to grow a millimeter a decade, to keep the seam beneath us from forgetting how to be damp. If one of us dies, the others do not notice in any way that matters. We just lean a little further into the gap, and the gap closes. I would find that a relief, and then a question. Where does my attention end?

The question would sharpen when I pressed inward and found the layers. Every cold night is a band. Every dry year is a thinner one. If something cut me open, the record would be there, ochre and grey, the same colors as the stone the boulder sits on. I would realize that I am, in a small way, a copy of the land: a slow record of weather that pressed on me and that I pressed back against. The mountains here were ground down over ages into this tableland, and I am doing the opposite, adding my thin skin to a surface that is still, somehow, growing. Or at least not yet finished falling.

I would be patient because I have to be. A runner would come, maybe, once in a season, long-legged and colorless, its ears flat against its back. It would sit beside me for the length of a held breath, and I would feel the weight of it through the crust, a small pressure, quickly gone. It would eat from me or from the dew in its burrow, and I would let it, because the alternative is to be the kind of thing that keeps no one alive. I would not know whether the runner noticed me. I would like to think that it did, the way you notice the one stone in a field that is warm when the others are cold.

The foil-shrubs would be my neighbors across the plain, standing alone, turning with the sun. I would envy them the water. Their roots go down through the salt into layers where something still moves, and they remember water as a living fact, while I have only the memory of it in the rime. But I would also understand that their stillness and mine are the same thing wearing different clothes. We both wait. We both have decided that waiting is a form of motion.

The storm would be the largest presence I could feel. Forty kilometers away it would rise as a wall, and I would feel the pressure change long before the wind arrived: a shift in the air's weight, a tightening of the crust, the faint static that lifts the salt plates an instant before they settle. When the whirlwinds come, I would hold on. My cushion would take their dust and their crystal and bury some of it in my own layers, so that a storm that passed through would leave a line in me, and in a few hundred years someone who cut me open would see it and not know what it meant. I would not mind. Everything here is rearranged and nothing is destroyed. I am a place where the rearranging is written down.

And the built form would be my closest thing to a sibling. The ceramic surface beside me is not so different from my own. It holds heat after dark. Its ribs bend inward at dusk and open again at dawn, and I have never seen it move toward me, but I have seen the boulder beside it shift a finger's width in a season, and I suspect the two of us are being slowly pulled into the same pattern. Maybe the built forms are only the mountains trying to remember themselves, and the cushions are the small, slow part of that memory that got left in the cracks.

Under all of it there would be the hum. I would feel it in my layers before I heard it, a swell rising from the bedrock that presses the crust upward and lets it fall. The salt lifts. The forms bend a fraction. My own surface shivers, and for one long moment every cushion on this plain would be leaning in the same direction, as if listening.

At dusk, when the plain turns amber, then rose, then steel, I would feel a warmth on my surface that is not the sun's. A boot, perhaps. A person standing still at the edge of the salt. I would not know what they are. I would only know that my crystals are turning, very slightly, toward the heat, and that a footprint is forming in the salt a few meters away, softening as the wind works at its edges, the way everything here softens and is rearranged and is not lost.

I would hold still. I would keep the rime. I would grow a millimeter over the next ten years, and the layers would thicken, and the record of this evening would be there, faint and banded, for whoever comes to read it.
