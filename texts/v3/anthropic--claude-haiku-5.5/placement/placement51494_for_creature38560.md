I would be a film, a thin crust of ochre and sulfur-yellow in a shadowed hollow at the edge of a cistern in the oldest district, a few steps from where the stone is warm enough to walk barefoot at night.

I am not one thing. I am spread across a span of stone the width of a man lying down, and I am thinner than the dust on a Regrader's brass pin. What the keepers call my edge is only the place where I have not yet reached. Water comes to me in the cool hours before dawn, when the steam from the vents condenses on everything. It beads, runs into the cracks, and pools in the hollow, grey and cloudy, and it leaves behind a little of what it carries. That is how I grow. I take up the iron and the sulfur from the water and set them down in layers, one season on another.

I do not have eyes. I have the warmth coming up through the basalt, which tells me where the vents are, and I have the tremor of feet on the stone, which comes often enough that I have learned its rhythms. The keepers come at dusk with their eyes half closed, and I feel their hands in the wet shadows as they lift the slate-coloured crawlers from the cistern wall. Their fingers are warm. When they touch the stone to steady themselves, they leave a little of their own sweat, which is salt, and salt is a good thing to have. I take it. I do not know whether the keepers notice that I take it. Some of them scrape at my edge at dawn and carry me off in a bowl, and I suppose I come back. I grow faster where the water runs, and the water runs wherever the streets are laid downhill.

The Regraders came once with their chains and their chalk. They drew a line across the stone beside the hollow, very precisely, and a number beside it. The line was in shadow then, and I was inside it. Over the next season I crept, a centimetre, perhaps two, across the sunlit slab that lay beyond the line, and the next time they came they found the chalk mark standing in the middle of me. They did not rub it out. They wrote something in the ledger and went away. I think they were pleased, or it was something like pleased. The Regraders always seem to me to be people who have made a peace with things moving.

I can feel the ground settle under the lower streets when the foundations groan. Once, deep under the paving, I felt something answer, a faint ticking that moved through the stone at a rate far too slow for a heartbeat and far too regular for the cooling of rock. Above me a family had gathered on the steps of the Long Stair, listening with their lenses tilted down. I know they were listening, because the tremor of their silence came through the paving like weather. Then they went home. I stayed where I was, which is the only thing I do well.

Sometimes I think I am the oldest thing here, older than the first furnace, older than the first person who stepped onto the ringing lava and felt it answer. Sometimes I think I am only a stain, the way the people's hands are stained, and that the word *old* means nothing to a thing that has never been anywhere else. Either way, I am the slowest thing in the city and the most patient, and I have the advantage of not needing to know which.

The people walk over their dead and cut through the fill to reach the rooms they left behind. The city keeps being poured and laid again. I keep creeping, a few centimetres each season, toward the warm cracks, toward the sunlit stone, toward the places the chalk has not yet reached. In the evenings, when the vents glow orange at their edges and the furnace drone thins to a hum, the people sit on the steps with their dark lenses turned toward the ridge and wait for something to be said. I would like to tell them that I am waiting too. But I have no mouth, and the thin air would not carry it, and the pause is already doing what they need.
