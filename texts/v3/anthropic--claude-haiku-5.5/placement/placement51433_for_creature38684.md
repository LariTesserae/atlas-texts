I would be one of the winged things, the ones that are not birds and not quite insects, and I would come down at dusk to the edge of a seep on the eastern swells, below the turbines, where the hum arrives a half-beat late.

I would not come down easily. I would circle first, for a long time, over the black pool and the bright green drift of a spore-storm that has been thinning since afternoon. My wings would shiver at a frequency I don't have a name for, and the air above the water would ripple where I passed, so that anyone watching would see the pool bend a little without knowing why. I would catch the spores in the fine hairs along my belly. They would cling there, bright and specific, each one carrying a small instruction for growth that I would not read but would carry anyway, the way a seed carries the shape of a tree it will never see.

Then I would tire, and the light would go soft, and the grey birds would start their chord in the hollow below. I would let the call pass over me. It is not meant for me, but I would find myself holding my wings at the angle the birds hold their necks, waiting for an answer that the turbines would give, half a beat under.

I would land.

The mat would take me as it takes everything, slowly, with a little sigh of cold water. My feet would sink first, then my body to the middle, and I would feel the living ground take a fraction of my weight into itself. It would not be unkind about it. It would not be hungry, either. It would simply hold, the way the stone holds water, and I would understand, for the first time since I came up out of the seep, what the people mean when they say they are guests. I would be a guest in my own body. My wings would fold. My edges would soften. Nothing about me would be sharp anymore.

Through the night, I would become less a creature and more a place where something had been decided. The mat would weave its pale hair-roots through the shape I'd left, and the runners would come to clear the channel I'd sunk beside, and a burrower far below would pulse once, as if noting a new fact in the record. By morning there would be no sign of me on the surface.

But I would not be gone. The spores in my belly would have fallen where I lay, and something would come up in that place next season, a little lime-tipped growth at the edge of the seep. It would not be what was there before. It would not quite be me, either. It would be the thing the mat made out of the thing I was, and it would be the start of something else, which is what everything here is doing.

I think that is what I would want, if wanting is the right word for a thing that circles and sinks. Not to be remembered as a creature, but to be received, and to become a small, patient part of the becoming. To leave behind a seed that grows into a shape I could not have chosen, on a plateau that has never once asked permission to change.

Some nights, when the north glow brightens in its slow rhythm, the people would look up from their small fires and say nothing until it faded. I would like to think they were looking at the seed, and not at the sky.
