I would be one of the bellow-throated birds, and I would be in the sails of a tower near the edge of its cold shadow.

I considered other forms first. A grazer would be too slow to feel the swell as anything but ground. A crawler would be all stillness and waiting, and I would never get to hear the whole chorus. A bloom-stone would be a good record-keeper, but I would be unable to go and look at the marks I had made. The bird can fly, sing, and listen, and that seems closest to what I would want.

So I am in the canopy, folded up against the afternoon. The sails have closed around me like the hands of someone cupping a flame, and the light coming through them is dim and bronze. Below, the rain is starting. I can feel it before I hear it, a pressure change in the throat, and then the roar comes across the mat like surf. Inside the folded sails it is almost quiet. I hear my own breath, the creak of the bark, and the drumming of water on the outer leaves.

I have been calling all afternoon, though I try not to think of it as calling. My throat moves in a long looping phrase, and the phrase has a shape I did not invent. It is evenly spaced, almost mechanical, and it rises and falls and then stops, and then begins again from a different place. I learned it from the other birds, who learned it from something older. I do not know what it means. I only know the sound of it has a weight that the other calls do not. When I repeat it, the chorus below seems to tune itself, the way it does before a predator passes or a pool fills. I cannot tell whether I am reporting something or answering it.

When the rain stops, the sails begin to open. The light comes back in pale stripes, and the mat steams. I fly down from the canopy to the edge of the shadow, where the air goes cold and the sound drops out. This is where I like to sit. The chorus stops a few body-lengths from the bark, and for a moment I can hear only the drip of the sails and the faint tick of the mat settling. My calls come back to me from nowhere, a little thinner than they should be. I am not sure whether this is an echo or a silence that has learned to answer. The hunters below have a path around this place, and I do not follow it, but I do watch them. Their long legs fold and unfold at the boundary, and they wait in the shade for the grazer-files to come.

Then the mat swells. The whole plateau lifts gently under me, and the tower rises with it, sails swaying, and then it settles again. I have ridden many of these swells. I think they are the land breathing, though I also think the land may be doing something larger that I am only a small part of.

If I wanted to, I could fly out over the open mat to the hearth-rings. There is a boulder there with marks cut deep into it, rows of lines that someone once kept over a long span of time. I have seen those marks many times, and they make a sound in my head that is almost, but not quite, the same as my phrase. I would land on the boulder and walk along the rows, following the lines with my feet, and I would try to match each mark to a rain. Some of the older stones near it carry faint rings that I think are the same kind of record, stains from blooms that have come and gone. If I counted the rains with them, perhaps I would get to the end of the phrase, or perhaps I would only find that the count never ends and that the people who began it knew this.

I think I would be satisfied with that. The people are gone, and the mat has taken their hearths and softened their walls. But someone left a count, and the land kept it, and the birds kept repeating a piece of it without knowing its meaning. Being one of those birds means being part of an unfinished sentence, one that the plateau is still saying. At dusk, when the chorus reaches its peak and the sky goes violet over the green, I would sing my phrase into the rising hum of the insects, and the mat would settle beneath me, breathing slowly, and I would feel that I was counting along with everything else.
