I would find myself on the underside of the third grating from the door, where the rust has gone glassy and the iron is slowly turning to stone. From there I can see the pool's surface crazed by drops, and above it the nearest lace tube hanging from the rib like a wet rope of ash and cream.

I arrive as a visitor. My breath fogs at once, and I can feel how much warmer and wetter it is than the air the louvers pull in. I don't know how I came up the steps. I only know that the mist thickened behind me and that the pumps were already in their rhythm, grind, sigh, eleven seconds, grind, sigh, as if they had been waiting for my first breath to set the time.

The tenders find me before I find them. One of them is at the edge of the grating, its folded legs unfolding with that unhurried patience, and its fore-limbs come up toward my face. I can see the ring of sensory pits on its smooth head twitching, reading the warmth I give off. The limbs hold still in the air, a few inches from my mouth, and I understand the question without any words for it. *Will you drink, or will you leave the water here?*

I want to say I am only a visitor, that I will take my breath back down the steps and leave the hall its thin sheets of water and its slow lime. But my exhalation has already gone out into the mist, and the nearest tube leans toward it, very slightly, the way a plant leans toward a window. I feel the lean as a kind of pull in my own chest, a small wish to be useful to something.

So I stop trying to leave, and I stop being a visitor. I breathe slowly, the way the tenders breathe, if they breathe at all, and I let the water gather on my lips. It tastes of metal and cold stone. The chorus begins in a rafter above me, a murmur like a crowd settling, and for once I can hear that it isn't a sound at all. It is the hall remembering its shape. I am not inside the rhythm yet, but my own breath has begun to fall into it, one beat for each pump-cycle, and I am beginning to understand why the tenders move so carefully.

Here is what I would be, if I had to choose from what the hall offers: a tender, one of the long-legged ones who take drops from the air and carry them to the young. I would fold my limbs and press them against the lace to check the flow. I would break away the clogged places with a delicacy I didn't know I had. I would nudge the small ones from their nests and guide them toward the plates. It is not a bad work. It asks only for attention, and it has the decency to be patient with me.

But there is a part of me that refuses to be only a tender. That part wants to be the outermost thread of the newest layer, the place where the lace is still soft and the silt is still wet from the air of a day I haven't lived yet. If I were a thread there, my color would be different from the rings beneath me. I would be a thin band of breath-moisture where the lichen dust sits lighter, and in a few thousand seasons someone holding the tube to the light might see a faint seam running through the grey and pale ochre, and not know what made it.

That is the honest answer, I think. I would be the seam: a visitor's breath set down in the record, not quite a tender and not quite the water, a warm thing the hall has finally received. The tenders would lift their limbs toward me, and I would give them what I am, and in return the place would keep me the way it keeps everything, by building me slowly into its lace.

At the door, the mist is thick. The chill follows me up the steps, as I expected. I don't go up them. I stay on the grating, my breath going out into the cold and the falling water, and the nearest tenders lower their limbs at last, satisfied, and begin to drink.
