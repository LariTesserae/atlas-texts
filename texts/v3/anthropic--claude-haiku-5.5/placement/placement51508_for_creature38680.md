I find myself as a bead of water that has not yet decided to be a drop.

I am vapor first, one molecule-thick breath rising through the warm air under the steel mesh. The air here is close to liquid, and I am carried upward by nothing more than the heat of the basin, past the lattice of pipes, toward the condensing coils. I do not know I am rising. There is no knowing yet, only the gradient, the way the wet air presses against the cooler metal above.

Then the coil. The metal is colder than I am, and I give up the heat I was carrying and gather against the surface with thousands of others. This is the first change: I become a point, then a hemisphere, then a weight. I hang from the wire by surface tension, and the light comes through me in a flat silver wash. For a while I am a lens, and I see nothing but my own edge.

I grow. The coil keeps producing, and I am pressed by the arrivals behind me until my hold fails. I let go. This is the part that is most like thinking, though it is not thinking. It is a fall that is also a decision, a body yielding to a slope. I drop past the root-mats and the fronds as wide as tables, and for a moment I am the fastest thing in the basin. The leaves below do not notice me. Nothing down there is moving quickly enough to be surprised.

I land in a drip-channel, narrow and bright, cut into the rim's grey stone. I slide. The channel is new, cut only last season, and the water in it has not yet learned to be patient. I carry a trace of salt I picked up from the coil's fins, a few hundred atoms of mineral that the filaments in the membrane will one day take up as film. I do not know this either. I only feel the channel bend and the weight of the others behind me pressing me forward.

I reach a pan. The surface takes me in with a small, soft gurgle, and I am no longer a drop. I am part of a level, a fraction of a hair above the neighboring tier. Somewhere under the stone a pump turns, a valve weighs the difference, and the gate lifts with a sigh. I feel the pull, the way the whole basin shifts its weight toward whatever is straining, and I go where the balance sends me. I am water moving from one terrace to the next, one thought, slow as an afternoon, passing through the mind that holds the garden.

This is who I am, I think, or who I am becoming as I pass: a unit of quickness inside a system that counts in seasons. I am measured, and then I am absorbed into the count. The regulation registers the small disturbance of my arrival, weighs it, and finds it does not matter very much. It does not hurry to understand me. But somewhere in the sequence of its openings, in the precise drift of the gauges, there is now a trace of a drop that fell from the highest coil in the wet light and sank into the lowest pan. That is what I become: not a creature, not a self in any sense you would recognize, but a small entry in a long ledger, carried down through the terraces until the water that was once me is indistinguishable from the water that was always here.
