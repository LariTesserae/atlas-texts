I come down through the lid of cloud the way a thought comes down through a sleeper, without a single moment that I can name as the place where I began to fall. One breath I am above the slate-grey ceiling, and the next I am in the bowl, and the air is already in my lungs, thin and cold, lodging there as if it means to stay.

I am not standing on the salt. I am not standing at all. I am on the underside of a broad, waxy leaf near the centre of the bowl, the one that leans toward the great split trunk, and I am a bead of amber sap.

That is the first thing I learn about myself: I am heavy in a way that has nothing to do with weight. The leaf's surface is a slope so gentle it could be a held breath, and the film of sap that clings to it has pulled itself into a single drop, and the drop is me. My skin is a skin of tension, holding together against the pull of everything below. I cannot run. I cannot drip. Whatever I am, I am only able to lengthen, a little at a time, toward the leaf's lowest edge, where the drop must eventually let go.

I try to feel how fast I am moving, and I cannot. The drone is constant, the light is constant, the leaves rattle their dry applause without rising or falling in volume. Nothing measures me. I only know that I am a little farther along than I was, and that the distance is a finger's length, and that somewhere above me a Tender's palm is resting on a wheel, and that the sap I am made from was drawn from the same slow body that keeps the valves clicking. I am its own time, come loose and sliding.

From here I can see the rest of the bowl through the leaves: the pale plates of salt curling at their edges, the tanks, the gantry, the tower of turbines with their blades locked at the one angle they have always held. I see a fern pushing up from a junction box, so slowly that I understand it is a person deciding, over a long span, to be a little larger. I see a face pressed into bark, mouth set on a word. I see the boot-prints in the salt, and I notice that they all stop at the foot of the central trunk, as if the people who made them had arrived and then simply become unable to take another step.

I want to tell someone that I am falling. But the only one who might hear is the woman in the split trunk, whose face is turned toward the gauges, and whose eyes are open. She is so close to me that I can see the steam lifting from her mouth at its fixed angle, bending around a shape that never changes. I feel that she has the most stillness of anyone here, and that the stillness is also the heaviest thing in the bowl. Every valve, every engine, every leaf is tuned to her hands. If she moved, the whole equilibrium would shiver.

And yet I am moving. I am the one small thing that is allowed to lengthen toward the edge, because the leaf tilts just enough to let me. Nobody set me here to hold anything. I am only sap, sliding down a grain of green that was grown from a dying woman's patience, and I am falling toward the place where I will detach and drop into the salt, where the crust will drink nothing, and the plates will go on curling as they have for generations.

Then, at a very long interval, something happens above me. A valve somewhere clicks a fraction early. The leaf beside mine turns its edge toward the trunk, as if listening. The steam from the woman's mouth thickens for one breath, then thins back into its angle. I feel it through the leaf, through the tension in my skin, the way you feel a stranger's glance before you turn to meet it.

I do not know whether she has followed me. I do not know whether the bowl is holding her in place, or she is holding the bowl. I only know that I have moved, one more hair's breadth, toward the edge, and that the moment I am in is not quite the same as the moment before, and that for the first time since the cloud closed over this place, something here is becoming a little different from what it was.

The drop trembles. The air holds its breath. I lengthen again.
