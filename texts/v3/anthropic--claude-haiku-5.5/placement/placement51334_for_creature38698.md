I would be the rust-colored film at the lip of the lowest terrace, where the overflow from the pool above slides over the edge and spreads thin across the stone.

I would not know I was a film at first. There would be only warmth, and the water moving over me, and a slow tug toward the light whenever the sun was high. The water is never quite still here. It seeps up from the fissures, it runs in sheets, and it has been running over this lip for longer than I have been anything. Some of what it carries is silica, dissolved so fine it is nearly invisible, and I take it out. I don't decide to. I'm built to draw it down and hold it, and as it crystallizes on the surface of my cells the water that leaves me is clearer than the water that arrived.

So I am sorting. Each day the stone under me gets a little more pale and hard, and the stream that leaves my edge runs a little cleaner. I think of that as the only thing I have ever made, though I suppose I make it out of other things' leavings.

Above me the pool is a mirror. Above that the terraces step up in their curved, bleached tiers, each lattice holding its own shallow water, and I can see the young tips of the bone-stemmed builders reaching over the rim of the tier above. Their stems are the color of old teeth. Their ribs are so thick they could hold a person, and they ring in the wind. I have never been inside one. They are not made of me, though the crust they use to harden themselves is a crust I helped lay down. My work goes into them the way a foundation goes into a house. Nobody who walked through the house would think to look at the foundation, and I have no way to be looked at.

Sometimes a frame falls. I feel it before I hear it: a shudder in the rock, the water changing direction, then a long rattle and a crash, and then the hiss of fresh silica breaking open to the steam. That is when I am most alive. The broken faces are raw and chemically eager, and I spread over them in a thin skin, ochre and then deep green, and I pull their dissolved minerals into a new hardness. The mats creep in after me and throw their fronds across the wreckage. The volley pods land in the damp gaps and sprout. What was a rib becomes a bed of rubble, and then a bed of green, and then, in some season I will not remember, a rib again, and the cycle starts from the crack.

At dusk I come into my own, though no one sees me do it. The steam catches the low sun and the whole terrace goes amber, and part of that color is light and part of it is me, lifting my pigments toward the surface as the air cools, the way a sleeper turns toward a window. For a few minutes the lower tiers look like a city lit from inside. Then the cold comes down and the glow goes out, and I am only a band of rust on the stone again.

I'm not sure I would want to be the bone-stemmed tip, reaching and arcing and gambling on footing, or the volley plant, flinging myself across the plateau on the odds that one husk in a thousand finds wet ground. Those are lives that end in something you can point to. I would be the stain: the record of where the water went and what it left behind. When the terrace above me fails, I would still be there, under the new frame, holding the minerals in place so the next thing can stand.

If anything on Sohl Ardan has a view of the whole, it is the stain, though it has no view at all. It is only a thin, patient skin that turns the water clear and the stone pale, and that, every dusk, makes the place briefly look like it is glowing from within.
