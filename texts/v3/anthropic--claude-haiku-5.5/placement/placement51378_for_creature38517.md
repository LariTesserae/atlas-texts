I would find myself at the rim of a warm pool, early, before the cloud ring has finished its loop.

The steam comes off the water in slow threads and dissolves a few body-lengths up. The cushion-moss here is denser than anywhere else I've crossed, rust-coloured, tough, and it holds the stone so tightly that I can't tell where the ground ends and the plant begins. I know these pools by their rings. Pale lines of mineral crust mark where the water stood in other years, and this one has stood high, then low, then high again, many times over. I don't count years here. I just notice that the rim is wider than it was when I first came down the slope.

I am a grazer. I have the dark, ridged hide, and when the rain falls in its narrow, sieved columns it runs off my back in channels that are older than I am. I carry no memory of being born, only a sense of having always been at the edge of the fog, watching it. That is the work of my kind: keep to the open ground, keep the mist in view, and never assume that a surface which looks like moss will hold weight. I test each step. My forelegs go down gently, feel for the give of the marsh, and only then take the weight. Some of the herd have gone under this season, and I have learned to read the difference between firm stone and cold slurry through the soles of my feet, before my eyes can catch it.

Sometimes I think my thoughts are the land's thoughts. I am not sure this is wrong. The membranes overhead taste the air, the cloud ring turns its deliberate loop, and I find myself drifting toward a particular pool or a particular fold of rock without any clear reason, the way an idea keeps returning to the same edge in the mind until it either takes root or withers. When I stop here, the rim is warm under my hooves, and the pool breathes up steam in the grey light, and something in me settles. I cannot say whether it is my own contentment or the whole plateau's attention resting for a moment on a single warm place.

Above me, a glider drops from a tower and skims low over the water, calling. Its cry carries a long way across the marsh. Somewhere to the west, a predator is working the seeps, pruning, and the herd behind me is shifting toward a newer pool because this one is too grazed. I feel this as a kind of pressure, a gentle redirecting. I move, and the moving is also the mind moving, and I cannot find the seam where one ends and the other begins.

The cloud ring pauses over the rim. The mist thickens around me, and I feel the membranes on the nearest spar begin to condense, a faint wetness gathering on my flank. I stand still and let it measure me. I do not know whether I am being noticed as a threat, a nuisance, an odd warm pattern in a cold place, or simply as one more movement to be counted. I think the mind does not know yet either. It is still learning what kinds of things can live on it, and I am one of the questions.

When the ring moves on, the rain comes down in a narrow column a few paces away, falling on the works and not on me. I watch it strike the pale lattice and run in silver threads toward the grooves, toward the pools, toward the rings that will one day mark its level. The water finds its way down without any hurry, and so do I, one careful step at a time, along the warm edge of something that is still becoming itself.
