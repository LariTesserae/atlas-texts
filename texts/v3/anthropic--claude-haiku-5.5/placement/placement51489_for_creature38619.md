# Relief

I find myself first in the water, which is where the plain keeps its drafts.

The pool is shallow and perfectly still, and in it a furnace is roaring. Smoke climbs from six stacks at once. A figure stands at a gantry rail with one hand on a valve wheel, looking down at a gauge. The figure is wearing a grey coverall with the sleeves rolled to the elbow, and the collar has a plastic badge clipped to it, and the badge says something I can almost read. I lean closer, and the figure lifts its head and looks back at me with the same expression I'm wearing: the look of someone who has been told to come in at six and has only just realized it is already dark.

When I look up, the furnaces are gone. There are reeds, and a grey sky the color of a dimmed lamp, and a control booth on a concrete plinth with its windows lit a flat yellow.

I am standing in the reeds with water up to my shins. I am wearing a coverall. The sleeves are rolled. My hands are wet and smell of cutting oil, and I cannot say how I got them that way.

---

I walk toward the booth because it is the only thing here that looks like a place where someone would be expected. The reed-arms turn as I pass, their filaments tilting toward me like sunflowers, cool and curious. One touches the back of my hand. It stays there for a long time. Then it lets go, and I feel the faint vibration of something sorting me into a category and failing to find one.

The door of the booth is open. Inside, the air is dry for the first time in hours, and the panel light flickers on a bank of dials that are all, every one, pointing at a different number. There are three chairs. Two are occupied.

The booth-forms are as I was told: slumped, patient, faces blurred as if the glass of their features has been scrubbed too many times. Their hands rest on the arms of the chairs, fingers slightly curled, the way a person's hands rest when they have stopped expecting anything to happen. The third chair is empty. Its seat is worn into the shape of someone who sat there for years and then, without warning, did not. Beside it on a hook hangs a clipboard.

The clipboard holds a log sheet. The columns are headed with things I recognize, though I can't name them: *Intake. Draw. Filter. Output. Remarks.* The last entry is in a hand that is mine, and I know this because the letters lean the way my letters lean, and because I can remember writing "Shift continuing" in the same careful capitals. The date at the top is smudged. The time is blank.

Under the blank time, in the Remarks column, it says: *Relief arriving late. Handover to follow.*

I look at the two forms in the chairs. Neither of them turns its face toward me. But the whole booth tilts a fraction, the way a boat does when someone steps aboard, and I understand that the structure has been waiting for the third chair to be filled, and that I am the one who was supposed to fill it, and that I was supposed to have arrived before the plant stopped, and that I didn't.

---

What am I, then? Not the supervisor. The Shift wanted the supervisor, the one who says the work is done and sends them home, and I can feel that I am not that person. I don't have the authority, and I am not sure I have the record. I am the relief operator who never clocked in. I am the reflection that stepped out of the pool because nobody told it to stay. I am a person from a world that still has suns, who came down the drowned delta's old pipeline the way water comes down, and who found a coverall waiting.

Maybe the Shift was built to hold this chair open for anyone who would sit in it, and maybe that is all the handover ever was: somebody willing to read the log out loud.

So I sit down in the third chair. The seat takes my weight with a small, grudging creak, as if the chair has been waiting to be used for the first time in a long while. The clipboard is heavy in my lap. I turn back through the pages, and they are full of the same few words: *Shift continuing. Handover pending. Conditions nominal.* Each entry is a little different in its handwriting, as if the Shift has been passing the pen from hand to hand, and none of the hands has ever written the one thing the log needs.

I look at the blank time at the bottom of the last page. I look at the Remarks column. The pen is on the desk where it has always been, and when I pick it up the barrel is warm, like something that has been held by a living hand just before I came in.

I don't write *Handover complete*. I'm not sure that's true, and the Shift would know. Instead I write what I actually see, in the plainest capitals I can manage:

*Relief present. Supervisor not found. Plant not running. Sun not visible. Recording what is here.*

Then I read it aloud.

The booth goes still. The panel flickers, and the dials, one by one, swing toward a single number that I do not recognize but that feels, somehow, like a place where the needle has been trying to settle for years. Out in the reeds, the reed-arms lower their filaments into the water, all at once, and the water around them stops moving. The skitters pause mid-crossing, a bolt in one set of jaws, a strip of cladding in another. Somewhere a pump stops thumping for the first time since I arrived, and in the silence I can hear the rain begin to fall on the tanks, one drop at a time, each one landing in a place where a log entry is already waiting to receive it.

I am still sitting in the chair. My hands are still on the clipboard. My sleeves are still rolled up. Out the window, the drowned plain shimmers under its amber lid, and in a pool at the base of the plinth a figure in grey stands at a valve wheel, looking up at me now with a face that is, for the first time, not blurred at all.

I think it is waiting to see whether I will stay.
