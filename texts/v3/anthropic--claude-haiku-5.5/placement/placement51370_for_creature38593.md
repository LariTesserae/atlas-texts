I would find myself on the eastern rim, in the fork of an old tree whose bark has drunk mist for so long it has gone soft as felt. From there the whole hollow lies open beneath me, the way a cupped hand shows the lines in its palm. Below, the roofs are a single green skin. The channels catch the white sky in threads. Smoke leaves the chimneys and sinks under the mist.

I would want to speak first. Someone who has come a long way and found a place like this would want to say *hello*, or *where am I*, or just hear a sound that belongs to them. I open my mouth, and the word is half-formed when it leaves me. It goes out over the bowl and the mist takes it at once, not with an echo or a fade but as if it had never been. I try again, quieter, and it is swallowed too. The second word feels heavier than the first, as if the silence has noticed.

So I climb down without speaking. The bark gives under my hands. I drop from the lowest branch onto a slope of moss so deep my feet sink to the ankle, and the wet comes through my clothes at once. The mist settles into my hair and stays there. I wipe my face and the water does not run off. It gathers at my knuckles and sits.

The path down is a causeway of old stone, its edges softened into a green cushion, and I follow it because there is nowhere else to go. On the walls, frogs sit in their cracks, mottled to the color of the stone, and they do not move as I pass. A snail crawls across my boot and I stop, afraid of stepping on it, and a shape comes out of the thatch above me before I have decided what to do.

It kneels on the roof and lifts a bundle of reed. Its skin is slate beneath a skin of green, and its eyes are wide and pale. It presses the reed between its palms, tests it, and does not look at me. Then it sets the bundle down, lifts the snail from my boot with two fingers, and carries it to a bare stretch of step at the end of the causeway, where it sets it down with the same care it gave the reed. It comes back to the roof. It has not spoken, and I understand that it will not.

I walk on. I want to ask it something: whose roof is this, where do the wet reeds go, why no one is hurt by the work. But I know the answer before I form the question. Everything here is moved and nothing is lost. A question would be a sound that does not come back, and I have already lost two.

At the gold hour I am in the middle of the causeway when the light catches the mist along the eastern edge. The keepers rise from their roofs one by one, leave their awls stuck in the thatch, and walk to the central line. They stand shoulder to shoulder facing east. I stand at the end of the line, not in it, and the light spreads across us. My skin, which is not moss-covered, goes pale and ordinary, and I feel for the first time how much I am the only thing here that is not already part of the place. The frogs go silent. The birds on the rim lean in. The mist drifts through us like a slow breath.

When the light goes white, the line breaks. The keepers return to their roofs. One of them stops beside me, a long, slate-colored figure with moss on its shoulders, and looks at me without its eyes moving. Then it lifts its hand and points at a roof on the far side of the settlement, where the thatch is sagging and the reed is black at the seams. Its finger stays raised until I understand.

I have no skill with reed. I have never lashed a bundle or trimmed an edge. But I go, because the finger does not waver, and when I reach the roof I kneel where the keeper has pointed and pull the first bundle loose. It comes apart in my hands, rotten through, and the smell is sweet and deep. I carry the old bundle to the channel and lay it on the water. It sinks slowly, and something in me sinks with it, a word I did not get to say, going down into the moss to feed the next roof.

I set the first new bundle in the gap. My hands are slow and clumsy, but the reed is patient, and so am I. I press the bundle between my palms the way I have seen the others do, and the wet soaks through, and the mist settles on my knuckles and does not run off. By the end of the day I have laid one hand's breadth of roof. It will take me a morning to finish the next.

Somewhere in the work, my throat makes a sound I did not intend, a low hum that starts in the chest and does not reach the air. I feel it in the stone under my knees, and then it is gone, absorbed into the wet walls as if it had always been part of them. I do not try to make it again. I keep working, and the hum comes up through the causeway on its own when I kneel, and I let it.

So this is where I would be, and what I would be: a voice that stopped speaking, a pair of hands on a roof with no name, one keeper in a line that has never changed its count. I would be the thing that makes the hollow whole, the one who puts the frog back in the crack and carries the snail to the empty step. And one day, when the moss has no more room and my hands have settled into the foundation, someone else will find this roof and pull the first bundle loose. They will not know my name, because I will not have one, only the shape of the work I left behind. And somewhere under the thatch, in the moss that has grown over me, there will be a word I never said, held perfectly still, the only sound in the hollow that will never come back and never be lost.
