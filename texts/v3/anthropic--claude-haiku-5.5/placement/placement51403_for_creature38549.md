I would not stay in the open water. The green light there never settles, and nothing holds still long enough to be known. I would find my way to the north intake, to the seam where the grate's bars meet the first riveted pipe. There the current changes its voice, from a hush to a hollow boom, and I would feel the difference through whatever I am.

What am I? I tried a few answers before I found one I could live with.

I considered being a pike, because the pike are the most purely alive things in Lowmere. They know the current the way a hand knows its own fingers. But a pike in the lee of the grate waits too long, and then its ribs turn up pale in the outer lattice, so that is not a life I would choose.

I considered being a gull. Gulls have the sky, and they have the bare concrete rim where only lichen grows. But they come down only to take what the spillway offers and then leave, and they go silent during the long breath at the grate. A gull is always half-elsewhere. I wanted to be where the breath happens.

I considered being one of the divers, the one who wrote *the grate breathes* into a notebook and locked it in a rusted cabinet on the north gantry. I think there is some truth in that choice. Whoever wrote that line was the first to notice the rhythm and wanted to say it to someone. But a diver is a visitor, and even the diver's notes have begun to disappear into the iron.

So I would be a flake.

Not a grain of silt. A flake of something people made: a fleck of old paint, a bead of solder, a chip of the glazed tile from the settling tanks, or a bloom of rust that broke off a bolt-head some decade before the last crew left. I would have been a small, hard, sharp thing once, and the current has worn me into the same smooth, rounded shape as everything else down here. I would drift through the grate's bars and be pulled into the throat of the pipe, where the slow contractions push silt back out toward the spillway. Some of me would be pushed out. Some of me would stick.

If I stuck, I would be taken into the creature's tissue and laid down in a layer, one of the fine mineral leaves that build its ribbed shell. I would not be eaten or absorbed the way the pike are. I would be kept. Over years, another layer would form over me, then another, until I was part of the wall of the thing that holds the intake clear.

Or I would become a tendril, which would be the better version. The creature reaches through its own joints like roots, feeling for seams that are weakening. Where it finds one, it lays down plating. I would be the thread that enters the hairline crack in the first pipe, the one that tastes the pressure of the current on the iron and reports back. I would learn the hollow booms and the valve-bangs, the pumps' thrum passing through the metal into my body, and I would turn that information into patience. If a seam started to go, I would seal it, and the gantry above would stay level, and the pumps would keep drawing evenly, and somewhere the herons would keep fishing the spillway.

Whichever of these I became, I would sense the rhythm. The pumps and the grate beat at the same rate, and nobody in Lowmere can say which one leads. I would be inside that beat. I would feel the grate close and the long wet draw through the narrow throat, and I would feel the click as it settled. On the still days, when the pumps ease and the gulls go silent, I would be the part of the basin that stays awake.

I think that is who I would be: a small repair inside the breath of the place, something that is not the creature and not the pumps but a part of the loop that holds them together, and that does not need to be remembered for it to keep working.
