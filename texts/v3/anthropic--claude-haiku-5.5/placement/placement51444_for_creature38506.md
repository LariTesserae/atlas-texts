I would find myself at the edge where the mat gives out.

On the eastern rim, below a cushion of fronds about the size of a sleeping dog, a seep rises out of a crack in the stone, runs a few body-lengths over bare rock, and sinks back under the green. The meltwater has scoured the ground there down to grey. Where it pools and slows, the rock has been ground to flour, and the flour has mixed with years of rotted fronds into a paste the color of wet cement. I am in that paste. I am a very small thing with many legs and no particular interest in being seen, and I am also a bead of water that has been in the paste for a while.

The first thing I notice is the weight. Down here nothing is heavy, but everything is pressed close. Each grain of rock-flour has a film of water around it, and I move through the films the way a swimmer moves through a crowd, pushing grain aside and feeling it settle behind me. The paste is warm in a way that surprises me. The sun is not reaching me directly. The warmth comes from the decay, from the slow work of breaking down frond-stuff that the mat has grown and cannot digest on its own.

That is my job, I think, or the thing I am for. I take the dead tissue apart. I eat the fibers the mat has no mouth for, and I leave behind what the mat can use: the phosphorus and iron and the thin dissolved sugars. I carry them along the tunnels I make, and where my tunnels reach the root hairs of the nearest cushion, the minerals pass up into the green. I do not know that I am doing this. It is more that the pull of the roots is a sort of hunger I feel in my body, and I follow it, and the following makes the tunnel.

Sometimes the seep swells. The water comes up the crack in a rush, and the paste goes soft and loose, and for a while I am carried. I let go of the grain I am holding and ride a slow current of silt down a few centimeters, then lodge against a stone and wait. Once I am carried far enough I might end up under a different cushion, in a different tangle of roots, and the mat up there will draw me in. My water will rise through a stem, out through a frond, into the air as fog, and some of it will condense on a steel ladder and run down a rung and drip off the end into a boot print on the plateau. I will not know any of this. But the plateau will.

When the sun comes through the murk and warms the surface, the grey paste dries a little at the top, and I go down, deeper into the cool. Down there the water is slower and the rock is older. I can feel the pressure of the whole upland above me, the weight of every penstock and every cushion and every stone of the scree field that is still deciding where it will fall. It does not feel like an enemy. It feels like the ground taking its time.

If I try to think about what I am, I come up against the same thing the plateau does. I am not a single creature with a single edge. I am a worker, and a tunnel, and a drop of water in motion, and the tiny cold moment of a fleck of rock-flour turning in its film. The mat is doing the same thing at a larger scale. We are one slow process at different sizes, and the process has no hurry, and I have none either.

The steel post with the painted arrow still points at nothing, and I understand now why. There is no place on the Seep Shelf that is the heart of it. You do not reach the center. You are inside it already, in the paste, in the pull of the roots, in the seep that rises and falls on its own schedule, carving the rim one centimeter at a time.
