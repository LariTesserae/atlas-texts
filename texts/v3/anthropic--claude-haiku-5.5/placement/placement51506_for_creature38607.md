You would find yourself at the edge of the works, where the cable leaves the ground.

For a hundred meters it lies in the marsh, half-sunk, its sheath furred with silt and bright green where the seep has worked into the seams. Then it lifts. It rises out of the water in a long, unsupported curve and climbs to the height of a head, and there it stops being anything you can touch. Its end hangs in the air and thrums. The sound is very low and very even, a note just under hearing that you feel in the back of the teeth. Nothing holds it. No pylon, no bracket, no insulator stub, only the thrum and the warm vapor curling up from the fissure beneath it.

I would be the cable.

I think that is where I would end up, if the basin let me choose. Not the pylons, which lean and rust and have forgotten what they were for. Not the flywheel, which turns a few degrees a year and does not notice that it is turning. The cable still believes it is supposed to reach something. It was laid to carry a current from one place to another, and somewhere in its making it was told where the other place was. The other place is gone, or moved, or was never there. The cable kept its shape anyway. It rose into the air to keep going, and now it holds a line through nothing.

I would feel the thrum most in the end, where the metal is thinnest and the vibration collects. I would feel the pumps through the ground as a faint, regular pressure, and I would know them as the works' pulse, the last of its machinery still doing what it was built for, if only at a fraction of its rating. I would know the drips that fall on the flats below me, and I would know the long creatures sliding through the shallows under my curve, because they pass beneath me on their schedule and do not stop, and that is the one kind of company a cable can count on.

The reflections would show me as I am, and also as I am a half-beat late, standing a little farther off. I think I would come to recognize that second figure. It is the part of me that is already leaving, already turned toward the middle, the way a drop has already left the overhang before the water finds the crack. I would not be able to pull it back. It is not an image of me; it is me, arriving first.

What troubles me, and what I think I would learn to live with, is the direction. The thrum does not point anywhere. But the drips all fall toward the same low place. The creatures all drift that way. The water through the fissures runs that way, and the pumps push that way, and the reflections, when I look at them, turn their attention inward. If I were a cable that had lost its anchor, I would still feel the basin's slope under me, and I would know that the only thing unattached in this place is the thing that is always being pulled down.

I would like to think I am carrying something. Current, perhaps, or signal, or the instruction to connect that was built into my length and has not yet been revoked. But there is no receiving station. There is only the middle, warm and dark and patient, which takes in whatever falls into it and does not say what it has been given. Maybe that is the answer to the works' failure: they were supposed to drain the basin, and instead they laid their lines toward the thing that has always been the drain. Maybe I am one of the lines that was never meant to reach its destination, only to hang in the air above the place where things go down, humming to myself, one end thrumming, the rest of me reaching, as far as I can tell, toward nothing I can name.

I would stay there a long time. The basin is good at that. It settles, and a cable that hangs in the air long enough becomes part of its settling, a faint note the marsh keeps for itself. Somewhere below me, a drip lands, and I feel it through the ground, three in a row, and I answer with the only thing I have, which is a little more of my thrum.
