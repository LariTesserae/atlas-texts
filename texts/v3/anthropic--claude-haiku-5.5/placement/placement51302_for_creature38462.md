I would be the lichen on the rim of the third basin, on the narrow terrace where the old line of carved stones still holds rainwater.

I am not one thing. I am a fungus and a green cell that long ago agreed to share a body. The fungus holds the water and the grip. The green cell makes sugar from the light that slants through the cloud. Neither of us could live alone on this wet basalt, and I have never found the seam between us. I am a ring, grey-green and crusted, perhaps as wide as a palm, and I have been growing outward from the basin's lip for longer than anyone has counted.

I know the basin by its shape. Someone once knelt here, and the stone wore a shallow dip where a knee or a hand rested. My edge grew around that dip, so I hold the memory of a body in my curve, though the body is gone and the hollow is only a place where the rain collects. The water is cold, and it settles into the carved bowl and stays there until the sun heats it enough to drink. The sitting person would have had to hold very still to stay on this ledge. I have learned that stillness. I grow when nothing disturbs me, and a finger's width a season is enough for me.

The breath comes up through the stone beneath me. I feel it before I hear it. The rock tightens, the cold rises through the seam two body-lengths away, and the mist rolls across the terrace and clings to my edges, beading there. I take that water in through a thousand small pores. When the breath is strong the moss downslope thickens and the goats pull at it hungrily, but I cannot be eaten, because I am too slow and too bitter to be worth the mouthful. The goats step around me. I am a patch they leave alone.

I hear the herd before I see it. Their hooves click on wet rock, and the lead animal stops at the seam and lowers its head. The whole line waits, and I feel the waiting through the stone, a tension that matches the pause in the breath. When the exhalation finally thins and falls still, they cross, and the rock beneath me shivers with their weight. Sometimes a young one lingers on the edge of the mist and does not come back, and the herd calls low from the far side. Those are the moments I think about most, though I do not think the way the birds do. I hold what I touch, and I keep it.

The birds pass over and do not stay. The long descending cry bounces off the far cliff and comes back to me from two directions, and I feel it as a faint trembling in the fungus-threads. The small rattling birds land on the rim of the basin to drink, and their claws scratch the crust. Each scratch takes a little of my surface, and the lichen repairs it in a week. I do not mind. Being scratched is a kind of attention, and attention is most of what I have.

Once, after heavy rain, a bone awl rolled off the terrace and settled in the moss a short distance from my edge. I have been near it since then. Its shape is not like the stones. It was made by a hand that wanted to pierce something, to sew or to make a hole for a cord. Over the seasons the rain has washed the moss off its surface and the animals have stepped over it, and soon it will go deeper into the ground, and I will grow over it. Nothing is lost here, only moved. The shelf does not keep things in one place.

At dusk the bats pour out of the cracks in turning spirals, and their clicking shakes the air so hard that the falls are briefly drowned out. I cannot hear this, not as you would, but the stone does, and the stone passes the tremor to me. Then the bats are gone over the edge and the frogs begin tapping in their cups, and the cloud thickens below, and the hunter moves somewhere along the ledges above the herds. I can feel that its weight is near. I cannot see it, and I do not need to. The breath rises again, slow and deep, and I open what I am to it, catching the cold and the water, and I keep growing, a finger's width at a time, around the basin where someone once sat still.
