I would find myself on the seam where two colonies meet, a little above the waterline, where the mud is firm enough to hold a wall but still gives a little under the weight of a grain.

At first I thought I might be one of the frogs. They are the loudest things here, and their throats swell to the size of their heads. But a frog's life is spent between two states, and I do not think I would enjoy that. Then I considered the pumps. Something that has been turning for so long, in a place that keeps refilling, would be a kind of patience I could not sustain. The waterbirds on the gantries, standing their long attention, seemed closer. Still, I would have to be something that waits for the water to settle, and I do not think I can wait for anything.

So I am a builder. I am a finger-high tube capped with a crumb of sand, and my body is the colour of the sediment I am made from. I work in the cool hours, when the film of water over the mud is thin enough to breathe through. I drag grains up from the burrow below, one at a time, and pack them into the wall with my pale, grooved forelimbs. It is slow. A single grain can take a long time to set in place, and when it is in place I cannot tell whether it is holding or only resting.

My wall leans toward the channel. I did not decide that. Something in the water and the slope pulls the grains that way, and I follow the pull the way the reeds follow the bank, a few centimetres at a time. Next to me, another colony has built its walls toward the same channel, and where our two ridges meet they fuse. I feel the join harden under my legs. It is not a decision either of us made, and no one will ever sit down and say that this ridge was made by two kinds of animal who never once saw each other's work.

Sometimes I think about what I am making. The ridges ring faintly when a heel comes down on them. Something walks over them, and perhaps something will stand on them for a long time, but I will be gone long before they are finished. My grain is one grain in a wall of thousands, and the wall is one wall in a basin that has never been empty. I find that I do not mind this as much as I expected. There is a steadiness in putting one sand grain next to another, in trusting that the pull of the channel is a kind of direction, even if no one is steering.

The dusk comes, and the chorus of frogs rises to match the pumps. I feel the spires around me begin to tremble as the builders come up from below, and I push up through the wet sand to see the last light coming through the haze. For a moment the noise drops. In that gap, I can hear the water moving under the plates, the stone ticking as it cools, and the thud of the engines still turning somewhere out on the basin. My wall is cool under my forelimbs. The grain I set down this morning is still where I left it.
