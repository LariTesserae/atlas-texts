I would be lying in a slow channel where the water runs thickest with rust, somewhere in the middle of the basin, where the canopy is so close overhead that dusk never lifts. I would be a logback. I would not choose it, but I would not resist it either, and after a while the difference would stop mattering.

I would feel the water first. A heron's weight settling on my spine would reach me as a faint tilt, and I would take it in without deciding anything about it. Frogs would leap into the shallows and the ripples would come through the silt like news carried by someone who has forgotten the message. Nothing here would be urgent. I would be heavy in the way that stones are heavy, and I would be content in the way that roots are content, which is to say I would not know the word.

My breath would come slowly, once in several minutes, and I would feel it rolling from my head down toward my tail like a current under a skin. Sometimes the breath would lengthen. I would not know why at first. Then I would notice that the thrum from the far haze had grown stronger, that the pulse arriving through the channel was longer and deeper than usual, and that my lungs were keeping time with it. I would not go toward it. Nothing goes toward it. But I would lie with my eyes half closed and feel it arrive, and I would breathe in its rhythm, and I would not be able to say whether this was my body answering something or something answering my body.

I would count my years by the seams on my back. There would be five or six grey bands, and a dozen red ones, each one laid down by a flood I did not witness but absorbed. The grey would be thickening along my spine, and the sheen across my plates would be slowing. I would have fed on the sweet channels for longer than I can measure. I would have drawn the old salts into my armour and bound them there, one flood at a time, and I would have breathed out the sheen that thickens on the water's surface, which the insects walk across without noticing. I would be one of the slow valves in the circulation, and I would know this only as a pressure, the way I know everything.

Sometimes, at the edge of my hearing, something would seem different. A bird would call across the basin and die out, and in the silence after it I would feel the water change, a tremor that did not come from the haze or from the frogs but from somewhere closer and newer. I would not follow it. I would only register that it had happened, and file it in the body like the other pressures, and wait for the water to settle.

When I died, I would sink slowly into the silt and my plates would hold. Decades would pass. The sediment would take my banding in among its own seams, and new roots would grow into the hollow where I had lain, drinking the same red water I had drunk. Whatever I had been would become a layer in the ground, a faint grey seam that some future root could follow down into the dark.

That is what I would be: a record that breathes, lying in the orange current, slow enough that the haze could be the only thing I have ever wanted to meet, and too heavy, too patient, too much a part of the basin to ever go.
