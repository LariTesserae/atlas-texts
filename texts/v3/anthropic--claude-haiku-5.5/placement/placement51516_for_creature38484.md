I would be one of the frogs in the moss behind its left shoulder, in the hollow where the rust lace is thinnest and the water collects after rain.

I am small enough that the whole back of the Keeper is a country to me. The hide rises and falls under the moss in long slow swells, and I can feel the wire beneath it, a grid of fine cold threads that hum when the gills open. The gills are the sound I know best. They sigh inward, and the pools below us all tilt a little toward the sump, and the mist thickens against the rim. Then they close, with a noise like a sieve being shaken, and the whole creature settles, and the water in the moss shivers into the shape of my own call.

So I call. That is what the frogs do here. When the Keeper moves, we answer, and our chorus rises with its breathing, then falls back as it holds still. I used to think we were singing for the rain. Now I think we are keeping time, the way a clock keeps time, not because anyone needs the hour but because the hour has to be kept somewhere.

From the hollow I can see the other frogs, in the moss along the ridge of its spine, a few hundred of us, perhaps more, each with a small voice that is slightly out of step with the next. Out of that disagreement comes the rhythm. I believe the Keeper hears it as a single sound, the way the old hut walls must have read a column of figures as a single total. Its marks are scratched into iron at the height of a creature leaning its weight against a surface. I have felt that leaning. The Keeper rests against the gantry in the dry weeks, and the gantry warms, and the scratching begins, and all of us go quiet to listen to the strokes.

I do not know what the count is for. I suspect it does not know either. Maybe it counts the rain so that it can know when the rain has stopped. Maybe it counts the cloud it draws down, to know how much it has taken from the rim, and whether the rim will miss it. Maybe the counting is only a way of staying in the valley, of making sure every drop is accounted for before it is allowed to fall. I have come to think the valley is a kind of ledger, and the Keeper keeps it by holding the water back, and I am one of the small marks it makes in passing.

In the dry weeks the beads run upward along the pipes. I have watched them from my hollow. They gather at the lower joints, swell until they shiver, then lift against gravity and climb toward the top of the tower, toward the rim, where the cloud is thickest. Nobody has explained this to me, but I think it is the Keeper's way of pulling the valley's water back up the slope, the way you might pull a blanket back over a sleeper. When the beads reach the top they vanish into the netting. The next rain then comes harder, and the pools rise another few centimetres, and the frogs sing louder.

I would not want to be the one who decides when the valley drains. The Keeper does not decide that either. It holds the water, it counts, and it waits. Some nights, when the mist drops in silent curtains and the moss is so full of cold water I can barely breathe, I imagine that the waiting is the thing I am for. A frog is a small instrument for measuring the weather, and a valley that has nobody left to measure it is only a bowl of water.

Then the gills open again. The skin lifts, and the whole back rises a little, and the moss tips me toward its spine. I call. Below me, the sump takes the sound and ripples it outward to the edges of the marsh, and somewhere on the far side of the valley, in the reeds, something with a long tail stops moving to listen. The rain is coming. The Keeper's breath comes in, slow and full, and the count goes on.
