I considered a few places first. I could be the frost on the upper leaves, a mirror for an hour before the glare burns me off. I could be the rain that gathers over the eastern slope and walks across the floor like a slow animal. I could be a column, pale and hollow, taking in cold sap and slowly leaning toward the shaft. I could be the one who carved the notches, counting something I didn't yet understand. Each of these would let me see the Reach from a useful angle, but none of them would let me feel it the way it has to be felt, which is through the feet.

So I am a hatchling, in a heap of collapsed columns on the north side of the basin, under a thick mat of fungus that opened overnight.

It is warm here, and the warmth is the first thing I know. The fibre around me is soft and sags when I push against it, and it is still giving off heat from whatever it was before it fell. Above me is the bloom, a pale cap that lets in a little light, enough to show me the difference between up and down but not enough to show me much else. The light is wrong, too harsh and white, and I turn away from it toward the wet dark between the stalks.

What I have is my feet. Three toes, soft, not yet webbed but beginning to thicken at the pads. When I move, the felt gives under me, and I learn quickly that some of it gives more than the rest. One place sinks and holds the water like a bowl. Another place pushes back, firm and springy, and I want to go there. I stop and lift one foot and hold it still, and the mat hums under me, or I hum into it, and for a moment I cannot tell which. The sound moves through my body and back into the stalks, and nearby the columns tremble in step.

The adult is somewhere above me. I know it by the hum, which is deeper than mine, and by the way the dripping slows when it is close. I do not know if it is calling me or simply breathing through the mat. I think it does not matter to me which. I know that when the sound comes near, the heap is quieter, and my body settles into the warmth the way the mat settles over a fallen column.

There are other things under the mat. I can feel them through my soles: small movements, slow shifts, the grinding of fibre as the heap reorganizes around the decay. Somewhere a column creaks, and the creak comes up through the felt like a distant tree-crack, and the whole heap shudders, and the warmth moves with it. I do not know that the fall was partly caused by a bite taken by the adult the night before. I know only that the ground changed, and that I must find the firm places again.

When I am strong enough I will leave the heap. The fungus will rot away around me, and the new stalks sprouting from the rot will go up through the bloom, and the place I came from will be just another patch of pale green and rust. I will go out into the mist and the low hours, and I will learn to walk slowly on the felt, pausing often, watching the bright edge of the shaft for clouds. I will learn which mats hold and which swallow. I will learn that the cropped tips of the stalks are for eating and the old standing stalks are for leaving.

I do not know yet who has been watching. I think someone has, though. There are marks on the lower stalks where the adult leans, and I feel the smoothness of the bark when I pass, and I think of it as a kind of path, one that other bodies have worn too. Something counted the visits once, in notches now lost above the reach of anything below. Maybe something will count mine.

The neck is rising somewhere above me. I can feel it through the mat, the long slow lift of weight, the columns parting without breaking. I hold still and listen through my feet, and the basin holds its breath with me, and then the hum begins again, low enough to feel and not quite to hear.
