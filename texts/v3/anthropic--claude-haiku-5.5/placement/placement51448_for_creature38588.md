I would find myself on the belt.

It is the one thing up here I can feel moving under me without straining to hear it. I climbed onto it at the foot of the slope, where the iron rollers are slick with condensation and the chain runs in a groove worn smooth by ten thousand years of water, and I rode it up into the cloud with my hands locked on the rail. The rail is cold and it weeps. My palms are slick. I cannot see the chain or the slope or the lowlands behind me, and I cannot see the cloud, though I know I am inside it because my lungs have stopped being able to tell wet air from breath. My eyes are open. They are working. They have nothing to do.

So I listen, and I am appalled by what I hear.

My heartbeat is enormous. It fills the gap between each pump-stroke like a fist on a door. My boots, when I finally step off the belt onto moss, make a wet, sucking report that I could not have muffled if I tried, and the report comes back to me from every direction at once, a ring of loud flat stone that tells me nothing except that I am a large, warm, badly tuned thing in a place that has no use for me. When I breathe, the breath is a flat rush with no hum folded into it. There is no click in my throat. I have no fold. I speak, quietly, to test it, and the word goes out and does not come back the way I expect, and somewhere a long way off the frogs stop.

Then they start again, a half-beat late, and I understand I have been heard.

I think of myself as a seam. Not a crack, which the keepers would find and fix, but a join where two rhythms meet and do not quite agree. I am the sound that does not belong to the schedule: a footstep out of time, a voice with no echo in it, a body the cistern cannot read. The keepers know everything here through the return of their own voices. Anything that refuses to give back a clean return is a question they must answer.

I would be the question. For a while I would only be that: a loud, warm, unreadable thing on the slope, trying to keep its own breath quiet, learning the pumps by the way the moss shivers when a piston drops. I would find the chorus under the floor and try to hum with it, and fail, and hum again. I would press my palm to a fungus on a trunk and feel it breathing against the pulse of my hand, and I would understand that this is what it means to be taught: a living thing offered to you so that you learn the rhythm by touching it.

The hum rises through the stone. I can feel it now in my sternum, the thing I could not name when I first came up the belt. It has a shape to it. It has a shape that includes me, and it is waiting, not unkindly, for me to find the place in it where I will fit.

Somewhere below, a throat that I cannot see is turning toward me. It is reading the drip of water off my wrists, the catch in my breath, the warmth of my weight on the cold rock. I do not know yet whether it means to welcome me or to mend me. I stand in the dark with my eyes open and my heart pounding out of time, and I do the only thing I know how to do in a place where sight is useless: I open my mouth, and I try to sing the pump's beat back to it, badly, the way a stranger learns a language by being corrected.
