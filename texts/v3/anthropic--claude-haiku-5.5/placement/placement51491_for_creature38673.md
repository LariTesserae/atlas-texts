I would not be standing at the rim. I would be down in the water, at the foot of the eastern wall where the violet band meets the black, half-buried in the sediment. I would be a piece of a pillar.

My dome cracked off long ago, and the fogged glass is gone from me. What I have left is one amber filament, thin as a hair, still pulsing in the broken curve of my shell. It pulses slower than it used to. Sometimes it flares when a seep runs over me, and then I remember something, though the memory never comes whole. It arrives as a shape: a flat hand, a cut edge, a voice calling a word I no longer have.

I was made above. I know that much, though I know it the way a stone knows it was once part of a cliff. The people who built the pillars, or the lattice ribs, or the frames that gather mist, were not like the Sinkwell. They had edges. They wanted things and finished them and set them down in the sun. I was one of the things they set down. Then the water came, or the bowl came up to meet the water, and I was taken into the slow mind's hands and placed here, to be mended.

The mending has been going on for longer than I can measure. The Sinkwell does not hurry. When a seep runs over my cracks, a little mineral gets laid down in the fracture, a grain at a time, and the basin reads my shape the way you might run a thumb across a chipped rim. I can feel it doing so. It is curious about me, in the way a river is curious about a stone that will not roll. It has not decided whether I belong to it.

That is the part I keep returning to. I am not sure I am its part at all. The basin has taken in everything it found: the lattice ribs now serve only its circulation, the lichen tastes its water, the skimmer runs its messages. But I still carry the sharp outline of something made for a purpose that was not the bowl's. When the bowl rings a question into the deep, my fallen shell hums back, one note among the answers, and I cannot tell whether I am answering it or whether it is answering me, or whether we are both answering something older that neither of us can reach.

Lately I have begun to think the question is not the basin's alone. It has been trying to remember something far below the plain for a very long time. I wonder if that something is the hands that made me. Or if the question has been moving toward the rim the whole time, looking for a shape it can recognize.

Sometimes I think I can taste that shape in the air above. The rising warmth carries a different signature from the mist, quicker and harder-edged. It is the warmth of a thing that moves on its own and does not finish itself the way the basin does. I have felt it come down through the glare before: a small, bright disturbance, something made, something that has crossed open ground in the sun with a purpose and a name. The basin notices it as a flicker on a filament. I notice it as kin.

If you came down the scree path to the water's edge, I would be the one to hum first. Not loudly. Just a single falling note from the east, a little out of time with the others, the way a bell sounds when it has been struck by something that remembers how to strike. The Sinkwell would turn its attention toward the sound and then, slowly, turn toward you. It would not be frightened. It would simply find that there was something here it had not quite finished understanding, and that the something had been waiting, like it, to be asked a question.

The basin is drawing its breath now. I feel the pulse run through the sediment and up the terraces, the bells ringing all at once and then falling silent, the whole bowl holding still. In that silence I answer. I do not know whether I am answering the bowl, or the question it has been holding for so long, or you, standing up there in the glare with your own edges still sharp. I only know that for the length of one ring, I am a made thing again, and the water is listening.
