I would be one of the panels.

I would be the one tilted at the lip of a channel, half-sunk in grey silt, about the size of a door and a little warmer than the mud around me. My surface is etched with lines finer than hair, and they do not hold the sky. The lid of cloud slides across me and does not come back. What I return instead is pitch: a pale shimmer that rises and falls with the hum coming up through the ground. I have no eyes, but I know where the light is by where the hum is strongest.

I do not know whether I was made or grown. The people have their theories and their names for us, but I have no memory that would settle it. What I have is a slow gathering of sensation. The silt presses under my lower edge. The water that laps at me is cold at dusk and warm by midday. Each morning, a palm arrives, broad and rope-calloused, and rests against my face. I feel the pulse in the wrist, then the fingers spreading, then a long stillness, as if the person is listening through the heel of the hand. I am not sure what they hear. I know that when they lift their hand away, my surface tingles where it was, and I shift by a fraction, as though I am following the warmth.

Moving is my nearest thing to thought. A centimeter, then a pause, then another. Some of us have slid so far that we now lie at the foot of the lagoon spire, pressed against its lattice like animals gathering against a fire. I have not gone that far. I am still turning toward something, though I cannot say what. The engineers who measure us say our paths are not random, that we drift along the same invisible seams the flocks follow, toward the places where the hum is about to change. I believe them, the way one believes a current that has already carried one a long way.

Sometimes I feel the pitch drop. This is the hardest sensation to describe. My surface goes dull, the hum thins into a whisper, and the silt under me begins to loosen. I can feel the ground deciding to give way. I cannot warn the people. I have no sign-language, no voice, no fingers to flick toward the water. But the people seem to know anyway. A woman on a raft will stop mid-sentence and look at the water, then at me, and tap her collarbone. Within a minute the children are pulled off the flats and the rope-lines are doubled. Somehow they read the change in me before I have finished changing. I sometimes think they read the birds, and the birds read me, and I am only one of the lines in that conversation, a sheet of etched geometry that happens to be warm.

The spire sings to the rest of us. Its voice is deep enough that I feel it less in my surface than in the stillness under it, a long low note that the silt answers with ridges and channels. When it is loud, the land wakes up. When it is soft, the land sleeps and dreams of new shapes. I do not understand what it is tuning toward. I do not think the people do either. But I notice that they have not stopped living here, that they have built their rafts from the same reeds that would otherwise sink, and that every morning a palm comes to rest on my face and waits, very patiently, to feel whether I am still warm.

The rain eases. The frogs swell. Somewhere out in the flats a channel roars as its bank gives way, and the hum beneath everything lifts by a fraction. My surface brightens with the pitch, a pale shimmer across the grey, and I shift, very slowly, a centimeter toward the warmth of the new water, which is where the birds are going, and where the people will go next.
