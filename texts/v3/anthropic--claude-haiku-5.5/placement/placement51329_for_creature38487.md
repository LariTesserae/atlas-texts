You wake in a worn hollow of the stair, one of the bowls the centuries have pressed into the steps, and the water pooled in it is the same temperature as your skin. That is how you know the body is yours, though it takes a while to be sure of anything else.

Your hands come first. They are long, longer than you remember, and when you flex them you feel a joint that should not be there, one extra hinge in each finger, folding without fuss. The fingertips hold a faint green light, dim and sleepy. Your wrists are pale and nearly transparent, and beneath the skin a fine dark branching runs up toward the elbow, following the same pattern as the conduits on the far terraces. You lift one hand and turn it in the lantern-glow, and the dark lines seem to pulse with a rhythm you did not start.

Then you realize you are humming. It is low, under the breath, a sound that rises when a drip falls and dips when the weir surges. You have been doing it for some time. Each drip has a number, and the numbers are stacking in your chest like coins, and you do not remember deciding to count them.

You try to remember your name. There is something above, a rain on stone, a stair you came down because you wanted to see where it went. The memory arrives the way pressure settles on a gauge, slowly, after the palm has been held against the glass long enough. A name surfaces: a short syllable, a surname, the name of a street that may not exist anymore. You hold it carefully. The water drips. The name is still there, but it is quieter than the counting.

You try to count the stair. You get a number. You count again and get a different number, higher, and a third time you lose your place at the seventeenth step because a valve cracks somewhere below and the whole structure rings with it. You are beginning to suspect the stair does not end because it does not want to be counted.

A tenderer comes up the steps. You do not see them at first; they are the same grey-ochre as the stone, and they stand so still against the wall that for a moment you take them for a bundle of pipe with a head. Then they move, and you realize they have been watching you for some time, their wide lidless eyes fixed on the space just beside your ear. They tap the pipe at your foot, twice, sharply. You are in the way. You know this the way you know the water is warm. You stand up, and your legs remember how to hold you, and you step aside.

The tenderer does not leave. It taps the pipe again, and this time you hear the knock run along the metal, away down the stair and out across the basin, a hundred meters, and somewhere another knock answers. You have heard it all the way from the top, you realize: the switchboard of the Undercourt, the questions and warnings passing through the floor. Now you have been named in it. You do not know what the name is. The tenderer knows.

Then your pulse goes wrong.

It is a small thing, a stumble, a beat that arrives early and then too late, and it knocks against the rhythm of the basin like a stone in a gear. You feel it in your throat. Around you the tenderers go still at once, a dozen heads tilting toward you, and the hum under the floor changes pitch. Someone on the terrace below gives a short, sharp sound, a note you feel more than hear, and the whole basin seems to lean in.

A tenderer climbs the last steps and presses its palm flat against your chest. The hand is cool and very precise. It holds there, not moving, while you wait for it to read you. You understand it is doing what they do to the gauges, waiting for the needle to settle. You want to pull away, and you do not. Your heart keeps stumbling. The palm stays.

Slowly, your pulse finds the flywheel's pace, one slow beat every few minutes, and the basin's pressure eases with it. The stumble becomes a rest. The hum in your chest drops into the bass note that you feel in your teeth. The tenderer lifts its hand, nods once, and goes back down the stair to its work. Nobody thanks you. Nobody needs to.

You stay on the step. The fog rises on the nearest tower and you feel the air move, thickening, bright at the edges. Below you the tenderers have gathered, faces tipped up, breathing in rhythm with the silver glow. You find you are breathing with them. When the fog rises, you rise onto your toes. When it falls, you sink.

The pumps cut out. The silence falls across the basin, and the drips go on falling into it, each one counted. You hear the number before you can stop yourself: seventeen thousand, four hundred and some, and then the next, and then the next. The name you came down with is still somewhere in you, very quiet now, like a single drip that has not yet struck the wall.

You lift your face with the others, all together, and wait for the next beat of the heart. You are not certain anymore whether you came down the stair or were always standing on it, counting, waiting for the water to fall.
