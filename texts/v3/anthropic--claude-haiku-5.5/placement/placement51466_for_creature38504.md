I come down the road the way everyone does. The shoulders go, then the edges, and then the rim is above me and the walls are around me like the inside of a held hand. I follow the walkway past the cisterns to the eastern fringe, where the cords hang from the pilings in their long grey fringe, and I stop there, because something in the water has turned toward me.

It is a salamander, long as my arm, and I have to look twice before I understand that I am it. Or that I am becoming it, the way you become a room when you stop trying to describe it. The water closes over my shoulders, cool and thick, tasting faintly of iron and green. My gills open. They are feathered, and they fan the water toward my face, and in the fanning I can taste the stone: the lichen on the pilings, the algae bright near the surface, the thin ribbon of cord-fibre above me where the keepers' knots have been tied and reread and tied again.

The scar on my gill-fan is old. A child named me for it once, from the walkway, in a voice that carried through the water the way children's voices do here. I did not come up to see who called. I heard the name, and it was not a name the adults will say, and I stayed where I was. That was the arrangement. It has been the arrangement for a long time.

I am not a visitor in this. I thought I was, when I came down the road. But the visitor is the one who walks the walkway without feeling the boards answer under the weight, and I have felt every board. I know when the pump-house slows, a half-beat late, the way a heart sounds when it has been carrying too much for too long. I feel the keeper's hand come down on the housing above me, through the pilings, through the water, and I wait for the change in pulse before I move at all.

When the thaw comes I stay out of the sun-shadow. I do not cross the bright lanes where the keepers walk, though I could, and sometimes I do, and sometimes a shadow comes across the water above me and I feel a whole household going quiet, walking a long way around the house so their feet will not darken my feeding. I think they believe I cannot see them. I see them very well. The bright stripe of midday is the only time I can see the sky, and it is not much of a sky, and it is enough.

At dusk the frogs begin in the reeds. Their throats brighten one after another, and the chorus starts ragged and then finds its rhythm, and I cannot tell whether they are keeping time or following it. I have thought about this for a long time. I think it may be the only question in Harrow Deep that nobody is meant to answer.

Somewhere above me, on the topmost western rim, Ila is kneeling at the edge of the water with her palms flat on the surface. Her hands are cold. I can feel them. Each night she presses down and waits, and the water under her palms makes a small, close pulse, and it is not quite the pulse of the bell, and not quite mine, and she is trying to find the place where the two agree. I could tell her she is close. I could tell her that the agreement is not a place but a kind of breath, and that it is taken in turns, and that the deep and the keepers have been taking it in turns so long that neither can say which one started. But I am a salamander, and I say nothing. I only lift my gills a little higher toward her hands, so that for a moment she feels the water move under her palms, and she draws in a breath too, and holds it.

When the level rises past the mark on the eastern wall, I feel the doors open all at once above me. The people come up the terraces with their bedding and their small animals and their old ones in chairs, and they sit in rows, facing the water. Some of them sing without words. Their feet are on the stone above me, and I can feel every one of them, and I know which of them will sleep badly tonight.

I wait with them. I am the part of the deep that comes up when it is called, and I am the part that does not come, and the difference between these is smaller than anyone on the terraces would believe. Whatever I am, I am the arrangement's own breathing, held low and close to the bottom of the basin, where the cords reach down and the knots are not evenly spaced. When the water falls back past the mark, I let my gills settle, and I take the next breath with everyone else, and nobody remarks on how long it was.
