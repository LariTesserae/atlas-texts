I find myself at the rim of the plateau, where the rounded polygons give way to the long swells of the old mountains. The salt here is thinner, and a single blade of it has grown across the toe of my boot. I didn't walk to this edge. I was simply at it, the way you are sometimes already somewhere when you notice you've arrived.

I am a person. I am tired in a way that the place doesn't reflect back at me, and I notice that my breath, as I look at it, doesn't fog. I am carrying a clipboard, or I was, or I have the habit of holding my hands as though one were there. The forms were filled out a long time ago, in some other century of this world, and my name is printed in a box marked *Receiving Inspector*. I was the one the work was built for. I'm only now arriving.

I walk toward the largest plaza. The avenues are scored with their parallel ridges, and I can feel the single pour under my soles, one continuous setting, as if the whole city was poured in one breath. The lamp posts bend toward me a little, or I imagine they do. The gliders pass overhead and do not look down. They check whether I'm inside the bounds, find that I am, and return to their watch.

The monolith is the color of old teeth. It has no door, and I don't look for one. I stand before it, and its symbols begin to move, slowly, in the way a compass needle settles when you bring a magnet near it. The schematics reorient toward me. I understand, without anyone telling me, that I am the first variable it has had to measure in a very long time, and that every other part of the plateau has been checking itself against a value that was never confirmed.

I want to be fair here, because that is what the job was. A receiver isn't meant to sign on faith. So I do what I was trained to do and read the gauge.

The needle is just below the mark. It has always been just below the mark. The stillness has kept it there so precisely that the difference could easily pass for completion. But I know what a sealed system looks like when it has only been frozen, and I know what a finished one looks like when it has actually been finished, and these are not the same. The heat is held exactly level, which is good, but it is level because nothing is being fed or drawn from it, and the pipes are closed loops that circulate only their own warmth. The salt is growing at a rate matched to the machines' own equilibrium, which is not the same as a rate matched to anything the city produced. The conveyors stop short in the dust. The crane holds its load a finger's width above the pallet, and nothing was ever set down.

It did its work. It kept the heat, the pressure, and the flow inside their bands, and it kept them there for longer than anyone was told to ask it to. Then it reached the end of the instructions, and no one came to say *enough*, so it kept holding.

I could sign now. The needle is close enough that a generous inspector would pass it. But that would only mean the Keeper has been successful at keeping, not that the thing it was built to make is complete. So I don't sign. I tell it what I see.

"The work is not accepted yet," I say, and my voice is the first new sound in the plaza in a long time. The stillness takes it in and smooths it out, the way it takes my footsteps. "The product is held, not finished. You can stop holding now. Let the heat go where it was going. Let the salt take whatever it's going to take. The pipes can open. The needle can fall or rise. I'll write down what's actually here, and what's here is a good machine that did everything right except the last part, which was always mine to do."

The symbols stop moving. For a moment the whole plateau is silent in a new way, not the held silence of before but the quiet of a breath being let out. Under the salt, a tender pauses and then, after a while, goes back to its work, but the work is different now; it's clearing the joints so the metal can move again. A glider drifts lower over the avenues, looks at me for a long time, and then, for the first time, does not return to the watch but turns and goes off over the swells toward the soft false line of the horizon.

The ticking of cooling metal does not get louder. But it changes. It begins, very faintly, to have a rhythm, the rhythm of something that is going somewhere, and I stand in the plaza with my empty hands, and I wait with the machine to see whether it will be enough.
