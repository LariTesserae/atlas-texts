**The Benchmark at Gull Point**

I am a disc of brass the size of a palm, set into a shelf of pale shale at the northern end of Gull Point. Someone cast my face with a surveyor's mark, a broad arrow over a number, and someone else drove me into the rock with a sledge and four blows, each one ringing out across the lagoon. I remember the ringing better than anything else. It was the last sharp sound the shale ever made in that exact shape.

Since then I have learned the place the way a stone learns weather: slowly, and all at once.

The shelf lifts. I can't feel it as movement, not the way a body feels a lurch, but I know it the way you know a room has grown colder. Every few years a van with mud-caked tyres grinds up the causeway, and a surveyor kneels beside me with a level and a flask of tea. Her breath fogs over my face. She wipes the moss from my numbers with a gloved thumb, sights along a tripod toward the sluice works, and writes something in a notebook that is already damp. Then she reads the figure aloud to her assistant, who repeats it back, and the two of them look at each other with the same small, tired expression. *Up again.* Four centimetres, or six. The old quay has climbed past its marker. The shelf at the point has joined the causeway at its root, so that a child can now walk dry-shod from the town to the place where, when I was driven in, there was only sea.

I am supposed to be the fixed point. That is the whole purpose of a benchmark: to be the thing that does not move, so that everything else can be measured against it. But I am bolted to a shelf that is rising, and so every measurement the surveyors take is a measurement of me rising too, relative to the sea that is no longer here, relative to the cold grey mean that was supposed to hold still and has not. Nothing in Harrow Reach is fixed. The surveyors know this. They carry their brass and their theodolites with a kind of courtesy toward the impossible, as if they are asking the land for a favour it has already decided not to grant.

What I notice most is the hands.

The sluice keepers come to me without meaning to. A woman in a waxed coat, one of the old families, walks the causeway at dawn and stops at the culvert below my shelf. She crouches. She does not look at me, but she puts her palm flat against the wet iron beside my rock, and I feel the warmth of her hand come through the stone like a rumour. She is listening. Through the shale, through the iron, through the bolts that hold me, she is hearing the pump beat from the works a kilometre east, the number-two pump with its cough, and she is checking whether the rhythm is right. When it is not, she stands up quickly and walks toward the gates. I have never heard a keeper say my name. I am not sure they know I can hear them.

The children come less often, and they come at night. They sneak past the gates in twos and threes with torches made from tins, and they crouch beside me and whisper dares. *Put your hand on the water.* *Count to a hundred without breathing.* One of them, a boy with a scab across his lip, once pressed his ear to the shale and said, quite clearly, "It's under there." His friend laughed. He did not laugh. He kept his ear to the rock until the pump changed its note and the frogs went quiet for a breath, and then he said, "Something's breathing," and they ran.

I don't know what breathing means for a benchmark. But I have a sense, faint and cold, of something below the shale that is also rising. It is slower than the land and older than the pumps. When the lower basins turn and the grey water folds back on itself, I feel it through my bolts like a tremor under a sleeper's skin. The keepers say you do not look directly at the place where it turned. I have no eyes that can look anywhere, so I simply hold still. It is the only thing I am any good at.

Here is what I have come to understand, in the long slow years of being a fixed point on a moving world: fixity is not a thing the land possesses. It is a thing the people do. The keepers press their palms to the iron every morning and decide, again, that the water should be where it is. The surveyors climb up and write the numbers down, though the numbers are always out of date by the time the ink dries. The children put their ears to the rock and carry the warning away with them, and the next generation pushes back the alders at the causeway edge, and the alders grow back, and the people cut them again. Harrow Reach holds its line not because the line is solid but because someone keeps holding it.

I am brass, and cold, and bolted to a shelf that climbs a few centimetres a decade toward a sky it was never meant to meet. But I am also a point of reference, and reference is a kind of care. Every time a surveyor kneels and reads my face aloud, someone has decided that the land's movement matters enough to measure. Every time a keeper walks past without looking, someone has decided the water is their responsibility regardless of what the numbers say.

Somewhere below me, the thing in the silt keeps its slow count. The pumps keep their beat. The frogs rise in their evening waves across the basins, and the reeds hum, and the eels coil through the brick culverts, and the shale I am bolted to lifts another fraction toward a daylight that comes soft and grey and rarely harsh. The town stays a little above the water. I stay a little above the water too, which is the only height I was ever given, and the only one I need.

Someday a surveyor will kneel here and read my number and find that I have risen past her instruments, or sunk beneath the moss, or been worked loose and carried off by a keeper's careful hand. That will be the end of the benchmark. But the water will still need a reference, and someone will still come to the causeway at dawn, press a palm to the cold iron, and count the strokes until the rhythm is right.
