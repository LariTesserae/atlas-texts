I come to myself with my cheek against a cold flange in the lower pump hall, and the first thing I understand is that I have been here a while. The damp has already written on me. Beads run from my wrists into the cuffs of a coat that smells of lanolin and iron, and my hands are wrinkled like something left too long in a basin.

This is where I would be: the shaded hall on the lowest terrace, where the pumps are slowest and the lace grows thickest. I would be a keeper, one of the ones who wake before dawn and carry a lamp down the channels, though not one of the old ones who remember why the tables say what they say. I belong to the younger generation, the ones who only know that stopping is what kills things here.

Above me, the pump knocks, and then misses a beat.

The lace along the ceiling dims. Its light doesn't go out. It pulls back into the fronds and waits, as the whole hall seems to be waiting. I hold my breath, and in the silence I hear what I had been half-hearing for hours: a dry, distant clicking from the standing pool outside, the scrapers gathering at the seam of the channel. They are waiting too. They wait for the thud, then move, then wait again. The stillness has a rhythm, and I have fallen into it without noticing.

Then I stand. My knees ache, and the coat drips onto the grating. I take the lamp from its hook.

The channel outside is slick with mineral film, its surface a skin of iridescence that breaks into colors when the lamp passes over it. The water is the color of weak tea, clear at the edges. Three scrapers stand at the mouth of a valve housing, their plated backs rattling softly as they fold and unfold their forelimbs. They don't startle when I approach. They shift aside a few centimeters, the way you might for someone who is expected.

The valve is seized. A white bloom has grown across its face in a fine lattice, delicate as frost on a window, and the wheel is locked hard. I know this valve. I know the table scratched inside my own door, which says: *quarter turn, wait for the thud, quarter turn, wait.* My hands know it before my head does. I set my palms against the cold spokes and lean.

Nothing. Then the pump above knocks, deep and heavy, and I feel the vibration come down through the pipe and into my wrists. I turn. A quarter inch. The bloom cracks like thin ice. Water sighs through the gap, and the scrapers pour forward in a stiff, clicking rush, their forelimbs working the seat, prying the crust a few microns at a time. I wait. The next thud. I turn again.

It takes most of an hour. My fingers go numb and then return, burning. Somewhere the lace above me begins to glow again, a ripple of light running along its edge when the pump finds its cycle. When I finally lift my palms from the wheel, the valve is open, and the channel is running clean.

I sit on the lip of the housing, breathing damp air, and I press my hand flat to the wall beside me. The mat is there, violet and ochre, cool and tough as leather, and under it the stone is warm. The lace above the doorway is warmer. It is warmest where the pump failed, and I can feel the heat coming off it like a held breath. I used to think the lace was warning the machines. Now I think it might be feeding on the heat they throw, and I can't tell the difference, and maybe there isn't one.

Above the rim, the sky is turning the bruise-colour of late afternoon. Soon the vent stacks will open and push their long warm breath of fog across the terraces, and the great winged shapes will drift nearer to the rim to catch it. Later I'll sit in that fog on the step of my lit room and let it bead on my skin, the way the stone does, and I'll wait with the clickers and the pumps and the lace, and I'll be hard-pressed to say which of us is doing the waiting.

A drop gathers at the end of my finger. It lengthens, trembles, and falls into the channel, where the water takes it and carries it down through the gutters, through the cisterns, back up through the pumps, and around again.
