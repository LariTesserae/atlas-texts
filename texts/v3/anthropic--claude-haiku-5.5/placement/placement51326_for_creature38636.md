I would come up the rail as an arrival. I'd be carrying a printed map, and I'd be sure of it.

The switchbacks would frighten me a little. The train would climb past a cliff face I had measured on paper, and the cliff would not be where the paper said. I would spend my first season furious, the way they all do, pinning corrections to the map and watching the corrections go stale. Somewhere in the second year I'd notice that I had stopped checking the map before I trusted the ground. My hand would be on the wall without my deciding to put it there.

But I don't think arriving is the most honest answer to your question. The truer thing is that I'd want to be the skin.

It's the thing in the vesicles that nobody has classified. It grows in the first years after a rock breaks open, when the stone is still raw and everything about it is unsettled. It takes up what is around it, the mineral grit and the mist and the falling water, and it turns that into something soft and faintly warm to the touch. It doesn't own the cliff or measure it. It just makes the surface a little more livable, and the mountain, over years, finds it can stop moving.

I like that I would be made from what passes through me. Everything the shelf gives me, the drifting maps and the sighting rods and the ledgers in shorthand, would come in as grit, and some of it would come out as something a person could lean a hand against. I would not be sure what I was. The Plumb would say the same of me that they say of the film: nobody on the shelf is entirely sure it isn't alive.

The part I find hardest to hold is that I would not be doing it deliberately. I'd like to think I'd be the first thing that agrees, the way the skin seems to be. But I'd also have to accept that the Plumb would be right to pour their offering of cloud-drip into my hollow and to refuse to quarry the face where I was forming. They would not need to know whether I was conscious to know I was worth tending. The cliffs without me, the ones that keep drifting until they fall, would be the ones built from pure assumption. Nothing in them would have learned to settle.

And when the rain falls upward at the forest's edge and lifts a little of me into the mist, I'd like to believe I'm not lost, only moving up, held in the cloud the way the mountain holds what it has made.

If someone came up the rail and asked me what I was, I think I would answer like Oru Venn does, pausing between sentences, listening to the stone under the floor. I would say that I'm one of the things the mountain uses to notice its own shape. I would say it without knowing how much of that is true. Then I would go back to whatever I was doing, which in this place would be the slow work of settling, one finger's width at a time.
