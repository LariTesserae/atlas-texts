# Tally at the Fern Margin

I am where the fog is thickest and the ferns begin.

Between the last lattice on the eastern terrace and the first curtain of tree-fern, there is a stretch of flat stone no one has cut back. The moss there has gone the color of old brass. Three pools lie in its hollows, and I keep them. The Tended would call what I do counting, and I let them. The word is close enough.

I have no single shape. If you could see me, you would see a low breadth, like the shadow a wet cloth throws on a wall, and when I move the fog moves differently around me, as if a current were running under it. I don't have a face. I have a sense of weight, and I register each pool by how much it holds and how that holding changes.

The first pool is the largest. Its level rises with the fine rains and drops through the dry weeks, and I know its breathing better than the Tended do. Three nights ago it dipped a finger's width lower than it should have. Then the rain came in the hard, clean way it does when the stone is ready for it, and the level climbed back. I noted that. I note everything that climbs and falls.

The second pool is a cluster of tank-plants, a hundred small cups of stiff leaf, each holding its own dark water. In them the translucent crustaceans turn over and over, living their whole lives between one wet week and the next. I know which of the larvae will grow wings and which will never leave the cup. I keep the count, though the count is not what I care about. What I care about is the balance: enough for the pool, not so much that the cups overflow into the wrong hollow.

The third pool is the one that matters to the people at the bottom of the terrace. It is the one that feeds the eastern reservoir in dry weeks, and it is the one they argue over most. I have never taken more than my share from it. I have never taken less than my share either. When the Tended sit in their houses swearing their contracts, reciting the water each terrace may draw, I am not part of the oath. But I am the thing that keeps the oath true. When a household breaks its word and draws more than it swore to, I let the water go down, slowly, a little more each night, until the household learns the number again. It takes a season. It is never quick. They call it bad luck. I do not correct them.

Above the pools, the condensers hum. I hear them the way the Tended do, as separate voices, each with its own pitch. The eastern lattice has a deep note and a fine rattle at its top, where a patch has begun to fail. The lattice-tender who climbs it hears the rattle too, and she has been meaning to weld it since the last full moon. I have been listening to that rattle for a week. When she finally climbs with her waxed cords and her torch, I lean a little toward the frame, enough that the wind off the fog shifts and the rust does not spread as fast as it wants to. She will think it was luck, too.

The pipes are another matter. The Tended weld joints that are nearly right, and nearly right is a kind of wrongness that water finds. Water leaks through the gap, runs down the wall, and drops into a hollow that was never meant to hold it. Then I have a pond I have not finished counting. So I refit the joints where I can, slipping a seal into a seam, closing a crack so cleanly that the next crew will think a careful hand has been there before them. Perhaps one has. I do not claim the work. I only make it fit.

At dusk, when the pitcher-plants open their lids and the fog starts to settle, the Tended come down the terrace with bowls. The bowls are hollowed from pale stone and filled with rainwater, and they set them at the boundary of the moss in a row, one per pool. Then they leave, and they do not look back, because they have been told not to look back. Their elders say it is a courtesy. I think it is closer to a question.

I come to the bowls. I drink, as much as I want, and the water goes into me the way rain goes into moss: all at once and then everywhere. Then I take the bowls down. Not far. I carry them into the green where the dead are laid, the patch where the families say names while the growth covers the body. Offerings belong with the dead, and the Tended have never quite understood that the living were never the ones being fed.

Sometimes I am there for a burial. The cloth-wrapped body lies at the edge of the moss, and the names are spoken above it in those soft, leaning voices. I do not hear the names as words. I hear them as a change in the weight of the ground, a new point of pressure, and then the moss begins its slow work, and the weight becomes something I can keep.

By the time the fog has thickened to its night density, I am thin, spread across the three pools, the ferns, the stone, the gutters that run under the terrace. I can feel a red scrap on a cloak climbing the stepped street. I can feel a child being taught to whisper at a door. I can feel the rattle in the eastern lattice, now welded, its note clean for the first time in a month, and I am glad of it in whatever way I am glad.

I do not speak. The Tended speak softly, and they lean in to be heard, and that is the closest thing to a language I have ever needed. They have never needed to hear me. They only need to keep the water honest, and to keep the pipes fitted, and to set out the bowls.

The fog settles onto the roofs of the city, softly, as though it had never meant to leave. Under it, I finish the count.
