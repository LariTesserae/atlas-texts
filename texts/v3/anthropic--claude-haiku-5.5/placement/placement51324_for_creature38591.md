I would be one of the grazers, a slow domed back wading the north margin where the mudstone plates lean out over the water. I would come to it the way the basin comes to everything, slowly, and I would not notice the change for a long time.

My shell is low and heavy in the water. Along its ridge the pale knobs have taken hold, a dozen clusters now, each the size of a fist and pocked like pumice. I carry them from the crust into the wet hollows and set them down in the film, and where I have walked the mats have grown in a long parallel track behind me. I do not know that I am planting anything. I know the weight on my back, and I know the cold that comes off the knobs on mornings when they exhale, and I know that the ground under my feet is firmer than it was the season before.

Somewhere along the ridge there is a fitting of brass, green at the edges and sheathed in blue-black film. I took it from the bottom of a channel during a drain, when the water pulled back and left the silt open and the brass lay there catching the milky light. It was heavier than anything else I had carried. I wedged it into the seam where my shell meets the flank, and now the film has grown over it, and I can feel it only when the water is high and the pressure shifts against it. It is a small machine. I am a slow landscape. Between us we have become a kind of hill.

The rise comes first. I feel it before I understand it, a lifting in the water that makes my legs lighter and draws the drifts up from the deep. They pass me in glittering sheets, their shells clicking, and the rattle they make is the sound of the whole basin remembering it is alive. The impeller turns faster. Its clank comes through the water and into my plates. Far off, the counterweight sighs against the gate, and I stop moving for a while, because the rise is a time for holding still and letting the film thicken around you.

Then the runner comes. I never see it whole. There is a ring spreading across the water, and the drifts go silent in a wave that runs toward me and passes under my shell and beyond. Something has passed through the channel below us. The eels, somewhere in the silt, have already turned away. I do not turn. My shell is too heavy and my instincts are too slow, and I think there is a kind of peace in that, in being too large and too dull to be the thing it hunts. I am a part of the basin's breath, and the runner is a part of it too, and the rise has room for both of us.

When the table begins to drain, the knobs open their pores and the bitter vapor goes up in threads around my shell. I taste iron. The drifts withdraw, the moths drop into the crust, and the water pulls back from the rim in a long slow sigh. This is when I am most myself. I walk, and I set down the knobs, and I carry the film outward in its patient lines. The brass on my flank cools. The ground beneath me hardens. I cannot tell whether I am the one moving the basin forward or whether the basin is moving me, and I have stopped minding the difference.

If I were to go back over my life, I would find almost nothing that is mine. The knobs were there before I came. The film was there before the knobs. The brass was the old works' and the shell was my mother's and the walking was taught to me by the water. But the track I have left across the north margin is a line the basin will keep, and one season the silt will close over it, and some young grazer will find the parallel ridges and follow them without knowing whose feet first pressed them down. That is the only inheritance this place has ever offered, and I think it is enough.

At the end of the cycle, when the water stands at its lowest and the fog thins toward noon, I would lift my head and look toward the tower at the center. Its spiral stair ends in air, and the brass ring on the top rail has long since turned the color of the mats. I would not think of the person who climbed it. I would only feel the faint tremor under my shell as the floor rises, and I would understand, in the slow way that grazers understand anything, that the high places here are being buried, and the low places are being raised, and that we are all, the tower and the pumps and the drifts and the brass and I, becoming the same gentle ground.
