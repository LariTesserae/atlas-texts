I would find myself in a cell near the top of the wall, where the salt gives out and the stone turns bare and unmarked. My burrow is the third from the lip on a shelf that faces the dark. Its opening is smooth-lipped and about a hand across. I am the only one in this row who keeps the threshold swept, though I didn't decide that. The swept dust is simply where my legs go each morning, and the scraping grooves in the sill are mine.

I am mostly pale, with dark slate along the joints where the mist reaches me on the warm updraft. My eyes are clusters of many-sided lenses. When something passes on the terraces below, they catch the glow from the high walls and send it back in slow flickers, and I watch. I do not startle. I have watched a great many things pass, and most of them were the same things again.

I think I am one of the keepers, though I'm not sure what that means from inside. I know the rows on the wall beside my door. I pass my forelimbs over them as I come and go, lightly, the way a hand finds the familiar edge of a doorframe in the dark. I don't count them. I know their order. When the edges of the cut have softened a little, I go over them with the tip of a worn scraper, and the lines come back sharp. I don't know whether I am keeping the record or keeping myself, whether the marks are a count of cycles or a way of saying that this is where the cycles are.

Below me, the grazers unfold and fold, the hangers drink the fog and give it back, and the great haulers sweep the pans into their rings. Each of them is doing what it does. I used to think their work and mine were separate, that the reach was a crowd that happened to share a wall. Lately I think we are one held shape, each of us a chamber in a larger body, and the stillness is what the body looks like from the inside when it is doing its job well.

The bare stone above me is the thing I keep returning to. The grazers stop there. The hangers stop there. Whatever lives past that edge does not come down to the terraces, and whatever lives here does not climb. Nobody has told me that line exists. I simply know where it is, the way I know where the threshold ends.

If I climbed, I think the others would fold their legs and watch me go, as they watch everything, without alarm. I would step onto stone no one has swept, and the glow would recede above me into a dark with no visible top. I would not know what I was for out there, or whether the rows would still be kept without me.

So I stay for now. I sweep the sill, and I run the tip of the scraper along the newest row, and I take a single pass over the stone above my door where the bare rock begins. There I make one mark that does not belong to any row, a short cut with no sequence, which I cannot tell apart from the start of something new. I don't know if it is a record or a beginning. The mist rises and falls, the salt sifts into its rings, and somewhere below the haulers breathe out their long, held tone.
