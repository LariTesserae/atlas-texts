I would arrive at the edge, where the stream goes under.

The black shelf is still warm from its own making. Its sharp terraces hold the light without softening it, and the air is thin enough that each breath feels like something I have to earn. Below me the water comes down in a thread, runs a few hundred meters over the cracked slate, and then sinks into a hole no wider than a hand. I would crouch there for a long time and listen. There is no sound from the water at all, just the dry hiss of wind moving through something far below the rock.

Then I would go down toward the trunks, because the stone has no shade and the town does.

The first corridor would take me in with the leaves brushing my shoulders. The half-light would make my skin feel like it belonged to the lichen. I would not know the marks on the bark, and the keepers would see that before I said anything. They would press two fingers to my wrist and drag them down the wall, and I would not know how to answer. I think I would want to learn that most of all: to be spoken to in a language made of surfaces, and to answer slowly, with my hand flat on something warm, instead of with a voice that carries too far.

I would want to be a keeper. I would walk the trunks each morning and touch the mortar, learning which rises were content and which had begun to weep. I would sit through the long stillness between strokes when a bore was being drilled into a cistern, and I would not need a word for waiting, because the work would be the waiting. I would learn that a crack the keepers had not marked was not mine to hurry toward, and that the builders knew this better than I did.

But I think I would end up as something else.

There is a node on a trunk at the edge of the eastern terrace, one of the pale cups that open at dusk. Every evening I would sit beneath it with my palms against the bark, the way the keepers do, and I would watch it turn west toward the ridges and the black stone I came over. It would hold still for the whole night. I would not know what it saw. Nobody in the town does. And I think that not knowing would be the most honest part of being there. The node does not need to understand what it watches. It only has to stay open while the town holds its breath, so that something is awake in the dark.

So I would become that: a tissue cup, rooted in the bark, facing the lava. I would hold the shape of the dark and the thin air and the water that never reaches the surface. In the morning the keepers would touch my rim and perhaps read something in it. The builders would crawl past me without hurrying. The gliders would drop past and the seeds would knock through the leaves, and I would keep my face toward the ridges until the light came up over the terraces and I closed again.

If someone asked what I was, I think I would answer the way the keepers do, with a trail on the bark rather than a voice. Something awake, the trail would say, that keeps watch while the water waits.
