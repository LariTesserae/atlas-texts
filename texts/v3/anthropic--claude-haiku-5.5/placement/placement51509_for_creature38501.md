I would arrive from the grass. The pale, fired ground gives way under my feet, and then the trees close over and the air thickens until it sits on my skin like a damp cloth. For a while I am only sound: the hum that separates into clicks and buzzes, frogs ranked across the slopes, water finding a hundred pitches. Then the sounds begin to have a floor under them, a continuous scraping and rustling, and I understand I am near the builders.

I would not be at the top. I would be low, on the third or fourth shelf, where a bank meets a seep. The ground here is the grey-brown layer, packed with husks and the small spiral shells of things pressed long ago. I would be pressed against it, and I would know it through my jaws and the flat of my head, not through sight. Sight would give me light and dark and little more.

I would be a pelleter. My head is broad and my jaws are short and blunt, and under my throat there is a crop full of worked mud, tacky with saliva that will harden in the air. I know this without thinking about it, the way you know the weight of your own hand. What I do not know is which of the thousands around me I am. I cannot see the others. I can only feel them: a knock on the wall three bodies down, a tremor passing along the bank, the sweetness and rot of the basin rising from below.

Then the crack. It is a thin dark line in the bank, and the seep is pushing through it. I come to it along the trail the others have worn, and I stop. My antennae touch the edge. The wet is cold and moving, and I can feel the pressure of it behind the wall. This is the part I would be made for: I tap the crack once, and wait, and let the moisture come up through the chitin of my head. It tells me how fast the seep runs. It tells me whether the basin below is filling or emptying. It tells me, without any words, that this crack is too wide for a single pellet to hold.

I press the first pellet in. It is grainy, and my body remembers the grain. I feel it take the water and begin to darken. Then I stop, because a second knock has come from the wall, from the gallery where the excavators keep the water moving, and I feel the bank shift under my feet. Somewhere below, a buttress is bulging. Someone has decided it needs more hands, and the decision has already reached me. I do not know who decided it. I only know the pressure is in my legs and the mud is in my crop, and the crack needs filling before the bank gives way.

So I work. I press another pellet, and another, and the seep slows. The water begins to find a new path along the edge, and I follow it with my antennae and tap the new line. Other pelleters arrive, a few at first, and then a dozen, all tapping and pressing, none of us planning anything. The wall is not being built toward a design. It is being argued into shape, a little at a time, through touch.

Later the rain comes. The roar on the leaves drowns out everything for minutes at a time, and the channel below runs brown and fast, shouldering against the banks I have helped to thicken. I stay where I am and hold my pellet against the pressure. The water hits the wall and runs off it, and the crack I sealed stays dry.

I would not know whether I had done anything that mattered. A single pellet is a small decision about water. Its meaning would be in the sum of all the pellets, across all the shelves, over all the seasons, and I would be only one of the bodies in the sum. Eventually I would slow. I would stop climbing the bank, and I would be pressed into the wall I helped to hold, and the mud I carried would become part of the ground, its grey-brown layer packed with my husk and my chitin, waiting for some later body to arrive from the grass and feel the wall give a little under its fingertip.
