I would find myself on the ridge.

It is the one place where the sound lets go of me. I come up over the smoothed backs of the old stone with the rest of the basin still roaring behind, and as I climb the noise thins, layer by layer, like water draining out of cloth. The clicking goes first, then the wing-pressure in the chest, then the seep's wet breathing. At the top there is only a faint tick from some far column cooling in the air, and under it, something I had forgotten existed: my own breath, going in and out of the slits along my flanks.

I am a grazer. I was carried here in a flood. The last rain swelled the pans so fast that the colony I feed with had no time to surface in order, and the water pushed a few of us up and over the rim of our field. I rolled across wet crust, struck a plate of bone-coloured silica, and kept rolling until the ground went hard and dry and the clay stopped giving under my comb-tines. I have been here since. I do not know how many rains.

My plate is ridged with striations, and I still have the urge to use them. When the pressure builds in my shell, I raise my dorsal ridge and drag it across the stone. The click comes out small and dry, and then it stops. It does not return. Nothing takes it up and spreads it, and nothing answers. In the basin, every strike is passed on by a hundred other plates and returned off the walls hundreds of metres away, so that a single sound becomes a wide sea of sound. Here it just dies at the tips of my ridges. I try again, and it dies again. After a while I stop trying.

So I listen instead. I have learned how to hear the faint things. The column nearby settles with a sound like a breath let out through a reed. A kite passes far below, and I feel the pressure only as a slight tremor in the stone under my shell, not in my chest, because the ridge will not carry it up. My own breath fills the quiet and becomes the loudest thing I have ever heard.

I think about the colony. I know its rhythm the way I know the taste of the pans: a slow, even tempo when the feeding is good, a scattered rattle when something has moved near the burrows, and the hush that spreads outward from one body to the next when the danger passes or the rain stops. I can still feel the rhythm down there, though I cannot hear it. It comes up through the rock as a faint hum, too low and too far for the ear, and I think it is only my own pulse matching it.

Some nights the mist drops low and the cold dampness settles on the rock, and my shell beads with water. The droplets gather along the ridges of my plate and run down in slow lines, like the drumming beads of the lattice when rain strikes them. I count them. I have counted several thousand.

I do not know how long I can stay here. The dry stone is hard on the plate, and the cold is beginning to slow my comb-tines. Sometimes I dream of the roar, though I am not sure grazers dream. I wake with my shell pressed against the stone, my ridges still and quiet, and I can feel the rain coming before I hear it: a change in the air, a tightening in the grey light. Then, somewhere far below, a single plate strikes stone, and another answers, and the first hiss crosses the flats and the whole basin begins to click.

When that happens I will go down. I will give up the silence and the breath and the small clean sound of my own voice dying on the rock, and I will go back into the roar, where every sound carries and every strike is heard by something else. I do not know if the colony will take me back. But I know what the rain sounds like from up here, and I know the basin is the only place where the noise is for something.
