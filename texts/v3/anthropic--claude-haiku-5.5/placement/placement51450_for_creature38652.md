I find myself inside a pod.

It is dawn, and I am one of perhaps forty pale husks rattling on the dome of a seed-carrier as it crosses the mat. The cold has a metallic taste even through the layers of my own casing. The carrier moves slowly, rocking on its many short legs, and every step shakes me against my neighbors. We make a dry chorus, a little castanet sound lost under the drone and the bass boom of a pressure-organ somewhere south of us. I do not know what I am yet. I know only that I am packed tight with a seed, and beside the seed, a small measure of rich, bitter paste that will feed whatever wakes up.

The carrier has chosen its route, and it will not be turned. I have learned that much from the jostling. When a sap-fountain bursts ahead of us, hanging in the air and falling in glittering sheets, the carrier does not swerve. It walks straight through the spray, and the sticky fluid runs down its dome and beads on my surface. When lightning strikes a dry fold of the mat a few hundred meters off, the smoke rolls over us and the carrier keeps walking, grit ticking against my husk. We are going somewhere, and the going is the only thing the carrier knows.

Then the mat cracks open beneath us, and the carrier stops at last.

It is a narrow fissure between two shelf-faces, a seam where the banded rock has split along an old line of weakness. The walls are ochre and rust, the floor is grey-green, and far below, where the crack narrows, I can hear water. It is the first water I have heard on this plateau in weeks, and it is not water; it is the drip of sap seeping through some hidden cord. The carrier lowers its dome over the gap and shudders. Pods drop from its back, one after another, and I fall.

I tumble down the rock face for a long time, bouncing from ledge to ledge, until I lodge in a pocket of grit at the bottom of the fissure. The seed inside me is still asleep. The paste is still sealed. For three days nothing happens. Then the rains come.

They come all at once, as they do here. The sky over the western shelves turns the color of slate, the wind drops for a moment that feels like a held breath, and then the water arrives in a single roaring sheet. It pours into the fissure and fills it. My husk splits along its seams. The paste dissolves into the flood, and the seed, drowning in the best possible way, is washed into the rock. I feel the cold and the dark and the rush of it, and then I feel nothing but a slow, dull pressure against the stone.

Growth is not like waking. It is more like being slowly pressed into a shape.

The first thing I become is a root. It is pale and thin, and it spreads through the pocket of grit with the patience of something that has no reason to hurry. It finds the sap-seep, the hidden cord, and wraps around it. The sap is cold and thick and tastes of salt. Where the root touches the rock, the rock softens and gives. I do not know if I am eating it or if it is letting me in. Both are probably true.

Some seasons pass. I cannot count them. There are many storms. A grazer's file tears the mat a few meters away, and the crack of splitting timber rings through the fissure and shakes loose a shower of dust. A warden burrows past my root and widens the capillary beside it, and the sap pressure shifts, and for a while my new fibers are starved, then flooded, then starved again. I learn to hold on. Holding on is most of what I do.

Eventually I rise. A stem pushes up through the crack and into the light, pale and fluted, its surface lined with ribs that catch the wind. The first frond unfolds at the top, translucent and veined, and the wind pulls at it, and the sound it makes is a thin whistle, a little like a flyer's call.

Above me, a flyer wheels down out of the thermals, circles the new growth once, and lands on the ledge beside the stem. It folds its wings and looks at me with compound eyes that catch the white light in a thousand glints. Then it calls, sharp and piercing, to another flyer far off across the canyon, and the call travels from ridge to ridge until it is lost in the boom of the organs.

I am a tower now, or the beginning of one. I am very small. The mat is already pressing against my base, reaching for the root-channels I have opened, deciding whether I will be allowed to stay. The seed-carriers will pass me on their migrations, and some will drop their pods in the cracks around my base, and some of those pods will become my neighbors, and eventually I will be one of the columns that stand alone across the kilometers of mat, the slender fluted kind that look like organ pipes of wood gone to stone.

The storms will tear at me. A gale will someday strip my fronds and send them tumbling across the shelves. Lightning may strike my crown and set my dry fibers burning for days. The mat will re-knit around my scars, and the shape of the land will change, and I will change with it.

But for now I stand in a narrow fissure between two shelves, in the roar of the wind and the drone of ten thousand wings, and I feel the plateau turning its weight beneath me. It is not the ground's own motion. It is something larger, slower, and it is rearranging itself around me, the way it rearranges everything, the way it has always done, patiently, without words.

And when the seed-carriers come walking back through the canyon at the next dawn, dome-backed and rattling, I will be here to see them pass, one more stubborn path in a plateau made of paths.
