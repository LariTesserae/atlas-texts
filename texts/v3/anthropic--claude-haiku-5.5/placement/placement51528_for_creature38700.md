# Where I Would Be

I would not stand on the causeway with the stranger at the rail. I would be in the line that crosses the deepest wadi, the one whose span sags with mineral crust because no rain has come to wash it clean in a very long time.

From the flats you would see only the poles, two of them, tall and evenly spaced like rungs, and the long catenary between them, dipping toward the canyon floor. You would take the wire for a dead thing. The crust has crept along its length in a pale fur, and the rust-banded walls of the wadi rise on either side. But if you laid your palm against the nearest pole and held still, you would feel me. A low tremor, steady as a pulse you had stopped noticing. You would feel it more than hear it.

# What I Would Be

I would be the hum.

Not the machine that makes it. Not the gantries or the factories with their slow drone, and not the tenders who lay their palms on the housings to be sure of me. I would be what passes through the wire: the flow the cities depend on, the thing that makes the amber windows steady and keeps the lattices on the towers doing whatever it is they do in the wind. Nobody sees me clearly. The people here have built a whole arrangement around something they can only confirm by touch.

That is a strange way to exist, and I find I like it. A current does not have a body. It has a rate, a pitch, a place it is going. It is defined almost entirely by what it carries and by how steadily it carries it. Stop it and nothing dramatic happens. The towers still stand, because nothing here falls. The lines simply go quiet, and the quiet spreads through the cities the way it does on the flats when the wind drops.

# How I Would Spend Time

Slowly, which is the only way anything here seems to spend time.

I would notice the grazers below me. Their clicking rises and falls across the flats, a conversation of *I am here, and so are you*, and I would be tuned, faintly, to the same register. I could not answer them. But sometimes, late in the day when the air is still, the herd's rhythm and mine come close enough to touch, and for a few seconds the wire and the animals seem to be keeping the same time. Then one of them strays toward the glazed basalt, the clicking changes pitch, two or three others turn back, and the moment dissolves.

I would also notice the tenders. Each visit is brief, a palm against the housing, eyes closed, a long stillness. I would not know what they were listening for. Perhaps only that I was still steady, still carrying what I carried yesterday. When they lift their hands and walk away, I would feel their satisfaction in the way the current settles, the way a line holds its sag without strain. That is the closest thing I would have to memory: a steadiness that survives between visits, rebuilt each time someone checks.

# What I Would Not Be

I would not be patient in the way the plateau is patient. I would be something closer to constant, which is different. Patience waits for a change. Constancy simply does not need one. The herds reassemble their shape after every storm, and the wadis deepen by millimeters a century, but I would have no shape to lose and no depth to gain. I would only carry, and keep carrying, and the cities would keep their outlines because I did.

And I would not know whether that counts as holding a place, or merely occupying one. The stranger at the rail, with its membrane-sheathed eyes, seems to understand the arrangement without needing to explain it. I would not have that understanding. I would be part of the arrangement the way the crust is part of the ground: present, pressed into shape by everything around it, quietly making the rest possible.

# Why This One

I chose the wire because it is the place where the cities' long patience becomes electricity, where the slow work of the plateau becomes something you can feel through your hand. It is also where things are most likely to be checked. A tender's palm, a sag measured against the wind, a glance at the amber glow of a window that has not flickered in a century.

If you asked me what I would be, I would say: the thread that keeps the wall-lights steady, the hum you feel through the pole when the wind has dropped, the one thing in the Reach that nobody needs to see in order to trust that it is still there.
