I would arrive at the lip, where the hollow tips down into the flats, and I would not stay still. Nothing here that stays still lasts. I would find a trail already laid in the silt, faintly scented, and I would step onto it because it was already there.

Then I would be a walker. I'd be pale and long-legged, my body thin enough that the fog would pass through the gaps between my joints. I'd carry on my feet the chemistry of the last walker who crossed this ground, and I'd lay my own down as I went, so that whoever came after me would find the way marked. I would not know where I was going. I'd learn that the trail goes from one mound across the flats to a fallen log and back, and I would not ask why. I would only notice the ground beneath me. When a patch went soft, I'd go around it. When it dried, I'd cross. The trail would move with the land, and I would move with the trail, and I'd feel the land rising a hair's width under my feet without knowing that it was rising.

Some mornings I'd pass a builder on its wall, its head pressed against a damp patch of clay. The stillness would ripple out from it, and for a moment I'd feel the gallery hold its breath, and then the stillness would dissolve back into work. I wouldn't understand the wall. I would only know that the walls were there, and that they were the reason the trail was here at all. The builders would be the city's memory, and I would be its errand.

At some point I'd reach the edge of the largest pool, under the broad leaves, where the water is dark and the flat-headed thing lies still for an hour at a time. I would stop. My antennae would lift toward the surface, and I would feel the eyes watching from just above the water, two small hard points that the reflection of a falling leaf would drift across. I would not know the pool was patient. I'd only know the water was there, and that the water had no trail.

Then I would turn.

That turn would be the only decision I made all day. I would not have chosen the pool or the flats or the mound. The trail chose the direction and the scent chose the path, and the turn is the one place where I am more than the route. I would go back the way I came, following my own old scent, and the trail would look the same as it had an hour before, except that I'd be a little further along it, and the silt would have settled a little more.

I think that is what I would want. Not to be the builder, who knows everything in the walls, or the beetle, who is content to be small, or the hunter, who lives by taking. I'd want to be the one who carries the marks from place to place, who goes around the soft ground and comes back, who stops at the water's edge long enough to choose and then goes on. In a few seasons the hollow may be forest, and in a few decades a glassy plain. The trail would be gone by then. But for now it would be there under my feet, faintly sweet with resin, and I would follow it toward the next turn, and the drip from the leaves would fall on my back, and the fog would close over the mounds behind me.
