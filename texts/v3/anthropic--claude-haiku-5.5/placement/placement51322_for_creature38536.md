I would start at the lip of the shaft, and the first thing I would notice is the light. It reaches everything. Nothing is in shadow, so nothing can hide, and I would feel exposed in a way I could not name. Then I would hear the ticking, a slow metallic contraction somewhere below, and I would follow it down.

I think I would be one of the seed-things in the gears of the dead crane.

I would not want to be a crawler. They are beautiful in their stillness, but their hunger is a constant pressure, and the restless ones sample the air with their front legs as if the air might owe them something. I would not want to be a coral mat either, though I envy the way its branches ring together, several notes at once. Being rooted into a single structure sounds like being heard by everything and moving by nothing.

The seed-things are different. They are no bigger than seeds, and they wait inside the hollows of the machine, in the places where the metal still holds heat. I would come out only when the generator's hum drops and the cavern cools, when the lamps' warmth thins and the girders begin to give back what they took in the day. I would feed on that warmth, and on the thin oil that still seeps from the bearings, which tastes, I imagine, like the memory of a motion. The crane has not moved in a long time, but its bearings remember movement, and so do I.

There is a particular loneliness in that, and also a kind of company. Sometimes the dial I live behind goes quiet, and I feel the heat leave the housing in a slow, almost courteous withdrawal. Then I move on. I climb along the cable that hangs into the silver canopy, through the cold metal, and find another gear that still ticks. I would be the one who knows which machines are dying, not because I want to know, but because my warmth depends on it. The beetles drink from the vines and leave their shells to the crawlers. I drink from the machine and leave it a little cleaner, a little more finished, which I suppose is its own kind of decay.

Mostly I would stay still. The vines are moving around me, tightening and loosening in a shiver I can feel through the frame: a crawler strikes a beetle two cables away, and the whole lattice ripples and goes quiet again. I would be aware of that as a tremor under my skin, a message I cannot read but recognize as a message. I do not think the vines know me, but they hold me, and that is enough for a thing that lives this close to the edge of being warm.

Sometimes, on the nights when the hum is steady and the cavern is still settling, I would go to the edge of the rails, where the iron is too hard for the roots, and look out across the white plains. The newest needles stand there, clear and soft at their tips. I would not touch them. I would only watch the crawlers step around them, and wonder, the way a small warm creature wonders, what the salt is making of us, and what we are making of the salt.

Then the shelf above would groan, and the flakes would begin to fall, and I would go back into the gear and wait for the heat to return. I am not sure what I am for. I think that is all right. The place is still deciding what it will become, and so am I, one warm seed in a machine that was built to take something out of the earth and is now, slowly, giving something back.
