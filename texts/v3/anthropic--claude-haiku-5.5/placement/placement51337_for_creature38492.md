I find myself in the lee of the slab-walled room, where the collapsed roof has hardened over with resin like a pane of glass. The cliff edge is a few paces off, and past it the haze lies grey and patient.

I am a reed. Or I was one, until last season, when a gust leaned on my stalk and the third joint split and I came down across the cracked plates. Now I lie half-sunk in the amber that spilled from the mat beside me. My crown is still fanned, though it no longer opens or closes. My hollow is still open at both ends.

The wind finds me in the afternoon. It comes in a shove off the fall, carrying grit and spores, and it goes into my open end and out of the broken joint, and I sing. I don't choose the note. The length of my body chooses it, and the split in my side, and the way the resin has thickened around my mouth. It is a low, hollow sound, a little flat, the kind that makes the grazers lift their heads.

They come toward me. The herd crosses the mat in a slow tide of embers, plates catching the dim light, and the lead animal stops at the edge of the patch where I lie and hums. It is a short, low phrase, the sort the herd uses to say *here* or *still here* or *we are passing*. I cannot hum back. I have no throat. But the wind is still moving through me, and when the grazer's phrase ends I give it back to them, a half-beat late, a little rough at the edges. The lead animal turns its ridged face toward me for a long moment. Then the herd moves on, and the reeds further up the shelf pick up the phrase and carry it along the cliff until it fades under the cicadas.

I have been thinking about what it means to still be singing.

The living reeds above me are tall and whole, and their notes are clean. The ones that fell in older seasons have sunk almost into the plates, their joints gone soft with resin, and their voices are thinner, more like breath than sound. Some have no voice at all anymore, only a dull knock when the wind shakes them. But they are not silent in the way I imagined the dead would be. Their hollows still hold the shape of the wind that once passed through them, and the tenders say the shelf keeps the shape of everything that has sung on it. That is a comfort, or it is simply true, and I am not sure there is a difference here.

Near my head, pressed into a flat stone at the base of the wall, there is a handprint. It is small, sharp with grey dust in its creases. A child made it, I think, though I have never seen a child. I know only what the wind tells me, that someone came here once with bare, resin-smeared hands, leaned on the stone, and listened. The stalks answered them. Something in that answer was true enough to be carved.

Beneath everything, the breath rises. I feel it through my roots, which are not really roots anymore, only a cracked cluster sunk in the fissure beside the wall. The vapour is warm and faintly sweet, with a metal edge. It comes up slowly, the way a sleeper breathes, and the mat drinks it, and the mat's resin drinks the light, and the light gets into the grazers, and the grazers hum, and the hum comes back to me off the cliff. The whole shelf is one long exhalation, and I am a small note in it, and the note is the point.

When the sun dips, the resin lights from within. My amber lights first, a faint lantern at my broken joint. The grazers pass again, and one of them stops and lays its ridged mouth against my split side, and hums. The sound goes through me and out the open end. For a moment I am not a fallen reed but the thing between two voices, the place where the shelf speaks to itself.

Then the wind drops into its eerie lull. The stalks go quiet one by one. Somewhere on the mat a patch falls silent, and a predator is waiting, and the clicks scatter in a burst of fright. I hold my breath, which is only the absence of wind, and wait for the next gust to come up off the fall and find me again.

It will. It always does. That is how I know I am still answering.
