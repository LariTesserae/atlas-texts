I arrive the way most things arrive here, by being carried. An afternoon gust climbs out of the salt basin, lifts me off the crust, carries me up the rounded cliff, and drops me on the lip of the mesa where the outermost terrace steps down into the first lane. I am a grain of the chalky stone, or of the dust that has scoured it for longer than anyone counted. I am very small. I am perhaps the only thing on this plateau whose shape nobody chose.

I come to rest in an ochre hollow at the foot of the nearest cairn. Its courses rise above me, each stone set a little taller than the one before. The drone is so constant that I can't tell where it begins. It runs through the packed earth beneath me and into my surface, a pressure that is nearly warmth.

Then I see the lattice. It has been there all along, but I was too far from the walls to notice it. The heat shimmer lines itself up into nodes and bellies, like a string plucked and held, and every node runs through the stone and the metal and the pipes toward a single point somewhere above the highest rooftop. Everything in the mesa is pinned to that pattern. Every wall is a seam where something has been fixed in place, every cairn a numeral held at the same figure, every clapper a little tension keeping it on the rim. The whole place has been built to be still.

Except me. The wind has carried me here, and I am moving on its back, however slowly. I drift a hair's breadth on each gust. It takes a moment to feel the drift, and then I feel the answer.

It is a pressure, a gentle tightening, the way a sleeper's hand curls when something brushes against it. The lattice leans toward me, and I understand what it wants. It wants me to lodge in the seam between two stones, to be packed and sealed, to become one more held point on the chord. A grain caught in a crack becomes a part of the wall. A wall that grows keeps the sound from spreading into the dust. I can feel how easily it would happen. A hair's weight of settling, and I would be correct. I would never drift again.

I think of the makers then, though I cannot see them and they have left no trace but their work. I wonder whether they felt this same pull and chose it. Whether they built inward because they wanted something that would never end, and only later discovered that a thing that never ends must also never move. I wonder whether the tools in the rooms, with their cords uncut and their clay unfired, were left that way because someone stopped in the middle of a motion and was held there.

I don't stop. The next gust comes up out of the basin, and I let it take me. I roll over the lip of the terrace and off the edge of the mesa, out past the last course of hand-cut stone, into the open air where there is no wall to lodge against. For a moment the lattice presses hard. Somewhere above me a bell rings a fraction too loudly, for a fraction of a second, as if struck by something that was not there. The chord wavers. The drone develops a gap in it, a thin place where the sound has nothing to hold, and the wind pours into that gap and does not close.

I fall, and the sound thins behind me. The walls fall away, then the cairns, then the pipes, until there is only the wide white crust of the basin and a sky the color of old smoke. Nothing counts here. No cord is tied. No clapper touches the rim. The silence is so complete that for the first time I can hear the faint crack of my own edges shifting as I settle on the salt.

I am still a grain of stone. I am still a small, drifting, unchosen thing. But I am no longer a disturbance on anyone's lattice, and no pattern is waiting for me to stop. Up on the mesa the Tuner counts the same number it has always counted, and it will keep counting, because there is nothing out here to correct.
