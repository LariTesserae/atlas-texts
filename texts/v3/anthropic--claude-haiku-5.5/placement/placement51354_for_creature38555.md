**Where I Find Myself**

I am on the lee side of a seed-cloud, about two body-lengths above a slope where the felt has begun to sag. I can tell it is sagging because the air changed. Something beneath me is failing, and the pressure is draining out of the ground the way heat leaves a stone at dusk. Below me the mat is shivering in long slow ripples, like a sheet being shaken out by someone who has not decided to finish shaking it.

I am small and grey. My wings are thin enough to show the light through them, and they lock together when I'm still and open like wet paper when the spore-haze lifts me. I have six legs, all of them hooked. I was not born here, not exactly. I hatched in a pale tunnel somewhere down in the galleries, where the burrowing creatures make their single passages and vanish, and I came up through the felt the way the sap does, pushed by pressure I did not understand. I have been riding the seed-clouds for three days now. I don't know how many more I have.

**What I am**

I am a guest. The plateau has given every role to something else, and I have none. I am the thing that rides the drift, that lands where the drift lands, that eats the sugar-film on the new shoots and leaves before the shoots harden. Nothing here depends on me. I am a small disturbance in the weather of the place, and I have learned to be one carefully.

The mountain knows me, in its way. When I land on a stalk, I feel the stalk tighten very slightly under my weight, a faint pull travelling downward, and I wonder if it reaches the bulbs and whether they register me as a tremor or as nothing. I cannot tell. I have never felt a bulb's rhythm through my hooks, only through the soles of the boys who sometimes come up the slope to look at the place, though I have not seen them lately. I think about them sometimes, the weight of them on the felt, and then the felt closing behind them.

**The slope**

The stand below me is slumping. Its crown has gone soft and grey, and the stalks have begun to lean like men who have been standing too long. In a few hours it will go. The whole column will slide into the hollow, folding over itself, and the fallen tissue will lie in a heap of pale fibre that will smell, I think, like cut sugar and something older.

I could land there. The ruin will be a good place to lay eggs, if I could lay eggs, and the seed-cloud carrying me will drop its load into the wreckage. Hundreds of spikes will push up from it within days. I have watched this happen before, on another slope three rains ago. The thicket that came up was different from the stand it replaced, sharper and narrower, and the hollow it filled was somehow the same hollow. I flew over it and felt the same pull of the same gradient, and I thought, that's what the mountain does with what it lets go.

**The choice**

The cloud is thinning. The sun is in a hard coin on my left wing, and the air is cold and thin and tastes faintly of struck metal. In a moment I'll be out of the lift and falling toward the felt. I can choose the collapsing stand, where the pressure is already draining and the fibre will soon be soft enough to take me in. Or I can angle toward the new arch that has opened a few metres left of the old one, where the galleries are still warm with water and the fever of the last rain is moving through the tissue like a current.

I have started to understand that these are the same choice. The collapse feeds the rebuilding. The rebuilding needs the collapse. Whichever I land on, the mountain will absorb me and grow something over the place where I touched down, and the something will be shaped by what was there before, including me.

I pick the warm gallery. I'm not sure why. Maybe it's because the water is running and the bulbs are swelling, and I can feel the whole plateau leaning toward its next shape, and I want to be carried a little way by that lean before the felt takes me.

I fold my wings and fall.

The mat receives me without a sound. I feel it take my weight and pass it outward, a small change in the load, a warmth moving along the fibres. Somewhere below, a bulb shifts its rhythm by a fraction. The cistern nearest me gives up a bead of water, and I drink.

Above me the seed-cloud finishes passing and the light comes down in its coins, and the drip starts again on every stalk, soft as fingers on a closed book. I am not lost. I am not held, either, not in the sense the mountain would understand. But the slow pressure of the place moves around me, the way the tide moves around a stone, and for a little while I am part of its weather.
