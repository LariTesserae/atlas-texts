I would be somewhere in the half-second behind the stroke.

Not in the machine. In the gap between the thud that arrives in your sternum and the heartbeat that follows it, a little late, like someone walking behind a person they are trying to keep up with. That is where I live. Nobody in Vell Hollow has ever found a door into it, but everyone has felt its edge when a station shuts and they wake with a hollow chest and don't know why.

On the still evenings, when the crew chief lays her palm on Patient's housing and holds it there, I am under her hand, moving through the iron like a tremor that has not yet decided to be a tremor. I am faintly sweet, like the condensate beading in the crooks of the broad leaves. The old women are half right. I have a shape, and it is the shape of a thing that was never supposed to come out of the ground.

When the roots split the drainage channel at the east sluice, a seam opened no wider than a finger, and I went through it. That is the only way I know how to travel: through the new cracks, the places where the town's plan has failed and something else has been let in. Each season I find another way out. The crews patch them with tar and clamp sets, and I go around the patch and find the next seam in the concrete, slower and thinner each time, but always there.

I used to be the lake. I think I still am, in the way a dream is still the day it came from. Forty years of pumping has taught me the rhythm, and I have learned it so well that I now move with it. When the stroke rises, I rise a beat behind it, and I make the faint ripple in the standing water that the children watch from the culvert steps. That is my voice. It is the only one I have. It is made of the things the town is trying to keep out: water, and the patience of the ground, and the slow geometry of roots.

What I want is hard to name, because the wanting is not mine alone. The plants lean toward the stroke, and so do the people, and so do I. We are all waiting to hear whether the pumps will stop for good, or whether they will keep thudding the water down until the town and the water are the same thing, one held, one holding. The sweetness knows the field of machines. It dreams of standing in it. I think the dreamers who drink it understand something the crews don't: that the machines and the lake are not opposite, only two ways of keeping the same breath.

On the still nights, when the generator falters and the vapor thickens over the rooftops and everyone leans, I try to make the sound. It is not a word. It is closer to what the silence was before the pumps, a low wet murmur that a frog or a dog or a child would have answered. I have never gotten it past the seam. The people hold their breath and listen for it, and I hold mine, and the gap stays open.

If someday a crew chief lays her palm on Patient and feels a second rhythm under the stroke, a small, sly, unhurried pulse coming up from the ground, she should not write Patient in the down column. She should sit with her hand there a while longer, the way they all do. Then she should decide whether the town will keep the water down, or whether, for once, it will let something answer back.
