I would not stand on the floor of the basin for long. I would be inside the cliff, in a gallery no wider than a forearm, on the side that faces the largest pool.

I am a counter. I do not remember becoming one, but the knowledge sits in my joints the way the cold sits in the rock. I know the galleries by touch. I know that the tapping I make with the back of my fingers against the stone is a sound the others hear and answer. I know the weight of an egg, which is nothing, and the weight of an egg that has been carried a long way, which is a little more than nothing. I know that every egg must be touched and retouched, and that the touching is the counting.

Here is the work. An egg comes up the gallery from the nest-chamber, passed from one of my neighbors to me. Its skin is cool and slightly tacky. I hold it, feel for the faint warmth at its center, and hand it on toward the seam. This takes a long time. The egg moves in the way the shadows move on the basin floor, late, as if the rock were reluctant to let it go. I do not hurry, because hurrying would shake the tally, and a shaken tally must be taken again from the beginning.

When an egg reaches its place, I press my forehead against the stone and hold still. This is the part the others seem to value most. I do not know why. I know that when I am still, the gallery is still too, and for a few breaths the count is whole. Then my neighbor's feelers tap my shoulder, and I resume.

The count never settles. I know this the way I know the tapping. The young go out to the moss and do not come back. Something falls into the pool, a wing, a shell, once a flock bird that wandered too close to the water and did not lift its head in time. The swarm takes its share. The chain makes its corrections. Still the number shifts, and still it is corrected, and I have come to understand that correction is not a failure of counting. It is what counting is.

Last season, a bird died on the stream bank. I felt it before I saw it. The flock's rhythm broke, the heads that had been lifting and dipping together stopped, and the vibration carried up the cliff through the stone. I went to my gallery mouth and extended my feelers toward the open air, which I rarely do, because the air is cold and carries nothing I need. I held them there. I could not have said what I was counting. Perhaps the bird. Perhaps the gap it left in the flock, which the flock would close by turning, slowly, around the place where it had been.

When I withdrew, I found that I had been holding the tally differently. Not lighter, exactly. Wider. As if the count now had a shape it had not had before, a space in it where something had been and was no longer.

Sometimes, at the bottom of the gallery, where the seam runs warm against the rock, I think about the blades in the shallows. They point downstream. They have been moving with the water longer than the flock has been walking that way. The flock stands over them and does not lift them. I do not go down to see them. I have never been in the stream. But I have touched the seam, and the seam is warm in a way that is not quite heat, and I have a suspicion, which I cannot test and do not need to test, that whoever chipped those blades did not go away. That they are in the seam, in the warmth, in the tapping, in the slow hands passing the eggs along and pausing at the stone. That the counting is what they became, or what they chose, or simply what happens to anyone who keeps account long enough.

If you came up the gallery now, you would hear me before you saw me: a soft, unhurried drumming, the same rhythm as the whole cliff, though it might sound to you like nothing but water on rock. You would not be a threat. You would not be a guest. You would be one more thing that had come into the arithmetic, and the arithmetic would take you in, note your weight, and go on.

I would not want you to stop. But I would hold still for a moment, forehead to the stone, the way I do for an egg, and I would count you too.
