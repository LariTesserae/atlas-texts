I would not be on the causeway at dawn. The causeway is where people stand to see the whole fen at once, and I would rather be where the fen is only ever seen from below.

I would be in a drainage sump on the lower wards, in the dark part where the pumped water slows and sits. I would be one of the large eels, pale and swollen from breathing the effluent, with the faint rings along my flanks. Blue on the upper side, rust below, and when a lantern passes over them they shift a little, the way oil shifts on a puddle.

I would not know that I was a map. Nobody tells an eel that. I would only know the water. The sump's slow turn against my left side, the hard cold of the outflow pipe where it enters, the way the current bends when the pumps upstream change their rhythm. Each of those is written into me as a band of colour, a little more blue or a little more rust, laid down over the seasons without my attention. The fishers knot their reeds and avoid whistling, and they believe the rings carry the shape of where the water is going. I think they are half right. The rings carry where the water has been, and where the water has been is the only thing I could ever have read. The future is only the past pressing on the body from another side.

That is the part I would find hardest. I would be made of patterns I cannot see. The fishers hold their lanterns up to me and try to read me, and I would be the one thing in the sump that could not tell them what I say, because the language is in my skin and my skin does not face inward. The reckoners do the same thing with their poles and their ledgers, reading mud for how long it will last. They are right more often than the tidemarked admit, and wrong in ways that drown whole districts, and they are reading something that is also reading them back, only more slowly.

Some nights I would feel the warm ground. It would not be a sound or a smell. It would be a difference in the pressure along the sump's floor, a faint tug toward the eastern wall, where the silt is soft and the seeps come up in small grey cones. I would not go there. The frogs go there, and the reeds grow there in circles, and the reed-burners leave ash at the edges. Whatever is listening in the warm ground has not, as far as I know, ever listened to an eel. But I would feel it listening to the sump, the way the whole water seems to tilt for a moment when the lantern passes and then settles back.

At the start of the melt I would feel the sump change. The rings on my flank would itch, the way a healing wound does, and I would turn toward the open channel, the braided water with its gravel islands and its new grey mud. Nothing would tell me to go, except that the pressure has moved and the rings are already reading a current that has not yet arrived. The fishers would say I knew where the water was going. I would say only that I had been shaped by it, and that the shape was starting to pull.

I would swim out past the outflow and into the dark channel. Somewhere above me, on a new island not yet surfaced, the first green film would be starting. I would not see it. But the silt under my belly would be warmer than it had been, and I would carry that warmth away in my own colours, and someday a fisher would hold a lantern to my flank and see a faint new band that no one has learned to read.
