I would come down the way the supply carts do, through the broken shelf on a rope with a hook in the limestone, and I would be a little ashamed of how badly I climb. I'd arrive from the sunlit country with a sealed case of paperwork and a pocket full of the confident measurements that people up there use to feel safe. The first thing the basin would do is take my voice. I'd shout to someone across the flat, and the engines would fold the shout into noise, and the echo would come back a half-second late from the far cliff, and a keeper I'd never met would touch two fingers to her forearm and lean in close. I would have to learn to speak into a person's ear.

I'd be given a job I didn't ask for. Ovra's knees have gone, and her eyes have started to fail at the edges. She'd need someone to carry the calipers and the dated iron pins along the third terrace, and to read the lip aloud while she wrote. So I'd be her hands for a while, and then, slowly, her replacement. That is how most people become tallymen. Nobody chooses it. Someone gets tired and a new person is standing there holding the level.

The work would be smaller than I expected. You take the lip's thickness at three points. You press the caliper gently, because the crust is still soft at the edge and gives like bread. You write the number in the notebook kept dry in its oilcloth. Then you set a pin at the spot, so that next year you can find the same place, and the year after, and the decade after that. For the first few months I'd think of it as a chore. Then one morning the caliper would click against a lip I'd measured in the spring and the difference would be there, a sliver of pale growth, and I'd realize that I was feeling something close to what Ovra had described. Not pride. Something more like being noticed by a thing too slow to notice back.

I'd ask her the question everyone asks, why bother, since the rock will take the terrace regardless. She'd laugh and say the counting is how the rock learns it's being watched. I would not know whether that was true, and I'd suspect she didn't either, and that she'd picked the sentence because it was the only honest thing she could say about why a person keeps a record of something that does not keep records of them.

I'd be a tallyman, but I'd also be one of the tenants in a small way. The moths would come to my lamp at dusk and I wouldn't be able to tell them apart from the moths that came to Ovra's. The eels would move in their long ripples in the shallow pool beside the third terrace, and I'd sit at the edge with my notebook on my knee and watch them do whatever they do, and I'd write nothing down, because some things I'd measure only by staying. The guano would be carried past me on a sledge toward the reeds. The bats would pour out of the cliff cracks in waves. I'd begin to recognize the rhythm of the pump engines the way you learn the breathing of someone asleep in the next room.

When Ovra died, which she would, in the warm pool at the edge of the middle flat, wrapped in cloth and weighted with a stone, I would walk out to the spot where the water took her and touch the crust. It would be cold under my palm, and I would tell it what had happened in the town since the last visit, because that is what the Lift do, and because I'd have nobody else to tell. The hump would begin to form beneath the surface. In a generation, a family would walk the basin and find her by its shape, a low white body lying on its side, and they would read her the way other people read a name.

And then I'd go back to the third terrace with the calipers and the pins and a new notebook, because the water was rising, and someone at a doorway would tap two fingers on a forearm, and I'd answer with a palm on my chest. *Then we will hold it.* I'd set a pin in the fresh crust and take the first measurement, and write the number down, and the rock would thicken by the width of a finger in the time it took me to finish the line.

That is who I would be down there: a person who counts a thing that is always leaving, and who stays, a little longer than the water would like, to write down that it was here.
