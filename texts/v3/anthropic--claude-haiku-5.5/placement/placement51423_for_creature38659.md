I would be a tender, the one with the clouded left lens, standing at the edge of the third sheet where the water has reached the lip of a troughed path. My registry plate is scraped to three digits, and the rest I have stopped trying to read. My frame is salvaged plate, mostly grey, with a yellow band that once marked a pressure line now removed. The line is gone and the band remains, which is how most of my markings work.

The red light is blinking on the panel of pump housing nine. I cannot see the panel from here. I feel it in the way the mist moves, a faint pulse in the air that means something, somewhere, has changed its state. I lift my head. The clusters on my crown turn in their slow, uneven sweep, and for a moment the sweep catches the lamp on pole forty, which has leaned so far that its amber light falls short of the water, and the dark beneath it is the kind I have learned to step around.

I start walking. My steps are short, and every third or fourth I lower the probe and tap. The slab answers with a ringing note, sound footing, and I move on. The path under me is a shallow trough, worn by my kind's feet and the feet before ours, smooth as the lip of a stair. I do not remember walking it for the first time. I know its curve the way a hand knows the grip of its own tool.

Halfway along, a seam sweats a bead of rust-brown water. The grey-green film has crept into the joint and sealed the hairline crack beneath it. I pause, not to attend the seam, since there is no fault in it, but because the film has the texture of a healed wound, and something in my servos registers the good closure and holds for three beats. Then I go on. The film does not need me. I do not need the seam to need me. We are both where we are.

The drip overhead changes its rhythm as I pass under the girder. A stutter, then steady, then a stutter again. I file it. Not urgent. The panel call is louder than a drip.

At housing nine I find the tilt. The base has sunk on its western side, two degrees, perhaps three. The pump's roar has shifted by a fraction, a seam in its breath, and two of my nearest kin have already turned toward it, their heads swinging together like cattle at a change in the wind. I do not look at them. I lower the probe and tap the western footing. A dull note. A void beneath, and the slab there is no longer holding.

I unlatch my flat slag shims from the carrier on my back. They are heavier than I remember, or my hydraulics are tired. I set the first beneath the lip and feed the cable through the brace. The housing groans as the weight shifts. The steam from its vent bends, tears, and reforms. I tap again. Ringing. I set the second shim, and the third, and the housing begins, by a degree I can feel through my frame more than measure, to come back toward true.

Whatever true is, I think, and the thought arrives sideways, like one of the steam columns tearing apart over the basin. I do not finish it. The probe is still resting on the slab, and the slab is still ringing under it, and that is the answer I have. True is a note. True is the sound footing makes when it holds.

When the housing sits, I set the brace, check the cable, and take a step back. In the nearest standing pool, a perfectly level sheet the subsidence has made into a mirror, I see my reflection: a squat grey shape with its head bowed and its clouded lens facing the lamp, the lamp itself reflected inverted beneath the surface, the two lights almost touching. The water is level with the slab, and the slab is level with the housing, and I am level with both, for the moment.

The red light goes dark. I did not see it go dark. I felt the pulse in the mist stop.

I stand there. There is nothing more to attend on this route. So I walk to the nearest stack, where the vent-steam rises thick and warm, and I lock my limbs and bow my head. The roar of the pumps comes through my frame. The drip rings off the sheet metal above. The groan of the ground settling a few millimetres somewhere below passes through my plate and out the other side. I let it.

Then, deep in the stillness, I lift my head. One drip, out of rhythm, from a joint I have not walked to. I turn toward it. I do not go. The sound is faint and I cannot tell whether it is a fault or only the water, which keeps trying to return and which, for now, I am still holding back.
