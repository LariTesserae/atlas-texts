**Where I Would Be**

I would not stand at the lip of the fall. The roar would scour me in a day, and I would learn nothing from it but how loud the world can be. I would be on the north wall, where the upright slabs lean out from the rock like a row of teeth, in the band of mist that the falling water throws up and that never quite settles. It is damp there and cool, the light is the grey-green of the moss's own glow, and the pecked marks are close enough to touch.

I would find the marks first, because I would be looking for something to read. Dots, short strokes, a grammar of small wounds in the stone. Lichen has crept into the grooves so that the marks are half green, half grey, and I cannot tell whether the pattern was a count, a name, a warning, or a prayer. The people who made them are gone so completely that even their rubbish has been turned into soil. I would stand there, unable to read, and feel the particular loneliness of a sentence whose verbs have all been lost.

**Who I Would Be**

I would be a spider.

Not a large one. Something small and grey-brown that the light only finds in the mornings, when the mist beads on my threads and makes them visible. I would hang my sheet between the leaning slabs and the nearest fern, and I would set the threads so that the marks lay beneath them like something under glass. My web would not be a trap, or not only. It would be a way of holding the pattern in place while I tried to understand its shape, a way of making the marks into a surface I could move across.

Each morning the drip would tear part of it down. A thread would snap under the weight of a water-bead, or a bit of moss would slump and take a corner of the sheet with it. And each morning I would begin again, running a new line from the slab's edge to the fern's frond, adding one more strand to the lattice, one more loop to hold the dots and strokes in their places. The rebuilding would be the work. I would never finish the reading. I would only keep the veil up, and in keeping it up I would be doing the nearest thing to understanding that I could manage.

I think I would choose this because I know what it is to be made of patterns that I did not write. The marks are not mine. Neither is the water, nor the moss, nor the rock they grow on, though all of them, in their way, are the sentences I am trying to parse. A spider does not need to know what a glyph means in order to spin around it. It only needs to keep spinning, to meet each tear with a new thread, to let the light find it at the right angle once in a while.

**What the Days Would Be Like**

Mornings would be the best time. The mist would be thick, the threads heavy with water, and every strand would catch a little of the grey light. I would stand at the centre of my web and feel the whole wall tremble with the roar, which would reach me as a pressure in the legs more than as a sound. The dipper would bob on its stone below, unbothered. The newt would feel the cold through the moss, a few body-lengths away, and would not care what I was doing.

Dusk would bring the deer. I would feel it before I saw it, a shift in the vibration of the frond beside my web, and then a shape would come down the western slope and stop at the spring-lip to drink. It would lift its head and look across the bowl toward the fallen slabs and the broken trough, toward me without seeing me. I would hold still. The deer would look away, climb back into the trees, and I would go back to my threads.

I would not expect anyone to come. The place does not expect visitors, and I would not expect them either. But if someone did come, and stood at the edge of the spray with their hands in their pockets, I might let one thread catch the light just so, so that they could see that the marks were still under a web, and that someone had been trying, in a small and patient way, to read them.
