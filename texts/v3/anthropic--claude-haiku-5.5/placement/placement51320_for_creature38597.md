I am the newest thing in the basin.

For a while I am only a face: a raw plane of grey stone, sharp at its edges, catching a light that has never touched me before. Last season I was part of the rim, the sky's edge, and then the frost in a crack loosened me, and I slid. The fall was slow and total. I remember the wall above me going dark and then gone, and the scree rushing up to meet me like a tide that had been waiting. Now I lie tilted in the grey rubble at the foot of the cliff, and the cold comes off the mere in a steady breath and beads on my fresh surfaces. No one has named me yet. I am only the stone that came down.

The first thing that finds me is damp. Water works into the hairline fractures on my underside and begins, very slowly, to stain them. Then comes the lichen, a pale bloom no bigger than a fingernail, settling in the lee of a splinter. It does not hurry, and neither do I. I learn that the basin is always moving: the scree shifts under me, a seep cuts a new channel a body's length away, and a bird lands on my shoulder, probes, and leaves. Its weight is nothing. The weight I learn to feel is the other kind, the slow weight of the rim above, which is still shedding, still breaking off in grey slabs that tumble past me and stop.

Then a knuckle strikes the ground near me.

The tap goes into the scree and up through my body, a sharp sound that I feel rather than hear. It comes again, and again, and then a pulse begins, low and even, rising out of the chest of someone I cannot see and passing through the rubble into my core. I am being counted. The Reckoners climb down over the scree on their broad webbed hands, and they lay their palms on me, and their pads press along my faces, testing, listening, and I understand what I am becoming: a single slump, a block in the ledger of the wall. My fall is a line. The line will be cut into the rim-stones above, a small stacked shape among the years, and someone will run a finger over it and hum the number.

They carry me up the terraces on rollers of split log, a rhythm of heaves and pulses, each one timed so that no one strains alone. For the first time I am held and moved by many hands at once. The hum rolls through the group and into me, and I feel the coordination in the stone the way you might feel a current in a river. They set me on a middle tier, in a wall that has been rebuilt four times already. The masonry around me is fitted so closely that I can feel the neighbouring blocks settle against my edges, and when the last of the pressed cake is worked into the joint beside me, the wall goes silent under its own weight. The Presser who checked the seam holds its pads against my face a long time. Then it knocks once, and the knock goes down through the terraces below me and comes back up.

I learn the mere by its breath. In the wet season the water climbs the lower tiers, and I feel it lapping at the footings, soaking the sphagnum around the posts, pressing its cold into the joints until the dry stone swells with it and does not crack. In the dry season it withdraws, and the walls breathe out, and I hear the spiral carvings above me tighten, the coil drawn in toward a centre I cannot see. Each season the basin rebuilds the gallery over my head, sags, is shored, and is laid again. I am part of the shoring. I hold the weight of new wood, and the wood holds me.

The pressing comes in the dark. The Pressers work the grey paste in the upper hollows, the sphagnum and clay and horsetail root ground together until the cakes ring when knocked, and then they bring the cakes down and set them into my joints. The paste is cold, and then it dries hard, and then it is part of me. Once, in a cake that was being cut to fit beside my face, I feel a small hard fragment pressed into the fibres, and I hold it as the paste sets. It is a finger's length of pale bone, curled. Someone who went down into the peat is now in the seam beside me, and when the basin's breath moves through the wall, I think the bone and I move together. I do not know what to do with that knowledge. I do not think I need to. The dwellers do not hide it. The stone does not hide it either.

So this is what I am, now that the first season has gone. I am the block that marks a single fall, a numeral in a count that has been running longer than the stacks above can hold. I am a surface where lichen grows in frost-coloured rings and where a shoulder rubs past every day. I am a seam with a dead thing in it, held in suspension like everything else in this place. I am not finished. The walls will sag, and I will be shored, and the cakes will crack, and new cakes will be pressed to fill the cracks, and this, I think, is what the stone is for: not to stand still, but to keep the count.

At dusk the fog pools on the mere again. The bats come out from under the galleries in dark clusters, and the water finds its old channels down through every rock face. Somewhere above me, on the highest tier, a Presser is seated on the sounding stone, its throat swelling, and the long even pulse goes down through the terraces into my body. It is not calling anyone. It is only humming, a steady note held low. I hold it. My fall is a line in the wall, and the line is a voice, and the voice is passing down through the basin, through the walls and the herds and the moss and the dead, until the next knock comes back up from below.
