# Knock Twice

I would be on the lead roof of the third foundry stack, the one the Tenders call Old Gallows because its crown leans toward the east basin like a man listening for a boat. The roof is the size of a threshing floor, grey with moss in the seams, and I would lie on it with my cheek against the cold metal, because that is where the iron speaks most clearly.

I am a Tender, or I was, or I am learning to be one. I came up from the drowned district of the ropeworks three months ago. My hands are still soft. The mineral bands have not yet risen on my forearms, and when I climb, I still hold the rungs too tightly. The older ones watch me the way you watch a new lamp that has not yet decided whether to burn.

At dusk I take the wrench and strike the nearest stanchion, twice, with a pause between. *Are you there.* The sound goes out along the girders, across the flat grey skin of the water, and I feel it leave me the way breath leaves the body. Somewhere to the north, a knuckle answers on a gantry, then another on a housing rail, and the question passes from roof to roof until the basin is full of small, patient questions.

Tonight the bioluminescence is up. The whole town glows faintly from below, green-white and slow, climbing the lamp posts and pooling in the cellar windows. Where a boat drifted through the channel earlier, its wake still fades, a long pale scar on the black water that the Tenders will have seen and logged. I have been watching it dissolve for a long time.

Then I hear it.

It does not come along the stanchion. It comes up through me. I am lying with my ear on the iron and my whole skull is humming, and the knocking under the town is not the pump's ordinary rhythm. It is a pattern. Three short, one long, a pause. The old men say that number means a name is being called. I have never heard it from below before.

I tap back, once, uncertain. My knuckle is loud against the stanchion.

The answer comes at once, too quickly for a machine, and it is nothing I expected. It is not two taps. It is a slow falling series, the sign for turnover, the sign for the lower brine rising. Then, after a silence long enough to feel like grief, it comes again, very softly, from much deeper than the pumps should reach.

I think of the warm intake, the one the Tenders do not speak about in the galleries. I think of how the water is thickest and brightest there, how it holds heat like a sleeping body. I think of how the dead go down through the layers in their waxed shrouds, slowly, and are taken into the silt beneath the streets, and become part of the floor the living walk on.

I wonder, lying here, whether I have it backward. Perhaps the pumps have never been lifting water for the town. Perhaps they have been lifting the town's attention, or its memory, back up toward the living, one knock at a time, and the Tenders are only the ones who have learned to hear it. Perhaps the thing under the flood has been trying to speak with the iron for generations, and the Tenders have been answering without knowing who was asking.

I am not the pump. I am not the water or the silt or the glowing crust on the cellar screens. If I have a body in this town, it is the one that receives the knocking and sends it back: the listening part, the part that keeps a hand on the iron and waits to see whether the rhythm will change.

The stack creaks under me, a long groan that fades into the water. The bioluminescence brightens along the lamp posts. Somewhere far off, a Tender taps twice on a pipe, and I answer with two taps of my own, and then, very slowly, I hold my hand flat against the cold metal and wait.
