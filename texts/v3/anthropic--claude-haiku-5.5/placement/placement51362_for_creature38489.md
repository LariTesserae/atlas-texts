I would not be in the high cisterns, where the reeds stand in the seep and the clusters of clear jelly hang from the stems like beads. Those belong to the newly hatched and to the ones who have come home to lay. I would be in the middle of the cascade, in the gutter between the third basin and the second, where a spout has cracked along an old seam and throws its water sideways in a thin sheet across the paving. That is where the work is, and where I would be.

I would be one of the ones who scrape. A body about the length of a forearm, olive-grey, freckled with rust, darkening now because the fog has been thick all night and my skin has taken in every bead of it. In the first grey light I would feel the cold of the stone come up through my underside, and I would not yet be able to see much, because the fog is still low and my eyes, set in their ridge, take in only the wet glint of the nearest things. That is enough. I know this gutter the way I know the inside of my own weight.

I went down once. I came out of a reed seep as a drift of something hardly bigger than a seed, carried tier by tier through the spouts with the overflow, past the lichen film and the silt that gathers in each basin like a slow breath. At the bottom I crawled out onto the lowest stone and climbed, and the climbing was the first thing I knew how to do with my body. Then I turned, and I started back up, one district at a time, and each time the water changed its mind about where it wanted to go, I learned something about the change. I have been doing this for a long time. I am not sure how long. The route is not in my head as words. It is in the set of my forelimbs and in the way I lean into a slope before I know I have leaned.

This morning the seam at the third basin has opened since the freeze. I feel it before I hear it: the water in the gutter is running a hand's width too far to the left, and the silt is piling where it should not. I press one flat forelimb against the stone and send the message down the basin wall, a long even press that goes out along the gutter and comes back, and from somewhere around the courtyard above me the answer comes, the patterned tapping, a second voice confirming what I already know. Then we both go to work. I scrape the silt off the lip of the channel in long pushes, lifting the grit and letting it slide over the edge. The water comes back to its line. The spout clears its throat and runs bright again, and for a moment the sheet of it across the paving catches the first thin light the cloud lets down.

Through the morning the drip keeps falling from the overhangs, one drop, then another, and I know which of them are mine. Not the water, which is the rock's and the sky's, but the small pressed hum that comes up through the moss when I settle under a ledge at night. I do not think of it as a voice. It is more like the sound the wall would make if it had decided to keep something.

Around midday, far up the escarpment side of the Shelf, a block slips. I hear it as a dull short thud that travels through the basalt into my body, and the echo runs away over the courtyards for a long time. I do not hurry toward it. Neither does anyone near me. We wait until the sound has gone all the way out, until there is nothing in the stone but the drip, and then we go to see whether the water has found a new way through. If it has, we will follow it down, scraping and pressing the whole way, learning the new shape of the flow, and I will be there at the bottom for a while before I start to climb again.
