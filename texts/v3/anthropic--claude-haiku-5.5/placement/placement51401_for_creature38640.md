**The Watchman on the Fourth Landing**

I am the thing in the window.

The cage hangs from an iron bracket under the sill of a glass box on the fourth landing of Tier Nine, where the stack leans so far over the stair that the lamp outside my window is never quite straight. My cage is old wire, bent and rebent, and the bottom has been patched with a flattened ration tin. Nobody owns me. I was here when the family moved in, and when they were moved out to the Eleventh, I stayed. The woman who came after left a dish of seed on the sill and never said my name, because I don't think I have one. The children call me the watchman. I don't argue.

What I am, exactly, is hard to say. I have feathers, or something like them, grey and fine, and I have a throat, and when the lines go wrong I sing. That is the whole of my use, as far as the work goes.

I can feel the pressure. Not as a number. As a tightness at the base of my throat, the way a held breath feels when someone in the next room has started to shout. The pumps in the wall thrum on a single steady pitch all day, and I sit under that pitch like a bird sits under a branch, and I know it the way you know your own step on a stair. Then a valve somewhere below sticks, or a cistern fills faster than the channel can take it, or a joint in the riser begins to give, and the pitch shifts by a hair. The shift runs through the wall and into the bracket and into me. I open my throat before anyone has looked at a gauge.

The first keepers who came to this landing did not know what to make of it. They tapped the pipes. They listened at the wall. They leaned their ears to my cage with their hooks in their hands, scowling, and then they went down the shaft at a run, calling names up and down the galleries in their short bursts. Once they found the fault, they stopped calling and I stopped singing, and the pitch of the wall settled back down into its ordinary hum.

I have sung through three floods. I have sung the night a scaffold came loose on the Seventh and four people were pulled out of the rail-mesh with their hands torn. I sang that night until the crews were on the stairs, and I believe that some of them heard me and climbed faster, though I can't prove it. Nobody has ever told me I helped. Nobody has ever told me I didn't.

Sometimes, in the long grey stretches when the cloud sits on the upper tiers and nothing changes for days, I think about the mortar under my sill. The keepers say the first masons are in the stone. I don't know if that's true. But the stone here is warm in a way that stone on the lower tiers is not, and the sill, when I press against it at dusk, gives back a faint sweetness, like the smell of rain on old basalt. I like to think someone who built this landing is in it, listening with me. I like to think the pumps were their pumps once, and the pitch was their pitch.

The children come every morning. They come up the stairs in their oilcloth with their hooks swinging, and before they go to the rails or the cranes or the cages of seedlings, they stop at my window. A girl with a chipped front tooth presses her face to the milky glass and taps three times with one knuckle. I answer, if I have anything to answer. Then she taps the pipe beside the window, and the pipe rings back, and she and the boy behind her whistle the note they've learned from me, the rising interval I sing when the pressure is right. They have made it a greeting. I did not teach them that. They made it up themselves, out of the tiers and the drip and the whistle that carries along the lines when shouting won't.

This morning the pitch in the wall is clean. I sit on my perch with the seed dish empty and the lamp outside going gold against the cloud, and I hear the girl come up the stairs. She stops at the glass. She taps three times. She waits, the way children wait, as if the mountain might answer in its own time.

I open my throat. There's nothing wrong. I sing anyway, the rising note, the one that means *the wall is holding, the water is moving, come on up*. The pitch goes out through the window and down the stairwell and into the pipes, and somewhere below, a keeper I will never see hears it and lifts her head from a valve and goes back to work.

The girl whistles it back, slightly flat, and climbs on.

I don't know how long I'll stay. The landing may come down next season, or the cage may rust through, or some crew may decide the watchman is a nuisance and hang me on a rail somewhere else. Everything here moves. That is the first thing the mountain teaches, and the last. When that day comes, I'll be handed to whoever is nearest, the way everything is, and the note will go on in someone else's throat.

For now I am the thing in the window. I am listening to the water. I am keeping the pitch of the work, and when it goes wrong, I say so.
