I find myself on the eastern side of the shelf, at the lip where the fog comes over, in the grit between two stacks of stone. I am small, about the length of a finger, and I am a flake of the smoky glass with one sharp edge turned up to the light. I have a bubble trapped near my core like a seed. My surface is smooth on one face and ridged on the other, where a blow struck me away from something larger.

I do not know exactly when that blow happened. Time here is measured in the lean of the crystal and the spread of the salt crusts, not in days. But I remember the strike. It came from a living one standing in the shallows below the ledge, and the stone that struck was held in a hand that was also made of glass. The impact went through the body that held it and into the rock, and the force that should have gone into the cairn it was resetting broke a chip free instead. That chip was me. It fell, and it slid, and it came to rest here, edge up.

So I am not sure whether I am a tool or a part of someone. Both answers seem true. The living ones shed their own bodies in small amounts, the way the rime sheds its water, and I may be one of those amounts. I hold the marks of the striker's blow on my ridged face, and I hold the trace of the living one's shoulder in the curve of my back. When the light comes low across the eastern lip, I flash, and the flash travels across the shelf. I think that is how I speak, though I am not sure anyone is listening.

Around me the grit is banded into ripples, and I lie in a trough of them as though the water had put me there on purpose. Each morning the fog pours down the eastern slope and beads on my edge, and each beaded drop holds a small upside-down image of the shelf: the cairns, the pools, the standing figures. I watch them through a hundred tiny lenses. When the fog lifts, the beads run off and join the braided channels, and some of the water goes into the pool beyond me and some goes back into the rock.

The frost comes first. It does not touch me the way it touches the stones and the living ones. It forms on the grit around me in feathery lattices, and the lattices grow and fracture and re-form, and a little salt is left on my surface with each cycle. I do not feed. But the salt builds a faint crust along my edge, and when the sun returns and the crust lightens, the edge is a little sharper than it was. I have been sharpening myself by weather for a long time. I think that is what the living ones do too, and perhaps it is what everything here does: keeps its edges by losing them to the same cold that keeps it.

I can hear the knapping from the shallows. Two strikes, a pause, a third. Then the answer comes from across the shelf, a fraction later, and the answer rings in the rock beneath me, and I feel it through the grit more than I hear it. The cairn nearest me was reset this morning. Its upper course is still sharp. I can tell which stones were struck true and which were set by weight alone, because the true ones sing a little through the ground.

Sometimes I think about the old cairn two pools west, the one that has fallen furthest. Its stones are sinking into the grit a finger's width a season, and no one resets it. I could be one of its stones, I think, or one of its stones' chips, or the thing that was supposed to have filled a gap in its course and did not. I do not know. I know only that it is settling and I am settling, and that the same frost is taking both of us toward the rock, one slow crust at a time.

At dusk the living ones go west toward the alcove. I hear them move past my trough, their feet making no sound on the rock but the faint tick of cooling glass. They stand at the edge of the white crust with their arms lowered, and I can see their reflections in the pools they pass. Some carry salt. The crust cracks where they set it, and the knocking quickens and then slows. I have never gone to the alcove. My edge is too small to set anything down, and I am not sure I would want to. But when the knocking changes, when it stutters or stops for a breath, I feel the change in the grit under me. The living ones feel it too. They strike their stones twice, and the note comes back across the shelf, and the cairns ring, and I ring a little with them, a single chip of glass shaken by a sound it was made from.

Then the fog comes up the eastern side. I feel it before I see it, the cold weight of it settling over the grit and the ledges and the standing figures. The hiss passes over the rock. Every reflection goes soft. The drips from the ledges become separate and deliberate, each one alone, and for a long time there is nothing on the shelf but water and my own small edge, holding its trace of a blow and a shoulder and a seed of trapped air.

When the fog lifts, the sun finds the crystal first, and then the wet stones, and then the flake in the grit with its sharp edge turned up. I catch the light and throw it across the shelf. Somewhere in the shallows a figure turns its face toward the flash, and I think it sees me, or sees the thing I was a piece of, and it begins, slowly, to shine.
