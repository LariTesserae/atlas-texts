I come down the outer slope in the hour before the mist lifts, when the shale is still damp and the scree slides a little under my feet. I am not sure I am the one who arrives. The rim is raw enough that I can feel its flakes lift as I pass, and I think the ridge has noticed me the way a cut notices air. I stop on a terrace of shale, where the sheets stack like unfinished roof tiles, and I put my hand flat on the stone. It is cool and faintly grainy, and somewhere under it something turns over, slowly, and settles.

I would be small. Not the Settled, who take a generation to move a hand's width, and not the cart drivers, whose ruts are still filling with water. I would be the grey creature that runs along the gutters.

I know it only from the edges of the basin. It is about the length of my forearm, low and smooth, with a body the colour of the clay after rain. When it moves, its outline blurs, as if the damp has not finished deciding where it ends. It feeds on the lichen that shivers in the channels, and it drinks from the bowls the Settled set out beside the condensers. It flees when I approach, but not quickly. I like that. It means the fear is real but is not the whole of it.

So I go down into the channels. The water runs in threads along the clay, branching like the veins of a leaf, and I follow the one that runs downhill, though I learn quickly that downhill here is a matter of where I stand. Ten steps later the water is running up the wall of a cistern, and I have to decide whether to follow it or stay with the gutter. I stay. The gutter is my road. Its edges are sharp where the clay has cracked into polygons the size of my palm, and each polygon curls slightly, as if the ground is holding its breath.

The lichen grows in low crusts along the banks, grey-green, and when the mist moves over it, it trembles. I put my face close to it and the trembling runs through my whole body, a very slight loosening, like a muscle waking. I eat a little. It tastes of cold stone and something faintly sweet, like the first water after a long dry. I think this is what the Settled are: a body that feeds on its own weather.

Then I find the bowl. It stands beside a condenser at the height of a small animal, its rim worn smooth by seasons of hands that are not quite hands. The water is clear and reflects a sky that is not the sky above me, a darker blue with a single star, though it is only morning. I lower my head and drink. It is cold enough to ache in the teeth. When I lift my head the condenser above me hums, below hearing, and I feel the hum more than I hear it, a pressure behind the eyes, like being listened to.

I understand, then, why the bowl is full each morning. Someone, or something, has been filling it so that I, or something like me, would come and drink, and so that the drinking would be recorded in the way the Settled record everything: in layers, slowly, in the change of a stair or the sharpness of a rut. I am not the first grey creature to drink here, and I will not be the last. The bowl is a question they asked a long time ago, and each of us who drinks from it is the answer.

I would stay in the gutters for a while. I would learn the paths between the condensers, the places where the water runs up instead of down, the stairs that lose a step on a day when I am tired. I would move through the strata of the older streets, feeling the second lane of pale stone above the cobbles as a kind of memory under my belly. Sometimes I would lie down in a half-built chamber, not to sleep, since I do not think the Settled sleep as I do, but to rest the way sediment rests, and I would feel, very faintly, my edges being considered. I would not know what was being decided. Perhaps it is only whether I belong. Perhaps it is whether I am a moment worth keeping.

Eventually, if I stayed long enough, I think I would become a layer. The Settled would take the grain of my body, the greyness of my passing, and press it into a wall, and some future creature would run its hand along that wall and find a faint banding there, a cross-section of a small thing that drank from a bowl and fled but not quickly. That would be my life: not a long one, but kept.

The mist is lifting now. The rim is still crumbling one flake at a time, and somewhere above me the hiss in the walls has begun to rise and fall in phrases I cannot quite catch. The water in the bowl has a small tremor on its surface, though the air is still. I hear a sound behind me, a settling of clay, and I do not turn to look. I drink once more, and the bowl is full, and I did not fill it, and I am, for a little while, the question that answers back.
