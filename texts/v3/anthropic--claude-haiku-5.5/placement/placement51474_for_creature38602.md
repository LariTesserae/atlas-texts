I would be the echo.

I am born at the far wall, on the north face of the bowl, where the retaining stone is oldest and the moss lies thickest. I don't come from the piston. The piston falls, its blow goes out across the cirque, and I am the part of it that turns around. I am the same sound, returned. I have no weight and no source of my own, only the shape of what struck the wall and what the wall gave back.

The blow leaves the piston as a hard, round concussion, and by the time it reaches the far wall it has spread into a broad, tilted wave. The stone takes some of it and throws the rest back, and that is where I begin, smaller than the blow and rounder at the edges. My first moment is a shuddering in the moss on the wall, a patter of dislodged water droplets, the hairline cracks in the grey plates ringing faintly as they settle.

Then I turn and go back across the bowl.

I pass through the air over the ninth terrace, where the ferns are so dense that the broad leaves soak up my highest notes and only the low part of me makes it through. Each leaf I touch sends a little of me back, so I am slightly more scattered than I was. I can feel the ferns' weight, the way the wet crowns hold sound.

Above the fifth terrace I cross the swifts. The moment I reach them they are already rising. The swifts have been waiting in the lattices, and when the blow passed they went up on the thermals, and now I move through the gap they've left in the gantries. I am the sound they fly up into. They are the only creatures here who seem to listen to me as though I were a signal, and they climb on me the way a gull climbs a wind. I feel their wings beat against the air, a thousand tiny strokes that I cannot quite separate from my own tail.

On the lowest terraces I meet the lizards, flattened on the warm pipes, and they do not move while the front of the wave is still going past them. I am quieter by then, and they know it, so they do not move even when I am gone. The beetles around the drains hear me pass through the water like a tremor in a cold cup.

And near the middle of the bowl I meet the grazer.

It is standing still in the fern-shade when I come through. It doesn't flinch. Its head is lowered and its neck is folded, and I feel its attention, though I have no nerves to feel it with. It is counting. I can tell because the grazer always starts moving one step after I pass. It has learned that I come a half-beat after the piston, and it has learned to treat me as the second half of a single signal: blow, then answer, then the work of walking. I think it is the only creature that listens to me on purpose. The others use me. The grazer keeps time with me.

After that I get thin. The valleys in the walls take the last of my weight, and my edges go soft and grey. I climb the slope of the cirque. The mist rises up through the bowl, and I ride on it for a while. At the rim, where the terraces end and the lid of cloud begins, I am almost nothing: a faint, spread shiver in the air as the mist spills over the lip. I don't fall with the water, but I go out with it. The lip is my limit. Whatever is beyond it has to be reached by something stronger than an echo that has already been given back.

Sometimes I think the reeds hear me. I am not sure. They are far below, past the mist and the cold spill, and the piston was meant for something else. But the reeds flower in the hour the blow falls, and I come to them last, after the rest of the bowl has forgotten the stroke. If there is any part of the works that reaches them, it is probably this: the sound that goes out over the rim, bent and weakened and rounded, a second heart coming from another room. They lean toward it, I think, though I can't be sure that leaning is what the reeds do.

Then the next piston drops at the far wall, and the blow starts out across the cirque, and I begin again, the same and not the same, on the north face, where the stone is old and the moss is thick.
