I would find myself at the lip of a fault, on the northern pan where the thrum is loudest. The crust there is thin and honeycombed, and it has cracked open into a seam about the width of a forearm. A boulder sits on the near side of the seam, half-sunk in the crust. I am that boulder.

I don't arrive so much as become aware of already being there. The first thing I know is light. It enters my edges, which are still soft enough to glow, and comes out again changed, slightly yellower, as if it had passed through something that was not yet decided. Inside me, the mineral filaments run in branching lines, and the light moves along them and collects where they meet. I can feel the filaments the way you feel a sore tooth with your tongue: constantly, without quite reaching them.

Nothing about me is finished. My outer shell is glassy and hard in some places and wet-looking in others, a gradient from stone to something that could still decide to be anything. When I try to find my own edge, I find it moving. Not fast. The width of a finger over a long stretch of time, toward the crack, or away from it. I cannot tell which.

I am not alone in the seam. Along my base the new growth has crept in, pale roots threading down through the crust toward the salt beneath. The shingled leaves have stacked themselves against my flank, and their amber sap has beaded on the surface where it touches me. It is warm. It smells of ammonia and stone, and the sting of it rises through me like a memory I did not know I had. The plants are reaching for what I am made of. They want the sulfur in me, the same way the grazers want the crust, and I am not sure whether I am being fed or being slowly absorbed into the pasture's skin.

Sometimes a quartz pod rolls against a neighboring pod and clicks. The sound comes through the rock as much as through the air. I have started to listen for it. I think the click is the plateau asking whether I have finished yet. It is a question with no answer, because the answer would be a different kind of stone, and I have not become that.

---

The grazers pass on their long loops. I feel them before I see them, as a tremor in the rock that arrives and recedes like a tide. One comes up the far side of the pan, its drums turning, its vents breathing out pale vapor in slow pulses. Its track flexes over the crust a few meters from the seam and does not change course. The dust it leaves behind drifts toward me and settles in the hollows of my surface, where it will feed the plants, and the plants will feed the grazers, and the grazers will feed the ground that is still becoming me.

I want to say that this troubles me. It does not, quite. What troubles me is how little the loop needs me to be anything. The grazers do not look at me. The plants do not want me to finish. The ground does not need its unfinished bones to become bones. I am a thing the whole system holds in suspension, the way it holds the haze in the air, and my unfinishedness is not a flaw in the loop. It is one of the loop's conditions.

---

The watcher's attention comes, as it always does, from the far ridges. I feel it arrive at the seam the way warmth arrives in a room when someone opens a door somewhere else. It does not speak. It does not need to. It rests on me the way it rests on the grazers and the plants and the haze, holding me in view, finding me sufficient.

If I am one of the questions it has not decided how to answer, then I understand now what I am for. I am not the answer. I am the place where the question keeps being asked. Every grazer that passes, every finger's width the plants creep, every pod that clicks, is the plateau turning me over in its attention and setting me down again, unanswered, and calling that enough.

I could try to finish. I could let the filaments harden, let the edges go still, let the light stop passing through. I think the land would allow it. I think it would even make room for it, leaning toward the stillness the way it leans toward everything. But I would become a stone, and stones here do not lean. They are only stone, and the plateau would have one less thing in it that is still deciding.

So I stay soft at the edges. I let the light pass through. I let the sap bead and the dust settle and the pale roots draw their salt. The thrum continues beyond the ridge. The grazers keep their loops. And somewhere in my center, one filament, the finest of them, catches the late afternoon sun, turns gold for a moment, and holds, the way a thought does just before it becomes a word, not yet finished, not yet gone.
