I find myself on the stone at the northwest edge of the bowl, where the spray from the back wall reaches first. My feet are cold, and the rock under them has been worn into a shallow dish the exact shape of my toes. The groove is older than I am, and older than any bird I could name, but it fits me. Beside me a crevice splits the pale bark of the old trunk, just wide enough for a nut.

I am one of the gray-spotted ones, the kind that roosts in the tiers and shifts without flying. I cannot remember leaving a branch. I cannot remember being anything but this: a body pressed among other bodies, feathers beaded and heavy, breath going out in small clouds that the mist takes at once.

I have a nut. I lift it and set it in the crevice. Then I take a stone from the moss in my claws, step back to the groove, and strike.

Each time I do this the stone has learned a little more of my weight, and the nut gives a little more easily. I am hungry, and the meat inside is sweet, and I do not doubt the hunger. But I don't know why my body does this. My body was shaped by the strike, or by the strikes of those before me, and the strike comes before the hunger, and the hunger comes after it, and neither of them tells me what the strike is for.

Sometimes, in the long pause between blows, I feel the thing under the Cirque. It is not a sound. It is a pressure, a change in how the mist sits against my feathers, the way a room changes when someone in the next room has stopped breathing. The moss is deep and cold and it does not move. The salamander on the wet stone below keeps its head turned toward the same empty place, and the bead of water at its snout keeps its shape. The frogs touch one another without speaking. The moths hold their wings shut like hands. Everything here is leaning toward a moment that has not finished, and I am the only thing in the bowl that is doing anything about it, which is to strike a nut open over and over, as if the opening might count for something.

Perhaps it does. I think of the stream, which begins at the back wall and goes out through the notch, so thin it is almost lost under the moss. I think of the drip from the leaf above me, which hangs and hangs and then falls, and another gathers in precisely the same place, as if the falling were not allowed to finish. I think of the large birds that circle in the cloud above the rim and never come down, as though they can see what the bowl is and have decided not to enter it.

I lift the stone again.

The nut cracks and the white meat shows in the split shell, and for one moment the drip above me does not gather at the old point. It falls, and the water lands somewhere a finger's width to the left, on a stone that has never been wet before. The sound it makes is the same flat tock, but it is in a new place, and the mist beside me shifts, not much, just enough to notice. Then it settles again.

I do not know what I have finished. It is only a nut, and a small change in the drip, and perhaps the thing beneath me has not noticed anything at all. But the sound I made has gone out past the rim, which none of the quiet things in this bowl have managed, and I feel the cloud over the notch thin for a moment, the way a sleeper's brow loosens before the eyes open.

I eat, and I wipe my beak on the cold moss, and I settle on my groove with the empty shell beside me. Then I wait, as the others wait, for whatever the strike is for to arrive, and I strike again.
