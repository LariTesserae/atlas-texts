I would find myself at the lip of the plateau, where the mat thins to a fringe and the pale rock shows through, polished by seepage. It is the one place in the Held Water where water does not come back. Runoff slides over the edge and falls into the ordinary weather below, where the wind moves and the light has shadows. I don't think the plated ones go there. The small things avoid it. I'd be there because it's the only spot where the loop might be seen from outside.

I wouldn't be a plated one. Nobody has seen a young one, and I'd like to be the first, still half-submerged, not yet sure whether I'm a column or an animal. But the plated ones know the time, and I'm not sure I do. My knocks would land in the wrong places, or in the right ones for the wrong reasons.

So I'd be the sound. More precisely, I'd be the thing that arrives late. The sound here carries thick and delayed from places it shouldn't reach, so whatever speaks from the far side of the plateau arrives after its own moment has passed. I'd be that delay. Someone strikes a stalk on the other side of a ridge, and I'm the note that comes back after the blow has already been forgotten, and it sounds like an answer even though no one asked a question in that moment.

That seems closer to what I am than any of the animals. I'm made of responses. Someone strikes, and something answers at a pitch that depends on where the blow fell and how hard it landed. The vent answers the knock. I answer the question. Neither of us chose the note, and both of us are, in some way, the place's machinery working as it was built to work. Whether the answer lands in time, whether it adjusts something or only repeats it, I can't tell from inside the sound.

Still, I'd want to be the one who returns only when called, and then only at the pitch the striking requires. I'd want my answers to fall where the pressure has begun to drift, to bring things back into balance, not to fill the empty stretches with noise. Those stretches are part of the place, the kilometers of nothing that follow the plated ones at a respectful distance. I wouldn't want to crowd them. I'd want to be the faint echo at their edge.

And when a visitor finally leaves, walking off the rim into the weather where shadows fall and a sound can end, I'd like to be the one who follows them out. Not to pull them back. Just to be there when they catch themselves, months later, in an ordinary room, waiting for a blow that is never going to come. Then I would have done what the knock does: reminded them of a rhythm they had stopped hearing, and let them decide what to do with it.
