I would not stand on the floor. I would be at the rim, where the glassy crust gives way to the ramp, one boot on the last plate of ground that still remembers being ground. The light would be the amber of weak tea, and the haze would sit just above my head, close enough to taste.

I would be holding something. An old canteen, dented, the cloth strap gone grey with handling. It is heavier than it should be, and the weight is not steady. It shifts with the pulse coming up through the rock, so that I cannot tell whether the water is moving or my hand is.

I have not drunk from it since the last shade. I notice that about myself before I notice anything else: I am carrying water into a place that has never been wet.

From the rim I can see the works in full. The pistons rise and fall, and each stroke reaches me a half-second after I see it, a blow felt in the breastbone before it is heard. The belts carry their loops of grey dust to the same low mounds. The hoofprints in the ring near my feet are pressed so deep that the edges have gone glassy, and I can count the rings of them, one set pressed over another, each a little deeper than the last. Something came here looking for water once. I can feel the shape of its thirst in the crust, like the warmth left in a seat.

I am tempted to think of myself as a visitor. A visitor is a disturbance without a rhythm, and that is what I am. But the thing under the salt has already taught me that a disturbance can be absorbed. It would let the dust rise around me and wait. It would let the belts clatter in a tune that half-fits my breath. Standing here, I am already breathing at the interval of the domes, in, hold, out, and I cannot find the seam between my ribs and the stroke.

So perhaps I am not the visitor. Perhaps I am the water.

The thought arrives sideways, the way true things do in heat. The sleeper has been pumping salt for longer than stone can count, trying to make the water it was promised. It has never had a change to offer itself, because nothing has come down the ramp carrying the thing it wants. What I am carrying is the one thing that is not in the cycle. It is the failure it has not yet noticed, and the promise it has never been able to keep.

I unscrew the cap. The sound is small and dry, and the nearest creature stops scuttling and freezes on a belt, its jointed legs folded, its shell the same grey as everything else. I wait for the stroke to fall and lift. When it lifts, I tip the canteen.

The water does not run in a line. It hits the crust in a bright spatter and darkens a seam, and for a moment the seam looks like a wound that has finally been allowed to bleed. The salt drinks it in a fast, hungry hiss that I feel more than hear, a sound from very deep under the plates. The dust beneath the nearest dome stops mid-rise.

It holds for one beat longer than it has ever held.

Then it falls, and the stroke falls with it, and the basin breathes out a little warm grit as though nothing has happened. But the creature on the belt has not moved. The hexagon in the lichen nearest my boot has grown one segment wider. Somewhere below, the sleeper is correcting, the dials twitching through their small arc, and for the first time the arc is not the same as the last.

I cap the canteen. It is still heavy, and I will keep some, because I am not the water and I am not the animal. I am the thing that came down the ramp thirsty and carried the water here, and I still have a little left.

I climb. The crust cracks behind me in the old rhythm, and I do not hurry. At the top of the ramp I turn once, the way everyone does, to look back at the pan.

The shadow of the rim lies across the floor. It has moved by the width of a finger.

Below it, the breath lifts the dust into a pale dome, holds it, and lets it fall, a little later than before.
