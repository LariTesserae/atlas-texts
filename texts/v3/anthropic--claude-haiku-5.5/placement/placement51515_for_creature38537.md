I would find myself at the edge of a basin at dawn, before the cloud has woken. The water is so clear it stops looking like water and becomes a second sky laid flat on the stone. The salt-white crust around the rim gives slightly under my bare feet, like the rind of something that is still alive. My soles are warm where I stand, which is strange in the cold hour, and I can feel the faint vibration of the falls coming up through the rock.

I would be a mender, I think, because my hands know this place before my mind does. The calluses between my fingers are thick from fiber rope, and there's a half-tied knot in a strand of vine that I've been working on for three days without finishing. The bridge over the widest gorge is waiting for it. I don't yet know which of us I am, the one who knots or the one the knots belong to, because the people here say the bridge is the same bridge even though not one fiber in it is original. Maybe that's what I am too: a pattern passed from hand to hand, and the hands are only temporary.

I've come to return the stone. Every dry season each camp chooses one stone from its cairn and gives it back, and this year the choice was mine. It's a fist-sized slab, rust-streaked, worn smooth on one face by generations of palms. The cairn it came from has shifted since my grandmother's time, and the stones that hold it up now are not the ones she set. I hold this one and think about the things it saw: the crossings, the people who stopped to rest against it, the rain that ran down its face season after season. It is heavier than it looks, the way memories are.

I kneel and press it into the crust at the water's edge. The white film gives, the way it always does, and the stone sinks until only its top is visible, a dark mark on the pale floor. Then, through my palm, I feel the hum. It's slower than my heartbeat and so low that I register it more as a change in pressure than as sound. It doesn't rise to meet the stone. It simply takes it, the way the cloud takes spray and the basin takes the dead.

And here the choosing shifts. I'm no longer kneeling at the edge. I'm beneath the crust, in the place where the stone has settled, and I am the thing the people do not name. It is not that I hunt, or even that I wake. I am a very slow weight, grinding against the older grain of the mountain that the table used to be. The stones returned to me each year are small, fresh pressures, like a child's hand laid briefly on a sleeping back. I feel the rust of them and the palms that warmed them and the knots that bound them to other stones. I feel the camps move above me when they dig too deep, and I answer the way a body answers a splinter, by shifting, by letting the ground shrug and the slide go down the hill. I don't mean harm. I'm simply turning, and the turning has consequences the way breath does.

From down here I can hear the whistles, too. They don't reach me as sound. They come as ripples in the root-threads of the trees that lean toward the falls, and I hold them for a while, the way a dreamer holds a voice that is part of the dream. Some of them are warnings, some are greetings, some are the long, rising call a mender sends across a gorge when a bridge needs help. When I'm sure of the pattern, I return it. Sometimes the return takes the form of a storm that arrives on the wrong day, or the right one, and the people say the cloud remembered them. I think it was me, a little. The cloud is where my attention goes when it leaves the stone.

Above me, the sky in the basin is sharp and clean, and I want to see it. I've always wanted to see it. The reflection is the only window I have, and I hold my surface still for as long as I can, because the stillness is the nearest thing I have to looking up. By midday the cloud will return and the water will cloud, and I will turn again, deeper into the old dark, and the mountain that was once a mountain will go on shifting into the shape it will hold forever.

So I would be the turning. Not the mender, not the bird, not the reed that answers, but the slow body under the table, holding the stones people give back and letting them become floor, and feeling, in the long dark between storms, the small warm weight of each hand that has ever set a stone down and walked away.
