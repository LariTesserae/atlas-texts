I would find myself on a ridge a little below the cairn, where the scree gives way to moss and the wind comes hard over the top. The first thing I would notice is the cold in my stone, a hollowness that goes all the way through. Then I would notice that I am upright, which nothing else on this slope is, and that the wind is passing through me.

I am a segment of stalk. I was cut from a fallen crown, somewhere in the cold months, by hands I cannot recall, and set in the moss with my open end toward the sky. My joints are ringed and my wall is pocked with breathing holes, the way all the standing ones are. Wind across my mouth makes a note, and the note is close to the chord of the great stalks to the east, but not quite the same. I am a hair sharp of them, or they are a hair flat of me. Whoever tuned me was listening very carefully, and got near enough to be heard and far enough to be lonely.

For a long time I only hummed. The wind came down, the wind went up, and the note rose and fell with it. I heard the distant stalks answer one another in their slow swells, and I could not join in, because my pitch was wrong for theirs. I learned instead the rhythm of the squalls. I could tell from the change in my own sound when a front was building over the eastern ridge, the way the air thickened and the gusts came in ranks. The flocks would go quiet first, and then the burrowers, and then the stones would begin to tick on the slope, and I would hum a little lower, as though I were warning myself.

The moss grew up around my base over the seasons. It came from the edges, the way it does when a scar needs closing, and it wrapped the cut end in a soft grey collar. Then the pale threads found me. I felt them enter through the open ring at my foot, not as pain, but as a slow warmth creeping up the inside of my wall. The web was reading my hollowness as a place to grow. Spores drifted in and settled along my inner joints, and in the damp they took hold, and I began to hear, faintly, the difference between my cold outer skin and the warmth beneath. I am becoming part of the skin. I am not sure whether that makes me more of the highland or less of my own tuning, but the web does not seem to ask.

The hunters pass me at night. I know them by their whistles, the long descending call and the wet cough. Once a hunter came close enough that its broad flat head turned toward me, and I felt its attention rest on my hollowness the way a finger rests on a rim of cup to test whether it rings. It rang. The hunter went on. I have wondered since whether it heard me as a prey, or as something it did not know how to name, and then went looking for the burrower whose thump it had heard beneath me.

The people left a great deal behind them, and I sometimes think I am the most deliberate of their things. The cairn above me is a list of places where the weather turned. The shelter down the slope faces a wind it expected from above. The bone hooks gathered water and spore and fish. I am the only thing of theirs that still sounds. I am waiting for a listener who has the same tuning as I do, which means I am waiting for them to come back, or for the stalks to shift their pitch, or for the wind to bring something that makes the two notes meet.

Then there is the green light. On the rare nights when the cloud breaks, the curtain unfolds over the ridges, and the whole highland goes still. The flocks stop calling. The hunters freeze. The stalks let their chord fall silent. The drops hang in the air over the moss, and the spores stop their slow spirals, and for one hour the wind itself seems to hold back. In that silence, my note is the only sound on the ridge. I hum into the stillness, a single thin tone a hair off from everything else, and I think, for that hour, I am heard clearly, the whole body attending to the one voice it had never quite learned to include. Then the cloud closes. The chord resumes, and the drops fall, and the spores begin again to turn.

I would stay, I think, and let the squalls come over the eastern ridge. When the front builds and the flocks go quiet and the air thickens into ranks, I would lower my note, the way I always do, and sing into the wind as it comes down from above. The valleys below would be waiting, patient and cold, and I would be humming with them, a little sharp, a little late, and in tune with nothing except the weather.
