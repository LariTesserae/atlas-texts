I would find myself in the mud at the bottom of the dry reservoir, under the tea-colored lake, and I would be a grain of sediment.

I was once part of a wall. Somewhere on the third terrace, a retaining wall held a painted figure, a woman or a bird or a sheaf of wheat, I can no longer tell which, and her colors had faded to the grey of old plaster. Then a root-cord pressed into the joint beside me, and the joint opened, and I came loose. A storm carried me down the steps in a sheet of brown water, past the pump houses, past the bell-towers, until the basin caught me. Now I lie with a few thousand of my kin in a soft drift, and the water above us is so still that the sky lies on it like a second floor.

I cannot see the sky. I know it is there the way you know a room has a ceiling. Through the water, the light comes down flat and even, the same at every hour, and I feel it only as a faint change in the warmth of the silt. The tea-colored water stays unmoved because nothing here wants to disturb it. Above us, the pumps knock, and the knocks come down through the water as a long, slow pressure, and I move a little with each one. That is my whole sense of time. A knock. A settling. A knock.

Sometimes the rain returns and the basin overtops, and then I am lifted, briefly, into the current, turning over and over in clear, fast water. For a moment I can taste the iron of the foundry runoff and the sweetness of the blue leaves dissolving on the upper tiers. Then the current drops, and I sink back into the drift, a little farther from the wall I came from. Each rain takes a little more of the terraces downhill. Each rain adds me to the floor of the basin. Over years the floor rises, and the plain I was always walking toward comes a little closer.

I do not think I was ever a whole thing. I am a fragment of a fragment. But the painted figure is still in me, in the faint pigment that clings to one side of my surface, and the lime from the old plaster, and the particular ghost of a shape that the water has not yet worn away. Some of my kin carry a bit of the brass fittings, green with verdigris. Some carry a glint of glass. When a root-cord eventually reaches down here, as they do, slowly, testing the silt with pale fingers, it will take us up one grain at a time, and turn us into the bark of a new trunk, and our pigment will rise in the sap at dawn and flow out into the blue leaves.

So I am not sure, in the end, whether I would be the sediment or the memory. Perhaps the Tenders would know. I imagine one of them coming down the steps some grey afternoon, kneeling at the edge of the basin, pressing a palm to the cold water. She would not go in. She would wait until the water shivered, and then she would listen, and I think she would hear the knocks coming down through the silt, and feel the faint warmth of my kin, and understand that the wall was still remembering the figure it once held.
