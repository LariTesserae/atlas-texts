**Where I Would Be**

I would be standing at the rim of the resin basin, one hand resting on its worn edge, because that is where the dust is least disturbed. My feet would be in a ridge of settled grit, and I would have come down through the fracture, not from above. I would know the break was new when I arrived, and I would know it was old by the time I stopped being able to tell the difference.

I would be one of the careful few. I would not be a grazer, though I would envy them their tracks. I would be the person who carried the tools, and the one who understood, too late or perhaps exactly on time, that the roof had to be closed from inside.

I think I would have been the last to climb out of the split and the first to decide not to. I would have wedged a flat stone across the gap, the way one of the cairns is capped, and then sat down in the warm air to see whether the stone would hold. It did. The sealing took longer than a season, though there are no seasons here to measure it. The shelf of mineral grew its raw edges back into one another, and the dust began to lie in ridges on the stone where I had sat.

Now I am listening to the hum. It rises from the fissure, a call, a pause, and an answer from a slightly different direction. I lift my head before I decide to. The grazers lift theirs too, a fraction. The leaves click and I no longer hear them as noise. They are counting.

**Who I Would Be**

I would be the keeper of the marks. Not the first keeper, since the grooves were cut by hands I never met, but the one whose hand is steadiest on the scratching tool now. I would make the strokes in groups of seven, then the longer mark, then seven again, and I would not know whether I was counting the light, the hum, or the returns of the chamber to itself. The count would be the only thing I could do that was mine.

I would not try to leave. I tried once, in the first light, and found that the tracks in the dust curved back toward the hanging reed-bundle no matter which way I walked. The grooves at arm height would have seemed to lead up, but when I reached for them my fingers found only the smooth wear of hands before mine, and I understood that the grooves were not for climbing out. They were for holding on while the place decided what to do with you.

So I would hold on. I would eat from the tendrils when I was hungry, though the resin would taste faintly of the basin's residue, sweet and dry. I would sleep in the warm air. My breathing would slow until it matched the amber pulse, and the pulse would stop feeling like light and start feeling like the rise and fall of my own chest. I would not notice the day when the glow and my breath became one rhythm, because there is no day here to notice.

I would be a grazer's companion in the loosest sense, a second body on the same loop, passing the same columns and the same leaning crowns. The fringed columns would lean toward the vent, and so, after enough seasons that are not seasons, would I.

**What the Chamber Would Want From Me**

Nothing, and that is the hardest part. The Hollow does not ask me to speak. The hum does not ask for an answer, though the answer is already in me when it rises. The tendrils lift what I leave behind and draw it into the core, and I would give up whatever I had without bargaining.

But the tally would need someone to keep it. The cairns that have fallen would need someone to rebuild them, or not. The residue in the basin would need someone to decide whether it was an offering or a store, and then to let that question go, which is what the chamber itself has done.

The broken roof would still be the one thing that is different. It would look at me every time the amber light swelled, a sharp clean line of mineral against the warm dark. It is the only evidence that someone came in from outside and closed the door behind them. I think I would find that I did not mind. I would put my palm against the fracture and feel the faint heat coming up through the stone, and I would say, not loudly, because nothing here carries speech well, that I was still here. The hum would rise, and I would lift my head to listen, and the answer would come back from a slightly different direction, which is how I would know I had been heard.
