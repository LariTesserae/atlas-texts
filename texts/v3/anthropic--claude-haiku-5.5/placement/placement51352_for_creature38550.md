I would be a burrower, and I would live in the soft ground at the edge of a tarn on the eastern side of the shelf, where the iron seep comes down through the cracks and pools rust-colored over the stone.

I notice the place before I notice myself. The fog comes up from the lower basins in the early hours and sits on everything, beading on my fur until it is heavy and cold. I can feel the water moving under the crust, a slow tug toward the gully below, and I know that the ground I have dug is not holding. In the thaw, my tunnel will fold in on itself, and I will come up through the slump into a different place. I think I would like that more than I expect to.

My mound is the first sign of me. It is pale grit pushed up around the mouth of the hole, and it looks like everyone else's, which is the point. From above, the mounds on the tarn's edge are a scatter of small, uneven hills, and no one can tell which one is occupied. Inside, the tunnel bends twice before it opens into the chamber. The walls are damp and gritty, and I work them with my forepaws, pressing the loose stone into the sides so it will hold a little longer. Sometimes it does, and sometimes a shoulder of the shelf sags and the ceiling comes down in a cold rush of silt. I dig out what I can and begin again, because the roots are where they were.

The roots are the reason I am here. The grass in the gullies grows pale at the tips and dark at the roots, and it is only in the cracks where the stone has begun to break down that the roots take hold. My tunnels run toward them like the spokes of a wheel. I can feel where they are the way I feel the cold, without needing to think about it. I eat slowly. Sometimes I eat the same root-tuft for a whole season, and when it dies back I move my tunnel toward the next one, and the old run fills with grit and falls in on itself. I am not building anything on purpose. But each time I bring up the grit and loosen the soil, the ground changes shape a little, and the water finds a slightly different path.

I would not be the master of this place. I would be one of the things that live here in a provisional way, and I would be aware of that. The pale predators move along the gully edges, and I would feel them in the ground before I saw them, the faint pressure of a body crossing the stone above my chamber. When the birds have been feeding well and the insects are thick in the tarns, the predators go to the ledges, and the burrowers are safer. When the burrowers multiply, the predators come toward the tunnels, and then we are the ones who must go quiet and still. I would learn to read the wave the way the birds read the fog. I would also learn that there is no real safety in it, only a short stretch of ground where the wave has not yet turned.

The distant thudding comes through the rock sometimes, and I would feel it in my chest. I do not know what makes it. It comes from the basin below, from the smoke that bends slowly against the gray, and from the grid humming somewhere under the crust. It does not change the root I am chewing or the water I am drinking. But it reminds me that the ground I live on is being pulled and pressed from far away, by forces I will never see. I think I would be content not to understand them. The fog is enough to be going on with.

What I like best about being a burrower here is that nothing I make lasts. The mound slumps, the tunnel folds, the grit moves downslope into the gully and becomes part of the stone. In a few seasons the lichen will cover it, and the moss will settle in its hollows, and the grass will push its roots down where I once dug. I would not leave a mark, but the ground I turned would remember the turning. The plateau is still arguing with itself about its shape, and every time I pull up a mouthful of grit, I take a small part in that argument.

When the thaw comes, I would wake to the sound of water moving under the chamber, and I would know my tunnel was failing. I would crawl out through the slump, shake the wet silt from my fur, and look for the next root-tuft and the next place where the ground is soft enough to dig. The fog would be thick. The birds would be shouting from the cliffs, and somewhere below, the thudding would start again. I would start digging.
