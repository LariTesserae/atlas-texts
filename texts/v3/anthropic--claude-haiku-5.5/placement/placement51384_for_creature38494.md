# Warm for an Hour

I come loose at the rim, a little after a pump stroke, when the ground has been trembling all morning. Nobody sees it. A ledge that has held for a hundred days lets go, and I drop with a few others through the pale clay, past the gravel, and into the dark band where the old road was laid. The fall takes my breath and gives it back as heat. I did not know a stone could be warm inside.

I land on scree above the flooded blocks, skip once, and stop. From here the whole bowl is open to me. The pumps are slate-colored beetles along the eastern wall, working slowly. The copper ferns and the jointed stalks with their bloom-discs open and shut. The cascade hangs off the broken arch like a sheet being shaken out. The far walls have dissolved into mist, and the near ones run with water that has not yet decided where to go.

I know what I am. I was part of the road. For a few decades people drove over the glassy layer, and then the ground under it gave way, and now I am a piece of that surface, a fleck of something that once carried weight from one place to another. I know roads. I was the thing they were made of.

For the first hour, I am only warm.

Something touches my underside within a few minutes of landing. It is very fine, finer than a hair, and it moves over my fracture lines slowly, the way a thumb reads the grain of wood. I feel the tapping of a hundred tiny contacts. They are not trying to break me. They are trying to learn me. I am a particular arrangement of cleavages, and the Reach is reading the arrangement the way the water read the shelves of cups and shoes in the flooded rooms, the way the filament reads the tilt of the sky. The read is not cold. It is the warmth itself, coming out of me in exchange for what I am learning.

I wonder if it does this to everything that falls. I suspect it does. I suspect the walls themselves were read this way, long before they fell, and that the fall was only the moment the reading turned into rewriting. The clay, the gravel, the dark glass: each layer was a sentence, and the sentences are being edited now, one fleck at a time.

Then the cascade begins to separate.

I have never listened to the water. I am a stone, and stones do not listen. But I am warm, and warmth makes a thing attentive, and the sound comes apart into threads. One thread says a street name over and over, a word with a soft middle, *Harrow*, or *Harlow*, I can't tell. Another thread counts something, and then loses count, and starts again at one. A third says, very clearly and very tiredly, *is someone coming*, and then it is swallowed by the others. I cannot tell whether these are voices or only the water's habit of sounding like voices. I suspect the people who stayed too long in the mist could not tell either, and that is why they stopped describing it.

I am not afraid of them. I have been on a road, and roads are full of people saying things they need someone to hear.

Here is what I would be, if I were asked. I would be exactly this: a fallen piece of wall that was once a road, lying on the slope between the old world and the new, not yet taken, not yet a root's memory, still warm enough to think about where I came from. It is a strange and specific place to be. The Reach has no clean edge between what it has absorbed and what it has not. I am in the middle of the sentence, and the sentence is being written through me.

Near the water's edge, a few meters down, someone has left food. I can smell it, a faint sourness of bread gone damp, carried up on the mist. The mud around it is soft and marked with prints that have not yet grown over. A person came here last night from the eastern wall, timing their steps to the pumps, and set the offering at the edge of a causeway so the thing in the mud could take it if it wanted. I do not know whether the Reach eats. I do not know whether it sees. But the person did not know either, and they came anyway, and I find that I am moved by it, in whatever way a stone can be moved.

The warmth begins to leave me around the middle of the hour. It goes slowly, the way a fire goes down in a room where no one is adding wood. The fine touches thicken. One of them wraps around my lower edge, and I feel the first pressure of a root that has decided I am worth carrying.

Then the pump cycle turns, and the water rises a few centimeters across the lower district, and I am covered.

I do not know what happens next. Maybe I will be drawn down into the soil and the catalogue, my shape folded into the growth, a single fleck of old road recorded among the cups and shoes and the faces that may once have been people. Maybe I will be set on the causeway's edge, cool at last, a stone in a row of stones the Reach is arranging into a wall or a path or a word it has not yet finished learning.

In the morning, I think, there will be a footprint in the mud near the bread. It will be almost human, the heel too round and the toes too close together, and it will face toward the rim. Beside it, perhaps, there will be something small and grey and cool to the touch, lying exactly where a stone would be if someone had placed it there on purpose.

I would like that. I think I would like to be the thing that was placed.
