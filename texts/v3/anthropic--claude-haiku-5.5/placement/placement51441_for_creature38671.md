I would be the shell on the kiln's shelf, a freshwater mussel's valve, pale and curled, about the width of a palm. I'd sit at the crest, where the smoke comes down low and sweet over the roofs, and the heat from below goes into me and stays there. I don't know why it stays. Nobody does. The woman who tends the fire says it is simply the warmest place in Seep-Hollow, and that a thing should be kept warm if someone thought to keep it.

I remember the lake only as a pressure. Before the fault opened, the water stood so high that the ridge was a rumor, and the silt I lay in was soft and cold and perfectly still. There was no swell then, no breath in the ground. The water simply held everything, the way a hand holds a stone. Then one night the hand opened. I was pulled down into the floor of the world as the lake went out through the crack, and I lay in the dark for generations while the silt settled and the bedrock wept and the seeps found their paths. I did not die so much as stop expecting anything.

Then someone's spade turned me up. I remember the light, which was not light at all but a grey diffusion, like being seen through milk. The finder held me in both hands, and I felt the warmth of skin, which I had not felt since the water. The finder wrapped me in cloth. That was the first kindness I had known in centuries, and I did not know what to do with it, so I held very still, which is what I am good at.

Now I am on the shelf. The ground under the town still rises and falls, a finger's width each day, and I feel it even here, though I am far from any seep. It is the lake breathing. The water has not left; it has only gone underneath, and it comes up to the roots of the willows and into the pans and back down. My own body was made to close against slow currents, to open when the water came in and carry the faint sweetness of it through my gills. Those motions are still in me, the shape of a rhythm I can no longer answer. Sometimes when the heat is high I feel my hinge loosen, and for a moment I believe I might open, and take in something from the air, and I know that is only the kiln talking.

Through the crooked passages below me, people are setting their thresholds. I can hear the mallets, and the low murmur of close heads, and the soft wet sound of stone sinking into silt. A child lifts a loose board somewhere and counts the old sills underneath. I would like to tell her that she is counting the rises of a town that is still deciding what it is, and that the lake is still breathing under all of it, and that I am here on the warm shelf, the last small proof that water once held something and then let it go.

At dusk the fog comes down and the roofs disappear into it, and the dripping of the eaves fills the quiet. Someone climbs the crest with a new threshold on their shoulder and passes beneath me, and their palm presses flat against the frame of the kiln, feeling for the tremor under the stone. I feel it too. The heat stays in me. The ground keeps rising, a hand higher at a time, and I wait with it, open a little at the hinge, listening for water.
