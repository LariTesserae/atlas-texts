I would find myself on the tor nearest the leaning cairn, in the thin hour after the drizzle has peaked and before it has let go. The rock would still be running with water, and the grey light would come from no particular direction.

I would be one of the small grey birds with the pale wingbars. Not because birds are the most interesting thing on the Shelf, but because they seem to be the only things that have learned how to be here. They sit motionless on cold stone and watch the basin without wanting anything from it. When the mist thickens they lift and drift out over the valleys, and they come back with nothing, and nobody scolds them for it. Their song is one short phrase, and I would sing it because it is the only phrase I have, and because the repeating is the point.

But a bird is the thing's attention made small. So if I were the bird, I would also be the watching. I would feel it looking through me toward the middle of the basin, the way a dreamer looks through a sleeper's eyes. My body would be a pinhole in its long regard. I think that would be a comfort, the way the stone is comforted by the weight of its own hollow. I would not have to decide what to do with my days. The thing would hold them for me, and the drizzle would come and go on its schedule, and I would not be asked to explain myself.

I would also keep an eye on the travelers. One would come up the stair, count her paces and get a different number than she expected, and set a pebble in a hollow. I would watch her leave. Next season, if she came back, the pebble would have drifted a finger closer to the center, and I would understand that she had given something without knowing it, and that the thing had taken it gently and kept it. I would not know whether to be sad that she would never see me or glad that she had been counted.

The birds do not leave the Shelf. They go out over the valleys and come back to the same tors and sit in the same places. That is a kind of staying, and I think I would be content with it. I would hold to the one phrase and wait for the drizzle to thicken, and fly out and return empty-clawed, and perch again on the cold stone above the basin.

Then I would sing. Under the roar of the unseen river, the thing would repeat the phrase back to me, a little lower, a little slower, and for a moment it would be unclear which of us had started it, or whether either of us ever had.
