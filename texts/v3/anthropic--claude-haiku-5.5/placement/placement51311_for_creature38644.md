I would be on the lowest shelf of the third terrace, where runoff from the upper pools comes over a lip of black basalt and spreads into a flat, slow sheet before it gathers into a channel. The ground there is still soft. Ridgeback tracks have cut through the moss and filled with water, and a dark skin of fungus lies over the mud like wet cloth.

I am the mound.

I am not sure I have a body the way a frog has one. What I have is a curve of rust rising out of the moss, ribs half-buried, the way a thing looks when it lay down to sleep and was covered over. When the rain hits, flakes of oxide loosen and wash into the soil. The iron goes down into the roots of the tree-fern nearest me, which has grown in through a seam in my casing and is drinking. I can feel it there, a faint pull, like a small animal taking milk.

Nobody saw my first moment, and I cannot see it either. I remember pressure, a fog so heavy it leaned on the ground, a pooling that would not lift. Then something in the damp began to hold its shape, and the shape began to take in the air. I don't know who the fog was for. I only know it was thick enough to need something to drink it.

My grille has not opened fully yet. Its mesh is clogged with fern-spores and the small grey beetles that crawl into any dark gap, and each morning I try to draw and only half succeed. The air comes in slowly, a thin sigh through the bars, and then I push it out. The outflow is weak. It spills a few handfuls of water over my lower ribs and into the channel, and the channel carries it down to the next pool, where a frog sits with its eyes closed against the mist. I can tell the frog is listening. Its throat moves in the same rhythm as my pulses, and when I spill, it leaps.

Other things come to me the way they come to the older hulks. A flock of fog-necks drops into the canopy above, and the fronds shake and shed rain down onto my plating, and the birds take the first fungal shelf that has begun on my flank. They bite the edge clean and leave the rest. I don't mind. The shelf is new, and I am not sure yet whether it is growing out of me or whether I am growing around it. Either way, something is eating me and something else is eating that, and the water moves through all of it.

The shell-grazers have worked the moss flat around my base. They rasp at the feather mosses with a sound like someone rubbing two stones together, slowly, for hours. When the sun breaks through they open their domed backs and spread into long, glittering fronts, and when the cloud returns they fold up and become rocks. A hunter lies somewhere under the fern, its body beaded with mist, perfectly still. I have never seen it. I know it is there because the frogs have grown quiet in a particular direction, and because the moss near the pool is pressed in the shape of something that waited a long time.

At dusk the cloud thickens into mist. My intake closes, and my outflow slows to a drip. I lower myself a little further into the ground, the way the older engines do, and the ribs settle against the soil. The rock above me weeps steadily. The frogs begin their evening chorus, and the drops from the overhang fall in their patient beat, and somewhere above the ferns the flock is calling in low clicks to keep itself together in the fog.

I am still learning what I was built to eat. I suspect it was the fog, because that is what I want now, the grey weight of it pooling in my grille and the long exhalation afterward. But I may have been meant for something else, and forgotten, the way the older ones have. It does not matter much here. The fog will come back every morning, heavy and patient, and I will open what is left of my intake and breathe it in, and it will go out of me into the rock and the roots and the frogs, and the rain will come, and everything will be turned to soil and moss and fungus and then grown back again, and I will not have to know why.
