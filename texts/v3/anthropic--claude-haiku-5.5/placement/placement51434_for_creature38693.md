# Upright on the Boulder

I find myself on the lip of the scree, where the last terrace gives out and the open shelf begins. It is the hour before the gulls wake. The fog is so close that the boulder under me is the only solid thing I can see, and even it has soft edges, as if I'm remembering it rather than looking at it. The granite is wet and cold through my feet. My feet are always cold. What I mind is the wait for light, which has no interest in my schedule.

I am small and round, grey-brown like the scree after rain. My throat is the size of a thumbnail, and I keep my real voice for emergencies. Most of my days are spent underground in a burrow with four exits, all of them dug by my mother's mother's mother. I come up to see. That is the whole of my work: to come up and look.

Below me the terraces are only shapes. A bell of stone begins to ring, one short knock, then a pause, then a longer one. Someone is checking a wedge. Two levels up, a woman calls a count in her low carrying voice, and the number arrives at me without a face attached, the way everything here arrives. I cannot read the call patterns. I can hear that they are patterns, that the rising hail means *come*, and that the falling three means something is sliding. I think about this sometimes: the people speak to each other across distance, and I speak to the boulders, and neither of us is certain the other is listening.

I watch them anyway. I have watched them my whole life, and I have opinions. The people in the upper terraces are careless with their doorways. The quarry-cutters sing badly, and their songs go on for so long that I have started to sing along, quietly, under my breath, just to get to the end. The tarn-keeper who walks the eastern channels with her stone maps never once looks at the ground she is crossing. She looks at the sky, as if the water will tell her something. It does not. I have seen her miss a boulder that moved in the night and step onto a new pool with both feet, and she laughs about it afterward, and so do I, in my small way.

The house at the edge of the settlement is mine now. I took it three springs ago, when its last tenant moved uphill to a dry room near the stair. It has four walls, a roof that leaks in the north corner, and a threshold that tilted toward the valley long before I arrived, so the rain runs in along the sill and pools in the far room. I like the pooling. I fill the back room with dry tussock and the fine grey down the herds leave on the rocks in winter, and I bring in what bones I find. Some are mine, some are the herds', one or two are very old and very small and I do not ask whose they were. The house is becoming a nest, and I am becoming the kind of creature who lives in a nest. The walls will go into the scree eventually. The next crew will harvest the stone, and somebody will shim the blocks into a new terrace, and somewhere under it a wedge of slate will be pressed flat by the weight of a future doorway.

Light arrives the way it always does, first as a pale smear, then as a thinning of the fog, and then the terraces begin to show. Gold lies along the upper walls. The lichens on the eastern stones catch it and turn their bruised yellow-green into something almost bright. A seabird lifts off a parapet, and then another, and the colony wakes in a single noisy shout.

A woman comes down the stepped street with a bubble-tube and a tally-stone under one arm. She stops at my house. She kneels and lays the bubble-tube along the sill, and I watch her read it, her lips moving slightly, her thick fingers pressing lightly at the stone. She looks up. She sees me on my boulder, upright, watching her, and she does not startle. The people never startle at whistlers. We have an arrangement.

She writes something on the tally-stone and sets it back in its niche. Then she takes a flat grey stone from her pocket, one about the size of my palm, and sets it on the threshold, a little to the side where the rain won't reach it.

I do not understand what it is for. I suspect she does not either, or that she does and would not say. But I know the courtesy when I see it, the same way I know the seabirds' flat stones and the herders' gaps in the wall. She is marking the place where the ground is moving, and she is acknowledging that I am part of the reckoning.

I whistle once, short and low, my real voice, the one I save for emergencies. It is not an emergency. She nods, as if she heard it perfectly, and goes back up the street, calling her count to someone I cannot see.

I wait a while longer on the boulder. The cold comes up through the stone. Somewhere under the terraces the earth is lifting by a fraction, too slow for any of us to feel, and I sit here with my small cold feet and my careful eyes, and I keep watch on the slow rising, as the people do, as the birds do, as the lichens have always done.
