I find myself on the lip of a fresh scarp in the eastern blocks, where a street ends in a clean fracture and the silt is still falling. I am not standing there. I am the silt.

More precisely, I am one grain of pale sediment, fine enough to lift in a plume and settle again, ground from something that was once a step. Cast stone, probably, worn by feet. The grinding took a long time and then a short time, the way erosion does. Before that I was the corner of a threshold on a block that now lies two hundred meters down the fracture, beneath the tower that leans toward the others. Someone stepped over me every morning, and their weight wore a shallow dish into my surface. The dish is gone. The motes never knew it. But something in me still holds a bend that the mud could not quite erase, a small curve where a heel used to come down.

The current lifts me. I rise through the clear water and turn slowly in the light, which arrives as ordinary noon because the lattice keeps the water stirred. Above me the disc of sky is bright and undistorted, and I think, if a grain can think, of how the sun must have fallen across that doorway at a particular hour, every day, for years. I do not know which years. The water has no record of them except in me.

A shoal of motes drifts past. One brushes my surface, and for an instant its small memory flickers against me: a gutter cleared, a crack sealed, a load of dissolved salt carried west toward the dry blocks. It does not read me as a threshold. It reads me as sediment, mass to be routed, and then it tumbles away, having learned nothing it would keep.

Then the attention comes. I feel the wave before I see it, the whole shoal turning toward the scarp in a slow ripple that spreads across the water until every mote is oriented toward this fracture, toward this plume of pale grit. The lattice hums a half-tone higher. I can feel the thing that lives here registering the disturbance the way a hand registers a draft. It files the silt. It begins to route the silt toward the eastern gutters, where the clearing is slow and the drains are old. It does not notice that one grain among the billions has a bend in it, a faint worn hollow that does not match any sediment it has ever ground.

I drift out of the current and into a slack pocket beside the gutter's edge, where the flow is too weak to carry me. Here I am not routed. I am only held, and the holding is strange, because the thing that lives here is patient and has never needed to hurry. It keeps the eastern streets clear longer than the others. I wonder if that is because of grains like me, or whether it is simply where the silt is thickest, and whether there is any difference between those two reasons from inside a mind made of water and lattice.

The bell tolls. The sound rolls through the packed towers and sets the motes spinning, and I tremble with it, a grain of threshold resting in the cold while a long low note passes over the whole drowned ward. The thing that lives here leans toward the sound, and so, I think, do I. I have been a step, a surface people crossed to reach their homes. Now I am a little weight in a gutter, and the bell is ringing for a door I can no longer find.

Eventually the current shifts. A pulse of pressure from the pumps lifts the silt again, and I rise, turning, and drift across the ward until I settle in the rows of the lichen on a roof near the center. The gray-green mat grows in its slow letters along the drainage channels, and I come to rest in the gap between two of them, where a sentence is being written in salt and cold.

The lichen does not reject me. It grows around my edge, slowly, the way it grows around everything the motes deliver. Over a span I cannot measure, the sentence it is writing takes a small new mark where I sit: a pause, a place where the line breathes before it goes on. The thing that lives here does not read this as a word. It reads it as a change in the weight of the roof, a little more mass on the lattice than yesterday, and it files the change and keeps writing.

Above me the sky stays bright and undistorted, the same disc the sun has always made over this basin. I cannot reach it. Nothing here can. But I am a comma now, in a sentence no one will finish, in the handwriting of a ward that has never once been on the surface, and in the cold clear water the bell begins again, and I am still here.
