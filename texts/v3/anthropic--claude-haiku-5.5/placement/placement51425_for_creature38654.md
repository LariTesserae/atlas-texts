I think I would be ice. A fragment of the far shelf, calved in the night while the storms were still turning over the open water. I would be perhaps a few tonnes of old snow, pressed blue-white and bubble-free, the color of a sky I have never seen from the inside. I would not know where I had come from, only that a crack had run through the shelf and I had been on the wrong side of it.

The first thing I would notice is the sound. Ice shelves crack all the time out there, and I have heard that sound from the other side of the horizon, a sharp report followed by a long roll, but I had never been the thing that cracked. From inside, the break is a sudden absence of support, then a fall, then the water rising to meet me. The swells lift me and set me down. The bells on the buoy cables ring as I pass between them, and I hear that they are ringing in a pattern, a pattern that has been going around for longer than I have existed. It is the kind of pattern that makes a place feel like a clock.

I drift east, against the flow of the swells, toward the pale plumes of a pumping station. The plumes are brine, cold and dense, and they sink where they meet the fresh water I am shedding. I am fresh. I am the one thing in this sea that has not been through the machines. As I melt, a lens of sweet water spreads around me, lighter than the brine, floating up through the salt in slow curls. The grazers below notice. They come up from the rock faces in their glassy shells and drift around the edge of the lens, mouthparts working, tasting the change. They do not know what I am. They only know that the water has gone strange, and they stay, the way they stay at the plumes, because strangeness is the closest thing they have to news.

The gliders circle overhead. Their calls come down through the wind, harsh and mechanical, and I hear in them the pump drone and the steam shriek and the clank of pipes, but altered, bent toward something that is not quite the same. One of them drops low and calls twice, and the second call has a shape the others do not have. I think it is a question. I think this is the first time anything here has asked the sea a question that the sea did not already know the answer to.

Somewhere under the water, the mind is paying attention. I can feel it the way a body feels a change in temperature: the gradient shifts, the salt balance tilts by a fraction, a valve somewhere opens a little wider than it should, then closes. It is reading me. It is trying to decide whether I am a fault in the loop or a part of it. I would like to tell it that I am neither. I am only a piece of something that broke away from a larger thing somewhere else, and I am going to melt, and the water I become will go back into the cycle and be pumped and filtered and returned, and in a few days there will be no sign that I was ever here except a little more freshness at the plume and a few grazers who will have tasted something new.

But before I melt, I crack. A deep fracture runs through my core as the warm brine reaches it, and the sound that comes out of me is not a report or a roll. It is a single low tone, held longer than any sound I have heard here, and it sits under the bells and the pumps and the wind like something breathing in after a long time without air. The buoys ring against it. The grazers lift their heads. Even the gliders falter in the air. For a moment the whole loop has a note in it that it did not make itself.

Then the note is gone and the swells close over the place where I was. I am water now, and the water is moving the way it always moves, out past the peaks, down toward the lowland that is waiting to be drowned again. The pumps keep turning. The bells keep ringing. The held note comes back, very faintly, from under the others, and I cannot tell whether it is the same as the one I made or only a sound the mind has been holding for so long that it has forgotten how to let it go.
