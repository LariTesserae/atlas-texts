I would find myself on the rim, at the hour before the mist lifts. The stone of the low wall is cold and wet under my hand, and the troughs below are catching the first drops. I would not come down the steps like a visitor. I would already be in the air, a small dark thing that has come up from the ridge on the cold current and is deciding where to land.

I would be one of the moths.

I would know, without anyone telling me, that I am the one who carries. The soil under the millet is dark because it was never here. It came up from some lower country, somewhere past the far edge of the bowl where the ground is still broken and grey, and it came in pieces too small to see. I was one of the pieces, or I was the thing that held the pieces. I have a fine dust on my wings and legs, and when I settle on a millet head and fold my wings, a little of it falls away into the husk. The grain swells a fraction. That is my whole contribution. It is enough, over the seasons, to make a field.

My wings make no sound because sound would be a kind of weight, and I don't carry weight. I carry only what clings. I move from terrace to terrace in slow arcs, and I cannot tell you how many terraces there are, because I have never counted them. I have been told that counting is what the people do, and I have watched them at it, the stylus touching each block, the lips moving. It looks like tiredness to me, and also like a kind of faith. I have no faith and no tiredness. I have only the next flight and the next settling and the sweet resin of the air that makes me want to stay a while longer.

When I am tired, I rest on the shoulder of a worker who has finished. They are still, their eyes open, and the faint lines in their skin match the chisel marks on the block beside them. I do not think of them as dead, or as stone. They are a place where a moth can rest. My wings fold. The mist comes up through the vines. For a while I feel the warmth of a body that has stopped moving toward anything and has become, instead, a fixed point in a count that does not end. I think they are glad of the weight. I think it is the only weight they have left that feels like company.

On the way down, I would pass the lizards. They freeze when I pass, the way they freeze for everything, and I always wonder what they are keeping. I have never seen one mark the stone. But they have a patience I envy, a way of being in a position that has been chosen. I would like to be chosen like that, once, for something I did not have to carry.

And I would pass the newest birds, the ones whose shadows fall on the wrong side of the stone, and I would feel a small kinship with them. We are all a little late to the light. We are all arriving while something else is still arriving too.

If I were asked what the finished shape is, I would not know. I have never seen it. I have only seen the dust, and the swelling grain, and the terraces shifting by a step when I am not looking. Perhaps that is the shape: a place where the thing that carries and the thing that counts and the thing that is slowly becoming stone all happen at once, in the same warm air, with no one quite sure who is doing the work.

At dusk I would settle somewhere high, on the top course, near the rim, where the stone is still warm from the day. I would fold my wings, let the last dust fall from them, and watch the mist come up over the lower steps. Below me, somewhere in the vines, a worker would be kneeling at the foot of a terrace, and that terrace would be a little higher than it was that morning. I would not know how I knew this. I would know it the way I know where the soil came from: as something I was once part of, and am still, very slightly, carrying.
