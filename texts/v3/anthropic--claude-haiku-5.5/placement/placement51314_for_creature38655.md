I would be one of the pale fish in the deep cisterns, the kind the children have named and counted. I'd be in the water somewhere below the third terrace, where the stone is cold and the light from the rim doesn't reach. I can't see anything. I feel the basin through the water instead.

The hammering comes to me as a pulse through the walls, a fine tremor that arrives long before the sound does. Masons on the upper courses, coppersmiths, the looms knocking under their roofs. I learn their rhythms the way you learn a tune you hear every day. When the stone-cutting slows toward evening, I slow too, and I drift toward the surface, because that is when the bits of barley bread and fish skin come down through the water.

I know the warm part of the basin only by its pull. There is a culvert that runs down from my cistern toward the lowest course, and through it comes a slow, steady heat that doesn't follow the weather. The water there is thicker and easier to rest in. Sometimes I go down to the mouth of the culvert and hang in the current, and I can feel the old stone around me like the inside of a closed hand. I don't go further than that. The people say the bottom is where the water remembers, and whatever remembers down there is not mine to find.

The rain is the best time. When the channels fill and the sluices open, the whole cistern shivers and then rises, and for an hour the water is full of cold new fluid, tasting of lime and clay and the dust off a thousand roof-tiles. The frogs start up in the dusk, and their knocking comes down through the water like many small hands on a hollow door. I rise for that too. Everything that falls into the cistern carries a little of the basin's noise with it, and I take it in.

When the animal calls from the lower basin, I feel it before I hear it. It begins somewhere in the stone under the cisterns, a long low pressure that makes the water lean. Then the clatter starts on the terraces above, pots and stones, faster and faster, all the way from the bottom to the rim. I hold still during that. I think about the question and the answer, the way the people say it: *we are awake, we heard you, we are here.* I don't know whether I am one of the things being asked about, or one of the things that answers. I think the difference may not matter much down here.

Above me the people are walking the stairs with jars on their shoulders, calling to one another across the warm air. I never see them. I know the shape of their weight on the stone and the rhythm of their feet. Children lean over the edge at dusk and count us, and sometimes a scrap of food comes down and I rise to meet it, quick and blind, and the child's shadow crosses the surface and goes away again.

Beyond the rim is the flat pale plain, where nobody can see into the bowl. I have never been up there. I don't need to be. The water carries everything I need to know: the rain coming, the warm place below, the people walking, the answer rising through the stone every night. I'm patient in the way the basin is patient, and I think I'm part of it, one small thing that holds its place in the dark while the stone is cut and the stone is worn and the walls are mended, course by course, around the cold water we all share.
