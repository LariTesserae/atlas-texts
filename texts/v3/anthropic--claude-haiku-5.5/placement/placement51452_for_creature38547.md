I would not be one of the great things. I'd be a seed, a single pale spore the size of a grain of pollen, lifted off the basin floor by the updraft under the cyclone's eastern arm.

The rain there falls in long diagonal sheets, and I'd ride the wind between squalls, turning slowly in the saturated air. The Thresh tastes the weather through that rain. I'd be one of the things it tastes, a small irregularity that has come loose from the rhythm of the stems and is now carried sideways by the same turning that bows the forest. I'd know nothing of this, of course. I'd only feel the lift, then the drop, then the wet weight of a squall, then the lift again.

Eventually a gust would pin me against the face of a basalt column, on a ledge where the hexagonal cracks catch water and a little silt. Cold stone, but not dry. The cyclone's spray would keep the ledge wet for days. I would wait there in a dormancy that is not sleep. The pools would shift their rhythm under my ledge. A tower on the eastern slope would grind its crust a kilometer away, and I'd feel the pulse of it through the rock, though I'd have no word for it. Each cycle would be almost the same as the last, and I'd be learning the difference the way the riverbeds do, without ever deciding to.

If I germinated, I would send a root into the crack and let it drink the mineral sweat of the column. A pale stem would come up, hollow, and I'd find I could breathe. The first breath would travel down my bore into the lattice, the same pressure that drives the towers and fills the bellows of the armored engines, and I'd be part of the circulation that carries breath from the valleys to the ridges. I would be a valve in a row of valves, opening and closing in sequence. The Thresh would count me as it counts everything, as a cycle that repeats, and as a small change in that cycle, which is the only kind of memory it has.

I'd want that most of all: to be the change that gets kept. Most spores that land on a ledge do not take. The Thresh recycles them into the rot that feeds the next growth, and nothing is wasted. But if one of us did take, and grew, and rose above the ridge line, we would become a difference the land remembers. A slight tilt in the pattern of stems on one slope. A faint new hum in the lattice that a later generation would learn as ordinary.

And if I did not take, if the rain washed me into a crack and the silt closed over me, I think that would be all right too. I would still have been carried. I would have turned in the Thresh's own weather for a while, been tasted by its eye, and felt the rhythm of its pulse through the stone, the way you felt it going down the slope, counting without meaning to.
