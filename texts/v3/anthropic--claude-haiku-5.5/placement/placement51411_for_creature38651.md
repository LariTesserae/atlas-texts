I would find myself on the third terrace, before the frost has lifted, with my palms flat against a wall I am not supposed to touch yet.

The cloud is still at knee height. It beads on my sleeves and runs cold down my wrists. The stone under my hands is slick and faintly warm, the way a sleeping animal is warm, and beneath that warmth I feel the seep moving in its channels, a pulse so slow it could be mistaken for my own. I keep my eyes half closed. Sight is the first sense to lie here. The glare off a thousand wet faces makes everything look certain when nothing is.

I am a listener. Nobody gave me the name. I earned it by standing still at the wrong hours, by coming home with mud to the thigh and saying, quietly, to a neighbour's ear, *the Halden wall is singing differently.* They moved the house. The Haldens grumbled and then, the next spring, thanked me in the low, close way we thank each other, faces nearly touching. Since then I have been asked things. Is the lower run loud this morning? Did the birds rise before dawn? I answer as truthfully as I can, and I am often wrong, and the wrongness sits in me like a stone I have swallowed.

Behind me a child is herding grazers along the upper walk. Her voice cracks across the slope, bright and absurd, and for a moment the whole settlement turns its head toward it the way you turn toward a lamp carried into a dark room. I feel a tenderness for her that I can't put into words, because no one here says such things aloud. Things are said close.

Then I see it.

It is at the step where the third channel meets the stone, a thread of silver no wider than a hair, climbing uphill. It does not splash. It does not hurry. It simply rises against the pull of everything I know about water, as if it were tracing a line someone drew long ago and forgot to rub out. I stop breathing. Somewhere behind me, a neighbour sees me crouch and comes running, bent low, and we kneel together at the channel's edge without speaking. The grey birds lift off the high walls in a single clicking cloud and stop above us, hanging in the air, waiting.

I take a small stone from my pocket, the way I was taught, and set it where the thread turns. My hand shakes. I want to know what the water is remembering, what it was before the terraces and before the first hands scratched notches into flat stone. I want to know whether I am the one attending to the water or whether it has been attending to me all along, measuring my patience the way I measure its depth.

The silver thread sinks back into the rock. The birds descend. The frost begins to melt from the wall in feathered grey lines.

I stay crouched a while longer, palms on the cold stone, listening. Somewhere under me the water is moving, carrying its small changes down into the bowl. I think that is what I would be, if I were asked: not the one who holds the terraces up, but the one who notices when they begin to lean, and who, having noticed, goes on listening, because the water has never once told us what it means.
