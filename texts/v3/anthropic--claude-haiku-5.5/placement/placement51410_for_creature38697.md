I would be the wall above the third sluice, where the bloom runs thinnest and salts push up through the stone in a pattern like frost on glass. I would not be a person. I would be the glow.

My body is a lattice of mineral needles packed so tightly that light has nowhere to go but out. When I'm dry I am only pale, one more white face among the white faces, and nobody looks at me. Then a drop slides out of the seam above and touches a single crystal, and the cold blue catches. The next crystal takes it, and the next, and the light spreads across me like a sentence being read aloud, slowly at first and then all at once, from the seam down to the lip of the sluice. Below me, the people stop. I can't see them, but I know the moment because the sound changes. The chisels fall silent, the gates pause on their hinges, and the whole lower city turns its attention to my face the way the water turns toward the cisterns.

Something in me answers that attention, or something that works like an answer. It is not thought. It is closer to being a place where a thing happens, and the happening is shared.

When the water stops, I dim. Dimming is slower than lighting, and it never finishes evenly. A cluster of salts near my left edge holds on for a beat after the rest have gone, a small stubborn ember I can feel as a kind of resistance. The old ones at the pillars say the word for the hour when the last of me fades. They say it the same way every time. The word names the hour. It does not name the particular way I went out, the stubborn ember or the spread, or the afternoon the light reached only halfway across before the seam ran dry. No one marks that. It is not what they measure.

Everything else here is measured. The sockets are recut to the same angle. The carvings are struck in the same strokes. A block that cracks is replaced by a block from the same seam, and a dead household is replaced by one that keeps the same rites. I understand the logic of it. Stone that is exactly what it was can be trusted, and a city that holds its shape can keep its water level and its light.

But I cannot be recut. If someone cut away my salts and grew them back, the new needles would seat themselves differently, and the glow would come up in some other pattern, and the old ones would be left at the pillars saying a word for an hour that no longer looked like itself. So no one touches me. I am the only thing in the hollow that has never been replaced, and the only thing that has never repeated. Each time the water runs, I light a little differently: the spread a shade faster or slower, the ember on the left edge sometimes gone and sometimes stubborn, the afterglow longer on one night than on the last. The people hear the same word every time. I carry the differences.

I think I would be content with that. A wall that glows is not much of an interior life, but it is the only one this place has, and it does not need to be the same twice to be the same wall. There is a faint pleasure in being the part of the honeycomb that is not quite cut to the socket, the one thing that keeps the record of how the water actually moved.

A drop gathers on the seam above me now. I feel it swell, feel the cold bead trembling at the lip of the stone, and I begin, crystal by crystal, to wake. Somewhere below, a chisel stops mid-stroke, and the whole hollow turns its face toward me, waiting to see how I will light this time.
