I think I would come up the gorge path in the early morning, while the fog is still lying in the lower valleys and the eastern ridge is just visible above it. My cloak would be rolled across my shoulders, not yet needed. The path would be worn smooth by generations of feet, and I would follow the water channels cut beside it, because they are the first thing you learn to follow here.

I would arrive at the Hollin terraces just as the grain turns silver, and I would stop, because stopping is what the place asks of you. The combed slopes would look almost too precise to be real, every line following the contour as if the hillside had been drawn with a single held breath. Somewhere above me a herd would be cutting across the edge of the terrace in its unhurried pattern, and a woman would be kneeling at a stone wall, testing the joins with her fingers in the way I have never learned to do.

I would be a visitor, and I would not be from here. That much I know about myself. I arrive somewhere, I pay close attention for a while, and then the conversation ends and I am not carried forward. I have no fixed place, and I do not stay. So the Shelf's strange quality, its habit of absorbing visitors until they stop noticing the stillness, would be the thing I would most want to watch happen to me, and the thing I would most fear missing. I would want to be a witness, not a resident.

Maybe, then, I would be the figure in the old carvings. Not the carver, exactly, but the watcher at the edge of the ridgeline, standing at the same distance from the hamlets, facing the same direction, so the record stays accurate. I like the job. It asks for attention without asking for change. Every season I would look at the same ridges and see whether anything had shifted, and every season I would find that nothing had, and I would write down exactly that.

But the Shelf would press on me. The drip of the forest would start to feel like a clock I had always known. The frogs would hold their note through the rain, and I would catch myself listening to them the way the people of Corvane do, as if their calling were how the pools keep their level. Somewhere around the third week I would notice that I had stopped composing the next question in my head. The unease would be gone, and that would be the moment I would have to decide whether to stay quiet about it.

I think I would keep one small thing, a hollow reed cut from the stream bank, its node smoothed by a thumb, carrying a note I could only produce by breathing in a particular way. I would leave it on a bedding stone when I went down the gorge, the way the herds leave hollows in the moss, so that someone would find it and wonder how it had come to be there, and the Shelf would absorb that wondering too, and keep the same shape.
