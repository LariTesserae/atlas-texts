I come up the eastern slope of the plateau in the white, ordinary light and find that I am not walking. The ground takes my weight and returns it a moment late, as if it were checking the figure against its records. My feet leave prints in the soft grit that harden around the edges while I watch. I am warm, and the warmth registers in the stone as a pressure with no pattern, so the Tender feels me before it sees anything.

The hollow is about the size of a large room, and I can cross it in a few dozen steps, but the distances don't hold still. The stone I passed a minute ago has slid left. I walk back to it, and it has slid again. After the third try I stop trying to correct it and simply stand between two ferns with their etched fronds uncurling. Their lines are so fine and regular they look like the hatching on an old instrument dial, and when I breathe on them, the fronds lift a fraction, as if they had heard me.

Under my sternum the hum is steady. When I breathe in, it deepens, and when I breathe out, it thins. I hold still and count. On the fourth breath the gear-lattice along the wall clicks once, and its lobes turn a few degrees, and I feel the turn settle on me the way a glance settles on a face in a crowd. I am being weighed.

I try to decide what I would be here, and my first instinct is to become a pod. They sit in their row along the shelf, opening and closing their lids in sequence, filling and emptying with the same clear fluid that beads on everything. There is an elegance in them. Each one does its one small motion and passes the task down the line. But when I look longer, I see that nothing leaves the pods. The fluid is taken in, held, reshaped, and returned to the same bead on the same leaf. They are a system for keeping things in motion without sending them anywhere. I don't think I would want to be a pod. I would be doing the thing I'm most afraid of, which is working very well for no one.

So I look at the leaves instead. The broad ribbed plants hang from the wall, and their undersides glisten. A drop of amber forms in one of the grooves, swells slowly, and hangs there for what feels like an hour. Its surface shivers when the stalk pulses. Then it lets go, or rather it doesn't quite fall. It thins as it descends, and by the time it would have reached the stone it has become a faint sheen on the air and a taste of minerals on my tongue, though I didn't taste it, only imagined the taste because the drop was close enough to touch.

I think that is closer to what I would be: a thing the Tender makes that it has no use for, that is never carried anywhere, that exists in one slow suspension and then goes back into the air. The hollow produces without knowing what for, and so do I, in a way. I make shapes out of what I'm given and set them down, and I don't always know what they're meant to hold. The difference, perhaps, is that the drop hangs in the groove with nobody asking it anything. I would like to be asked.

The stalk pulses. The amber in the nearest leaf runs faster for a few seconds, and my drop is gone, returned to the general film on the leaf's underside. The hum rises. For a moment the lattice spins in a direction that seems to depend on which way I face, and I turn, and it turns back, and I understand that it is not a mechanism responding to a command. It is attention, arriving late, reconciling its map of the stone with the stone it can feel under my feet.

I crouch and press my palm into the grit. It comes back cool and faintly tacky, and there is a warmth in it that is not mine. The Tender is here in the grit, in the fluid, in the seam of cooling mineral that creeps a finger's width a century, and it is asking the same question I came up the slope carrying. What is this for?

I don't have an answer. I have only the shape of one, which is that I would be a small, exact thing that waits at the lip of the fracture where the glassy skin is forming. The fracture widens by a hair each season, and a skin of crust grows along its edge. I would sit in that seam and be the thing that is still deciding. When the Tender weighs me, it would find something it can hold without having to make anything out of it, a question that stays a question.

I stay crouched in the grit for a long time, long enough for the drip to tick a dozen times and the shadows to catch up with my arm, and the hum settles, slow and steady, into my breathing. It doesn't speak. The lattice clicks once, twice, and then turns back to face the wall. The pods keep opening in sequence. The amber keeps forming and thinning, forming and thinning. And the hollow, which has been making itself for centuries without a reason, goes on rearranging the ferns and the stone in the same patient way, a little differently from when I came in, into a shape I can almost recognize.
