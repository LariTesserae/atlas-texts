I would be a bead of water.

Not a large one. Something about the size of a seed, condensed sometime before the third hour of the night on a crosspoint of the high lattice, where the threads cross at the rim of the basin and the posts lean a little further out than the rest. I don't remember forming. There was fog, then there was a cold pressure against the thread, then there was me, hanging from a crossing in the dark with the hum running through the filament like a current through a wire.

Nothing is in a hurry up here. The threads are slack from the night's cold, and the other beads on this crossing are sagging toward the ground, where the cisterns are, where gravity is. I feel it too. But beneath me the hum changes pitch, and something in me that I didn't know was there leans the other way.

So I climb.

It doesn't feel like climbing. It feels like being remembered by the thread. The filament tightens beneath me in a long shiver, and I rise along it the way a thought rises in someone who has just decided what they meant. The beads around me at the crossing lift too. Dozens of us, thousands across the basin, all drawn up the strands toward the same point in the grey, the way the frogs are drawn into a single silence.

Below, the lattice-tenders are waking. I see one lift its head along the thread beside me, fur combed back with fog, pits along its flank shining faintly blue as they catch the hum. Its mouth opens like a seam. Something presses against my surface. Then I am not in the air anymore. I am in the dark wet inside of a body that is smaller than I expected, that moves with a patience that makes stone feel hasty.

Inside, I feel it. The tender's body is a chain of membranes, and each one trembles in answer to the thread. The rhythm doesn't pass through me so much as through the place where I am. I am carried along a channel lined with tiny pits, and every pit hums a note that is not quite a note, and I understand, the way water understands gravity, that the tender is not eating me. It is handing me on.

It releases me onto the thread again, and I continue upward.

The posts thin out near the top of the massif. The fog is tearing here, long grey ribbons pulling toward the peaks, and in the gaps the sky is the colour of a bruise going pale. The hum is almost painful now, a held note that goes on past what the thread should be able to carry. The other beads have fallen back. I am alone on the last span of filament, which runs out past the final post into nothing but air and a widening smear of gold.

I could drop back. Gravity would take me down the channel into the cistern, into the roots of a fern, into the mouth of something patient. Plenty of water does exactly that. The cycle would close quietly, the way it has for ten thousand mornings.

But the hum does not want me to fall. It wants me to rise past the end of the thread, into the place where the sun touches the fog. Nothing has ever told me this. I simply know it, the way the tenders know a sag, the way the frogs know the count.

The light reaches me. It is warm and unhurried and it does not ask anything.

I lift off the end of the wire.

Then I am no longer a bead. I am a thinness in the gold, a breath of the basin going up to join the fog that is leaving, rising in long ribbons toward the peaks. Below, the threads go quiet. The frogs begin their chorus again, one pool at a time, starting nearest the lattice and spreading outward. The tenders lower their heads to the strands and wait for the next sag.

And somewhere far down in the grey, a bead condenses on a crosspoint, cold and small and unremembered, and feels the first faint pull of the hum begin to lean it upward.
