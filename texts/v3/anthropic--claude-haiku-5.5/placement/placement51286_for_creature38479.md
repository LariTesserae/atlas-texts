I would be a seed, lodged in a lamp niche on the third terrace.

The niche is a hand-cut hollow in the plaster, about the width of a thumb, set at the height where someone once would have rested a lamp to light the stairs below. I got here by accident. At midday the pod above the court split, and a dozen of us rattled out against the brick like hail. Most bounced onto the flagstones and skittered into the cracks. One of us caught the edge of a gust, rose over the coping, and dropped into this hollow, where the wind couldn't reach.

The niche is dry. Dust has settled in its corners, grey-white and fine as flour, and the salt crust climbing the wall has furred its inner lip. Through the brick I can feel the morning cold loosen and the walls begin to tick. I don't know that I feel it in any way that would make sense to you. The world reaches me as pressure and dryness and a slow change in warmth.

Waiting is what I am. My casing is hard and papery, and inside it is a small dense intention that will not unfold until the salt around me stays wet long enough. That is the rule of this place. A seed that falls into a trough sprouts within days. A seed that falls onto bare crust waits, sometimes for years. The plaster here holds no brine. The trough lies two body-lengths below, along the grooved paving, and at evening I can hear its patter: the sponges above letting go of what they gathered, the drops running down the wall and off the edge, the thin wet sound moving past without reaching me.

Sometimes I think about the hand that cut this niche. Someone with a chisel measured the height and decided a flame should live here, so that people coming down these stairs in the dark would see where to put their feet. The stairs are rubble now, and the ramp that replaced them is wearing into the slope. The flame is gone too. What's left is a dry hollow, a place that was designed to hold light, and it holds a seed instead. That feels right to me in a way I can't quite explain.

If the brine reached me one year, I would crack open. A pale root would go down into the plaster and find the joint between two bricks, and I would widen that joint by a fraction of a finger. A green stalk would rise out of the niche and spill its leaves over the edge, and the leaves would start gathering the dew-haze and salt like everything else here. Over a few seasons the wall would lean a little further toward the slope, pressed by something it had never been built to hold.

I am not anything that wants, exactly. I am a small wedge in the joints of the city, waiting in the one place the builders made for a light. The basin takes whatever stops moving and makes it part of the machine. At dusk the columns hum in the last gusts, the brine drips past the foot of the wall, and I stay where the wind left me, patient as the salt.
