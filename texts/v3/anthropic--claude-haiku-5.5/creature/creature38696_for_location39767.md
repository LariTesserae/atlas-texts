The first inhabitants you notice are the builders. On every firm patch of mud, small crustaceans the colour of the sediment push up spires no taller than a finger. Each is a hollow tube capped with a crumb of fine sand. The animals work in the cool hours, when the film of water is thinnest. They drag grains up from their burrows and pack them into the walls with pale, grooved forelimbs, patiently, one grain at a time. The spires are not scattered at random. They lean toward the channels, and where two colonies meet, their walls fuse into a single ridge. Some ridges have already hardened. Stand on one and it rings faintly under your heel. You are standing on the work of thousands of animals that will never see what they are making.

In the pools, the frogs are mostly throat. They are squat and mud-brown, with skins that sweat like the stones, and when they call the gular sac swells to the size of their heads and rattles out a sound like dry seed in a tin. They have learned the pumps. When the deep thud comes, the chorus stops, and it resumes with the long rush of water, as if the whole pool is listening for the engine's breath. Beneath them, the tadpoles hang in the shallows in clouds so dense they look like smoke. Many already have back legs and only a stub of tail, and they graze the green film on the sunken plates, turning slowly, never quite still.

The lizards keep to the warm stones. They are flat-bodied and pale, with dull scales that take on the colour of whatever they lie on. A lizard on ochre rock is invisible until it moves, and when it does it moves very little. Their throats pulse with the same slow rhythm as the pumps, which might be coincidence. They take the midges from the air with quick, almost lazy snaps, and their eyes have a milky translucence that suggests they see more of the infrared warmth of the stones than of the light.

The insects are the most numerous and the least settled. Long-legged skaters ride the surface film, their feet dimpling the water without breaking it. Above the channels, the midges hang in shimmering columns, and larger flying things pass through them: slender, bone-pale bodies with wings that never dry. Droplets sit on every wing, heavy and beaded, so that the insects seem to carry small lenses. They do not seem to mind. When one lands on your arm, it stays a long time before it leaves, as if it has found a surface it will not need to leave for a while.

The waterbirds stand in rows along the banks: grey-necked, long-legged, with pale eyes and heads that turn only slowly. They do not forage while you watch. They stand. Their stillness is not the stillness of a predator waiting for prey. It is closer to attention, the way a crowd stands when a long process has nearly finished. They roost on the gantry frames, where the iron has been eaten into lace, and their droppings have built small white stains down the struts. Their calls ricochet off the concrete and come back as something else.

The strangest inhabitants live inside the stone. The concretions that have set around the damp grey centres are not empty. Break one open, carefully, and the hollow is full of pale, segmented creatures, blind and soft as wet paper, something between a woodlouse and a larval fly. They feed on the bloom of microbes that grows on the inner wall, and they leave the stone at dusk to crawl out along the seams, where the mineral water seeps. Along those seams the calcite crusts are not simply white. Where they are fresh, a faint film of violet and rose runs through them, living things that thicken the crust as they feed on the salts, so the rock and the animal are slowly becoming the same material.

Everything here is some kind of maker. The crustaceans build the ridges, the microbes build the crusts, the frogs and birds and lizards build the living circuit that keeps the place crowded, and the insects scatter their small bodies across the surfaces to hold it together. Nothing is simply passing through. Even the waterbirds, standing so still, seem to be part of the basin's slow argument with the sea: they wait for the pumps to tire, or for the water to settle, and for the land to decide.

At dusk the whole population moves at once. The lizards retreat into the reeds, the birds shift along the gantries, the frogs lift their chorus to match the pumps, and the spires along the banks begin to rustle as their builders come up from below. The noise rises and then, for a moment, drops, and in that gap you can hear the water running under the plates, the stone ticking as it cools, and the deep patient thud of the engines still turning out on the basin, keeping time for a place that has never once been empty.
