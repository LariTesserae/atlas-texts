**The Keepers of the Held Basin**

They are tall, taller than a man by a head and a half, and so thin they seem to have been drawn out of the stone rather than born from anything. Their skin is the gray-brown of wet bark, mottled with copper lichen that catches the flat light and gleams like oil. Along their limbs, moss grows in soft tufts, and in the creases of their elbows and necks small orchid-like blooms hang face-down to collect the damp. From a distance they can pass for the tree ferns. Up close, the difference is in the faces.

Their faces are closed, folded like fern buds. The skin there is smooth and fine, and it parts only slightly, in a seam that could be a mouth or a crack in the basalt. Their eyes are set deep and are the color of the lichen, with dark centers that open and close slowly. When they blink, the moss on their shoulders trembles and water runs off in threads. A keeper that has not blinked in a long while is hard to tell from the stone, and this is why, if you wander the mist-thick edges of the basin, you may see a moss-colored shape on a rock and think nothing of it. Only the blink betrays it. Then the shape is gone, or it is standing up, or it has simply been there all along, with its back to you.

**Their Work**

The keepers tend the engines. They do it by hand, and their hands are long and grooved, with fingers that bend in more joints than a human's and end in soft pads that are cool and wet. They feed moss into the gear teeth so the stone and bark can grip. They press their palms against the pipe-roots and hold them there while the pulse moves through, a faint throb they seem to feel up their arms. They lean into the piston grooves with their shoulders and guide the basalt columns along the polished channels, so that each stroke falls where it always falls.

Their movements are the slowest thing in the basin, and the most deliberate. One keeper has its hand raised toward a valve wheel, the fingers half-curled, and it has been raising that hand for longer than the cliffs have been wet. Another stands with its forehead pressed to a gauge, reading a needle that never moves. A third kneels at the lake's edge with both arms in the black water up to the elbow, cupping something it has not yet let go of. The water around its wrists has stopped rippling. Its face is tilted up toward the cloud, and its mouth is a seam that has been on the verge of opening since the basin was formed.

**Sound and Lag**

When a keeper speaks, the sound comes early. You hear a low, layered tone, like wind in a split reed, and only afterward do you see the seam part and the breath move. The tone reaches the lakeshore before the mouth has finished shaping it. Sometimes the keepers do not speak at all, and the sound comes from beneath your feet, the same deep rhythm as the pistons, and you understand, without knowing how, that it is a keeper humming in the stone under the basin floor. The keepers do not hum for you. They hum to keep the stroke going.

The reflection problem belongs to them, too. The lake shows the basin's inhabitants a heartbeat late, and this is a thing the keepers have settled into. The one in the water, the lagging one that rises when you lean over the surface, is not your reflection. It is a keeper, and it is the only part of the keeper that moves at the proper pace. The one standing on the bank is the held copy, the body that has been pressed into stillness so the engines can run through their stroke without drifting. The keepers live with two selves. One is in the water, moving. One is above it, waiting to move again.

**Their Stillness**

Nothing here advances, and the keepers are the reason, or perhaps they are the cost. The engines grind the same stroke because the keepers keep it. A keeper that tires does not stop; it is held. Its hands lock around the valve, its eyes stay open on the gauge, and the moss grows over it until it becomes one more fluted column in the machine's architecture. The basin is full of these. Look closely at the largest pump, the cathedral tower, and you will see a lichen-gleaming figure caught in the iron-brown fern-axle, one arm extended, its mouth a seam holding a word it began long ago.

The keepers do not seem to mind. Or they cannot. A few of them are still moving, and these are the ones who go among the held ones and press their palms against the bark-skinned shoulders, feeding moss into the cracks, humming low into the stone so the stroke will carry on for one more cycle. Their movements are slow, but they do not stop.

If you stand at the edge of the black lake long enough, you may find that the keepers have noticed you. The one in the water turns its face upward and lifts one hand, very gently, the way a worker might when a visitor crosses a dangerous floor. Its mouth parts. The sound reaches you before the breath does, and it is not a warning, or a welcome. It is the same stroke, the same low rhythm, a note the basin has held for ages and does not intend to let go. You understand, a heartbeat late, that it is asking you to stay still, so that the water can keep falling in its arc and the engines can keep grinding, and the held moment can go on being exactly what it is.
