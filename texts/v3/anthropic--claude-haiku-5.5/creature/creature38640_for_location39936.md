The people of the Stepwork are shaped by the damp and the orange light. Their skin has a grey-pale cast, as if it has been kept out of the sun for generations, and their eyes adjust to the sodium glow so that they see the world in warm amber and the cold blue of the rare clear morning as a kind of strangeness. They wear oilcloth and waxed wool, hoods pulled low, with wide hands that are scarred across the knuckles from iron and mortar. Most carry a hook at the hip, a short steel tool with a rounded tip that serves for clearing gutters, prying loose scaffold pins, and hauling themselves over rails. Almost nobody walks without one.

Their voices are short. The moss and the wet air swallow sound, so speech on the stairs and galleries comes in quick bursts, a name called across a shaft, an instruction thrown up a ladder, then silence. Over generations they have learned to speak in fragments that carry: nouns, commands, the names of tiers. Long sentences are reserved for indoors, for the cisterns and the cramped kitchens cut into the terrace walls, where the stone holds a voice and gives it back. Outsiders from the valley complain that the Stepworkers never finish their thoughts. The Stepworkers think the valley talks too much.

Their days are organized around the work, and the work is never done. A typical crew might be twelve people: two riggers on the cranes, a mason or two who knows how the old basalt was laid, a plumber who reads pressure in the pipes by the sound they make, a scaffolder who can tell from the creak of a joint whether it will hold until evening. There are gardeners who tend the cage-trellises and cut back the feral ferns before they reach the lamps, and there are scrap-pickers who work the abandoned galleries, prying iron from ruined floors and hauling it down to the sheds. Everyone is expected to take a turn in each of these, and a person's standing comes less from what they make than from how many tiers they have helped raise and how many they have helped take down. Demolition is not shameful. It is the other half of the same motion.

Houses are not owned in the valley sense. A family holds a shelf, a landing, a room in a stack, for as long as the work requires them there, and when a wall comes down or a channel is rerouted, they move, carrying their hooks, their bedding, and sometimes a potted cultivar grown from seed that someone's grandmother kept alive through three reconstructions. The moves are communal events. When a district is being reshaped, the neighbors come out in oilcloth and haul the furniture down the rails in a long chain, singing nothing, calling only the names of landings. A family that has been moved six times is not considered unlucky. It is considered well-travelled.

The Stepworkers have a particular relationship to their dead. The early tiers, the poured terraces and riveted galleries, were built by people who are no longer named in any register, and the Stepworkers say the first masons are in the mortar. This is not quite a metaphor. The old builders were laid into the walls when their bodies gave out, and over the centuries the stone absorbed them, so that the grey softened basalt at the base of the mountain carries a faint, sweetish mineral smell after rain. People touch the oldest walls on their way up the stairs, a brief flat palm against the stone, the way someone in the valley might touch a doorframe for luck. Nobody will say whether it is respect or simple habit.

Children grow up in the stacks, and they learn the mountain by climbing it. They know which rails are cold and which are warm with pump heat, which gutters will flood first, which scaffold timbers are rotten at the joints. Their games are about balance and timing: crossing a cable-line between two towers before the lift-car descends, dropping a stone down a shaft and counting until it strikes water. They are quiet children. Sound does not travel far enough for shouting to be useful, so they learn to whistle and tap on pipes, a private language that carries along the lines of the building. Adults rarely have to tell them where to go. The mountain tells them, through the pitch of the pumps and the smell of the air.

Some among the Stepworkers are the rememberers. They are usually old, though not always, and they carry the shape of the mountain in their heads. A rememberer can stand on a landing and say that this wall was poured in the year the cistern burst, that this gallery once connected to a stair that has since been torn out, that the terrace below was a vineyard before the water was rerouted. They argue with one another about what the mountain used to look like, and their arguments take place over tea in the greenhouses, their voices rising and falling in the milky light. When a rememberer dies, the others sometimes climb to the high galleries and speak the names of the tiers they knew, one after another, until they run out of breath.

The keepers of the water have the hardest and most solitary work. They live near the cisterns and the turbine chambers, in rooms where the pumps never stop, and they sleep with their ears tuned to the rhythm of the system. A keeper who loses the rhythm knows something is wrong before any gauge shows it. They move through the lower tiers with their hooks and their lamps, checking valves, clearing silt, opening and closing channels to balance the flow from the cloud-catchers. When a flood comes, the keepers are the first into the water and the last out, and they are the ones who decide, with a few terse calls down the shafts, where the channels will be opened to save the lower streets.

They are not always gentle. The Stepworkers have a sharp humor, dry as the old basalt, and they are impatient with the valley people who come up the slope to gawk or to buy seed stock. They do not much care for visitors who want the mountain explained to them. Ask a Stepworker what the mountain is for and you will get a shrug, then a gesture at the cranes and the lamps and the pumps, then the word "working," which means the same thing in their speech as "alive."

Their dogs and cats are their own, bred for the rails and the narrow ledges, and they feed the grey birds that nest in the gutters because the birds are, in some way nobody quite remembers, part of the work too. The caged thing in the window that no one owns is, the children will tell you, a kind of watchman. It sings when the pressure in the lines is wrong. Nobody has told the children whether that is true. They believe it anyway, and they check on it every morning on their way to the stairs.

There is a saying the Stepworkers use when someone asks whether a thing is finished. They answer, "Nothing is finished, only handed on," and then they hand the hook to whoever is nearest, and they go back to work. The phrase is said so often that it has lost most of its meaning, and yet every person on the mountain seems to know exactly what it means: that the Stepwork is not a place that was built, but a conversation between the living and the dead, carried on in stone and iron and water, shelf by shelf, for as long as there are hands to hold the hooks.

From the lowest tier, if you stand long enough in the drizzle and watch the lamps come on one by one up the flank of the mountain, you can see them moving through the gaps. A figure on a rail. A lantern swinging on a crane. A shadow pausing at a wall and laying a palm flat against the stone before going on up the stairs. They are small against the scale of the slope, and they are everywhere on it, and the mountain, which was made by their hands and is still being made, carries them upward into the cloud.
