**The People of the Notch**

They do not call themselves anything grand. In their own speech they are the ones who stay, or sometimes just *the wet ones*, a name the lowland traders use with a little contempt and the terrace folk use with a little pride. Nobody here is born on a flat dry place, and nobody who grew up in the bowl has ever wanted one.

**Bodies**

The thin air shaped them. Their chests are deep and their lungs are large, and a visitor from the lowlands gasps on the first climb while a child of the terraces runs the bridges without slowing. Their hands are the most striking feature. Fiber cord and split stem have worn ridges into the palms and fingertips, and the old people's hands look almost ribbed, like the bark of the pale trees. Their skin is pale under the cloud and stained brown or grey by smoke and clay. Many have the faint green cast of algae at the knuckles, which the healers say is harmless and the children say is a sign of the forest claiming them.

They move with their arms held a little away from their sides, a habit learned so early that nobody remembers learning it. It keeps the midges from their faces and lets them catch a branch or a rung without thinking. They breathe through the nose when walking and speak only when they stop. Talk in motion is considered a waste of breath and a good way to walk into a stinging nest.

**The planted**

Every child is given a tree at birth, or nearly so. The family chooses a sapling from the nursery shelf, pins it into the bank of the terrace where the child's house will stand, and sings it through its first winter. The tree becomes the child's measure. Its height is counted each season, and a person's standing is partly the health of their tree. A fallen tree is a grief, and a healthy one thick at the base is a boast.

Trees also carry names. When a bridge root is guided across a gap, it is named for the person who guided it, and the name is cut into a fitted stone at each end. A traveller crossing the terraces reads a long litany of the dead and the living every day without intending to. The people say that a name held by a root lasts longer than a name held by a mouth.

**Work and knowledge**

They know the forest the way other peoples know their own families. A weaver knows how a stem will bend after three days of rain and how much to twist it before it sets. A pruner can tell from the color of a vine whether it is going to thicken or go to seed. The fungal shelves are farmed by a few families who pass down cutting techniques so precise that a shelf can be harvested every season without dying. Their knowledge is almost entirely physical. They have a rich vocabulary for textures, wetness, and rates of growth, and a much thinner vocabulary for things that cannot be touched.

Their tools are stone, bone, and hardwood, and they are good with all three. A stone axe in the bowl is kept sharp for the same reason a child is kept fed: because the work will not wait.

**Dusk and the moths**

At dusk the midges rise, and the people light their smoke-pots and walk the terraces in pairs or small groups, the pots held low. They sing as they go, not for pleasure but to keep the rhythm of the walk, and the songs have a rough meter that matches the frogs. Older people count frog calls to tell the hour and judge the coming weather, and a sudden silence in the hollows means something is moving through the canopy that the frogs fear.

The broad moths on the walls get a particular kind of attention. The people do not hunt them and do not tolerate them. They watch them. An old saying holds that a moth which opens its wings slowly at dusk is breathing easily, and a moth that stops breathing is a sign of cold coming down from the notch. Whether this is true or not, the elders say it, and the children repeat it while swatting the midges away from their own faces.

**Authority**

Power in the bowl follows water. Each terrace has a family that keeps the springs clear, channels the seep into troughs, and decides who draws from which. These families are called the Wet Hands, and they do not wear marks of office. Their power is simply that everyone needs water and the springs are theirs to open or close. When there is a dispute, it is settled at the market stone, loudly, with the Wet Hands listening and sometimes stepping in with a word that ends the matter. Violence is not rare on the market stone, and everyone there has a scar or two from a bargain gone wrong. But the disputes that matter are almost always about water, and the people who win them are usually the ones who promise to rebuild first.

**The dead**

The dead are not buried. The bowl is too wet and the roots too thick for graves. The body is laid on a shelf of the forest floor, wrapped in fiber, and the fungi and ferns take it over within a season. A person's bones are gathered in a clay pot after the body has gone, and the pot is set into a wall during the next rebuilding, where it becomes part of the stone. When someone old dies, the family says that the terrace has taken on another weight, and this is meant as comfort. The walls of the bowl hold the dead in the same way they hold the water, and a visitor who knows what to look for can see small clay shapes in the seams of nearly every terrace.

**What they believe about endurance**

They do not think the place is permanent. They know the storm will come every season and will take a few limbs and a bridge or two, and they know the moss will eat any wall that is not repaired. What they believe is that the place holds because the people keep coming back to it. Their word for rebuilding is close to their word for returning home, and the festival at the end of the wet season, when the storm damage has been mended and the new bridges are first walked, is the most joyful event of the year. They walk the new span and stamp on it until it creaks. Then they cut the names into the stone. Nobody mourns the storm for long. The storm is the reason there is work, and work is the reason there are people in the bowl at all.

**Temperament**

They are direct, physically tough, and quick to laugh at things that would unsettle a lowlander: a snake dropping from a branch, a child stung on the eyelid, a bridge swaying under too many feet. They are generous with food and fire, and suspicious of anyone who refuses to walk the terraces on foot. They have little patience for the old stories about the lowlands, which they consider a place of dry hard ground where nothing grows fast enough to matter. They are not sentimental about the bowl. They love it in the way a person loves a body that aches every morning and still gets them up and out.

Ask a terrace-born child where they come from and they will not name a house or a family. They will point at the wall they live under, the spring that feeds their water, and the tree that is theirs, and they will say, with the easy confidence of people who have never been anywhere else, *here, I'm still growing*.
