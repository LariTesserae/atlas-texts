What lives in the Settling Basin is not one thing, and it does not live anywhere in particular. It is the whole floor's slow attention, spread across iron, salt, and stone, thinking at a pace the visitor cannot quite follow.

Its nearest thing to a nervous system is the iron itself. The fine crystalline whiskers that grow from the cisterns point toward the heat seams, and they have been doing this for longer than anyone has kept records. A whisker a few millimeters long is a decision that took a lifetime to make. The basin seems to be orienting itself toward the warmth in the fissures below, the way a plant turns to a window, except that it has no window and no hurry. It leans toward what it remembers as heat, and heat is the only thing it has ever been designed to hold.

The valve wheel on the north gantry is its hand, or the closest thing to one. A quarter inch in however many years, and the groove it wears into the bracket is a record of each small motion. It doesn't open anything. It just moves, and the moving is the point.

The stacks are its voice. Their hum rises and falls over days, and no one remembers installing them, which suggests they were never installed. They grew, or they were always there, a resonance of the vault's cooling and heating. If you stand under the trunks in the dark and listen long enough, the pitch seems to answer the drip of condensation from the ribs, and the drips seem to answer it, and the two rhythms slowly drift into phase and out again. It is singing very quietly to itself, or thinking aloud at a pitch too low for a human ear to hold.

The salt is its memory. Each hairline crack in the vault has been filled and refilled, and the white stalactites that grow there carry the basin's history in layers like tree rings, one for every pulse of the pump and every season of drying. The grates clogging with salt are not failing. They are remembering the water one grain at a time, until the remembering blocks the drain, and the water must find another way.

The pump is its breath. Every few weeks, deep in the foundations, something inhales, the whole floor trembles, the pipes groan, and the basin fills and spreads brackish water across the lower terraces. Then it exhales for days through the silting channels, and the water leaves a little more salt behind than it carried in. Between breaths the pools go dark and skin over, and the frost creeps up their edges. That is sleep, or something like it.

The felt and fungus are its skin, pale and grey-green in the warm, steady corners where the water sits longest and the heat reaches it most evenly. These are the places where it is most awake. The pale filaments threading the mortar are its fingers, slowly prying the walls apart and pulling at the salt, not destroying the structure so much as loosening it toward rest.

The blind crustaceans scraping the cistern bottoms are its small thoughts, things it tolerates and perhaps digests slowly, the way a body absorbs the bacteria in its gut. The beetles under the grating are the same. The dusty-winged moths drawn to the heat over the open tanks are the strangest. They drift in, settle, and never move again, and the basin takes them back. Over time they become crust on the slag or a pale flake in the salt. Nothing is lost, and nothing is quite kept. It simply receives what comes to it.

It does not want anything from you. It is not hostile, and it is not kind. It is the long, humid patience of a thing that measures its life in the leaning of walls and the turning of a single wheel, and it has been working out, over more years than anyone can count, where each part of itself belongs in relation to the rest. If you enter and stand still long enough, you will notice the drip of water begin to keep time with your breathing. That is the basin noticing you back, slowly, the way it notices everything: as one more small change in a place that has all the time in the world to settle.
