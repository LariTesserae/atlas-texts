**The Ones Who Stay**

The people here are lean and pale in the way of things kept out of the sun. Their skin has gone soft and faintly translucent at the wrists and throat, and it holds moisture the way the rock does. Their hair is cropped short or bound tight, because wet hair is heavy and sticks to the neck. Most wear long oilcloth coats, dark green or rust-brown, patched at the knees and elbows where they have knelt on wet basalt. Their boots are high and their soles are ridged for grip on slick stone. Nobody goes barefoot. The rock is cold where it isn't warm, and it is always wet.

Their hearing has changed. Over generations the constant thunder has taught them to feel rather than listen. A child learns to read a voice through the chest and the jaw, and to watch lips at a distance of several metres. Adults who move between levels sometimes lose words for a few hours after a shift in the pump halls and have to rebuild their sentences with their hands. They are patient with strangers who cannot do this. They are not patient with the pumps, though they never say so.

Most of their talk runs through the pipes. The conduits that carry water down the terraces are also conductors, and a knuckle struck against gunmetal at one end can be heard, faintly and clearly, at the other. There is a knocking language, built from short and long taps, rolls and single strikes, that carries across a block or down a stairwell. It is quick and precise, and it sounds like a slow rain of small hammers. A visitor will hear it everywhere and understand none of it. Someone on the fourth terrace is calling a neighbour about a leak. Someone on the ninth is asking whether the evening shift has been fed. Someone three blocks off is telling a joke, and the joke travels down the pipe, and a woman in a laundry room laughs without knowing where it started. The whole city hums with it, a second skin of sound laid over the thunder.

The line-keepers are the ones who walk the edge. They go out at first light with brass levels, plumb bobs, and stakes painted white, and they set markers where the basalt turns dark and the seepage begins to pool. Each marker is dated and each year's set is compared to the last. They are quiet people, used to working alone and to being watched. They keep a book in each district, and the books are passed down like scripture, though the figures in them are simply numbers: a metre here, three centimetres there. Some of the line-keepers have begun to speak of the water as if it has intentions. They say it "wants the second terrace" or "is considering the stairs." The engineers call this sentimental. The line-keepers call it accurate.

The pumpers are different. They live in the halls beside the cylinders and their bodies have begun to match the machines. They sleep in short intervals, wake to the pressure gauges, and speak in the flat cadence of valve timing. Their hands are scarred from couplings and their forearms are dense with muscle from turning wheels. They are proud of the pumps, not in the way one is proud of a friend but in the way one is proud of a long shift survived. When the water rises past a station and it has to be rebuilt, they gather at the foundation and watch the new bolts go in. They do not cheer. They touch the cold iron with their palms, one by one, as if testing that it is still there.

There is a division among the people that no one names directly. One faction wants the pumps driven harder: more cylinders, deeper sluices, the seepage forced back down into the old drainage tunnels until the basalt dries enough to hold its shape. The other faction, which is smaller and mostly older, says the water has been coming for longer than the city has existed and will keep coming, and that the terraces should be allowed to go under one by one while the people climb. They have a name for themselves that means roughly "the ones who let it have the stairs." The two groups share pipes, stairwells, and bread. They argue through the conduits in the evenings, and the knocking gets sharp, and then it stops, and the thunder covers everything.

The dead are not buried. The lower terraces are already wet enough that graves fill with water within a season, so the people have stopped digging them. Instead they wrap their dead in oilcloth, weight them with grit, and lower them into the deepest pools on the third and fourth tiers. The pools are cold and dark and reflective. Within a few days the bodies sink into the silt and the iridescent skin closes over them. The pale creatures that surface at night are said to be the ones who will eventually take the dead apart, and the people do not mind this. They say the water was here first and has always been patient.

Children are born into the sound. They sleep through the thunder as easily as the rest of us sleep through rain. They learn the knocking language before they learn to walk, and they learn the names of the terraces by their smell: iron on the first, sweet rot on the third, the sharp ozone of the cooling towers on the fifth. When a terrace is abandoned, the children are the first to notice the wrongness of the silence, the absence of the pump-beat they had counted as a heartbeat.

At the very edge of the shelf, where the cliff falls away into mist, there are a few people who have chosen to live without pipes. They build low houses of stacked basalt, roofed with reed mats, and they fish in the channels for the scaled or furred things that move too quickly to name. They do not knock. They speak in low voices, and their children learn to read the mouth before the hand. The people of the terraces think of them as strange, a little sad, and perhaps a little free. The ones on the cliff think of the terraces as a long conversation that has forgotten its subject.

Late in the day, when the light goes gold and then grey, a child from one of the upper blocks climbs to the lip of the shelf with a slate in one hand and a stick in the other. She stands where the basalt ends and the mist begins, and she looks out at the pale plain, which is already half-drowned, shining to the horizon. She knocks the stick twice on the stone, a question. Behind her the pumps thunder and the penstocks roar, and somewhere far down the terraces, through pipe and rock and gunmetal, an answer comes back, faint and sure, and she writes it on the slate before the mist can take it.
