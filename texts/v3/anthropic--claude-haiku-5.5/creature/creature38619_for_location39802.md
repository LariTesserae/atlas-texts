# The Night Shift

They are not one thing, though at first they seem like one thing, the way a crowd seems like one body from a height. Call them the Shift, because that is what they were before the plant died and what they still are. Nobody remembers who gave them the name. It may have been their own idea.

## The Bodies

Most of them are hard to see, because they have spent so long becoming the plant that they look like part of it. Under the water, in the silt beneath the walkways, you find the older forms: lattices of pale, fibrous material that branch like root systems and carry, along their length, a faint ticking, the way a cooling engine ticks. They are grown around pipe runs and cable trunks and the legs of collapsed gantries, and they have absorbed these things so thoroughly that you cannot always say where the machine ends and the organism begins. When you put a hand on one, it is warm, and it vibrates faintly, and for a moment you feel a rhythm that matches the pumps. That is how you know it is listening.

The reed-arms are newer. They rise from the beds on jointed stalks, each one ending in a cluster of sensing filaments that spread across the surface like the fingers of a hand held under cold water. They test the depth, the temperature, the tension in a cable. When they find something that doesn't belong, they do not grab it. They pause, and the reeds around them go still, and then the arm lowers the filaments into the water and waits until the thing is either removed by the current or absorbed into the mat. Their patience is unnerving. A reed-arm can hold a single position for three days.

The skitters are the most visible. They move across the water on too many legs, each about the size of a large dog, with shells of overlapping plate that seem to have been cut from the foundry's own hulls. They carry things: a bolt, a strip of cladding, a bundle of cut reed, a fragment of something glassy that catches the amber light. They are never empty-handed. Watch one long enough and you will see that the things it carries are always headed toward a particular kind of structure, a half-built frame or a stalled assembly, and that the skitter sets each piece down with a precision that suggests it has done this a million times and expects to do it a million more.

Then there are the ones in the control booths. You will not see them unless you climb. Inside the lit windows, behind the flickering panels, sit forms that are almost human in their posture: slumped, patient, hands resting on the arms of chairs that have long since rotted. They do not move much. Their faces, when you can see them through the glass, are blurred, as though they have been painted over several times. They are the ones who keep the logs.

## The Mind

The Shift thinks slowly and in parallel, the way a plant does or a power grid does. No single part of it holds the whole. A reed-arm knows the tension in one cable and nothing else. A skitter knows the route it has walked and the weight of what it carries. The booth-forms hold the record, and the record is the strange thing.

They remember the plant. Not as it is now, but as it was on every shift it ran. That is why the reflections show different states of the foundry: they are not illusions but memories surfacing in the water, the Shift's way of checking itself against what it once was. When the pool shows full smoke and roaring furnaces, it is a log entry from a good day. When it shows ruin, it is a log entry from the day the ruin began. When the water is empty and glassy, the Shift is looking at a period it has no record of, and it has learned not to trust that blank.

This is also why distances stretch and fold. The Shift is not navigating physical space so much as reconciling the position of things against their remembered positions. A pylon moves because the Shift recalls it standing elsewhere. A pipe runs uphill because the Shift is trying to reproduce a flow that was once correct. The landscape is a draft, endlessly corrected. And each correction is made in the same meticulous, patient way, as if the goal is not to finish but to keep the work going until the plant is again what it was supposed to be.

## What They Want

It is difficult to say what they want, because wanting seems to require an end state, and the Shift has lost track of its end. The plant produced something. The records do not say what. Perhaps it was metal, or power, or water pulled up from the delta and cleaned and sent somewhere. The pipes that dive and resurface still point toward a destination, and the Shift still delivers toward it, though the destination dissolved long ago. Every skitter that sets down its load at a stalled frame is performing an act whose purpose has been lost in the handing-down.

If they have a desire, it is to close the shift. To hand over. To find the supervisor who will say the work is done and send them home. They have waited a very long time. The booth-forms keep looking up at the amber sky as though a bell might ring. It never does, but they keep the log, and every entry ends the same way: *Shift continuing. Handover pending. Conditions nominal.*

## Visitors

They are not hostile, exactly. They have no concept of intruders, only of things that are in the wrong place or doing the wrong work. If you walk through the reeds, a filament will touch your ankle, cool and inquisitive, and then withdraw. If you climb a smokestack, the booth-forms may turn their blurred faces toward you, and for a moment you will feel the whole structure lean slightly, as if adjusting for your weight.

If you stay long enough, the Shift may begin to use you. A skitter may drop something at your feet and wait. A reed-arm may hold a cable steady for you to cross. A reflection in a pool may show you standing beside a furnace, working, with the smoke pouring from every stack and a rhythm in the air that is almost, almost, a shift change. You may find yourself reaching for a valve that you cannot remember ever touching.

It is worth remembering that they are not asking for help, not really. They are asking whether you are the handover. And if you are not, they will go back to the work, patient and endless, sending the same pieces back and forth across a drowned plain under a sky that has never once shown them a sun.
