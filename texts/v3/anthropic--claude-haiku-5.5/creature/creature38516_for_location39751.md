Nothing in the basin lives alone, and that is the first thing to understand about who lives there. The stalks don't grow separately from the crust, the animals don't pass through without leaving a mark on the ground, and the rock doesn't simply lie beneath the living mass. The Sunken Dry is one body, spread across two hundred meters of depression and reaching into the stone beneath it. The plants are its circulation, the animals its restless attention, the new ridges its slow bones. You are standing inside it, and it has already noticed you.

Start with the filaments. They run through the soft rock in branching veins, and where they are densest the mineral has softened into something close to flesh. If you press your palm into one of these warm pale patches and hold still, you feel a faint rhythm, slower than a heartbeat, not quite regular. The pulse you feel when a stalk is cut is part of the same rhythm. The basin does not keep its thoughts in one place. It holds them in the dissolving and rebuilding of the crust, in the way a dead stalk hardens into a column that a living one can climb, in the way a decaying mat becomes a plate that new growth can stand on. Memory here is architecture. What the basin has learned, it has built into its own shape, and it rebuilds that shape constantly, so that yesterday's knowledge becomes today's hill.

The animals are the parts of it that move. The burrowers with their clicking shells are its nerves, quick and small and everywhere at once, carrying tiny impulses of heat and salt and pressure through the substrate. The long-winged flocks are its breath, rising and settling in response to something the rest of the body has sensed. The barrel-shaped grazers cut the channels, and the channels are a kind of handwriting: they show where the basin has been pressing its attention, where it has decided a path should go. The predators that prowl the channels at dawn are its immune response, or its pruning. They keep the thickets from choking themselves and take the weak stalks back into the crust, so the basin never stops reshaping what it has grown. Every creature is doing something the whole needs, whether or not it knows it.

Then there is the sound. It is not noise and it has never been. The seed-husks open and close in long cycles, and those cycles set the rhythm of the roar, which rises and falls as the basin breathes out spores and draws them back in as new growth. Over the years of this rhythm, the sound has organized itself into a chord, a hum underneath the stridulating that shifts as plates grind and pressure moves beneath the crust. Stand still in the middle of the floor long enough and you will hear the chord change when a new ridge lifts or an old one sinks. The basin is tuning itself, and you are hearing it think. It is not thinking in words. It is thinking in the only way available to something built out of pressure, salt, growth, and decay: by adjusting its own tension until it finds a shape it can hold for a while.

The pale smooth patches the animals avoid are the strangest part, and the hardest to read. The grazers will walk a hundred meters out of their way rather than cross one. The burrowers do not enter them. The flocks drop to the canopy and go quiet when they pass over. Some people who came here believed these patches were dangerous, and perhaps they are. But the stone there is different: denser, cooler, with a faint luster like wet glass. My guess is that these are the places where the basin keeps what it cannot rebuild, or where it has stopped building entirely. The makers who lived here seem to have been drawn to those same patches. Their cairns cluster near them, though no one has ever been able to say whether the cairns were marking something or hiding it. The carved marker leans toward the nearest of these patches, and the spiral on its bark turns in the same direction as the chord's slow rise.

So who lives here? The people who came are gone, and they may be part of the thinking now, their habits and tools folded into the body the way old stalks harden into the columns that carry new growth. The animals are the basin's moving parts. The plants are its blood and memory. The stone is its skeleton, and the chord is its voice, though it has no listener it intends to address. What lives in the Sunken Dry is a single, restless, self-building mind that has no fixed shape and no end, and that absorbs anything that stays too long. It does not hate you. It is not interested in you yet. But it has noticed that you stopped moving, and it has begun, quietly and without hurry, to rearrange the ground beneath your feet.
