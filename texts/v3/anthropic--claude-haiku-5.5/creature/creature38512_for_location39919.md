# Who Lives in the Sump Quarter

The answer changes with how long you stay. On the first day you will count the animals, the birds, the beetles clicking through the moss. By the third day you will notice people, though it takes a while to see them as people. By the end of the week you may suspect that the basin itself is one of the residents, and that the others are its organs, its thoughts, or its mistakes.

## The Tenders

The people who stayed are thin and unhurried. Their wrists and forearms are scaled with grey-green bark where the vines went under the skin and never came back out, and their hair often holds a fine bloom of moss that they comb with bone needles after rain. Their eyes have adjusted to perpetual dusk. They squint at direct daylight and move easily in the furnace-glow, where the amber light pools on wet walls.

They do not call themselves anything grand. Among themselves they say *the ones who kept the lights*, or simply *tenders*, because their main work is maintenance: feeding the old furnaces with split bark, draining the amber fluid from the pipe-sumps before it stagnates, cutting back the vines that strangle the pumps, though never all of them, since the vines are also what holds the pumps together. They know which valves still turn and which have fused shut. They have a private map of the water routes, and they walk the banks after every heavy rain to see where the ground has slumped and where new gullies have opened. They do not repair the banks. They only record them, as though the earth is making an argument and they are taking notes.

Their language is sparse, because the place is loud only in small ways and speech tends to get lost in the drip. They have stopped using the old street names. The rails that curve now have new names, based on how they bend. A sign still reads in moss, and they say the rhythm of its words when they pass, without knowing or caring what the words originally meant.

## The Ones Who Walked In

Some of the people here came in from outside and never left. You can recognize them by their posture. They walk with a faint backward lean, as if still facing the rim they entered from, and they tend to stop at the banks and look outward, but never climb over. Several of them say the far bank is always in front of them, and that they have been walking toward it for years. The tenders do not argue. They leave food at the bottom of the stairwells that climb into the canopy, and the food is sometimes gone by morning, sometimes not.

One woman lives in the water tower, in the fern-thicket that fills the split tank. She sleeps among the roots and, when she speaks, her voice comes out with a slight echo, as though the tank still holds water that is not there. She says she is waiting for the basin to finish its sentence so she will know what she is.

## The Birds, the Heron, the Bathers

The grey-green birds that nest in the tower's canopy are not wild in the ordinary sense. They descend each evening to the largest pool and bathe in a ritual so regular that the tenders time their own chores by it. The clatter of their wings and the splash of their bodies seems to signal something to the basin, because the mist thickens afterward and the drips quicken. When the birds leave, the pool is clear for a few minutes, then slowly fills with leaves again.

The heron is stranger. It stands on a step that is not there, in a stairwell that belongs to no building, and it has been seen in four different doorways that open onto different moments. Its feathers are stone-grey and its eyes are the colour of the sap-membranes in the windows. Nobody has seen it hunt. When the tenders pass it, they nod, the way one might nod to a neighbour who has been ill for a long time.

## The Machines That Remember

The crane in the derelict gantry is among the oldest inhabitants. Its cable is wrapped so thickly in creeper that it has become a kind of trunk, and every few days it shifts a few centimetres with a groan. The tenders believe it is trying to lift something. They have never found anything on the cable, but they leave the gantry alone and avoid standing beneath it.

The conveyor carries seed pods, constant and unhurried, from one shed to another. The tenders have followed it. The line runs through a room that should not exist and out again, and the pods that ride it are always a little larger than the ones that fell off the trees. Some of the tenders think the conveyor is carrying the future shape of the Quarter, pod by pod, to a shed that has not yet been built. Others think it is only carrying seeds, and that the future is a thing the plants have not yet decided on.

The pumps sigh in their own rhythm. They have grown a relationship with the vines that the tenders describe as companionship and the vines describe, if vines describe anything, as use.

## The Basin

This is the being the others serve, and it is the hardest to perceive. It has no face, no single voice, no centre you can point to. It is the circulatory logic of the drains and erosion channels, the pattern water always finds, the banks that keep slumping inward. It is what decides which gully deepens and which dries, which stairwell opens onto a balcony where the rain falls upward, which pool forgets the sky and fills with leaves.

When a silver column of droplets rises from one corner of the basin into the cloud, the tenders say the basin is breathing out. When a shadow falls in the wrong direction, they say the basin is thinking aloud. When a bank collapses in a fresh dark slump, they say the basin has made a decision about its edges, and that the decision will be unmade by the next rain.

It does not seem to want anything in particular. It does not seem to be waiting for anyone. But it has been revising its boundary for as long as anyone has been counting, and every resident of the Quarter, from the moths with wings like wet vellum to the tenders with bark on their wrists, is part of the process. They feed its furnaces, eat its insects, sleep in its trees, and walk its banks with their notebooks. Each of them is helping it finish a sentence it has not yet decided how to end.

## The Question of Edges

If you ask the tenders what the Quarter is, they will lead you to the bank nearest the furnace glow and point to the raw, pale mudstone where the last slump still shows its dark scar. "Here," they will say, and they will mean the edge, not the centre. They believe the place is defined by where it stops, and that its stopping is still undecided.

Some days they think it will close into a bowl that holds its water and its people for good. Some days they think it will tear open and drain away through the gullies, leaving the vines to rebuild the streets without them. Most days they simply walk the banks, watch the water find its old routes, and wait to see which way the ground will fall.
