# The Inhabitants

Nothing in the Settling Basin lives alone. Each creature is a small loop inside a larger one, and the loops meet and interlock until the whole place runs like a gearbox that no one remembers building. The animals here do not seem to have histories. They have routines, and the routines are older than any record of them.

**The Clingers**

A single clinger is hard to find. You may see one lifting its edges on a pipe, but the colony is the real creature, a mat of several thousand plates that shares a single metabolism. When one plate flexes, its neighbors flex in answer, and the ripple moves across the wall like a slow breath. The chiming starts scattered and irregular, but over a few minutes the walls fall into rhythm with one another until whole rows of sheds sound in unison. Then the clock strikes eleven, and every colony in the basin shudders at once, and for a few seconds the town chimes as a single instrument before drifting apart again.

They graze the films of oil, rust, and condensate, and in doing so they perform a task that no one assigned them. The rust skin that keeps the metal stable is only stable because the clingers keep it thin. Each mat scrapes just enough to stop corrosion from pushing through, and never so much that the metal is exposed. Strip a patch clean and the neighboring colony reaches across within a day, closing the wound with a sheet of new plates. The clingers are the upkeep crew of a machine that does not need upkeep, and they have been doing it so long that the upkeep has become indistinguishable from the rust it prevents.

Their plates are dark and layered, and in the rain they open their edges wide, drinking. In that moment, a wall of clingers looks like wet slate that has grown a skin of light.

**The Rattlers**

The rattlers are the only creatures that seem to rise above the basin's floor, and they do it every morning at the same time, when the mist is thickest. Flocks lift off from the cranes and the pipe lattice and drift through the low fog with their wings half open. In the mist, their jointed wings catch the moisture, and they fly with a slowness that looks like wading. As the sun burns the mist away, they fold their wings and descend, and by midday they are roosting again, packed shoulder to shoulder in the gantries and stacks.

No one has seen a rattler eat. They take nothing from the clingers or the pipes, and they do not drink from the channels. Some observers think they feed on the mist itself, drawing the fine droplets through their wings the way a heron takes fish from shallow water. Whatever they draw, it is enough to keep them flying for twelve hours a day and roosting for the rest, every day, without fail.

Their droppings are the only thing the basin's animals add to its ground. The gray paste builds up on the rooflines, hardens into crust, and scrapes underfoot, slowly thickening the surfaces that the clingers graze. It is not clear whether this helps or harms the colonies. The clingers seem indifferent to it. The rattlers never seem to notice the mess they make.

**The Burrowers**

The burrowers are the quietest creatures in the basin, and in some ways they are the most mysterious. They have no visible eyes, and their heads are blunt and featureless, so they seem to navigate by touch and vibration. They dig through the fine soil of the channels, and the mounds they leave are roughly thirty meters apart across the basin floor, a pattern so regular it looks surveyed.

Each mound marks the territory of a small group, perhaps a dozen animals, that maintains the nearby channel. The channels have not changed course in living memory, and this is largely the burrowers' doing. When a stretch of channel begins to silt, a burrower works the sediment back out. When water tries to cut a new path, the burrowers fill it in. They are the reason the braided channels have agreed to stop, and they keep them stopped with a patience that resembles stubbornness.

They rarely surface in daylight. At dusk, though, they emerge in small numbers and drink from the channels, sometimes lying almost flat in the shallow water with their blunt heads just breaking the surface. For a few minutes the basin's floor is busy with pale shapes, and then the light goes and they sink back into the ground, leaving their mounds to catch the last of the gray glow.

**How They Live Together**

The creatures do not interact in any way that looks like cooperation. The clingers do not know the rattlers exist, and the rattlers have no use for the burrowers. Yet their loops fit together like the parts of the machinery. The rattlers' droppings feed the crust. The crust is grazed by the clingers. The clingers keep the metal from corroding, and the metal holds the cranes and pipes where the rattlers roost. The burrowers keep the water in its channels, and the water keeps the mist rising and the rattlers flying. Remove any one loop and the others would, in theory, adjust. No one has ever tested this, because no one has ever been able to remove anything.

The creatures also share one thing with the machines. They do the same things every day, and the sameness is not a failure of imagination but a kind of completion. The clingers graze at the same rate. The rattlers rise at the same hour. The burrowers dig at the same pace, and when they emerge at dusk they do it exactly when the light begins to fail. They are not waiting for anything. They are simply continuing.

**At Eleven**

When the clock strikes eleven, the whole basin answers. The clingers ripple in a single wave across every wall. The rattlers, if they are in the air, turn together over the rooftops like a shoal changing direction. The burrowers, if they are above ground, lift their blunt heads toward the sound and hold still.

Then the strike ends, and the animals return to their loops. The clingers graze, the rattlers settle into the gantries, and the burrowers slip back into the soil. The clock will strike again in four minutes, and the animals will answer again, and they will go on answering for as long as the place has existed, which is longer than anyone can say.
