You will not see them at first, and not because they hide. Your eyes have nothing to work with here, and the keepers have never needed to be seen. You learn them the way you learn everything in this place, by sound. A voice comes up from the lower galleries, and then another, and you realize the low hum you have been feeling in your sternum has a shape to it, a chorus of many throats held just beneath hearing.

They are human in outline, or were. Their bodies are long and narrow, the skin the color of wet stone and pale where no sun has ever reached, with a faint sheen that is not sweat but the same condensation that beads on every surface up here. Where eyes should be there are smooth, shallow hollows, closed over and ridged with thin scar tissue, as if the eyes were once opened and then grown shut. The keepers do not seem to miss them. Their heads are broad at the jaw and taper toward the crown, and down the throat runs a fold of soft, vibrating tissue that reaches to the collarbone. That fold is how they speak, and it is how they see. They send out a hum with a click buried in it, and the sound goes through the water and the stone and comes back altered. A keeper standing at a pump can tell from the return where a cistern has cracked, how far a pipe has bent under its own weight, whether the cloud overhead is thickening or thinning. They read the world in its echoes, the way you might read a face.

Their hands are long and fine-fingered, the pads broad and sensitive, and they lay them flat against iron and listen with their palms. When a bearing begins to run hot, a keeper knows before any gauge does. The needle pinned at its stop on the stump was never a broken instrument to them. It was a message, and they have answered it in their own way, leaving the stop where it is and tending the thing it was meant to measure.

They live in the corrugated huts and in the cool galleries beneath the pumps, where the rock stays warm from the engines' heat. They sleep in shifts, slung in hammocks of braided vine, their bodies loose and still in a way that makes them hard to notice until one of them turns over and the moss sighs. They eat the fungi they cultivate on the trunks in pale, pulsing tiers, and they eat the frogs from the pools. They do not hunt the large animals with the heavy, deliberate steps, and those animals do not seem to hunt them. The long low calls that answer the engines are not accidents. A keeper can lean over a pool and sing, and the frogs will shift their chorus to match, and the chorus carries the timing of the pumps out over the valleys, so that every creature in the forest knows the beat of the machines and lives inside it.

The machines are not wholly theirs. Some were built once by hands that had eyes, and the keepers inherited them the way a river inherits its bed. They maintain what was made, but they have also learned to make small things anew. A valve is cast from clay and bone. A cable is laid in moss and sealed with a resin that hardens to the color of old ivory. Their repairs are soft-edged and grown over the iron, so that in their hands the boundary between the built and the living blurs and then disappears. Some of the fungi that climb the towers were seeded on purpose, to close the joints and to pulse with the flow of water through the pipes. When you press your palm to one and feel it breathing, you are feeling a seam that a keeper once sealed with its own living tissue.

The pulse is the thing to understand. The keepers do not simply tend this land. They tune it. The hum that rises through the floor is the sound of many of them singing together, and the pumps are kept in step with it, the fungi and frogs and winged things all bending their rhythms to match. The cloud breathes with the engines because the keepers have learned that water must be lifted in certain cycles, and the weather is a rhythm they set in motion long ago and still maintain. The rain you feel on your neck is not an accident of climate. It is a schedule, kept by many throats in the dark.

Why the dark? Perhaps it was here first, and the keepers arrived in it and found it suited them. Perhaps light would scatter the sound-map they live inside, flattening the echoes that tell them where the cistern cracks and where the cliff stands and where the next valve is failing. Perhaps the dark is not something they endure but something they made, one more thing kept in balance: the wet kept wet, the dark kept dark, and both held steady by the slow grinding machinery and the voices that never stop.

As you move through the forest the hum shifts. A call comes from far off, from a pool you have not reached, and it answers the pumps with a precision that is almost courteous. Somewhere below, a throat that you cannot see is turning toward you, reading the sound of your footsteps, the pitch of your breath, the warmth of your body against the cold stone. It is not hostile. It is listening, the way everything here listens, to learn what you are and whether you belong to the rhythm, and it is patient, because the keepers have been patient for longer than the stone has been worn smooth.
