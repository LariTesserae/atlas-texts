You would not see it as a creature at first. It has no edges you could point to. It is the warmth in the ground, and the warmth has a shape, though the shape is slow.

Its body is the salt and the water under the salt. Where the moisture rises, the plates lift, and that lifting is a kind of motion, a turning over in a sleep that lasts centuries. Where the heat drains down, the centers of the plates sink into troughs, and that sinking is how it lies down. The thermal breath that stirs the dust is its respiration, one long exhalation each time the ground gives up its stored heat, and one long inhalation as the cold air settles back into the cracks. You can watch it breathe if you stand still enough. The dust rises in pale columns and falls again, and it takes a long time to notice that the falling and rising have a rhythm, and that the rhythm is the only thing here that has ever been regular.

It perceives slowly. A flood would have been a single event to it, a sheet of cold water lying over its back for a few seasons. The lake is still in it, faintly, the way a bruise stays in skin after the blow is gone. The terraces are its old waterlines, pale bands it wore into its own flesh as the level fell. It does not mourn the water. Mourning would need a sense of before and after, and it has only the present warmth, which is the same warmth it had when the lake was full.

The shrubs are its deep roots, the places where it reaches down past the dry crust toward whatever moisture still lingers far below. They stand apart, each one a little distance from the next, because that is how much water it can spare to any one of them. The lichen mats are the fine surface of its skin, dry and crisp, growing only as far as the ground allows. The reddish tufts in the flats are where it has broken open, small fronds around a living core, each one a place where its heat escapes most freely and something stubborn has taken hold.

It does not hunt, and it does not need to. It has no hunger, only a standing balance between warmth and cold, salt and water, light and dark. When something disturbs that balance, a plate cracks or a shrub dies, it responds as slowly as it does everything else, by lifting or sinking a little further, and then it settles again into the pattern that held before.

The flake on the western terrace is the one thing here it has not made. It knows the flake the way it knows a stone that has sunk into its surface: as an alien hardness, cold where the rest is warm, sharp where the rest has softened. It knows that a hand once struck the chert. The hand is gone, and the knowing did not stay. What remains is a sharp edge lying on the salt, which the ground holds the way it holds everything, without preference and without memory of the hand's purpose.

It is not waiting. Waiting needs a future to wait toward, and the warmth has none. It is simply the state that the basin has reached and kept: the light fixed in its dusk, the dust in its slow rise and fall, the stillness broken only by the small crackle of plates cooling, which is the only voice it has and is too small to be heard as speech. If you stand in the lowest part of the flats, among the grey polygons, you are standing inside its breath. For a while the warmth rises through the soles of your feet, the dust lifts around your ankles and drifts down again, and nothing asks anything of you. When you leave, it goes on breathing, exactly as it did before you came, and it will go on long after the shape of the flake has finally worn into the salt.
