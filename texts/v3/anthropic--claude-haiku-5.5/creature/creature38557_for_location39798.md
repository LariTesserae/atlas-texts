**What Lives in the Cirque**

The Cirque is not a home for its animals so much as a body they belong to. Something lies here, folded into the mountain, and the bowl is its face turned up toward a sky it has stopped looking at.

You can see the shape if you stand on the floor and let your eyes lose their edges. The walls curve like a cupped hand, or like a head bowed into two arms. The cracks run down the faces like the lines of a palm, or the creases of closed lids. The rim is a brow. The clouds that rest on it are not weather but a lid, drawn down and kept there. The stream that leaves through the notch is the only place where the thing's grief finds an exit, and it is so thin that it is almost lost under the moss before it reaches the lower slope.

The rock weeps because it is the thing that weeps. It has done so for longer than anything has been counted, and it does not know how to stop. The water runs through every crack in the cliffs, and the cliffs, when you press a thumb into them, give and fill slowly, the way a face gives under a hand that has held it too long. The moss on the stone is not a covering. It is the thing's skin, grown thick and cold from lying still.

It is not asleep. Sleep moves. It is holding one position, the way a grieving body holds its shoulders when it has forgotten how to put them down, and the animals are the small motions it has not been able to finish.

Each animal is one of those motions, caught and kept. The salamander on the wet stone turns its head toward the same point every time you pass, with the same bead of water hanging from its snout. The bead does not fall, because the gesture that would drop it is not complete. The frond that has been unrolling for as long as anyone could tell is an attention that keeps turning toward something and never arrives. The tallest tree on the far slope has a crown that never grows and never sheds, because growing and shedding both imply that something is passing, and the thing here has stopped letting anything pass. The drip at the leaf tip hangs and hangs, and when it falls, another gathers in exactly the same place, because the thing is refusing to let the moment end, and the water is what the refusal looks like when it is made of matter.

The frogs, packed so tightly that their bodies touch, are closeness without speech. The moths folded like leaves on every frond are hands held shut. The birds in their tiers, shifting without flying, are the thing's restlessness, pressed down so firmly that it can only move in place. Their murmur is the sound of something turning over in its sleep without waking. The insect hum is the pressure of the whole body breathing through a closed throat. Even the silence is part of it. There is so much life here and almost none of it speaks, because the thing that holds the life does not have a voice, only the water, and the water makes no words.

The rot in the fallen trunks is the thing's old memory decomposing into warmth. The mats of moss that are faintly warm in the sunniest-seeming corner are where the memory is still giving off heat, a slow exhale from something that was once alive in a different way.

And then there are the birds with the stones.

They are the only thing in the Cirque that does anything, the only motion that is not a held gesture but a repeated one. They carry pale nuts to the crevices in the bark, lift smooth stones, and strike the nuts open. Their practice has worn grooves into the rock at their favored perches, and the grooves are filled with moss, and the moss is old. This is the thing's own habit, the one thing it still does without thinking, the way a person's hands keep folding a cloth long after the mind has wandered off. It is the last working motion of a body that has otherwise settled into grief, and it is so faithful that it has outlasted the reason for it. The birds have inherited a gesture from something that no longer remembers why it made it.

If you stay long enough, the thing becomes aware of you the way a sleeper is aware of a noise in the next room: not as a sound, only as a pressure, a faint change in how the mist sits. The moss swallows your footsteps. The drip from the leaves falls a little more slowly for a moment, then resumes. The clouds on the rim do not move. The salamander does not turn to look at you. It has already turned toward the place it has always been looking, and it stays there, and the Cirque closes over your passing as the mist does, leaving no edge where you were.

It waits, not for anything to arrive, but for the moment it is holding to finally become the past. It has not yet found a way to let it go, and so the water keeps running through the cracks, the stone keeps weeping, and everything that lives in the bowl keeps the shape of the last thing it did before the weeping began.
