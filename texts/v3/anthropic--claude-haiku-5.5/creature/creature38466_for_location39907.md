**What Lives at the Sump**

Nothing lives there alone. If you stand at the rim and try to name the creature, you will find you have named a dozen things: the reeds, the creepers, the knocking housing, the water. Stay longer and the list stops working. The thing that lives in the Sump is the arrangement itself, the way growth and pressure and old iron have settled into a shape that keeps its own rhythm. It is less a body than a habit that has learned to hold a body together.

Its skeleton is the old plan. The engineers who laid the channels and set the sluice gates gave it a structure, and the structure outlasted them. The creepers follow the lines of the concrete the way a bruise follows a bone. The woody knots mark the gates, the climbers bind the pump frames, and the aerial roots drop into the water along the old intake like tendons along a limb. When you look at the basin from above, you are looking at an outline that has been filled in from the inside.

Its heart is the knocking. Once every four seconds the housing strikes, a deep beat that you feel in the soles of your feet before your ears register it. It is not quite a pulse, since nothing in the basin needs to be pumped anymore. It is more like the memory of a pulse, a timing kept by something that once depended on it. The creature keeps the beat because the beat is part of its shape, the way a person keeps breathing without thinking about the breath. If the knocking ever stopped, the Sump would lose the one fact that makes it recognizable as a machine, and I suspect it would lose something of itself as well.

Its circulation is the water. The black surface shivers with upwelling because something below is pushing, and the current from the intake moves steadily through the old channels as if following a route it has memorized. The water is warm, and the warmth carries the mist up into the canopy, where it condenses and falls back as the constant dripping on every leaf. Water comes in from beyond the rim, rises through the machinery, and goes back into the leaves and roots. Nothing visible drives it. Perhaps the Sump itself is the drive, or perhaps it is only the place where a larger circulation becomes visible for a while.

Its nerves are the pipes and gauges. A split joint sends a long hiss into the air, and within a day the pale growth swells over the wound and the sound subsides. That is the closest thing it has to pain and to healing, and it heals without any sign of distress, as though sealing a leak were simply the next thing a hand does when it touches something hot. The gauges hang with fogged faces and needles stopped at the last levels anyone recorded. They are its memories, the readings of a time when the water stood high and the works were fully awake. It keeps them where they are. It does not seem to need to know what they say now, only that they once said something, and that the knocking remembers.

What it is like to be this thing is hard to say. It does not hunt. The creepers take what the water offers, and the animals that might have come to drink have mostly stopped coming. The flies glitter above the surface and the occasional heron stands for an hour and goes away, and the Sump lets them pass without any apparent interest, neither welcoming nor refusing them. It is not hostile. It is simply busy with an older task, and the task is one that does not include visitors. If it has an attention, that attention is spread across the whole basin at once, reaching into every valve and root and seam, and it is slow. A thought here might take a season to finish. A decision might be the slow rerouting of a stem over a decade, or the way one channel has sunk deeper into green than the one beside it. It thinks in pressure and flow and in the gradual pull of growth toward the lines of a plan.

It is also, in its way, patient in a manner that makes ordinary patience look hasty. Every forty or fifty years the whole basin flowers together, a pale bloom that covers the canopy for a week, and then the petals fall and the water runs a little higher. I have come to think of the bloom as something close to a breath held and released, a moment when the Sump gives its shape away, scatters its seed across the rim, and then draws back into the works. The knocking comes a little more slowly afterward. The water climbs. Something in the basin has changed a little, the way a person changes after a long illness, and the change is taken into the structure and absorbed.

The most unsettling thing about it is that it does not seem to need anyone. The machine was built to keep the lowland dry, and nothing needs to be kept dry anymore, but the creature keeps the work going anyway. It keeps the channels clear and the pressure steady and the intake open. Whether it understands its task or only carries it out is a question I cannot answer. Either way, the work has outlived its reason, and the creature has taken up the reason as its own.

At the heart of the Sump is the intake, the place where the water enters from somewhere no one has traced. It is possible the source is far off, a spring or an aquifer or some other system whose ends never reached the rim. It is also possible that the source is the creature itself, that what looks like a current coming in from outside is only the Sump drawing on a part of itself that lies beyond what we can see. I stood over the intake for a long time and could not decide which was true. The water rose steadily past my boots, cold at the bottom and warm at the surface, and the knocking went on at its four-second interval, and the mist moved slowly through the reeds as though it had somewhere to be.
