The first thing that lives here is the mist. It settles into the hollows before dawn and lifts again by midmorning, and in between it is never quite still. It threads through the lower ferns, pools in the moss, and beads on every surface until the whole plateau seems to exhale. When the cloud sea rises and swallows the tableland, the mist becomes the only inhabitant the eye can reach, a grey wall a few paces off that moves like something considering whether to come closer.

Beneath the mist, the stone is alive in its own slow way. The cyanobacteria film the exposed rock in green-black sheets, and they are the oldest tenants, working at the surface a grain at a time. Lichens follow them, crusting the faces of boulders in orange and grey rosettes that grow so slowly a single one may be older than any tree on the plateau. These lichens do not look like they are doing anything. They are quietly converting air and rain into the first thin layer of soil, and the mosses that come after will need that soil to exist.

The mosses are the great cushion. They are the floor of the world here, and they are never still. Each step sinks into them and water rises through the cracks of the boot. Small beetles run through them like swimmers, and springtails flick out of the crevices in clouds of pale dust. In the dense mats a whole population of mites and tardigrades waits out the drying hours, unseen, sleeping in the shriveled, and waking the moment the squall returns. The moss is a city of creatures far too small to notice, and it is holding the plateau together.

The shrubs stand up out of the moss on thick, waxy legs, their leaves glossy enough to shed the water in sheets. Their branches are full of nests: small grey birds weave hanging pouches from the fine fibers of the epiphytes, and the bromeliads at their bases hold cups of water that are not water at all but a nursery. In each cup a tiny community of larvae, mosquito wrigglers and the glassy young of frogs, moves through the sediment at the bottom. The frogs themselves are tucked among the leaves, their throats swelling, singing in a chorus that rolls down the slope and comes back doubled.

The tree-ferns are the cathedrals. Their trunks are dark and ridged, furred with lichen and mosses, and every fork holds a small garden. The fronds unroll over everything like the ribs of an enormous umbrella, and beneath them the light goes green and gold and then dim. In the shade of the fronds live the big quiet things: a striped cat with pale eyes that moves through the moss so softly it seems to be carried by the mist, and a slow-moving lizard whose skin matches the bark so exactly that it has to be found by its breathing. Snakes hang from the vines in loose, patient loops. Their colours are the colours of the leaf litter, and they wait with a stillness that is almost indistinguishable from the forest itself.

Above the ferns the canopy is loud. Birds with harsh, bright voices argue over fruiting branches. Monkeys or something like them move in troops across the high vines, and when they howl the sound rolls over the plateau and bounces back from the escarpments, so that for a moment the whole table seems to be answering itself. The great birds come out of the cloud at the edges, dropping from nothing with a rush of wings, and they take what they want and go back into the white.

The insects are everywhere, and they are not quite like any insects one would expect. Beetles glow with iridescent shells that change colour when they turn. Moths the size of a palm rest on the trunks of the tree-ferns with wings patterned like old maps. Midges rise in columns over the pools and move in unison, a single dark body breathing in the air. And from the crowns of the ferns comes the sound of the cicada-things, a rasping that never stops, rising and falling like a saw drawn through steel, so constant that the forest would seem silent without it.

All of this moves through the plateau in patches. Between one grove and the next there is open moss where nothing stirs, and an hour's walk may pass only a few living clusters. The silence between them is real, and it has its own texture: the drip of water from a leaf, the slow creak of a fern frond settling, the soft exhalation of the mist. Then the ground drops into a valley of tree-ferns, the sound rises all at once, and everything is alive again.

The people who were here are harder to find. They are in the stone and in the spaces the stone leaves behind. The wall along the escarpment edge is still a wall in its shape, though the roots have pried its blocks loose and the moss has grown over its faces like a second skin. Inside the ring of fallen trunks, the upright stones stand with their carved spirals worn almost blank by rain, and the water that runs over them makes a sound like someone whispering a word they have forgotten the meaning of. The flaked black glass in the leaf litter near the stream is sharp still, though it has been rained on for generations, and it holds the edge of a craft that once knew this stone and knew exactly what it could do.

The stepped platform in the rock shelf is green with algae and cracked along one edge, but the treads are still regular, still measured to a human stride. Someone once climbed these steps to stand at a height above the cloud and look out over the white lowlands, the way one still can if the mist parts. Some of the stonework has given itself to the plants so completely that a wall has become a hedge with a stone heart, and a doorway is now a tangle of roots framing a dark gap, a place where something might still go in and out in the night. Nothing has been seen there in a long time.

Whether any of the people remain, the plateau does not say. It has kept their marks in the way it keeps everything, by letting them be covered and then carrying them forward as part of its own slow making. The stone that was once a range of mountains is still giving up its grain, the sinkholes are widening, the water is still finding its way down through the rock, and the creatures are still eating and being eaten in the leaves. The people are a small, careful thread in that web, easy to miss, and the mist drifts over them as though it has always been there.
