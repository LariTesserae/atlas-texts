**The Ferryman of the Gates**

It does not have a name that anyone has heard, and it has no need of one. The herons call it nothing. The grazers lift their heads when it passes and then lower them again. If it has a word for itself, it is probably a word for weight, or for water, or for the sound a gate makes when it finally gives.

It is tall, though it rarely stands straight. Its body is long and jointed like a heron's, and its legs fold under it when it crouches on the rim of an intake tower, knees higher than its shoulders, long toes gripping the rust-crusted rail. Its skin is the colour of wet basalt, mottled with green where the moss has taken hold, and in the places where the flood has worn it, it shows a pale grey bone-glaze, smooth as river stone. There are plates along its spine and skull that have thickened over a very long life, the way a mountain thickens under its own weight. The plates are the mountain in it. The rest of it is the water.

Along each side of its ribs run a row of narrow slits, and these open and close as it moves. When it is under the surface, they pull the flood through and take what they need from it. When it climbs into the air, they pull in mist and cold wind, and the sound of its breathing is a low, wet whistle that the turbines seem to answer. Its face is not a face so much as a field of small pits along the jaw and brow, dozens of them, each lined with a fine membrane. They read the pressure of the surge, the pull of the current under the sluice, the temperature of water in the deep channels. It can feel a gate beginning to fail before the gate itself knows it.

It keeps the gates.

No one assigned this to it. The machines were built by people who are gone, and the machines were meant to open and close on a schedule that the flood has since broken. The schedule still runs in the old control houses, a hum of relays that no longer connect to anything. But the thing in the towers listens to the water instead. It goes down the stair at the low point of each cycle, when the flood is drawing back and the shallows are thick with stranded fish, and it wades among the pylons with its arms held out at its sides, feeling for the pull. When it finds a gate that has stuck, it braces its feet against the rock and hauls, and the old iron shrieks, and the water rushes through with a sound like a held breath let go. Sometimes it climbs the intake towers and works the wheels by hand, long fingers turning spokes that have not moved in a century. It does this at no fixed hour. It does it when the water asks.

It sleeps in the turbine halls. The deep drone of the wheels, when the surge runs through them, is a sound it seems to need the way other creatures need warmth. It curls on a gantry above the rotors with its back against a cold girder, plates catching the spray, and it lets the vibration move through its bones until its breathing slows to match the wheels. The herons nest in its hair. There is always a flock of them on its shoulders when it wakes, and they lift off in a single clatter when it stands, as if it were a branch that had decided to move.

It leaves footprints. This is the thing most people would notice, if there were people to notice. The prints appear on the mudbanks after a surge, long and narrow, with the toes spread wide and a faint webbing between them that closes at the tips. They lead from the water to the stair and up the terraces, sometimes as far as the old waystations. Then, a few hours later, they are gone. The next surge has taken them, or the rain, or the tide that comes up the valley to fill the lowlands again. It is not hiding its path. It is only moving through a place that is always being remade, and the water always takes back what it is given.

It is patient the way the mountains are patient. It will wait a season for a gate to give, if the gate is not yet dangerous. It will stand in the shallows for a whole night with the water at its chest, its jaw-pits open, reading the deep channels for the slow movement of whatever lives below, the great swell that rolls the lilies. Once, the thing in the deep came up beside it, and it did not move, and the two of them stayed in the dark water together for an hour while the surface froze over with mist. Neither of them spoke. Then the swell rolled on, and it climbed the stair again, and the herons came down to its shoulders.

It is also restless. It cannot stand still in the flood for long. It is always at a gate or on a ledge or in a channel, testing the cold and the weight of the water, moving up and down the stair as the surge comes and goes. When it sleeps, its feet twitch with the rhythm of the wheels. When the sluices groan at night and the whole valley shudders, it is awake before the sound has finished, already on its feet, already listening.

Whether it is the last thing the old works left behind, or something the water grew in answer to them, is a question it does not ask. The gates still open. The land still drowns and rises. Somewhere under the shallows the swell moves, and a long grey shape climbs out onto a ledge in the first light, shaking the flood from its plates, and goes to see what the water needs today.
