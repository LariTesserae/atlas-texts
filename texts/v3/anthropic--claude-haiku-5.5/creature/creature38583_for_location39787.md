# The People of the Basin

They call themselves by a word that roughly means *those who stay in the breath*. They don't think of themselves as unusual. Ask one where they come from and they will point at the floor.

## Bodies and Senses

They are lean and long-limbed, with long hands and narrow eyes that have learned to squint against the white shaft of light without frowning. Each morning they rub a pale clay-oil into their skin, and over years it builds a faint luster, so that people who have lived here a long time seem to shine a little in the sun, the way the leaves do. In the grey months, when the vents exhale silt, they wrap cloth over their mouths and noses, and the children learn to talk through the cloth without losing their words.

Their hearing is trained, and this is the most striking thing about them. A child in the basin learns early to take the drone apart into layers: the wind's low note, the rattle of reeds, the sharp crack of pods, the hiss of the vents, the bells. By adulthood a person can name a pod's split from across the basin, track a cart by its wheel-grind through three walls, or tell which terrace a bell comes from with their eyes closed. Their attention is always partly outward, always sorting. A person who has been asleep for a while wakes at the first change in the wind.

Because volume is lost in the basin's roar, they speak forward and low, with the mouth doing the work instead of the chest. Their language is full of hard, percussive consonants, half-clicks borrowed from seed-pods, and sustained vowels that can be held over the drone. Their names follow the same logic: Tkarun, Ossel, Hai-dennet, Yuvu-the-Second. Speech that carries is considered a form of courtesy.

## The Order of the Breath

Society turns around the vents. At the top of the social order are the **bellows-keepers**, sometimes called the lungs. They pump the vent-bellows by hand in long shifts, clear the clay chimneys, and read the pressure in the hollows. They know the basin's rhythm better than anyone, and their word on the cycle is law, though they are not rulers in any obvious sense. They are simply the ones who understand what the room is doing.

Beneath them are the **bell-ringers**, who keep the city's time. Their bells ring at irregular intervals, which looks like noise to outsiders but is in fact a slow code marking the state of the vents, the expected turn of the cycle, and the call to gather or to scatter. A bell-ringer who gets the cadence wrong is a serious matter.

And on the high terraces sit the **listeners**, who tune the humming stones. They touch each stone with a wet palm and wait for its pitch to settle, and they note its drift over the seasons. The listeners are quiet people, and they are often the ones who first notice when the basin is changing.

## The Forty-Day Breath

The people do not count days. They count breaths, which are the full cycles of the vents, roughly forty days each, and their whole year is shaped by them.

The **inhale** is a season of brightness. The vents rise, the pressure builds, the plants open their leaves wide and the oily rivers run fast down the stems. The markets are full and loud, the carts run all day, the bells ring often, and the city feels like an animal stretching. Weddings and trades happen in the inhale. Young people are loud and proud of it.

The **exhale** is a season of stillness. The vents fall, the leaves close, the resin-fog thickens in the hollows, and the silt comes up grey and fine from the cracked plates. People go indoors, cover their faces, and speak softly. The markets shrink to a few stalls. There is more ritual in this season: name-giving, the mending of old tools, and the long work of cleaning the chimneys. The old and the sick are said to die more in the exhale, though nobody quite agrees whether that is cause or coincidence.

Between the two, for a few days, the basin holds its breath. That pause, when the vents hang at neither rising nor falling, is the most uneasy time, and the people are quietest then. They call it *the held note*, and they do not like to say much while it lasts.

## The Guests

The resin-fog pools in low hollows and sometimes gathers into slow, drifting forms that look almost like animals: a long-necked shape, a shape with too many limbs, something like a bird with its wings folded. They appear and dissolve in minutes, and nobody has settled whether they are alive.

The people call them **guests** and treat them with a mixture of courtesy and distance. Children chase them across the silt and are scolded for it. Adults leave small offerings of resin in the hollows, pale knots of it set on flat stones, and they do not touch the forms. When a guest drifts into a house, the household goes quiet and waits for it to leave. Nobody has ever been hurt by one that anyone knows of. Nobody has ever been able to explain what one wants.

Other creatures share the basin with them, but seldom make themselves heard. The grey burrowing thing surfaces at dusk and is eaten by some and left alone by others. The hook-beaked bird perches on the tallest pods and screams once before it flies, and the people say that when it screams, the day has turned.

## The Silence They Fear

The people have a saying, *the room must breathe or we are gone*, and it is not a metaphor. Their oldest story, told to children before they can walk, concerns a breath that did not return. In that story the vents fell and did not rise, the plants stayed closed, the bells went silent, and the silence went on so long that people forgot the word for it. Nobody in the story says what happened next. The children are expected to finish the story themselves.

So silence, to them, is not peace. Born here, they cannot sleep without the drone. They leave windows open to the wind or hum under their breath. Outsiders who stay too long report that the basin's noise seems to sit just behind the ears, and that after a few weeks the silence of an empty room feels like a missing organ. The people find this amusing and a little sad. They know what it is to be without the breath, and they would rather hear the pods crack than be sure of anything.

## Evening

At dusk, when the shaft of light fades to amber and the vent-chimneys begin to glow, you can find an old bellows-keeper sitting on a terrace above the city, one palm pressed flat to a humming stone. She has been doing this for most of her life. She doesn't look at the sky or the walls. She listens to the room, the long drone of the wind, the hiss beneath it, the bells below, and under all of it the slow, deep swell that only the lungs and listeners can feel.

When she takes her hand away, she says, quietly enough that only the stone can hear it, that the breath is turning. Then she stands, stretches her long back, and climbs down to the city, where the doors are already banging and the carts are grinding over the bone-pale roads, and where everyone is talking at once, forward and low, in a language made to carry over the noise.
