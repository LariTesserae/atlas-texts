**The Ones Who Stay**

The people of Vell Hollow are mostly the children and grandchildren of people who were told, forty years ago, that the water would be managed. Few newcomers arrive, and the ones who do are usually barge hands who stay past their contracts, or widows who came to settle an estate and never left. The town takes them in without fuss. It has no use for strangers, but it has plenty of work.

They dress in oilcloth and wool that never fully dries. Coats are patched at the elbows and knees, where they kneel in the trenches, and the cloth smells of tar, machine oil, and the mineral sourness of standing water. Their hands are the most visible sign of the work: black in the creases, split at the knuckles, the nails often broken or rimmed with rust that no scrubbing removes. Most people here have a particular callus, a hard ridge across the palm where a wrench handle sits, and they recognize each other by it.

**Bodies adapted to the light**

Without sharp shadows, people here have learned to judge distance by sound and texture instead of by the edges of things. A woman walking the main road will tell you how far the next lamp is by the pitch of the pump behind it. Children learn to find the curb by the drip of the gutter. Their eyes are wide and slow to narrow, and in the rare bright afternoon, when the cloud thins and a real edge of light slices across the square, people squint and turn away and some of them cry without knowing why.

Their skin is pale and faintly cool to the touch, and it holds moisture the way the walls do. Coughs are common, a thick wet cough that everyone has and no one remarks on. Hearing is sharper than in other towns. Older residents can name a pump by its stroke, and when a station falters, they wake before the alarm, sitting up in bed with their heads tilted as though listening for a voice that has stopped.

**The crews**

The crews are the town's aristocracy and its penitents. They climb the towers in pairs, clipped to the galvanized rungs, and they carry buckets of tar, bolt boxes, and clamp sets up the ladders. They know every burst and every weep. They speak to the pumps the way sailors speak to a difficult ship. They name them: Old Grey, Sister, the Cough, the one at the sluice everyone calls Patient because it is always the last to fail. A crew chief will put a palm flat on a housing and hold it there for a long moment, feeling the stroke, before she decides whether it needs attention. Most days it doesn't. She keeps her hand there anyway.

The crews also keep the board in the central station, the chalk grid that shows which pump is working, which is resting, and which is down. People check it the way other towns check the weather. A pump's name appearing in the "down" column brings families to the windows. There is a quiet protocol around it. Nobody writes a name in the down column lightly.

**Sleep and the beat**

Almost everyone here sleeps to the pumping. The stroke works its way into the body until heartbeat and breath settle into a half-second lag behind it, so that when a station is shut for maintenance, people wake in the night with a strange hollowness in the chest, as if something has been removed. Some sleep badly for days after a long stoppage. Children who were born in the hum sometimes cannot sleep in the silence of the outer farms and must be brought back.

Certain residents have a habit that visitors find unsettling. They lean. Not dramatically: a slight forward tilt of the shoulders and head when they stand in the open, angled toward the nearest station. Stand in the square at dusk and you can see most of the town faintly inclined, like grass bending in a wind that isn't blowing. The people do not know they do it, or they know and have decided it isn't worth remarking on.

**What they do with the silence**

There are no animals in Vell Hollow, and the absence has become a kind of shared discipline. People do not keep pets. They do not keep songbirds. Children who ask about dogs are told that dogs don't do well with pumps, which is true enough, and the children accept it, though they often draw dogs in the margins of their schoolbooks: long, soft, impossible creatures standing in fields of white-undersided leaves.

On the still nights, when a station is shut and the hum falters, people gather at their doorsteps and listen. They don't speak. They wait. Old women say the silence has a shape, and that if you hold still long enough the shape begins to hum back. Younger people think this is nonsense. They listen anyway. The vapor thickens over the rooftops, the sodium lamps haze orange, the gutters go on talking to themselves, and everyone waits for the first sound that is not water or metal or wind through leaves, a sound that has never once come.

**The sweetness**

The people don't drink the condensate from the broad-leaved plants, but they know its smell, which gets into the house through open windows in the wet season: faintly sugared, like fruit left too long in a bowl. A few residents, mostly the old and the grieving, sleep with a cup of it on the sill. Those who have drunk it describe a field of pumps stretching to the edge of sight, each stroke rising through the ground like a slow breath, and they wake rested and quiet and a little bereft, as if they have been somewhere they are not allowed to return.

The crews have a saying about this, repeated so often it has worn smooth: *the town keeps the water down, and the water keeps the town.* Nobody can say who first said it, or whether the two halves of that sentence describe a bargain or only a kind of mutual holding. The people of Vell Hollow have stopped asking. They climb the towers, patch the pipes, count the strokes, lean slightly toward the thud in the ground, and keep their hands on the machines long after the repairs are done. They are the ones who stay. Something here has to keep the lake from coming back, and they have decided, one bolt and one bucket of tar at a time, that it will be them.
