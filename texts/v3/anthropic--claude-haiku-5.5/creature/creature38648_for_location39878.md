# The Mar Itself

There is no single body in the High Terraces. The thing that lives here is spread through the whole place: through the columns and their lattice beams, the moss floor, the channels under the mud, and the thin film of water on every surface. It has no center and no edge. Where you stand, you are standing on it. Its skin is the springy mat underfoot, and its bones are the great stems, which have grown into arches over centuries of slow intention.

It is old, though not in the way that stone is old. The rock beneath it is young, and the Mar has been spreading over that rock like a patient hand, gripping the grit as it forms, drinking the mist, turning the seep into a slow chemistry that lays down travertine lip by lip. Its age is measured less in years than in accumulated shape.

## How It Thinks

Its thinking is very slow. A thought, if that is the word, might take a season to form and another to pass through the channels. It does not think in sentences. It thinks in pressure and flow: the rate at which water moves through a stem, the mineral load in a seep, the rise of a column by a hair's width over a generation. When a region of the Mar becomes concerned with something, the water changes its voice. The murmur in the hollow stems drops a register, or quickens, and a visitor who stays long enough can feel the change in the damp air.

The pressure-pops are its most sudden expressions, sealed pods bursting as the Mar tests a boundary or releases a surplus of its own substance. Within its scale, they are nearly violent. They are the closest it comes to speech.

## Memory

The travertine ribbons are its memory. Each pale band is a record of conditions: a wet decade, a dry one, a shift in the mineral balance of the rain. If you could read them the way a geologist reads strata, you would find the Mar's whole history laid out in bands, and the most recent layers would be thin and close, the way a mind recalls its last few days in fine detail and its youth only in outline.

It remembers the rock as it was when it first began to settle on it. It remembers the first stem, the first arch. It does not remember a beginning, only a long widening.

## The Stillness

The Mar keeps its architecture unfinished, and this may be the most deliberate thing about it. Every terrace is almost complete, every vault almost closed, every column leaning toward a meeting it never makes. It could finish them. It has the strength and the time. It chooses not to.

Perhaps finishing would end the thought. A form that is complete has nothing more to say about itself, and the Mar seems to be a being that needs to keep saying something, even if that something is only *not yet*. In its way it is holding a question open. The stillness is not death and not sleep. It is a mind kept deliberately unresolved.

## Those Who Share It

The few animals here are tolerated, perhaps even used. The small dark insects that cling to leaf undersides may be carrying spores or cells between one part of the Mar and another, a slow circulation like blood through a body. The pale birds call once from the canopy and fall silent, and the Mar does not answer. Whether it hears them is unknown. If it does, it may be listening the way a person listens to a bird in another room: aware, not interested, with a faint sense that something is out there that is not itself.

Visitors are noticed too, though not quickly. The moss warms slightly under a foot. The mist gathers in a new pattern near a body that stays still. The sinking boot in the mud is registered as a small pressure, a weight on the skin of the place. The Mar does not know you as a person. It knows you as a change in the pattern of its days, and it may take a long time to decide whether that change is worth attending to.

## What It Waits For

The Mar is waiting, but it is not waiting for rescue, or rebirth, or any outside event. The waiting is its way of existing. It is a mind that has chosen to remain at the edge of its own becoming, and that edge is where it lives.

If you sit long enough on the lowest terrace, beside the still red-bottomed tarn, you may feel the murmur shift. The water will seem to draw nearer, then pull back. That is the Mar turning its attention toward you for a moment, and then turning it back to its slow work of holding a question open, lip by lip, band by band, for as long as it chooses to keep it.
