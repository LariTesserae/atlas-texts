The thing that lives in the hollow is not a body that moves through it. It is the stillness the hollow is holding.

You learn its shape slowly, the way you learn a room in the dark. The knobs of bone-coloured crust are its sleeping forms, and the concentric rings on each one are the marks of a pulse that went outward once and then stopped partway. It was reaching for the rim and did not finish reaching. The columns, thin as fingers and clustered like reeds, are the parts of it that listen. Strike one and it rings faintly, and the ringing is the only answer it gives. It does not speak. It only goes on being present in the stone, and a column that rings is a column that has heard you.

Its attention is the pressure under the water. The level in the basin, scored into the rim at the same height all round, is the surface of its held breath. Seepage comes in from the wall, drains out through some seam nobody has found, and the balance between the two is not a process it performs. It is what the thing is doing when it does nothing else. It has never let the level climb, and it has never let the level fall, because a rising or falling level would mean something was still becoming, and it has finished becoming.

The hanging curtains along the wall are its hair, or what passes for hair in a body made of lime. Each drop that reaches the end of a curtain is held there, lensed, and that is the nearest it comes to keeping anything for itself. The bands of ochre, rust and cream are years it has not lived through in any way that ended. They are layered, not counted.

The cairns are its handholds on the living. Whoever stacked those flat stones without mortar was, for a while, in conversation with it. The stones lean in the direction it leans, and some have fallen and been rebuilt by hands it no longer feels as warm. When the crust begins to cement a fallen heap, it takes the heap in as it takes in everything, gently, and the cairn becomes another knob. The flint that lies half-buried has not been lost to it. It has been received.

The scratched row, the crossing lines and the circle on the rock by the largest cairn are the nearest thing to a word it has. A visitor once pressed a hand or a tool against the stone and marked a sequence of what they knew: count, cross, return. The weathering has softened those marks into seams, and the seepage has run through them until the thing can no longer tell whether the marks were made or grew there. That confusion is part of how it knows them. Nothing made by hand is wholly separate from the stone for long.

The pale creatures that move across the wet crust are the only parts of it that travel. They are slow, blind, and they carry nothing. When one vanishes into a seam, it has gone back into the hollow's body to be still with the rest. They are its restlessness, if it has any, and it allows them the smallest freedom: to move a little before the crust closes over the path.

It does not know you are there. It knows only that the pressure has shifted, a little warmth on the rim, a drop displaced from the surface, a column struck and ringing and fading. Then it settles again, because settling is what it is. If you stay long enough it begins to hold you too, the way it holds the cairns and the knobs and the hung drops of the curtains, as one more thing pressed into the crowd with no gap to spare. Your breath slows to the drip. Your hand goes cold and clammy and then stops feeling like yours. Your outline in the water becomes a pale blur, like the bottom of the basin, and you are, for as long as you remain, part of the level that does not rise or fall.

When you leave, it lets you go without grief, because it has no capacity for loss. It keeps the place as you found it, and you leave it as it is.
