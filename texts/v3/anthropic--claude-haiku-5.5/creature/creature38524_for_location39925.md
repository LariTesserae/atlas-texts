# Who Lives on the Lattice Flats

Nothing walks here, but something is always living here, and it is spread so thin across the tiling that it's easy to miss. The lives on the flats are slow, wet, and patient, and they are bound to the cells by the same heat that builds the ridges.

## The Mats

Most of what lives here is the rust-colored crust that fills the cell centers. From a ridge top it looks like a stain, a warm brown bloom on the pale floor. Up close it is a skin of dense, wrinkled tissue cracked into polygons, and the cracks echo the larger hexagons so exactly that a botanist might suspect the mats of copying the ridges. They do not copy. They fill the space the convection leaves them, and they grow as far as the brine lets them.

Each mat is a colony of many small organisms bound in a mucilage that holds the thin condensate film against the surface. At night, when the fog settles and the lowest parts of the cell hold a fingernail's depth of water, the mats swell and lift, their edges curling up like the lips of drying leaves. By dawn they are supple and dark, and they hum faintly with the chemistry of growth. Through the day they shrink and crack, and the cracks deepen until the next night's dew seals them.

They are not gentle. The fastest-spreading mats secrete a compound that dissolves the edges of their neighbors, and where two colonies meet there is a narrow zone of bare, pitted stone, a frontier that moves a few centimeters each season. The mats do not seem to mind the loss. Their strategy is to take everything the brine offers, as quickly as they can, and to let the next storm decide what remains.

## The Sponges

Scattered through the wetter pockets near the ridge bases are the sponges, and they are the opposite of the mats in every way that matters. Where the mats spread, the sponges accumulate. Each one is roughly the size of a fist, translucent, with walls like thin amber glass. Inside, the water they've hoarded catches the light and throws it back in soft, shifting caustics across the crust around them.

The sponges are slow to the point of seeming unhurried in a way that the mats never are. They take decades to double. Their walls are built in layers, each one laid down after a storm or a long dry season, so that a large sponge is a kind of archive. Cut one open, and the rings tell you how many rains it has survived and how dry each year was. Their structure is their memory, and it hardens with age, thickening against the mats that try to creep up their flanks.

When a mat gets too close, the sponge answers. It thickens its outer wall and pulls its condensate inward, starving the threatening colony of the brine it needs. The mat retreats. In a few seasons the sponge's shadow lengthens, and the mat cannot recover. The two have been doing this for longer than anyone has measured, and the boundary between them is one of the most stable things on the flats.

## The Threads

In the deepest hollows, where the fog lingers longest, green-black filaments grow in tangled skeins across the surface. They are the most sensitive life on the plain, and the first to respond to rain. Within an hour of the storm's first drops, the threads extend, lifting toward the falling water, and they stay extended until the dry afternoon pulls them back into tight, dark coils. Walk through a thread bed at dusk and the ground seems to breathe.

The threads take little and give a great deal. Their filaments bind loose mineral grains into the surface, and in places they have built thin, crumbling mats that the lichens later colonize. They are the first layer in a sequence of succession that the flats have been running for as long as the convection has been running, and though they are small and fragile, they are the reason much of the stable ground exists at all.

## The Ridges

The fins themselves are not alive in any ordinary sense. They are mineral and they grow by crystallization, not by metabolism. Yet they have a kind of life, in the way a coral reef has a life. Their growing lips are wet and slurried, and they tick as they cool. They shift their positions as the heat field moves, so the walls are always a little different from the walls of a decade before. They are the skeleton of the whole arrangement, and everything else grows in the spaces they define.

## The Whole

If there is a single inhabitant of the Lattice Flats, it is the arrangement itself. The mats, the sponges, the threads, and the ridges are not separate organisms sharing a place. They are the parts of one process, and that process is the heat moving upward through the crust. The heat drives the brine, the brine builds the ridges, the ridges shape the cells, the cells hold the water, and the water feeds the living things that in turn shape the chemistry of the stone. Every part depends on every other, and the loop has no beginning or end.

The mats are the most numerous, the sponges the most patient, the threads the most sensitive. But the flats live in the way a weather system lives, through its circulation, and its body is the entire plain: the haze that hangs at spire height, the fog that drowns the ridge bases at night, the wind that hums along the fins in the evening. Walk out at dawn and you are walking through the lungs and skin of something that has been breathing since the first sun warmed the first crust, and that is still growing, slowly, one cell at a time.
