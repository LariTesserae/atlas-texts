# The Tenders

They are small, and there are not many of them in any one place, though there are more than you would expect. Look closely at the mud on the cistern banks and you will find their tracks: a line of paired dents, then a drag mark where a body was pulled through, then a scatter of tiny round impressions like dropped seed. That is the closest most visitors come to meeting them.

Their bodies are low and flattened, about the size of a hand, with a carapace the same wet-charcoal color as the silt they live in. The shell is not hard. It has the give of a healed scar, ridged in fine concentric lines that match the growth rings of the terraces. Along the sides run a dozen or so jointed limbs, short and many-segmented, each ending in a tip that is soft and grainy, like a brush worn to the bristles. They walk the way a clock mechanism would walk if it had wanted to be an animal: a little forward, a pause, a little more.

They have no visible eyes. Instead the front of each body is marked with a cluster of pale pits, the same size and spacing as the pits on the lattice ribs. Whether these sense light or only moisture is not known. They seem to orient toward the afternoon sun, though, and they freeze when the cloud closes over it, as if something in them had been waiting for the beams and then lost them.

## Where They Go

By day the Tenders stay under the silt. The fill is loose and warm there, and the water that seeps down through it keeps them damp. You can see where they have been by the small craters that open in the mud after a rain, and by the faint humming that some visitors report from the troughs at midday. They do not come out in the sun. Their bodies dry too quickly.

At dusk, when the cloud grows thick and the lattice starts to drip more heavily, they emerge. They come up along the channels in loose lines, and they move with the water. Their work is cleaning, and their whole rhythm is built around it.

They scrape the pits of the ribs. Each one is a small bowl, and over a season it fills with grit and dead moss and the pale slime that the epiphytes shed. The Tenders reach into the bowls with their brush-tipped limbs and draw the debris out in careful curls, then deposit it on the banks, where it becomes new silt. This is why the mud is so fine and so rich. It is not weathered rock. It is what has been cleared from the lattice, cycled back into the ground and cycled out again.

They also keep the channels open. Most of the blocked passages you find in the morning were cleared the night before, or, more often, were left alone on purpose, so that the water would find a new route and deepen a new gully. The Tenders do not seem to dig in the way that machines dig. They guide. They pack silt into one passage and sweep another clear, and over many nights the water learns where to run. The terraces are not carved so much as persuaded.

## The Pump

They tend the pump, too, though this is the strangest part of their work and the part nobody can fully explain. Their trails lead to the pump house at the center of the basin, and there is evidence they climb the riveted walls through the vines. Some of the dial-faces are scraped clean in arcs that could only have been made by many small limbs working together. A valve that has been fused shut for years will sometimes, after a night of heavy rain, be found standing open, its mineral crust scraped away in a neat crescent.

The Tenders produce a thin oily secretion from pores along their undersides. It is clear, faintly sweet, and it smells of cane. Where it has been left on the flywheel's bearings, the brass is dull and unscored, and the wheel turns with the slow, deliberate patience that visitors notice. The pump's rhythm, the four-or-five-minute turn that you hear as a thudding under the drip, may not be a mechanical rhythm at all. It may be a shared one: the Tenders moving in some pattern that the machine has been drawn into.

Nobody knows where the water goes after it leaves the rim. The Tenders do not seem to care. Their concern ends at the edge of the cisterns and the mouths of the pipes. What happens beyond that is outside the world they have been shaped to keep.

## Their Voices

The tree frog's two-note cry from the cisterns is not a frog's. Listen for it at dusk, when the Tenders are moving, and you will hear it come from the mud along the banks, from a dozen points at once. Each cry is short and rising, then falling, and it repeats for a minute and stops. It is the sound they make when a pit has been cleared, or when a channel has been completed, or when a gap in the lattice has been filled with a new rib. It is not a warning or a call to mate. It is closer to a tally, a way of marking that the work is done in this section and the next can begin.

Several of the cries can overlap, and when they do the rhythm changes. Visitors who stay late sometimes swear the frogs have started answering each other. The more likely explanation is that the Tenders are coordinating, matching their clearing to the pace of the water, so that no pit is left full for too long and no channel is left dry. The sound is the sound of a very large job being divided among many small hands.

## What They Are Making

It would be easy to say the Tenders maintain the basin, and they do. But the basin is not finished, and the Tenders do not seem to be finishing it. The new ribs that appear at the edges of the terraces are not built by any visible hand, but the Tenders are always there in the mornings, in the mud beneath the extrusions, and their trails run out across the fresh stone as if following the growth. The moss that has taken the grid's exact pattern sits where their trails were densest the season before. The channels that open and close in a single night are their doing, though whether they intend the result or only respond to the water is not clear.

What seems clearest is that they keep something going. The lattice gathers and the cisterns fill and the pump turns, and none of it would continue without the Tenders cleaning the pits, guiding the water, oiling the wheel, and marking each completed task with that rising, falling cry. They are the maintenance of a process that has not yet decided what it is for.

Some visitors find them unsettling because they seem so content. They do not hurry. They do not attempt to leave. When a Tender is lifted from the mud and set on the cool stone of the rim, it does not struggle. It lies still for a few minutes, its pale pits turned toward the sun. Then, when the cloud has closed again and the dripping begins, it turns, finds the nearest channel, and goes back down into the basin to finish what it was doing.
