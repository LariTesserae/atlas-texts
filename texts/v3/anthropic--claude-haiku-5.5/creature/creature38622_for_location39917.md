**Who Lives on the Shelf of Breathing Stone**

Start with the stone, because the stone is the first thing that breathes here.

The rock is vesicular, full of gas-bubble pockets from some old fire, and its pores are open to the damp air. At dawn, when the shelves are cold and the mist is thick, the pores draw moisture in. By midday, as the sun warms the faces, they loosen and exhale: a faint warm sweetness rising from the cracks, a shimmer over the crust. At dusk the stone sighs out the last of its heat and the air above each terrace bends and wavers. You do not hear this. You notice it the way you notice someone in the next room has stopped moving. The shelves are not alive in the sense that the frogs are alive, but they keep a rhythm, and everything else on them has learned to keep time with it.

Beneath the stone's skin, in the hot pools and the runnels where the springs spread thin, live the oldest tenants: mats of microbes, orange and green and a purple so dark it is nearly black, layered like the pages of a book that is still being written. They eat sulfur and breathe gas and make their living from the same heat that would boil a human alive. They are the first to colonize a fresh lava scar and the last to leave a dying pool. Their slime holds the pale crusts together, and the crusts crack into polygons under the sun, and the cracks are where the mosses find purchase. Nothing else on the terrace would exist without them.

Then the moss, sponge-thick and sagging, holding water the way a body holds blood. Then the lichens, gray-green and rust-orange, growing a few millimeters a year across the bare faces and furring the pale bark of the high, twisted trees. The lichen is a partnership, a fungus and an alga that became one organism and never looked back. The whole upper forest is built on such partnerships. The trees are partnerships with their fungi, which thread through the soil and trade phosphorus for sugar. The bromeliads are partnerships with the tiny red crustaceans and mosquito larvae that live in their cups, feeding the plant with their waste. You could say the forest is a crowd of individuals, but it behaves more like a single slow body that keeps putting out new limbs.

Near the vents, where the stone is warm through your boots, the frilled lizards bask in the sulfur haze. They are the only creatures that seem to wait for you. They turn their heads as you pass, tracking, and then they lower their throat frills and go still, and you realize they are measuring you the way you are measuring them. The vents are their sun. The fumaroles hiss their continuous low note, and the lizards have grown up inside it.

In the canopy, the long-limbed tree dwellers move in silent groups. They do not call. You see them as flickers of dark between the fern fronds, a dozen forms crossing a patch of light in the time it takes to blink. They are patient and watchful, and their silence is a kind of courtesy. Where they have passed, the epiphytes shake loose a few drops that fall through the leaves in a fine rain.

The frogs are everywhere, and they are the most unmistakable voices on the shelf. Their choruses overlap across the pools in rolling layers, deep and bell-like from the lower basins, thin and glassy from the crevices of the high stone. Some of them have made homes inside the iron cylinders, where the water collects and stays warm. They sit in the rusted curves like residents of a building nobody else remembers, and when you walk past, one of them lifts its head from the condensation, considers you, and lowers it again.

In the pools themselves, the mottled fish hang in the current with their mouths open, breathing the slow flow, waiting for the small things that drift down from the ferns. Beneath them moves the larger thing, the one you never see, whose passage shows only as a ring spreading outward on the still water. It has been there longer than the pipes. It will be there after them.

And above the gorges, the great birds turn on the thermals that rise from the sun-warmed stone, wings held rigid, circling so high that they seem to stand still in the cloud while the shelves slide beneath them. Their shadows cross the terraces slowly, one after another, like the hands of a clock. They do not hunt the basin; they survey it. They are the eye that the place keeps open above itself.

Then there is the machinery, which is the strangest tenant of all, because it is not alive, and yet it has been taken in. The pipes provide ledges for ferns and channels for the water that the mosses drink. The valves host sponge-growths and tiny red crabs. The cylinders hold frogs and algae. A spider has built its web across the glass of a gauge, and the condensation has fogged the glass so completely that it no longer reads pressure or temperature, only the weather. The machines were built to take something from the springs. Now they give back shelter, and the springs flow through them and out again, leaving their striped mineral drips on the iron like slow annotations.

What lives on the Shelf of Breathing Stone, then, is not any one thing. It is the arrangement: water moving through rock, heat moving through water, and a dense, patient, overlapping crowd of living things that turn the whole cycle into more of itself. The stone exhales. The mist rises. The lichen eats a little more of the cliff. The frogs sing into the fog. The birds watch from above. And the dead, when they fall, are not wasted. They become soil on the shelves, a bed for the next seedling, a new ledge for the next frog, a fresh bit of rock for the next thread of green to climb.

The place is inhabited less by creatures than by the process of inhabiting itself, which it does without pause, one layer at a time, taking in whatever falls and breathing it back out as life.
