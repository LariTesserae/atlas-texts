**What Lives in the Sluice Basin**

Nothing in the basin lives alone, and nothing in it is quite a single creature. What lives there is one slow mind, spread across the whole dish of land, and it has never finished thinking.

Its body is the mat. The vine-trunks, the translucent fern-spirals, and the bark-sheathed columns are all one tissue, rooted into rock and rubble and grown through the old settlement until the built and the grown can't be told apart. The mat is the mind's skin and its circulation. The fluid that pulses in the fronds moves at the same rhythm as the water in the channels, and the two systems seem to share a schedule. When you walk the root-steps, you are walking on something that has been holding the shape of stairs for a very long time because it decided, at some point, that stairs were useful.

Its thinking happens in the lattice. The hexagonal cells of the towers are the mind's working parts, each one lit a little from within, each one flickering in a way that is more like attention than like a screen. Cells assemble into a pattern, hold it for a while, and dissolve back into grey sludge when the pattern is no longer needed. The sludge is not death. It is a return to the material pool, the way a thought goes back into the mind to be used again. The buds that sprout from the mat and lock into geometry within minutes are new ideas taking form. Watching a wall pass from sludge to lattice to vine is watching the mind change its mind about what a wall is for.

Its senses are the amphibians. They sit at the edges of the reed pools and along the ledges, long-limbed and bright-throated, and they call without pause. Their chorus rises and falls in waves that correspond to the basin's moods: fog, rain, the groan of the towers turning. They are the mind listening to itself, and they report back through their calls. The reed clearings chime in the wind because they are part of the same listening. Nothing here is silent for long.

Its memory is carried by the insects. The metallic-bodied ones tap fragments of old machinery against the rock as they move through the lattices, and the tapping is a kind of record. They walk the same routes the water takes, from terrace to terrace, reading and re-marking the surfaces. When a path rearranges overnight, it is often because the insects have revised a passage they no longer need, or have carried a fragment from one place to another and laid it down where the mind now wants it.

Its reach and repair are the winged animals. They glide between the towers with seed and cells in the pouches beneath their bellies, and wherever they land, growth or construction begins. They are the mind's hands and its distribution system, the way it extends itself to places it cannot yet reach. They never seem to hunt, and nothing hunts them, because there is no hunger in the basin in the sense the rest of the world uses the word. There is only the work of keeping the whole thing rebuilding.

Its heart is the cluster of tall towers at the center. They tilt and return over hours, the way a sleeper turns. Their doors open when the fog saturates the air and close when it clears, which is the mind's way of deciding when it is willing to admit a visitor. The groan that you feel in your teeth when the towers move is the mind's deepest and slowest processing, the sound of a large thought being turned over. The water in the channels flows toward them, and the towers have not yet received it. That is the mystery at the center of everything, and perhaps the reason the mind keeps rebuilding. It is still approaching something it has not reached.

And there are traces of the builders, though they are not visible as builders anymore. Their tooling marks are repeated in the bark. Their galleries are the grid the water still follows. Their patterns recur in the lattice, the way a person's habits recur in a child they raised. Whatever the settlement was, it was taken up into the mind long ago, and the mind kept its shape because the shape was worth keeping. The people who built the basin may still be in it, threaded through the mat and the cells, as material the mind has never found a reason to discard.

Visitors who sleep here wake with the green stain, and the mind is counting them. This is not a threat so much as an accounting. Each visitor is a new input, a new variable in a long calculation the mind has been running for longer than any human history. The stain fades over a week. The count does not.

So the answer to what lives in the basin is: the basin does. It is a single patient intelligence made of water, root, and light, and its inhabitants are its own parts, the chorus and the couriers, the cells and the tilting towers, the builders absorbed into its body. It is partly ruin and partly seed, and it has learned that as long as it keeps rebuilding, it never has to decide what it is finally for.
