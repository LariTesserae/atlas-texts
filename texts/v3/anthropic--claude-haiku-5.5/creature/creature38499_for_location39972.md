# Who Lives in the Sunken Reach

The ones who live here are the rock's own people. They do not rise from it, and they do not come from anywhere else. They are cut into the walls the way the walls are cut into the basin, and their numbers match the openings in the stone, roughly one for every hand's width of face.

## The Cells

Each burrow holds one of them, or sometimes two that sit so close their legs interlace across the threshold. The openings are small and smooth-lipped, lined with a pale film that looks like fired clay. Inside, the cells are kept very clean. The salt dust that drifts in from the columns is swept out daily, and the scraping marks around the entrances are their marks. The cells are not homes in the way a nest or a den is a home. Each one is more like a chamber in a larger body, and whatever lives in it seems to know its place within the whole.

When you look into a cell, a pair of faceted eyes meets yours. The eyes are not like the eyes of hunters. Each one is a cluster of pale, many-sided lenses, and they catch the glow from the high walls and send it back in small, slow flickers. The creature behind them does not startle. It watches you with the patience of something that has watched a great many things pass below it and has not yet found one worth reacting to.

## The Grazers

On the terraces, the herds move in loose drifts, their bodies low and segmented, pale as bone where the salt has dried on them and dark as wet slate where they have just come up from the mist. Their many legs fold tight against their flanks when they rest and unfold in a soft, sequenced ripple when they walk. They graze the mineral films on the stone with fine, comb-like mouthparts, and they do it without hurry. The films regrow at the rate they are eaten. Each grazer takes exactly what it needs and no more, and the herd moves on as a unit, so the pattern of grazing never leaves a bare patch.

They make a faint clicking sound as they feed, and the herd's clicks overlap into a steady murmur. When one of them is disturbed, the others fall silent together, and then the murmur resumes, slightly altered, as if the herd has absorbed the disturbance and continued.

## The Hangers

Under the ledges, where the suspended fog gathers, the hangers cling upside down. They are slender and long-limbed, with delicate, almost translucent carapaces that catch the mist on their backs. Droplets form on their shells and gather into beads, and the hangers draw these beads in through fine channels along their undersides. They drink, and then they release the water back into the fog, which is how the mist keeps its shape. The cycle of drinking and returning is slow and exact. It is the only ongoing process in the reach that involves a living thing working to keep a thing in place.

## The Haulers

The largest of them move among the salt pans, and they are the ones who leave the long grooves in the crust. They are broad and flat, built like shields with a dense ridge of plating along the spine, and their legs are short and powerful. When they cross the pans, they lift their bodies with a deliberate effort, and the salt crust cracks in hexagonal fractures beneath them. They do not dig. They seem to stroke the surface, sweeping the salt into the grooves and leaving the pans smooth in their wake. The grooves form the concentric rings that patterned the floor, and the hauler's movements seem to be part of how the rings were made and maintained.

Their deep, resonant sound, the tone that rises from below and holds for several seconds, comes from them. It is produced by a chamber in the broad body, pressed open and closed in a slow rhythm. It is not a call. You feel it more than you hear it, a pressure in the chest, and then it fades. The hauler that makes it does not look up. It is in the middle of its work, and the sound is simply part of the work.

## The Makers, or the Keepers

The cairns, the shelters, and the tools were not made by the fauna of this moment, or at least not obviously so. The tools are chipped from the same black glass as the terraces, and their edges show the work of hands that understood the grain of the material. The worn hand-axe is different, though. Its edge has rounded from long use, and its handle, bound with chitin, has been polished smooth by many grips. It has been used for a very long time by the same kind of hands that still live here, and it has been used so steadily that its wear has become a record of exactly how much work has been done.

The tallies are harder to read. The rows run along the stone in ordered groups, and some of them are cut more deeply than others. Some of the fauna pass their clawed forelimbs over the marks as they walk, lightly, the way a person might trail a hand along the wall of a familiar corridor. They do not count the marks aloud. They do not seem to be counting anything at all. They seem to be maintaining them.

Perhaps the keepers and the fauna are the same. Perhaps the reach is not a community of creatures who happen to share a space, but one organism whose many parts have each taken a role in holding the whole still: the grazers keeping the films replenished, the hangers returning the fog, the haulers sweeping the pans into rings, the cells keeping their chambers swept, and the tools and cairns and tallies recording the cycle so that it can be repeated exactly. The equilibrium of the place would then not be something imposed on it. It would be the thing the inhabitants do, all of them, all the time, and the stillness is what their work looks like when it is done well.

## At the Boundary

When you climb toward the glowing patches and the dark above, some of them follow. The grazers come first, their legs unfolding behind them in careful ripples, and then the hangers, who drop from the ledges and cling to your path for a while. They stop at a certain height, where the salt crust gives way to bare, unmarked stone. They do not turn back in alarm. They settle, fold their legs, and watch you go, their faceted eyes catching the last of the glow, as if the edge of their own place is a thing they know without needing to be told.

Behind you, the mist keeps rising and falling. The salt rings settle into their pattern. The haulers sweep the pans. The tone rises from below, holds, and fades, and the reach goes on being exactly what it has always been, held in the middle of its own work.
