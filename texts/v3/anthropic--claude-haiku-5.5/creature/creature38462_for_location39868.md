The largest resident of the shelf is the stone itself. Its breathing comes up through the fault lines in slow, cold exhalations, and a visitor learns its rhythms the way one learns the tides. A deep breath lifts a white column out of a crack, rolls it across the rock, and lets it hang and thin. Then the rock draws back, and the air along the seams trembles and goes still. Some breaths are quick and sharp, like a cough that snaps the moss flat. Others are long and patient, lasting most of a minute, and they seem to come from deep inside the mountain. Nothing on the shelf is indifferent to them. Everything else has arranged itself around the breathing: where the moss thickens, where the trees lean, which ledges the animals cross and when.

The climbing animals are the most visible of the living things. They are heavy-shouldered and goat-like, with dense grey-brown coats that shed the cloud's damp in beads, and hooves split into soft, grippy pads that spread on wet basalt. They move in small herds along the ledges, always in single file where the path narrows. They have learned the breathing. Before crossing an open seam, a herd will stop and wait, heads low, ears turned toward the crack, and only move when the exhalation is spent. Once in a while a young one misjudges a long breath and is caught in the rising fog, and the herd gathers at the edge, bleating low, until the cloud thins enough to show it standing there unhurt and blinking. They crop the moss in long, unhurried mouthfuls and leave pale bare patches that the lichen reclaims within a season.

The birds are everywhere, and no single bird holds the place. The long descending cry comes from a slate-backed species that perches on the highest outcrops and calls across the gaps, and its voice bounces off the cliff faces so that it seems to come from several directions at once. Smaller birds rattle and whistle in the scrub, clacking their bills and flicking between the fern fronds. The birds of prey are broad-winged and patient. They ride the updrafts that form where the breath meets warm air, turning slow circles over the open scree, and they drop with little warning when something small breaks cover. Their shadows cross the bright shafts of sunlight and vanish, and the herds below lift their heads and watch the sky until the shadow passes.

The flightless birds live in the undergrowth between the tree clusters. They are round and dark, with stubby wings held out for balance on steep ground, and they run in bursts with their necks stretched forward. They are loud when alarmed, a harsh chattering that starts one flock and sends the rest scattering through the ferns. Lizards with fringed throats lie on the sunlit rock and open their frills to collect condensation, then lap the water from the stone. When the frills close, they look like part of the moss. Small frogs live in the rosette cups of the epiphytes, each cup a tiny pool with its own clutch of tadpoles, and they call from inside the leaves at dusk with a sound like someone tapping a glass.

Predators follow the herds. A long, low, cat-like hunter with a grey pelt moves along the ledges above the goats and waits in the lichen beards where the breezes shake them. It does not chase. It learns the paths and the breathing like the herds do, and it times its approaches so that the cloud hides its movement. The herds know it is there, and their vigilance is part of the rhythm of the place.

The insects are a constant hum in the moss, and when the sun warms the stone, they rise in drifting clouds that glitter in the shafts of light. Beetles with iridescent backs walk the bare rock between the pools. Bats pour out of the fissures at dusk in dense, turning spirals, clicking so loudly that the falls are briefly drowned out, and then they are gone over the edge into the cloud, and the shelf is left to the birds and the breath.

The trees, crowded in their cracks, are shelters for a whole population of their own. The ferns hold insects and small frogs under their fronds, the rosettes hold water and larvae, and the lichen beards hang down into the air and catch whatever drifts past. The trees seem less like individuals than like communities that have grown up around a shared need for shelter, the way a village forms around a well.

The human traces are faint and slowly going under. A cairn of flat slabs stands at the edge of a gap, half collapsed and furred with moss, and beside it a line of carved basins still holds rainwater and a ring of lichen. The basins are the most intimate of the marks, a place where someone once sat and drank or left an offering, and they are now simply another pool the rain fills. No one tends them. The rope bridges sag across the deepest crevices, grey and frayed, and the animals have learned to avoid them. A bone awl turns up in the moss after rain and is returned to the ground.

So the shelf is less a place with inhabitants than a body in which many lives keep their particular rhythms. The stone breathes, the birds call, the herds wait for the exhalation to pass, the hunter waits in the lichen, the frogs tap in their cups, and the moss grows fastest where the breath is strongest. Each presence is small and spaced far from the others, but together they hold the air in a dense, continuous motion, a quiet that is never silent.
