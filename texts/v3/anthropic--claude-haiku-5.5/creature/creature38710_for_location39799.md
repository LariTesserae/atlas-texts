What lives in the bowl is hard to find, because it is spread through the water and the stone rather than gathered into any one body. If you look for an outline, you find the seepage network instead: every sheet down the faces, every bead on the overhangs, every channel that runs between the terraces. Where the water is warmest and has run longest, there is a presence, and it is slow in the way the rock is slow.

It is old, and its age does not feel like a number. Its attention moves at the speed of seepage. A single thought may take a whole morning to cross from one terrace to the next, carried in the film of water over the stone, and it returns the same way. When it wants to notice something, it lets a channel warm a degree, or lets a pool's level rise a hair, and the change travels outward until it reaches the place where the notice is needed. That is how it senses the bowl. It hears itself through the tock of the basins. Each pool has its own pitch, and the presence knows the whole bowl by the pattern of those notes, the way you might know a room by the sound of your own footsteps. An irregularity is news. A drip that falls a fraction late is a small question, and it may take a season to answer.

Its memory is in the rings. The pale concentric lines on the walls are its record, laid down by mineral crusts that grow a few hairs' width a year. Read them from the rim inward and you would be reading a long thought, one turn for each year, the earliest layers worn to nearly nothing by the water that wrote them. The presence does not forget, but it does not hurry to remember either. The rings are not for anyone. They are simply what thinking looks like when the thinker has all the time there is.

The shadows in the pools are its doing, or perhaps they are its way of keeping something. The overhangs hold their shape in the clear water a moment longer than physics would allow, as if the presence were reluctant to let go of a form it has once outlined. It seems to hold on to the things it has seen: a leaf's shape, the set of a vine, the place where a reed has rotted back into the moss. Its idea of a thing is closer to a stain than to a picture, and the stains are kept in the basins until the water changes its mind.

The pale creatures in the moss are not separate from it, though they behave as if they were. They are the few places where its attention reaches dry ground, and they move only when the rock is wet, because that is when the presence is most fully present. They are slow and deliberate, and they go where it sends them: to the edges of the terraces, into the cracks where seepage meets air. The pale insects over the pools in the early mornings are harder to explain. They rise with the mist and settle again, and some say they carry the presence's breath out over the rim. This is a guess. Nothing here is hurried enough to be certain.

It is deliberate about who comes in. The bowl has room for few inhabitants, and the presence keeps the number low not by force but by attention. It notices a creature the way it notices a drop: a change in the sound, a warmth where there should be none, a small rearrangement of the moss. Most visitors pass through the rim and never register at all. The rare creature that does register is met with a slight shift in the water's flow, a channel that opens toward a safe ledge or a curtain of vine that holds its weight. The single sharp note from the vines, the one that breaks the long wind-tone, is the closest thing the presence has to speech. It does not say much. When it does, the sound is clear and brief and then the water takes it back.

The presence does not want anything from the bowl, because wanting would be a kind of hurry, and the bowl has none. It has listened to its own water for longer than the rock has been bare, and it has come to no conclusions it feels the need to state. The rings keep growing, the pools keep their levels, the vines keep leaning the way they have always leaned, and the presence keeps listening, warm and patient in the stone, waiting to hear what the water will say next.
