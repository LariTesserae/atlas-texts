The shoals come first, because you see them first. Long before the vents or the ice, a mass of pale bodies rolls up out of the middle depth and fills your field of view, and the water seems to bend around it. Each creature is a sliver of translucent tissue, ribbed along its length like a leaf held to the light. Through the ribs you can see a faint internal glow, and along each flank runs a row of small lights that pulse in sequence from tail to head. A single fish-length shows perhaps thirty of these lights. A shoal of ten thousand shows them all at once, a slow wave of pale color rippling through the mass, and you realize the wave is what the turning is for. The lights are how they keep their shape. Each body reads the flicker of its neighbors and adjusts by a fraction of a degree, so the whole school bends and folds like a single cloth caught in a current that is not quite there.

They have no visible eyes. Whatever they sense the world with is spread through the flank, and the lights seem to be part of that sensing, a conversation carried on in pulses. When you approach, the nearest bodies flare brighter, then dim, and the flare travels away from you through the shoal faster than you can swim. You are reported, and the report moves on. Nothing panics. They open a lane, close it, and go on turning.

Deeper, and mostly still, are the stalks. They stand on the shelves in crowded forests, tall and narrow, built from a chitin that has taken up so much mineral that it chimes faintly when touched. Their tops bristle with fine filaments that comb the falling particles from the water. Individually they seem simple, almost like reeds. But the stalks do not live individually. Every few seconds the whole colony contracts at once, each stalk folding inward and then unfolding in the same breath, and a wave of light runs along the shelf and up the slope in one motion. The sucking sound that accompanies it is the sound of a thousand filaments drawing water through themselves. Watch long enough and you notice that the contraction does not come from any one stalk. It starts at a place in the forest and spreads, and the place moves, and when a vent thumps somewhere under the rock, the colony tends to answer a moment later, as if the pulse had been passed along the stone to them.

The collars are the strangest of the three. They drift in the middle water between the shoals and the stalks, small disks with fringed rims that open and close like shutters. There is no mouth, no gut you can see. Each collar fans the water through itself, and the particles caught in the fringe are carried inward to a glowing core that brightens as it digests. The collars are never still. They rise and sink with the currents from the vents, and when the warm water columns shimmer up from the floor, they gather in them and turn slowly, feeding on the rain of silt the heat has lifted. Some collars cluster around a stalk forest like moths around a lamp. Others go out alone to the glass slopes and cling there, opening and closing against the rock, leaving faint pale rings where their rims have scraped the mineral crust.

What binds them together, as far as you can tell, is the rhythm. The shoals turn to a beat you can barely hear beneath the hum. The stalks pulse on the same long cycle as the vents. The collars brighten and dim in step with the sudden bursts of bubbles that rise from the floor. The basin's machinery and its animals appear to share one slow pulse, though it is hard to say which keeps time for which. You might suspect the creatures are merely responding to the vents, moved by heat and gas the way leaves are moved by wind. And yet the stalk colonies sometimes pulse before a vent fires, which argues the other way. The question has no clean answer, and the animals show no sign of needing one.

They do not build, they do not guard, and they do not seem to leave anything behind. A dead collar sinks slowly into the silt and becomes part of the sand. A stalk that has gone brittle breaks and is recycled into a crust of white silica on the glass. Nothing in the basin remembers anything in the way a memory is kept, but the layers of sediment remember for it, a few millimeters a year, and the shoals return to the shallows in their own cycles whether or not the cliffs have cracked again. They live the present in full, without a sense of waiting for anything else, and in that way they match the place itself: cold, loud, patient, and continuous.

If you stay long enough, you begin to feel the pulse in your own chest and wonder whether it is yours or the basin's. The water holds you, the light bends, the shoals turn their lanterns over your head, and the stalks breathe in unison along the shelf. You are one more body in the rhythm, and it does not seem to notice you have arrived, or that you will leave.
