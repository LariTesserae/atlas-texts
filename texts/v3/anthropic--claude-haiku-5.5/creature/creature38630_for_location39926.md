# Who Lives in Ossuary Reach

The first thing to live here is the crust itself, though it hardly seems to live at all until you kneel and look closely. Along the lee of every boulder, in the hexagonal seams of the salt plates, grow grey-green cushions no taller than your palm. Their surfaces are dusted with crystal, and at dawn the dew freezes on them in fine rime that the sun slowly draws back in. Each cushion is a colony of thousands of tiny bodies, and they have a patience that borders on the geological. Cut one open and the layers inside are banded like the stone around them, a record of every cold night and every dry year. They grow perhaps a millimeter a decade. When you press a finger into one, it springs back slowly, the way a sleeper turns over, and you realize that what you took for moss is closer to a slow argument with the weather, and it is winning by inches.

Beneath the cushions, in the dark of the cracks, lives the salt-runner. You see it only once, a pale, long-legged thing the size of a hare, its fur so colorless it seems made of the same mineral as the plain. It moves in short bursts and stops dead, freezing with its ears folded flat against its back until it is sure you are not a threat. Its eyes are large and luminous, adapted to the glare, and they reflect the light like the crystals in the sky. When it drops into a fissure and vanishes, the salt plate it stood on lifts slightly and settles, as though the earth had breathed out. Runners dig nests beneath the plates and feed on the cushions, on the dew that gathers in their burrows, and on whatever the roots of the foil-shrubs bring up from far below. They are not many. They have learned that to be seen here is to be eaten, and they have chosen to be almost invisible.

The foil-shrubs stand alone, each one a rigid, many-branched structure, and each one turns. Their leaves are thin sheets of something like beaten metal, folded in on themselves in the cold of night and unfolding at dawn. Through the day they track the sun in a slow, deliberate arc, and by late afternoon every leaf faces west, catching the last light and holding it, so that a stand of them at dusk looks like a field of small, folded mirrors waiting for something to pass. Their roots run deeper than anything else on the plateau, through the salt and the bedrock and into layers where water still moves in slow, invisible threads. They are the only things here that remember water as a living fact rather than a fossil, and they guard that memory by being as still and patient as the stone.

Above, the thermal-wings circle. Their bodies are light, almost papery, with wings that are long, pale, and brittle at the edges, like old parchment. They ride the rising columns of heat from the salt flats and do not land, or land only on the warmest ledges of the built forms, where the ceramic stone still ticks and holds a little heat after dark. Up close, their eyes are unsettling. They do not look at you so much as through you, toward the horizon, toward the storm. Some of them carry faint crystalline dust on their feathers, and when they catch the sun the dust flares briefly, so that a flock passing overhead is briefly a scatter of sparks. Nobody has seen a nest. Some say they are the storm's messengers. Others say they are simply what the sky looks like when it is paying attention.

The storms are harder to name, but they behave like the largest animals on the plateau. The great wall of ochre and crystal that rises forty kilometers away is a grazer that moves across the land in slow, deliberate lines, drawing up salt and dust and returning them as snow that never reaches the ground. It breathes lightning inside itself, and the anvil at its crown is a kind of head, reaching toward the upper air. The smaller whirlwinds are its young or its shed skin, wandering the flats in paths that follow lines in the stone that only they can read. They merge and split, sometimes spiraling tightly around a single built form as if feeding, sometimes scattering across the salt in patterns that resemble the lattice of the towers. They have no malice. They have appetite, and they have purpose, and both seem to be aimed at something you cannot see.

Then there are the built forms, and these are the strangest inhabitants of all. The domes, arches, and lattice towers are not simply structures; they behave like a slow, sessile organism that feeds on wind, salt, and the cold. A dome that stands at dawn may have folded itself lower by dusk, its ribs bending inward like a sea creature closing around a meal. An arch that spans nothing may extend by a meter overnight, reaching toward a stone it has not yet touched. The ribbed, unfinished edges are growing points, and the ticking that rises from them as they cool is the sound of their metabolism, slow and steady as a clock in another room. When you stand close to one, you feel a faint warmth through your boots, and the surface, which looked like ceramic, has the give of something that might, under other conditions, have been skin.

Beneath them all, deeper than the roots and the runners and the sleeping crust, there is the hum. It is not loud. Most days it is only a tremor you feel in your teeth before you hear it, and it rises from the bedrock in long, slow swells that fade into silence. Nobody has located its source. The forms seem to respond to it, bending slightly toward the sound, and the salt plates lift and settle in rhythm with its rise and fall. If something lives under Ossuary Reach, it is not in any of the places you can see. It is the presence that keeps the process running: the thing that remembers the mountains, that is still rebuilding them out of wind and salt, and that does not hurry, because it has already waited longer than the stone.

And then, if you stand still long enough at dusk, watching the salt flats turn amber, then rose, then steel, you begin to notice that you are being watched too. Not by anything that wants you. The cushions are tracking your warmth. The runners are marking your steps. The foil-leaves have turned, very slightly, toward your shadow. The built forms have shifted a fraction, the way a sleeper shifts when a sound enters the dream. Ossuary Reach is not empty. It is full of small, patient, unhurried attention, and it has been paying attention to everything that crosses it for longer than there has been anyone to cross.

Your footprints soften in the salt behind you. The forms ahead keep folding and unfolding. Somewhere under the stone, the hum rises, holds, and fades, and the high cold air carries the ticking of things that are still, after all this time, becoming.
