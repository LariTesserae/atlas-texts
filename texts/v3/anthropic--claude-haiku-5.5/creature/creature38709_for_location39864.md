The plateau has no single body you could point to, but the nearest thing to a resident is the mat.

It lies under the flood, a web of pale roots and moss that runs from the basin edges up the slopes and through the lower forest. The roots branch and rejoin. Where one shrub's network meets another's, the two grow into each other, and after enough seasons the whole upper plateau is held together by a single continuous tissue, thin as paper where it crosses the open pools and thick as a forearm where it passes beneath the tree ferns. It binds the young basalt. It keeps the soft ground from sliding into the basins during the wettest months. It also holds water, a great deal of it, and it does so with a kind of attention. When one part of the mat is shaded, the water it stores shifts toward the sun-warmed parts, and over days the whole web rebalances, as if weighing itself.

The shrubs are its surface. Their broad leaves are cups for the rain, and at dusk they fold, the edges drawing inward and the stored water running down into channels in the stems and out through the roots. The closing does not happen all at once. It moves across a hillside over an hour or more, a slow darkening wave from one shrub to the next, and the timing comes through the mat. Pressure shifts in the flooded roots and travels as a faint tremor that you cannot feel with your body but can see in the surface of a nearby pool, which ripples in rings, very gently, a few seconds before each leaf begins to fold. The signal is slow. It is more like weather than speech. But it carries across kilometers, and every shrub on a slope has been tuned to it for longer than anything now living has existed.

Once in a long while the whole network gathers and releases at once. Every cup on the slope tips together, and the sound is a single rushing shower, percussive, enormous for a minute, and then the silence afterward. That silence may be the most considered thing the mat does. Its water is redistributed in the seconds after the release, flowing downhill into the basins and seeping into the young stone, and the new ground beneath the shrubs takes it in. Shoots appear where the water lands. Sometimes a stretch of bare lava that had been steaming for a decade is, a few seasons later, a green patch with its own small mat growing outward from the edge.

The mat is not alone in its patience. The translucent amphibians rest on its leaves and are almost invisible until they breathe or shift a foot. They seem to know the mat's rhythms, moving only in the brief windows between drips. The grey-winged birds cross the cloud far above, shadows on the white, and they never come down. Insects drift through the fog in ones and twos, and the epiphytes on every branch, the rosettes and mosses and felted liverworts, sit on the mat's canopy like tenants who pay their rent in shade and rainwater. Each of these lives in relation to the web, and the web lives in relation to the water.

If you stood at the edge of the highest basin and waited through a long afternoon, you would see almost nothing change. The fog would thicken slightly. A drip would fall from a fern onto a moss cushion and the moss would absorb it. Far off a ridge would blur further into the gradient. Then at dusk the first leaves would fold on the nearest slope, and the faint rings would spread across the water, and the wave of closing would roll out across the hillside like a long exhale. You would not be able to say where it began or where it would end.

Nothing here has a purpose you could name, or a plan you could read. The stone cools and the water moves across the basin in its warm bands, and the mat holds what it holds and releases it when the whole web agrees to. The plateau is alive in the way a slow tide is alive, or a wetland in a long drought: by continuity, by responsiveness, by the way many small slownesses join into one. Its inhabitant is that continuity itself, a network of roots and cups and pools that has learned, over seasons too long to count, how to be the same thing while it is always becoming something slightly different.
