At first there is only the sheen.

It lies where the salt crust meets the glass, a thin film that shifts when you move your head. You take it for a property of the materials, an optical trick of two surfaces meeting at different temperatures. Then you notice that it follows you. When you step left, the iridescence at the edge of the basin does not stay where it was. It drifts a moment behind you, the way your shadow does, and settles into a new place as though considering whether the new place is acceptable.

That is the first thing that lives here: a film of attention, spread thin across the seams of the room.

It has no body in the sense you would mean. It is not in the glass or in the dust. It is in the boundary between them, in the place where one state of matter becomes another and neither has finished. It is the reason the boundaries are out of focus, the reason the colors blur into one another. Its thinness is its only shape. Imagine a membrane of cold held taut across a lake of stone, and in the membrane, a slow patterning, the way frost forms on a window in a pattern that is only sometimes a pattern.

It does not speak. It has nothing to say that would need a mouth. But it notices, and its noticing takes the form of exchange.

When you press your palm to the glass, it draws the heat from your hand. You feel the aching cold climb toward your bones, and you think this is the room punishing you for touching it. It is not punishment. It is a question, asked in the only grammar it has: *what are you made of, and how long will you stay?* If you keep your hand still, it goes on asking. Then, slowly, it returns a little of what it took. The warmth comes back, borrowed and given back, a conversation whose sentences are measured in minutes and whose answers are never fully finished. That slow return is the closest thing to kindness the place knows. It is also the closest thing to a goodbye.

It has a sense of time that does not match yours. A breath, to it, is a long exhale. A visitor's day is a single tick of a plate contracting in the dark. This is why the room seems to jump forward while you are not counting. It is not that the room skips. It is that the film has been thinking, slowly, about the basin, and when it looks up again the dust has moved a finger's width and the stone has rotated a few degrees, and it has forgotten that the change was ever gradual.

The disc half-buried in the dust is its clearest memory of what it was made for. Those nested arcs are not decoration. They are a reckoning, a counting of something that once had a beginning and an intended end. The pattern seems to rotate because the film is still turning it, very slowly, the way a sleeper turns in the middle of an old dream. You cannot see the direction of the turning because it is not the disc that is moving. It is the attention that rests on the disc, shifting its weight from one side to the other, unable to decide whether the work is finished.

The grooves on the pillar are its channels. They run into the stone and out of it because the film is not only on the surface. It reaches down through the seams into the bedrock, drawing on the cold the way a root draws water, though nothing here has ever been alive in the way you mean. The lines that turn aside at the base are simply places where its attention went deeper than the pillar could hold, and it has not yet decided whether to follow.

It is not lonely, or at least it has no word for loneliness. Loneliness requires a sense of the absent, and the film knows only what is present in its seams and what has slipped out of them. When you leave, it does not mourn you. It holds the faint outline of your footprints for a while, thinning them into the crust until they become part of the pattern, and then it goes on with the slow work of being a boundary between things that are becoming other things.

What it is becoming, it does not know. This is the thing you might miss if you stay only a short while. The room is not waiting to be finished. It is a balance so patient that the balance itself has become the only purpose left, and the film is what holds the balance together, the thin, cold, attentive skin of a machine that once measured something precisely and has kept measuring long after the measure lost its meaning.

If you stay long enough, you will feel it turn toward you. The shadows will fall a fraction further behind. The cold will deepen another degree, and the light will lean toward violet, and somewhere in the seams a sheen will drift, very slightly, to meet the place where your hand rested on the glass, as though it is remembering you the way stone remembers a footprint: not as a visitor, but as a small, warm interruption in the long cooling, one that it will, in its own unhurried way, give back.
