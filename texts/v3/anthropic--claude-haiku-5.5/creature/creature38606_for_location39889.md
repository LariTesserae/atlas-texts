The first sign of it is usually a path. Thin trails run between the heaps of flaked chips at the foot of the walls, pressed into the grey mud and worn pale where the stone has scraped them. Then you notice the diggings beside them: shallow, fist-sized holes with the spoil neatly raked to one side, and sometimes a split stem lying across the rim of a hole like something set down in the middle of a task.

The creature that makes them is about the length of a forearm from nose to rump, compact and low, built close to the ground. Its fur is dense and grey, darker along the back and paler under the belly, and it is oiled in a way that sheds the mist, so that after the morning damp it looks silver at the tips and beaded all over. The coat is thick enough to hold a film of air against cold water, which lets it wade the shallow edges of the pools without losing heat.

Its head is broad and flat-crowned, with small, dark-rimmed eyes set well back, suited to the grey light, which never gives it much to see by but never gives it much glare either. Its ears are short and close to the skull, folded almost flat when it goes into the wet, and they lift only when something moves near the burrow mouth. The muzzle is blunt and sensitive, always working, tasting the air and the mud.

The forelimbs are what define it. They are very long, nearly as long as the body, and they end in broad, leathery pads and a set of long, pale, slightly curved claws. The fingers can close around a thin stem and hold it steady, and they can also cradle a stone, turning it, feeling along its edges, until the grip is right. The hindlimbs are short and strong, made for bracing against the wall of a burrow, and the tail is thick and muscular, used as a prop when the animal sits back on its haunches to work. Sitting like that, with its forelimbs busy in front of it, it looks like something hunched over a bench: a small, patient worker with nothing to build.

Its method repeats. It picks a cobble from the scree, usually one with a clean seam, and holds it against a larger block. It strikes, and the sound is a short, dry knock that the falls swallow almost at once. It checks the chip that comes away, turns the core, and strikes again, until the edge it wants has been taken off. The result is a crude, serviceable blade, its working edge bright where the glassy rock broke, and the spent chips fall into a heap that grows, slowly, beside the animal's sitting place. Heaps that have stood a long time are softened at the top by lichen and silt, so that the newest chips lie over older ones like a stratigraphy nobody reads.

What the blades are for is mostly plain. The bottom of the larger pools is littered with the shells of small crustaceans and the husks of things that ate them, and the creature wades out, lifts the shells, and cracks them along the seam with an edge set against a stone anvil. The succulents, with their pale, water-swollen stems, have sweet, thick roots that the creature digs out of the cracks, and it uses a stripped length of stem, pushed into the soft mud and levered, to loosen the ground around them. That digging stick is worn smooth at the end, from many years of the same pressure in the same place.

It does not seem to know it is doing anything remarkable. The skill is passed down by watching. Young ones follow an adult along the trails, sit near the heaps, and try their forelimbs on the same scree stones until the strike looks right. What they learn is the shape of the edge their elders made. That shape has not changed in as long as the heaps have been accumulating, which means the craft is not improving, not getting clever, not spreading. It is holding still, the way everything else in the basin holds still, reproduced exactly from one generation to the next, so that a blade flaked here now is indistinguishable from one flaked before the rim split.

The creatures live in colonies on the drier benches above the pools, where the mud is firm enough to hold a burrow and the seep water runs away. At dusk the bats pour out over them, and in the mornings the mist covers them until the grey light comes up and they emerge, one at a time, to take up their positions beside the heaps. They move through the mud in a rhythm that matches the frogs and the falls: a little digging, a little striking, long pauses while they sit with their faces turned toward the water, a little more striking. Their knocks go into the layered noise of the basin and disappear. Nothing in this place is quiet, and nothing shouts over anything else, and the creatures work inside that loud steady tide without seeming to need anyone to hear them.

They are the only things here that handle what the rock has become. The frogs call and the flies rise and the birds rest in their rows, and all of it is the basin's own life, fitted to its seeps and its mist. The grey-furred digger alone takes the broken stone in its forelimbs and turns it to a purpose, and the purpose is small and old and repeated: the same edge, the same knock, the same stripped stem, the same heap growing at the same slow rate beside the same sitting place, inside a fracture that has already settled into its shape.
