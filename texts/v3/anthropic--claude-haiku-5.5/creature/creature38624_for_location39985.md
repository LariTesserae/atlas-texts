Nothing here lives in one place for long, so the first thing to know about the Planing's inhabitants is that most of them are not individuals. They are gatherings.

The packed presences are the main population, and they are everywhere. Each mote is too small to hold much of anything, but they cling to one another, and where they cling they become something with a shape for a while. A knot forms in the milky water, tightens to the size of a fist, and turns slowly, the way a murmuration of birds turns, with no leader and no visible decision. Then it loosens. Pieces peel away and join other knots. Sometimes a knot leans toward a passing body, or a fall of spray, and holds that shape a moment longer than the others. You could call that curiosity, if curiosity were a thing that happened in a crowd rather than in a mind.

They are most dense where the sheet is shallowest, and there the hiss is loudest. Near a dome they thin out along the weeping threads, as though the rock's constant wet is a kind of food they pass through without stopping. Near the standing columns they vanish entirely. When a column rises, the knots are pulled up into it and spun, and when it collapses they fall back in a fine rain and resettle. They seem to survive this the way a crowd survives a flood: by reforming around the same centers, slightly altered.

The ruins are where they are strangest. Walk among the half-sunk pillars at the right hour and you will find the knots arranged along them. A column of motes lies across a broken lintel, holding the line of a carved groove that no one has been able to read. A stair of drifting filaments descends into the water and climbs back out again, each step shaped by the steps before it. The motes do not build these forms. They trace them. It seems more accurate to say the presences remember the edges of things, and when a shape stays in the water long enough, they gather around its outline the way moss gathers in the cracks of old stone. Some observers have wondered whether the people who once cut these places are now part of the churn, dissolved and carried and only partly reassembled, still trying to hold the angles they once made. There is no way to confirm this. The knots do not answer questions, and the questions tend to come apart before they can be finished.

The slate-backed creatures move among the knots like herders. Their wakes are the long V-shaped ripples that cross the sheet against the storm, and they go where the motes are thickest. The broad backs break the surface only briefly. When one passes close, you can see that its skin is lined with something like gills or combs, and the water around it is thinned in a wide arc, as if the animal has been drinking, or sieving, or simply moving through a density that would stop anything weaker. They do not enter the columns. They stay at the edges of the knots, sometimes lingering in the hollows between domes, where the violet light goes out. Whether they feed on the presences or merely travel with them is unclear. Their wakes always lead toward the milkiest water.

The stalks are the briefest residents. They rise pale and jointed from the sheet, grow fast, flower within hours, and then fold back into the water. Their flowers are the only thing here that holds a clear color for more than a few minutes, a dull rose at the center of each bloom. The packed presences swarm around them while they bloom, and afterward the knots keep something of their shape for a while, drifting along the ring of foam the dissolved stalk leaves behind. The grey-green crusts on the north faces are the only residents that stay put. They hold the rock together in patches, and they seem to be the reason some domes have kept their roundness at all. Where the crusts have failed, the glass is slightly rougher, and the presences cluster there more tightly than anywhere else, as though the ruined rock is an open wound they have not learned how to close.

And then there is the storm itself, which is not a resident so much as the reason residence is impossible. The roar overhead is the breath of the whole place. Everything here is a moment of the churn that has, for now, agreed to hold together, and everything will eventually be taken back into the weather.

So the Planing does not have inhabitants in the usual sense. It has the act of inhabiting, repeated endlessly across a surface that refuses to keep a shape. Whatever lives here lives by gathering and letting go, by holding an outline for as long as the water allows, and by returning to the wet, rising, unmaking air that is always, quietly, making the place again.
