The basin has no owner, but it has keepers, and they are not all the same kind of thing.

The smallest and most numerous are the seam-walkers. They are long, segmented, and so many-legged that their movement looks like liquid poured through the joints. Their bodies are the colour of wet slate, and they carry grains of crushed shell and charcoal in their mandibles, the same materials the builders packed into the seams. When a joint loosens and water begins to scour it, the walkers gather there in a dense band, working the grit into the gap with a patience that looks almost like craft. They rebed slabs and re-pack the mortar, and they do it as if they had been told how, though no one told them. A seam-walker that has never seen the basin's original plan will still lay its grit along the old lines, because the lines are still there in the stone and the walkers follow what is already set. They are the basin's maintenance, and they are the reason the courses have not yet collapsed into silt.

The eel-bodied things are the readers of the channels. They move in slow spirals through the deep runs, and where they turn, the flow turns with them. A channel that has begun to fill with silt will soon have a pair of them working its length, rising and sinking, their bodies scouring the bed in long strokes until the current runs clean again. They seem to know which channels are meant to be open and which have been allowed to close, and they open only the ones the old plan requires. When one dies, its body is taken by the rushes and the sump-birds within a day, and the channel it kept begins to narrow.

On the north faces of the upright stones live the flat colonies. Each one is a shingle of small bodies pressed edge to edge, and they seem to share a single nervous net beneath the moss. When the mist thickens, they open their gills together, a wave of motion that passes across a whole stone in the space of a breath. They are the basin's clock and its barometer, and in the grey hours their synchrony is the only sign of unity anywhere in the place. Nothing else moves in step.

The birds come in the cool hours, skim the sump, and rise again with something glinting in their beaks. They are scavengers, but they also carry things. Shells, bones, the glassy eyes of small creatures, scraps of the pale creeper. They drop some of it on the terrace rims, and the creeper grows over those drops within the week, sealing the rims the way the builders once sealed the joints. Whether this is intention or accident does not matter much to the basin, which has never needed intention to keep working.

The pale shapes in the deep joints and under the arches are the young, or at least the least finished. They are soft-bodied and nearly blind, and they do not leave the shadow except to feed. They are watched, in a sense, by the frogs, which call from the channels above them in a rhythm that seems to match their feeding. When the chorus shifts in pitch, it is often because one of the pale ones has been taken, or has grown large enough to leave the shadows for the lit channels, where the water shines and the birds are waiting.

And under the sump, where the water boils up from below, there is something that the rest of the basin never sees. Its presence is known only by the boiling, which comes in slow pulses that are not quite regular, the way breath is not quite regular in something very large and very old asleep. The sump's blocks are dressed to a shape that suits the boil, the channels curve toward it, and the cistern mouths are placed so that its call comes back doubled. The bellow from the cistern is partly its voice. It may be the reason the basin drains where it does. It may be the thing the builders were really building for, and the basin is only the lid it placed on top.

The hinge-voiced call from beyond the rim is different again. No creature in the basin has ever answered it, and every one of them goes silent when it comes. It is not clear whether it belongs to the place or whether it is only passing through. The basin does not seem to fear it so much as wait out its arrival, the way the mist waits for noon and the channels wait for the next wet season.

So the basin is not empty and not wild. It is inhabited by a set of tenders, each keeping one part of an order that was laid down before any of them existed: the walkers that mend the mortar, the eels that keep the channels open, the colonies that count the weather, the birds that seed the rims, the young that will one day take the shadowed joints, and beneath it all the slow thing in the sump whose breath sets the rhythm for everything else. None of them remembers the plan. All of them keep to it, and in keeping to it they keep the water moving toward the centre, the courses settling a finger's width toward the sump, the basin draining into itself as it always has.

If you stand near the sump at dusk, you may feel the boil under your boots before you see it, a faint pulse through the blocks, and the frogs will fall silent for a moment, the way they do at the hinge-call. Then the knocking resumes, the mist lifts a little off the channels, and the tenders go back to their work. They will go on doing it long after the last person who could read the plan has gone, because the plan was never really written down. It was laid into the stone, and the things that live here have been reading it ever since.
