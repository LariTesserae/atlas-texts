**The Residents**

The first thing you notice is the travertine curtains, and that they are not quite still. Look closer and the creamy folds are pocked with small, closed hollows, each about the size of a thumbnail. They are the mouths of the **Tallows**, a colonial animal that has grown into the stone the way lichen grows into bark. Each individual is a soft, stalked bell, translucent and pale as candle wax, with a ring of fine cilia at its crown. Singly they are nearly nothing. Together they form a sheet that covers the terraces for hundreds of metres, linked by a fine network of tissue that runs through the mineral crust and shares what it catches.

They feed on the silt. The drifting particulates that catch the light are their food, and as the slow curves of water carry the silt across the walls, the Tallows draw it in. Their bells stay closed most of the time, folded tight against the stone, and open only in brief, shuddering flowers that let the cilia comb the water and then fold again.

Here is the strange part. The Tallows open together. Not in a wave that travels across the colony, but all at once, the whole quarry opening like a single lung. The timing is exact, and it is keyed to the valve. When the gantry releases its plume and the soft pressure-clack sounds through the water, every bell across the basin flares open for perhaps three seconds, then closes. Whatever the colony is doing with that signal, it has been doing it long enough that the rhythm is part of its body. The pump's thrum is the colony's tide. Without the machinery, it is likely that the Tallows would drift apart into scattered, sleeping colonies. With it, they are one animal, and the quarry is its heartbeat.

**The Grazers**

Across the dark glossy manganese of the lower terraces moves a second kind of creature: the **Slates**. They are flat, broad, and nearly the colour of the rock, with a mottled grey-black dorsum that matches the patina almost perfectly. You will not see one until it moves, and even then only the faint shimmer of its edge gives it away. Along that edge run dozens of tiny eyespots, pale and unblinking, which track light and motion across the whole basin. They graze the mat from the stone with a sweeping, ribbon-like foot, leaving clean scraped trails behind them, pale lines on the dark skin that fade within days.

Slates are slow. They seem to have no hurry at all. But they do seem to know the valve. When the clack sounds, a few of them will lift their edges and turn toward the gantry, every eyespot aimed upward at the plume, then settle again. They do not react to anything else.

**The Column-Riders**

In the bubble column, where the valve's plume climbs toward the surface, live the **Thread-shoals**. These are small, eel-slender animals, no longer than a finger, silver with a faint pink flush along the flanks. They hang in the rising bubbles like threads in a draft, riding the updraft from the valve up through the water, then dropping back down along its edge in a slow, spiralling descent. Their whole life seems to be arranged around the gas and the turn of the water it makes. They eat the tiny organisms that cling to the bubbles' surfaces, and they cluster most densely in the moments just after the clack, when the column is fullest.

They nest in the gantry, too, in the crevices of the heron-neck where the metal has fused with the stone. Their eggs are strung on pale filaments along the inner surfaces of the frame, and the young emerge into the bubble stream and are carried upward before they learn to fall.

**The Attention**

None of these creatures is quite the thing that most visitors would call the quarry's keeper, but there is one presence that sits underneath all of them. It is the colony's attention, the thing that makes the Tallows open together and the Slates turn toward the plume and the Thread-shoals gather in the column. It does not have a body you can point to. It is spread through the tissue linking the bells, through the cold shadow under the gantry, through the sediment that holds the machine's joints in place. It has no interest in the pump's purpose, which has long since been forgotten. It has only the clack, the rhythm, and the three-second flare of the colony's breath.

When you hold still in the water and wait, you can feel it notice you. The silt slows near you. The curtains of Tallows tilt a few degrees in your direction. A Slate eyespot rotates, slowly, to find you, and considers you for a while, the way the water considers stone.

Then the valve clacks, and the whole basin opens around you like a flower, and for three seconds everything in the drowned quarry is breathing at once.
