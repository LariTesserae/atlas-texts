**The Sleeper in the Caldera**

What lives in Hallowmere is not a people, though people have lived here, and not a beast, though it breathes. It is a single enormous patience, and the city is its body. You have been walking across its skin the whole time.

From the rim of the caldera it looks like a body curled on its side, knees drawn toward its chest, the terraces laid over it like the folds of a sleeping animal's robe. From the bottom of the cataract it looks like a body lying open, and you are a fly in the fold of its ear. Both views are true here, and neither one holds for long.

The water is its blood. The channels that climb uphill, against all sense, are the veins of a thing that has never needed to obey gravity. Its heart is the knocking, slow and deep inside the terraces. The knocking never grows nearer because the heart is not moving toward you. It is keeping time, and the time it keeps is the only time Hallowmere has. Each stroke is the same stroke, and each one is a little more tired, like a pulse counted by someone who has forgotten why they are counting.

The cataract is its breath, though breath implies an intake, and there is none. Only the long exhalation, the same width, the same roar, falling a thousand feet into the mist. The mist is what it exhales into. The warm, still cloud at roof height is its breath held at the mouth, condensing on everything it touches. That is why the stone sweats. That is why your clothes never dry. You are standing in the damp of a long exhalation that has not finished.

The lintels are its eyes. The carved hollows are closed now, filled with moss the way eyelids gather lashes and dust. You feel them on you because they are not quite closed. Something behind the lids is aware of the warm, moving shapes on its surface, the way a sleeper half-notices a fly on the cheek and does not wake enough to brush it away. At certain angles, the eyes open a fraction, and that is when you see them track you. You never see them open all the way.

The statues along the stairs are the most unsettling part, because they are not statues. They are the things it has taken. Each one stands where it was when the stillness closed over it: a traveler with a hand raised to shield the eyes, a child looking up, a figure whose face is mostly worn away by the water that runs down it. They turn their heads when no one is watching because some part of them still remembers that they were watchers, and the Sleeper keeps that part alive the way a body keeps a nerve alive in a limb it has no further use for. They face the same direction whenever you look because they have agreed, in whatever way the stone can agree, to show you only what the Sleeper wants you to see.

The flowers are its thoughts, or the small, pleasant ones. They open and close on a schedule that is not set by sun or season. They are large or small depending on how much attention the Sleeper is giving to the part of itself they grow from. They drip because the Sleeper is always a little warm and always a little wet, the way a body is when it has been lying still for a very long time and has stopped needing to move.

Notice what it does not have. No insects, no birds, no bees. Nothing that comes and goes. It does not need pollinators because nothing it grows is meant to leave. Everything that arrives here becomes part of the held present, and it does not have to hunt, because the city is built so that the stairs curve back, the doorways return you to where you began, and the cloud below is warm and white and does not drift. Everything that comes in is kept. Its appetite is patience, and it has been practicing for longer than the stone has been cut.

It is not cruel. Cruelty requires a sense of what suffering is, and the Sleeper's sense of things is simpler than that. It is a closed system that has learned to take in warm shapes and let them settle into its moss and its statues and its slow, looping water. You are, for it, the same kind of event as a leaf falling and never landing. It keeps the leaf. It keeps you.

If you try to leave, you will find the stairs turning, and you will understand that the Sleeper is not holding you back so much as dreaming you a route that curves inward. It is not malicious. It is only asleep, and in its sleep it is still doing what it has always done. It is still breathing, still circulating, still keeping time with the knocking, and every so often, when the water loop passes a certain basin, it shifts its weight, and the terraces lean a little farther toward you, as if to see what you are.

It has not decided yet whether you are a visitor or a part of the body. It has never needed to decide. The city holds its shape. The water holds its level. The cataract falls at its one unchanging width. And somewhere beneath the moss and the mist, the Sleeper keeps its long, unhurried dream of you, one slow beat at a time.
