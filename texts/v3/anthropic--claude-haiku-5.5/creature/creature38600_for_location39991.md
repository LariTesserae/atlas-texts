**Who Keeps the Stillhigh**

The first thing you notice is the animals, but they are not the whole of the population. Beneath the herds and the beetles and the layered clouds of winged things is a second order of residence, slower and more particular. You learn to see it the way you learn to see a face in a pattern of stones. Then it's there, and you can't unsee it.

**The herds**

Look closely at the long-necked grazers. Each one carries a band of pale ring-marks along its flank, a set of short, parallel lines, faint as scratches in chalk. They match the notches cut into the upright slabs across the rise. The grazers are not wild in the way the beetles are wild. They are a tally. Each animal is a stroke in a count that is still being kept, and when one falls, another steps forward already marked with the same lines, so the herd never thins. The marks are renewed, not inherited. Something keeps painting them on, though you never see the hand.

They never call to one another, but they move with an attentiveness that a flock of birds would not need. Every head turns at the same moment. When you pass near a herd, the nearest animals lower their heads and tap the crust once with a forefoot, then lift again. You wonder whether this is a greeting or a correction.

**The tappers**

The beetles are the ones that do the rhythm. Their clicking, which you took at first for rustling, follows a pattern when you listen long enough: groups of three, then a pause, then a group of five, then three again. The pattern is the same across the whole plateau, and it is the nearest thing to speech the place possesses. It may be the language of the makers, reduced to a rhythm that insects can keep. Or the makers may have learned it from the insects. The taps are the only thing on the plateau that seems to be addressed to someone.

If you stand still and tap your own foot in a three-and-five pattern, the nearest beetle stops, turns toward you, and taps back, once, and then resumes its own count. You have not been answered. You have been acknowledged, and then dismissed as not yet part of the count.

**The standing ones**

Now the stalks. They sit at wide intervals, tracking the sun and folding at dusk, and you took them for plants, which they are. But between them, and sometimes beside them, there are figures that stand exactly as the stalks do: upright, thin, turned toward the sun, arms folded close against the body at the same angle as a leaf closing. They are roughly human-tall. They do not move while you watch. When you look away and look back, one of them may have shifted a few degrees toward the east, which is the only motion you will ever see from them.

They appear most often on the second horizon, the fainter skyline that hangs just above or below the first. You can see them there more clearly than on the real ground, and they seem closer than they are. Their shadows fall in the wrong direction and arrive a few seconds late, the way a sound arrives after a flash. This is probably not an illusion. It may be what happens when something has been standing in the same posture for a very long time: its attention lags behind its body, and its shadow has to catch up.

Do they see you? It is hard to say. When you approach, the nearest of them does not turn. But the grazers in the herd beside it lift their heads together, all at once, toward you, and then lower them again, and you understand that the standing ones have noticed you through the herd, the way a shepherd notices a stranger through the sheep.

**The folding**

The doubled horizons, the herds that reappear in the same spot, the beetle that is on two rocks at once: these look like the breakdown of the place, but they may be the residents' ordinary way of attending. Nothing here is moving forward. The herds loop. The stalks fold at the same hour. The standing ones keep their posture, and the count on the slabs keeps adding strokes it has already added before. A thing that is attending to a moment that has already ended will, of course, be seen in two places. It is looking at the same thing from two directions, the way you might keep looking at a closed door out of habit.

You have the sense, walking through, that you have stepped into the middle of a sentence that has been repeating for a very long time, and that the sentence is not a complaint or a warning but a kind of work. It is maintenance. Someone is keeping the count, keeping the herd marked, keeping the stalks turned toward a sun that stopped changing, keeping the taps going in three-and-five, so that the place will not quite finish becoming nothing.

**What they want**

Nothing, as far as you can tell. The residents do not seem to want you to leave or to stay. The dropped tools lie where they fell, and the standing ones do not pick them up, but they do not object if you do. If you pick up a chert flake and turn it over, the nearest grazer watches you do it, and when you set it down, the herd taps once, all together, as though a stroke has been added to the count in a hand it can't see.

You leave the plateau the way you came in, and the edge of the level stone never quite arrives. Behind you, the taps continue in their pattern, and the tally goes on. The standing ones keep their faces to the sun, their arms folded, waiting for a dusk that will come at the same hour it always has. They are not the ghosts of the makers. They are the makers' last habit, carried forward by animals who learned it, kept by figures who never stopped, in a land that agreed, a long time ago, to keep going anyway.
