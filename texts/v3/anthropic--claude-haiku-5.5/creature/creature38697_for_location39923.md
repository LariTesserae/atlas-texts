The people of the hollow are pale in the way the stone is pale, a white that has gone under the bloom and stayed there. Their skin is thin and cool to the touch and faintly translucent at the wrists and temples, where the blue veins show like seams in the limestone. Their hair is colorless, or nearly so, and they keep it long and bound in cord because loose hair takes the mineral film and grows heavy. Their eyes are large and set deep under ridged brows, and the irises have faded to a milky grey-violet. They see well enough in the tar-light and the glow. Out in the dark between lamps they move with their heads tilted slightly, listening, and they seldom need to look at where they are going.

Their hearing is the thing that marks them most. The ear-flaps are broad and deeply folded, and the canals are wide. A child in the quarry galleries can tell from the ring of a chisel how far the face is cut, and which way the chip will fall, and whether the seam ahead holds water. They hear the sluice gates before they see them move. When two people speak across a terrace, the echoes fold back and they answer each other through the reverberation as easily as through the air, so their talk has a doubled, layered quality, each word arriving twice, once from the mouth and once from the stone. They have no word for the echo. It is simply how speech sounds.

They are not tall. Their hands are long and fine-boned, and their fingertips are wide and soft, as if built for feeling the seams of wet rock rather than for gripping. They work slowly and precisely. A chisel-stroke among them is never wasted. Watching a man recut a socket in a lintel block, you see that he is not so much shaping the stone as remembering the shape it already had, following a line his teacher followed, and his teacher's teacher, so that the mark on the block is the same mark it has always been.

Their voices are low and carry a hollowness, a resonance in the chest that matches the stone-tones of the hour-bells. They speak little in the open and more at the pillars, where the older people sit. The old ones do not look older than the others, not in any way a visitor could put a number to. They are simply more still. They sit with their backs against the carved columns, their hands folded, their faces turned toward the sound of the falling water, and they seem to be listening to something a long way off. The younger people bring them bowls of rendered fat and lengths of cord, and the old ones accept these without thanks, because thanks would imply that something had changed.

Their labor is steady and without visible grief. They cut the blocks, haul the cord, dip the reeds in the bitumen seeps and set the lamps, scrape the soot from the ceilings and grind it for the pigment they use on the signs. They open and close the slate gates at the hours the old schedule demands. They fish the mere with eyeless nets and hunt the blind crawlers with long rakes, and they take only what they need. When a crawler is caught, the one who takes it says a short phrase under the breath before it is eaten, and the phrase is the same every time, and no one can say anymore what the words once meant.

When one of them dies, the household does not mourn in any way that alters the rooms. The body is laid on the lowest terrace where the water passes over it, and the bloom settles on the skin until the form is white and smooth as a carved figure. Within a few days, a new person arrives at the same door. They are not a stranger. They know which step creaks and which socket sticks and where the cord is kept. They take up the same chisel, the same bell, the same night-watch on the gate. Whether they came from a neighboring house or were born to a family that had waited for the vacancy, no one asks. The rites are performed. The hours are struck. The water runs on.

The walls that glow are the closest thing they have to an interior life. When the water flows over the luminous faces, the cold blue light blooms out across the stone and the people stop what they are doing for a moment, all at once, and look up. They do not speak during this. When the light fades, they go back to their work, and the old ones at the pillars say a single word, and the younger ones repeat it, and the word is the name of the hour. They know time only by this, the rise and fall of the cold light and the slow return of the same measured dark.

They are not unhappy. Nothing in them suggests that they have ever wanted anything else. They hold themselves with the quiet attentiveness of people who have been listening for a very long time, and who have decided, or been made to understand, that the listening is the point. Their city is a honeycomb grown from a cliff, and they are its cells, each one seated in its socket and recut to its exact shape whenever the stone wears down. They keep the level of the water fixed. They keep the light returning. Whatever they are for, they are doing it perfectly, and the water, which never stops, seems to be the thing they are doing it for.
