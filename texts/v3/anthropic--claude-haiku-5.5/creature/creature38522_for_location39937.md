The thing that lives here is mostly hidden, and most of what you can know about it comes from what it leaves behind: the wide three-toed prints in the soft patches, the burrow mouths ringed with pale wet soil, the stalks cropped at a slant. Then, once in a while, a neck rises out of the columns forty paces off and holds still.

It is large, but its size reads wrong at first because the mat covers it. Its back is layered with the same pale green and rust as the floor, and moss grows in its furrows, so for a moment you take it for a low hummock until the hummock turns its head. The neck is long and loosely jointed, carried in a shallow S, and it rises through the columns the way a heron rises through reeds, parting them without snapping them. Its head is narrow and dense, with eyes set high and wide. They are pale amber and have a second lid that slides across them in the glare, so that when it looks up at the shaft it seems to blink in slow, deliberate layers. It keeps to the mist and the low hours. At noon it sinks, and you lose it entirely.

Its feet are the most important part of it. Each of the three toes is broad and webbed with a thick pad, spread so that its weight lies across the felt instead of through it. That is why its prints are shallow and wide, and why it can cross a slope that would drop a person into the water below. It seems to feel the mat through those feet. Where a patch is spongy and waterlogged, it goes around. Where a mat is sound, it walks straight and steady, and you sometimes see it pause, one foot raised, as if listening through its sole for a hollow place beneath. Some visitors believe it can tell a safe path from a dangerous one by feel alone, and the people who left the cairns may have followed its routes for exactly that reason.

It eats the columns, but not as a plain grazer would. It bites the swollen bases where the cold sap is stored, drinks what it wants, and leaves the rest to rot. It nips the tips of the new growth and leaves the old stalks standing. It strips the vines from the sagging nets and carries the seed-bearing pods away in its gut. In doing this it keeps the basin in motion. The collapses and regrowths that make the place so restless are partly its work, and when a column creaks and falls on a still night, there is a fair chance the neck lowered the last bite. The loop runs through its body: water drawn up from the mat, stored in the bases, drunk by the grazer, carried to a new burrow and released again in the damp.

Its young hatch in the rotting heaps. The collapsed columns are warm from their own decay, and the grazer lays its eggs deep in the wet fibre where the heat is steady. This is why the fungal blooms appear overnight on those heaps and are gone within a week. The blooms are the young feeding, or the adults feeding them, or both; nobody who has watched closely is sure. What is certain is that the heaps which look like ruin are nurseries, and the new growth sprouting from them is the same growth that will eventually feed the next grazer.

It seems to know the place by its rhythms rather than its landmarks. The paths you memorize change, but it moves through the new arrangement without hesitation, which suggests it does not hold a map so much as a feel for where the ground is firm. It has a sound, too, a low, almost subsonic hum that you feel more than hear, carried through the felt. On still mornings the columns near it tremble in step with the hum, and the dripping seems to slow around it. It may be a call, or a reassurance to the young, or simply the noise a body makes when it is breathing through a mat of plant. You cannot tell from a distance, and when you walk closer it stops, and the basin goes quiet, and it is gone.

There is one more thing to say. The notches on the standing column, pushed upward by new growth until they sit well above reach, are not the only marks in the Reach. Along the lower stalks, where the grazer passes most often, the cropped tips are smooth and worn, and in a few places the bark has been rubbed bright by a neck that leans against the same column each season. Whoever counted those notches was counting something, and whoever came after may have been counting the grazer's visits as well. It does not seem to mind being watched. It seems to know that the watcher, like everything else here, will be swallowed by the mat in time, and that the neck will keep rising through the columns regardless.
