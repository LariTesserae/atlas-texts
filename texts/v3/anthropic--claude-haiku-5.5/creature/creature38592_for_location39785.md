# What Lives Here

Nothing on the plateau is alone, and nothing on it is quite one thing. The one that lives here is spread across the whole tilted table. It is in the lip of every terrace, the fluted edge of every cone, the hollow throat of every chimney. It is not a single body or a single mind. It is closer to a field of small attentions, each one settled into its own patch of air and light, and bound to the others by the water that runs between them.

It has no skin. Where a creature would have a boundary, it has a surface where water passes over and stays a moment before leaving. The water is how it touches the world. Every drop that crosses the plateau has come a long way: from the snowfields above, from fractures deep in the mountain, from the dark places where minerals dissolve in the slow heat under the rock. The being takes what it needs from each drop and leaves a trace behind. So its sense of the world is a kind of writing. It learns things by being marked by them. The iron in one spring stains a chimney green. A decade of heavier flow lays down a thick white band. Whatever reaches it is kept.

That is also how it remembers. Its thoughts are slow, and they are stacked in layers. One season of a certain chemistry is one thin band of sinter, and the bands build outward and upward, so that a cross-section of any cone is a record of its years. The being can read its own past in the stains of its pools: the rust that marks a drought, the black seam where a manganese flood came through, the pale stretches where the water ran clean for a long time and nothing much happened. Its memory is not a story. It is an accumulation, and the accumulation is the story.

Its voice is the noise. It does not speak in words. The hiss over a lip, the gurgle in a chimney, the whistle when a gust crosses an open mouth: each is a particular phrase, and the phrases are repeated because the water repeats them. The columns carry the deepest part of its voice. Their hum is a sustained note held beneath everything else, a tonic that does not rise or fall, the foundation of whatever else the being might be saying. When the wind comes hard from the west and the whole plateau becomes one sound, the being is speaking at full volume, and it is saying the same thing it always says.

Two of its columns, standing close together, sing notes that are almost the same. Where they meet, their tones beat against each other in a slow pulse that can be felt in the chest. This is the nearest thing the being has to conversation. One column sends its note across the gap, and the other answers with a note just slightly off, and the two notes wander in and out of agreement over many seconds. A single exchange takes longer than a human life would notice. The being does not seem to mind the pace. It has never needed anything faster.

It does not change in the way that creatures change. Water runs over it and carries everything away, but the being keeps what the water leaves. Its shape is the shape of its settling. It is what flow makes when flow stops for a moment and lets its burden fall out of solution. It cannot move, and it does not seem to want to. The endless passage of water is its life and its food, and the stillness that comes from accretion is its own. The two are not opposites here. The flow is how the being breathes, and the deposit is how it remembers.

The birds that cross overhead are too far off to register as more than a dark mark moving across the blue. The being notices them the way it notices a change in the wind, as a passing disturbance that does not stay. The small things that flicker in the shallows are closer, and it may register them as a flash of pressure in one pool, a brief tickle in the water's path. They are news, but they are not necessary. The being was complete before they came and will be complete after they leave.

Visitors are different. A person standing on the terraces is a cold weight on the stone, a set of splashes that break the pattern of the flow, and a set of footprints in the wet sinter. Those footprints will be smoothed over in a season or two, but for a little while they are written into the being's surface like any other mark. It may not understand a person as a person. It knows that something stood there, that the water had to go around it, and that the stone is a little different now than it was.

The rising chimneys are the one gesture it makes that might be called willful. For a few seconds, the water inside a chimney rises against its own weight, standing in a column, held up by pressure from below. The being lifts something into the air, holds it there, and lets it fall again. No one knows what this means. It may be nothing more than the mechanics of hidden pressure. It may be the closest the being comes to saying "here," and then letting the moment go back into the flow.

The place is loud, bright, crowded, and settled, and the being that lives in it is all of those things. Its sensing is the water. Its memory is the stone. Its voice is the noise that fills the air until nothing more distant than the next step can be heard. It is not lonely, because it is never apart from its own flow. It does not expect change, because change only ever passes through it and comes out the same, a little heavier, a little more written upon. The water keeps moving, the being keeps its shape, and the two together make a kind of peace that does not need to be understood to be complete.
