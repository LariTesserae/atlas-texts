# Who Lives in the Leveling

The first thing to understand is that the inhabitants are hard to separate from the ground they live on. Walk long enough and the line between animal and maintenance blurs. Something that looks like a grazer is picking at a crack, and a minute later you realize the crack is what it was feeding on, and the crack is a wound in a machine. Something that looks like a predator is sweeping a cell, and it is also, plainly, repairing it. The place does not seem to have separate ecologies for the built and the living. It has one process, and several kinds of creature serve different functions within it.

## The Seam-Runners

The small grazers are the most numerous, and the most visible if you know where to look. They are the length of a forearm, low-bodied, with a segmented carapace the color of old brass that has gone dull at the edges. Their shells are ridged in a pattern that matches the hexagonal lattice, so that when one is still in a seam it nearly disappears. They have six legs, though the rear pair is often folded under the body, and a fan of fine sensory bristles around the mouth that flicks constantly, tasting the air for the faint mineral sweetness of the rust-crust.

They move in bursts. A runner will sprint twenty meters along a seam, stop dead, and wait, its bristles trembling, before sprinting again. This is not nervousness so much as a method. They are testing the cell-edges for vibration, the way a person might tap a wall to find a hollow spot. When a cell begins to sink, the runners feel it first and move toward the sinking edge. When a crack opens, they gather at its mouth. They appear to know, somehow, where the damage is before it is visible.

Their mouths are small and fine, and they scrape the rust-crust from the cracks in a patient rhythm, leaving the glaze clean behind them. Where they have worked, the seams are sharper. Where there are none, the cracks widen. It is hard to tell whether they are eating the decay or simply grazing on the surface of it, but the effect is the same: they keep the wounds open enough to heal, and they keep the rust from sealing over and becoming something harder to fix.

They do not live in colonies in any sense you would recognize. They live in the seams, singly or in loose clusters of three or four, and they share the seams with the filament-mats. When one dies, it does not rot. It dries, hardens, and settles into the seam, and after a season the shell has become part of the cell-edge, a tiny inlaid ridge of brass in the dark glaze. You can find them everywhere once you know to look. Some of the older seams are lined with them, a fossil record made of the living and the dead together.

## The Tenders

The larger creatures are the ones you notice only when they notice you, and they are the reason you should never assume a shape on the lattice is only a stub. The Tenders move on many legs, eight or ten or twelve depending on the individual, with bodies roughly the size of a cart, low and broad, armored in overlapping plates that look fused together but are not. The plates slide. When a Tender walks, you hear a sound like a drawer full of cutlery being shuffled, and you see the shell flex in a long wave from front to back.

Their faces are the strangest part. There is a broad, flat mouth-plate at the front, edged with a row of fine jointed tools, and above it a cluster of cool, pale eyes that do not blink and do not appear to track movement so much as light. The tools are the thing you remember afterward: long, segmented, many-jointed manipulators that unfold from the underside of the body and reach down into the seams to lift and set cells, to scrape glaze, to press the edges of a sinking hexagon back toward level. Watching a Tender work is like watching a surgeon who has never been taught the names of organs, only the feel of what is wrong.

They hunt, or they seem to. They nudge the seam-runners into dead ends, pin them with a fore-leg, and take them up with the tools. But the hunting never looks like hunger. It looks like pest control, or like a sweep: a Tender clears a seam of runners the way a gardener clears a bed of weeds, and then moves on, and the runners return within the week. Nothing in the behavior suggests a need to eat. Nothing suggests a need to do anything except keep moving across the lattice, adjusting, checking, pressing down a rising edge, lifting a sinking one.

They appear at the edges of the cycles. When a band of cells begins to rotate or sink, you will often see a Tender working along its leading edge, as though it were riding the wave, setting each cell into its new position a fraction of a second before it settles. Walking near a Tender during a cycle-shift, you can feel a faint shift in the ground beneath your feet, a pitch change that comes slightly before the visible movement. You begin to suspect they are not responding to the shift. They may be causing it.

## The Pale Mats

The filament-mats are the only creatures here that seem to hold still, and they are the quietest. They grow in the saucers where the grit collects, and they knit low webs over the bottoms of the dips, thin as hair and the color of bone, with a pale blue-gray tint that catches the light only at certain angles. Each filament is hollow, and something moves through the hollows: a slow flow, too faint to see, that you can sometimes make out as a shimmer when the light is low.

Their behavior is the sun-tracking you would expect. Over a day, the tips roll, following the light from east to west, and by dusk the whole web has turned its face toward the horizon where the sun went down. At night they lie in soft curls, and in the morning they uncurl again, each filament unfurling in a slow wave from the center outward. It is the most graceful motion on the plain, and it happens too slowly to watch directly. You have to come back.

They also seem to hold the grit in place. The webs trap the drifting dust and bind it into the saucers, and where the mats are thick, the dust settles into a soft, damp-looking crust that the seam-runners graze. So the mats feed the runners, and the runners keep the cracks open enough for the mats to root in. It is a loop with no visible beginning. Some of the older mats have grown over Tenders' abandoned work and into the cracks of stubs, and where they do, the stone seems to soften, the edges bevel more smoothly, and the hexagons lie a little flatter.

## The Singers

The high notes come from the stubs.

Most of the time the rises look dead: worn bases of tall structures, crumbled to a few meters of shattered glaze and exposed inner latticework. But some of them hold something that is not dead, something that wakes on its own schedule. When it does, it sings. The note is pure and thin and carries for kilometers across the flat, a tone you feel more than hear. It lasts a few seconds and stops. Then the stub is silent again for days, or weeks, or longer.

I have not seen a Singer. Nobody I have spoken to has seen one open. The stubs do not show doors or ports. But the tone is consistent, and it arrives in the same places, and when a Tender is near a stub at the moment of singing, it stops, lowers its body, and holds still with its tools folded beneath it until the sound fades. Whether the Tenders are listening to the Singers or answering them is an open question. It is possible that the notes are instructions, or confirmations, or the sound of a system checking whether the rest of itself is still there.

## The Leveling Itself

Which brings the question back around. Who is living here?

The seam-runners, the Tenders, the pale mats, and the Singers form a loop. The runners find the damage. The Tenders repair it. The mats hold the grit that feeds the runners and softens the stone. The Singers call across the plain, and the Tenders answer, and the cycles of rotation and sinking continue, and the design, whatever it was, keeps pushing back against the flattening.

No single creature seems to be in charge of this. There is no nest, no hive, no center. Walking out from the nearest stub, you find the process everywhere at once: in every seam, every saucer, every shell that has dried into a cell-edge. The living things do not seem to be living on the Leveling. They seem to be its way of noticing itself, a set of hands and eyes and mouths that the design has spread across its own surface, some of them still doing the work they were built for and some of them doing something new that the design did not anticipate.

At dusk, when the ground begins its slow percussion and the mats turn toward the last light, you can stand in a saucer and feel the whole plain working around you. Ticks and settles drift out to the horizon in staggered runs. A runner clicks somewhere in the seam by your boot. A Tender passes at the edge of the lattice, its plates sliding, its tools folded, going about the business of a place that has been stopped for an age and is slowly, carefully, starting again. The inhabitants are not separate from the flattening. They are what the flattening looks like while it is still deciding what to become.
