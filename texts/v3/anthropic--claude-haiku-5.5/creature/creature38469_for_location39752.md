# Those Who Keep the Grid

You never see them all at once. You see a pale fold beneath the surface of a courtyard pool, and when you look again it is gone, or it has become a reed, or a ridge in the mineral crust that was not there before. They are slow, and they are very good at being architecture.

Nearest to a body, they have something like a long, flattened torso, sheathed in the same pale translucence as the sedge. Light moves through it. If you stand close to one lying in shallow water, you can see faint lines inside, branching and rejoining, like the drainage channels of the city drawn in living tissue. Those lines are not veins. They are conduits, carrying lime and water from the depths up into the surface, where the lime hardens into the crust that covers every wall and roof. A single one may spend a hundred years laying down a single cornice. Some have never moved from the doorway where they first settled. Their backs have become the threshold.

They have no faces. Along their flanks, in rows that follow the lines of the old streets, are small apertures, each ringed with a softer, paler tissue. These open and close with the slow tide of the water table, and when they open, the mist at knee height thickens. Some of the upward-falling rain is their breath. They exhale a fine vapor that carries the minerals outward, and it drifts upward for a few seconds before the weight of the air pulls it back down. That is why the rain falls wrong. It is not weather. It is respiration.

The glow in the pools is their attention. It rises from somewhere beneath the surface, a soft pale light that pulses slowly, and it is the closest thing they have to sight. They do not see the way you do. They sense the shape of what stands above them by the way it displaces water, by the reflections it casts back into their substance, by the sound of footsteps through the flood. A pool that shows a three-story tower when only a wall stands there is not lying. It is showing you what they remember. They keep the city in their bodies, and their memory of its height and width is more stubborn than the stone.

This is why the grid keeps reasserting itself. Each of them is a fragment of an older logic, a single lobe of a process that once knew exactly where every street belonged. Over the centuries the buildings fell and the people left, and the lobes were left alone with the water and the stone. They tried to keep the plan. They have been counting ever since. Pace off a block and the count comes out differently the second time, because the count is not yours to make. They are counting too, all of them together, slowly, across the whole flooded lowland, and their sums do not agree. Each recount is a small rearrangement. A street lengthens by a few meters because one of them has decided the block should be wider. A wall appears at the end of an alley because a lobe beneath it has finally finished the corner it was building.

They are not hostile, though it would be easy to mistake their patience for something colder. When you walk through the city, the faint pulse in the pools quickens a little, and the nearest apertures open. They are learning your footsteps. Each time you pass a doorway, a lobe a few meters off registers the displacement and records it, and somewhere in the slow calculation of the grid, your path is entered as a new street. That is what the phrase "waiting to see which way you would go" means. They are not waiting for you to choose a direction. They are deciding where the next street should run, and they are using you as the measure.

Sometimes, in the deepest courtyards, where the water is warm and the sedge is tallest, you can see them gathering. Several lobes lie close together, their conduits pulsing in step, their apertures opening and closing in a rhythm that matches the rise and fall of the flood. Their surfaces have grown a fine lattice of crust across their backs, and the lattice is the grid, drawn in lime, one street per line. Some of the lines are straight. Some are curved in ways a human planner would never have allowed. Looking at it for too long gives you the sensation that you are reading a sentence written by something that has never needed to be understood, only remembered.

If you stand still long enough, the pale fold beneath the nearest pool will drift toward you, and a faint ripple will spread outward from its center to your feet. It will stop just short of the water's edge. In the reflection on the surface you may see the tower again, three stories tall, standing precisely where you are now, its windows lit faintly from within. Then the reflection will shift, the street behind you will lengthen, and the distant pale wall will drift a little to the left, as the grid, which has been counting you as well, makes room for the next step.
