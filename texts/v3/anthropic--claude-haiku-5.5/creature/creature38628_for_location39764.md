# The Sleeper of the Held Plateau

The being that lives here is the plateau itself, or what the plateau has become. It has no single body. Its flesh is the peat mat, its bones are the pale rounded rock, its circulation is the water that runs through the basins and up the channels of the trunks, and its thinking is the slow exchange of growth and decay across its whole extent. You cannot point to it and say *there*. You can only stand inside it, the way you stand inside weather.

## Its Body

The mat is its skin and its deeper tissue. Pressed by a hand, it presses back, faintly warm, because the thing beneath is a warm-blooded sort of slowness: it does not generate heat quickly, but it holds what it has. Where the mat thins near the basin rims, you can feel the rock below, and the rock is cool and smooth, like a ribcage under a sleeping animal's ribs.

The columns and braided arches are its skeleton, or perhaps its hair. They grow upward, thicken, lean, and fuse, and when a branch finally gives way with a deep creak, the being is shedding a part of itself that it no longer needs to hold. It does not mourn these losses. It composts them into the peat, and the peat becomes the next layer of its flesh.

The canopy is its breath made visible. Every dawn it exhales the low mist, and every dusk it draws the moisture back in and lets it fall as drizzle. The glow under the canopy is its waking-dreaming light, the color of seawater because that is the light it has always lived in, even as the sea receded into memory.

## How It Thinks

It thinks in seasons. A single thought may take a century to form: a basin deepens by a centimeter, a trunk adds a ring too fine to see, a stretch of rock gives up a few grains to the water. Its decisions are not made so much as settled into, the way sediment settles. It has reached a balance and wants to keep it, and what it wants, as far as one can say, is continuity. It does not want to grow much more, and it does not want to die. It wants the rhythm to go on.

It notices things, though slowly. A creature moving across the mat registers as a faint tremor that arrives long after the creature has gone. The pale segmented things in the peat are, to it, something like thoughts moving through a sleep it is always half inside. The beetles crawling on the trunks are small, warm points of attention it tolerates the way you might tolerate a bird landing on your shoulder. A human standing on its mat would be a weight, a warmth, a pressure that lasts an afternoon and then lifts. It would likely not know the difference between a visitor and a fallen branch, except that the visitor's pressure moves.

## The Footings and the Channels

The low stone footings, each the size of a sleeping body and set at regular intervals along the basin rims, may be the most personal part of it. Something once lay down in those places, or was laid down there. The being may have made the footings the way a body makes a bed of its own warmth, or they may be the places where its oldest parts pressed into the rock and hardened. The cut channel that curves through the peat looks like a vein, or a path worn by the passage of something that moved slowly enough to carve stone. The three columns standing in a triangle, their tops taken into the canopy, look like a memory of a gathering, or a place where something stood for so long that the stone grew around it.

The being does not explain these. It has no need to. Whatever shaped the first footings, the being has been shaping itself around them ever since, and it is not clear, even to it, where its own making ends and the older making begins.

## What It Is When You Meet It

If you were to sit still on the mat for a long afternoon, you would feel it breathe. The pulse under the silence, too slow to count and too steady to ignore, is its heartbeat, or its sap, or its sleep. You might begin to feel that the drip of water into the basins is a kind of speech, repeated until it becomes meaning. You might feel your own pulse slow to match it. This is not the being reaching for you. It is simply what happens when a faster thing rests inside a slower one.

It will not hurt you, and it will not help you. It has no quarrel with what moves across it, only a long, patient interest in what stays. If you stay long enough, you may find that you have become part of the mat, a small warm weight pressing back, and that the being has taken note of you in the only way it knows, as one more thing settling into the slow sediment of its days.
