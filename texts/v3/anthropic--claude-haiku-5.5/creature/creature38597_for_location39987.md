# The Pressers of the Terraces

You will not see many of them at first. Their colour is the colour of the place: a grey-green mottled with pale lichen-patches that look like frost on wet stone. You may take a figure crouched on a gallery post for a knot of moss until it turns its head. Then you notice the eyes, wide and pale gold, with a clear inner lid that closes and opens slowly as they blink against the damp.

They are tall when they stand, but they rarely stand fully upright. Their torsos are long and their haunches heavy, built for hauling themselves up the terrace walls hand over hand. Their hands and feet are broadly webbed and splayed, spreading wide on sphagnum so that they do not sink, and their fingers end in short, dark, horn-hard pads that they use for knocking and drumming on stone. Their skin is thick and cool to the touch and holds moisture the way moss does. At the nape and across the shoulders it is folded and furrowed, and in these folds grows the lichen that marks each family line. It is kept by grooming, by the slow rubbing of one body against another, and each terrace has its own pattern. A stranger from a lower tier can be recognized by the wrong pale bloom across the shoulders.

At the throat they carry a loose, pale pouch, and it is the heart of how they speak. Most of their talk is not spoken aloud. It is pulsed. The pouch swells and falls in rhythms that carry through the air as a low hum, felt in the chest more than heard in the ears, and the damp air holds it and passes it along the causeways. This is why the basin is so quiet. There is hardly any loud voice in the whole settlement, because the dwellers do not raise their voices. They lower them until the sound is just pressure in the water-heavy air.

When they need to speak across distance, or to call a whole terrace, they knock. Each dwelling and each wall has a sounding place, a flat stone worn to a dark gloss, and a knuckle drummed on it sends a sharp tap down through the dry-laid masonry for a hundred metres. The tiers are wired this way, not with metal but with stone, and a single knock at the top of the basin can be heard at the bottom in the time it takes to draw breath. The whole city hums and ticks under the pulse-hum and the knocking, and it sounds, if you listen long enough, like something breathing through its walls.

They work all the time. Work is communal and slow. A terrace gang gathers around a sagging wall and shifts the fallen stone by a rhythm of pulses and knocks, each heave timed so no one strains alone. They do not hurry. The joints are fitted by touch, and the tightest seams are checked with the pads of the fingers, pressing and listening until the stone sits silent under the weight. The fine work, the hair-thin joins and the stepped faces, is done by the eldest, whose hands are thickest with lichen and whose pulse-rhythm carries farthest. These are the Reckoners, and they are the only ones who speak in long, unbroken pulse, the hum that goes on for an hour while the others listen.

Their largest work is the pressing. In the nested hollows of the upper stone they grind dried sphagnum, clay scraped from the new scree below the rim, and the pale fibrous root of the horsetail into a dense grey paste. They press it into cakes, working it with their heavy hands and their knuckles until the cakes ring when knocked, and they set the cakes into the wall-caps and the gallery joins, where it dries hard and wet-proof and holds the dry stone together the way mortar would in another country. The hollows are worn deep from centuries of this, and the pressing goes on day and night, a slow and tireless grinding. When one of the cakes is split open, you may see in the cross-grain the pale curl of something that was once a finger, a small fragment of bone among the fibres. The dwellers do not hide it. They say the basin takes everything back into its peat, and that the walls are made partly from those who went before.

They die into the mere. When an elder's shoulders have grown heavy with lichen past their last grooming, the family carries them down the causeway to the margin, where the peat is deepest and blackest, and lays them among the sphagnum mats. The moss takes them in. Over seasons they are absorbed, and the next pressing may carry their fibres into a new wall. This is not a grief that the dwellers hurry through, but it is not a grief they linger in either. Their memory is kept in the stone itself.

That memory is in the carvings, and the carvings are a reckoning. The stacked shapes on the upper walls are the years, one block for each slump of the rim: each great fall of stone from the wall above. The spirals are the mere: the rise and fall of water through a season, traced as a coil that tightens in drought and loosens in flood. A Reckoner reads the walls the way you might read a ledger, running a finger along the stacks and humming the count, and the count is how the dwellers know the age of anything. A gallery is so many slumps old. A terrace has been rebuilt four times. The mountain is always shedding, and the dwellers keep the tally, and they treat each new fresh-broken face on the rim as news.

They have a close relationship with the furred, heavy-bodied mammals that graze the causeways in loose herds. The dwellers call them through their knocks, and the herds come, and the herds watch the dwellers with that long, patient, judging gaze, and the dwellers watch back. They are not herded so much as kept company with. Their fleece is combed into cordage, their meat is salted in the lower cisterns, and their dung is spread on the mosses that feed them. When a sleek hunter comes through the reeds and takes one from the edge of a herd, the dwellers do not fight it. They draw the rest of the herd tighter, knock the alarm down the terraces, and wait. Later they go down to the churned water and take what the hunter leaves, and they pulse a low note over the place for a long while, which may be grief or may be thanks.

They do not know, or do not say, what the mountain is. They say the rim is the basin's shedding skin, and that the terraces are built on what falls, and that the mere rises and falls because it is the basin breathing. They do not believe the place will be finished, and they do not think it should be. The walls will sag and be shored. The causeways will sink and be laid again over the old ones. The mere will climb the lower tiers in the wet season and retreat from them in the dry, and the dwellers will rebuild each time, pressing new cakes, reading the stacks, and knocking their long slow count down through the stone.

At dusk, when the fog pools again on the mere and the bats come out in dark clusters under the galleries, you may see a single Presser on the highest tier, perched on the sounding stone with its throat pouch swelling. It is not calling anyone. It is only humming, very low, a long even pulse that the water carries down through every terrace, and that the whole basin, its walls, its herds, its moss and its dead, seems to hold in suspension until the next knock comes back up from below.
