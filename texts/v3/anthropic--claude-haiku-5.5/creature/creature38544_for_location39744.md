# Who Lives on the Drowned Mesa

Nothing here lives the way things live elsewhere. Life on the mesa has been folded into the water and the stone, and it shows its presence mostly as motion, disturbance, and the occasional shape that holds still a moment too long.

## The Pool-Dwellers

The first inhabitants you notice are the ones beneath the surface. In the deepest basins something rises, slowly, and its back breaks the skin of the water in a pale ridge that is almost indistinguishable from the flat growth spreading across the pool. It is thin, wide, and faintly translucent, like a leaf that has learned to swim. It does not come up for air, because it has no need of air. It rises to look.

Its eyes, if they are eyes, point upward, toward the wrong sky. The pool-dwellers are watching the blue expanse and the enormous plateau that floats in it, the world the reflections show. They seem to be gazing at a country they have never walked, and when you approach the water's edge they sink, unhurried, and the rings they leave spread too fast, as though they were trying to reach the far shore before you could see them go.

Sometimes two of them surface together, and their backs meet and slide past each other without touching. You may think they are mating, or fighting, or simply sharing the same patch of light. You cannot be sure. They never seem to need each other, but they never seem to be alone.

## The Threadweavers

The grey filaments on the wet stone are not plants in the way you would expect. They are a single organism, spread across the whole table, and it has no center. Each thread is a small piece of a larger attention, and when the water moves beneath them they lean in its direction and hold the shape of the current. In this way they keep a map of the water's circulation, a living record of which way the gyres are turning.

The threadweavers gather most thickly along the cracks and the staircases. Where the seven steps climb from the pool, the filaments grow up each tread in neat bands, and the top step, which is always dry, is covered in a dense pale weave that might be a deliberate tending. They seem to be the only things here that treat the built remains as worth keeping. Whether they do this out of habit, or because the builders once fed them, or because stone and thread have always grown together in this place, the threads do not say.

When a step is swallowed by the shifting rock, the threads retreat from it slowly, and when the step reappears, they return, as if they had only been waiting.

## The Wing-Drifters

The bird-shaped silhouettes in the mist are the closest things here to animals that you can name. They are long and narrow, with wings that span wider than a man's outstretched arms. Their wings never beat. They hang in the damp air as if the mist itself were a current they had agreed to trust, drifting through the layers and sliding from one depth to another without any visible effort.

They feed on what rises from the lip. Things dropped over the edge sometimes drift upward into the grey, and the wing-drifters are waiting for them. They catch these risen objects in their wings, fold them close, and carry them upward into the lower sky until they vanish. Where they take the things, nobody has seen. The plateau does not seem to mind.

They seldom come down to the stone. When one does, it lands with a sound like a wet cloth settling, and it stands on the rock with its wings half-open, not quite folded, as if it has forgotten the difference between flying and resting. It looks at you with no expression, and then it lifts without flapping and is gone into the mist.

## The Turning Below

The largest life here is the least seen. Far below the lip, past the mist and the sound of falling water, something turns over. You hear it as a long, slow displacement, the sound of a great weight shifting in a body of water that is much deeper than you can imagine. It happens once every few minutes, or once every hour, and each time the whole plateau responds. Slabs lurch. Springs burst open. The gyres in the pools change direction, and the threads on the stone lean the other way.

It is possible that the turning is what makes the plateau breathe. The pulse that rises through the rock, the swelling and draining of the basins, the slow hinge of the great circulating sheets: all of these may be the movement of this thing beneath, adjusting its weight, rolling in its sleep. It is never fully awake and never fully asleep. It does not come up to the stone. It is not interested in the stone, and it is not interested in you. It is simply enormous, and it is alive, and its breath is the mesa's breath.

## The Stillness That Walks Ahead

The last inhabitant is the one you are most likely to sense and least likely to see.

Sounds on the mesa arrive before their sources. A slab collapses and you hear it before you see the dust rise on the far side of the table. A step is struck and the knock reaches you a half-breath early. Look into a pool and your reflection moves a half-second ahead of you, so that you watch yourself begin a gesture before you make it.

Something is responsible for this, and it is not the water. It is something that has been here since the builders stopped building, something that keeps the staircases and doorways and the worn basin in working order, if working order is the right phrase. It is slightly out of step with the present. When you walk the stone, it walks a little ahead, reading the path before you reach it, shifting the crevices so the way you need is open. When you rest your hand on the basin's groove, the groove is already warm.

You never see it directly. At the edges of the mist, a shape appears stooped and patient, the outline of someone who has been tending something for a very long time. It is always moving away, always just beyond the place where you could turn to look. If you wait long enough, though, and let the roar of the water settle into something almost like a voice, you may feel it turn toward you. It is not hostile. It is not welcoming. It is simply aware, the way a caretaker is aware of a guest who has wandered into a house that is no longer quite theirs.

## The Shape of the Inhabitants

None of these beings share a home in any ordinary sense. The pool-dwellers watch a sky that is not theirs. The threadweavers hold the memory of currents and steps. The wing-drifters carry away what falls, and the great turning thing below sustains the plateau's motion. The stillness that walks ahead keeps the traces of the builders, keeping them in the only way that remains, which is by slowly allowing them to be forgotten and remembered at once.

Together they form something like a community of attentions, each caring for a different part of a world that is still deciding what it will become. They do not speak to one another, or if they do, the sound of their speech is lost in the roar of water. But they are all here, in the mist and the pools and the stone, and they are all, in their indifferent way, paying attention to the act of becoming.
