# The Ones Who Keep the Edge

Nothing in the basin is a stranger than the pale things that live in the clear pools, and nothing is easier to overlook. From the shore they look like tangles of wet thread, or the bleached roots of something drowned. You have to kneel at the rim, where the turquoise goes clear over the crust, before you see that the threads move. They are slow and deliberate, and they are not roots. They are bodies, or parts of one body, spread out across the floor of the pool like a net being knitted by hands that never tire.

They have no name for themselves, or if they do, it is a sound too low and too spread out for a mouth to make. The reeds call them *the lace*. The frogs ignore them. The birds, which wade the shallows with such care, never step where the pale threads lie.

## Their Bodies

Each body is soft and nearly translucent, the cream of skimmed milk with a faint blush where the warm water passes through it. Looking closely, you can see the internal channels, fine as hair, carrying the mineral-rich fluid from the fissures to every part of the body. Along each thread runs a fringe of cilia, thousands of them, that stir continually. They comb the water, drawing in the films of organic matter and the dissolved calcium and iron, and they sweep the spent water away in slow, pulsing currents. Where the fringe brushes the crust, the surface shimmers faintly, as if breathed on.

The bodies do not have a single center. You can cut a thread and it will not die. The cut end closes over, grows a new fringe, and begins feeding again, and the severed piece crawls off to find its own patch of crust. Whether this makes one creature or a thousand, the question has no answer here. They do not seem to need one.

## How They Feed

They eat the films, and in eating, they build.

When a body draws in the mineral water and takes what it needs, it secretes what it does not. The excess calcium and iron leave the body in fine, sticky exudate that hardens almost at once when it meets the air at the pool's edge. Each exudation is a small lacy bead, and the beads join, and the joins become the ridges. Every ridge in the basin is a record of feeding. The soft ripples, the folds that look like a cloth lifted and set down, the bubble craters with their identical rims: all of them began as the waste of something that was hungry.

The craters are the most curious part. The bubbles rise from below, from the fissures, and the lace has learned to wait for them. When a gas bubble breaks at the surface, a body beneath it extends a single thread up through the wet crust and seals the ring around the broken bubble, so the crater is finished in one motion. This is why the craters are all the same. They are made by the same gesture, repeated by bodies that have never once varied it.

## What They Want

The lace does not grow outward in a rush. It creeps. It can only advance where the water is warm, and the water is warm only where the fissures breathe, and the fissures breathe only at a pace set by the ground itself. So the threads push at the rim of every pool, laying down their soft white edge, and then they stop.

They do not cross the rim where the stone is brand new. The green plants press against that white line and never pass it, and the lace does not pass it either. It is not that they cannot. The fresh rock is still too soft to hold a body. It would crumble under a fringe, and the thread would break and fall back into the water, and the building would undo itself. So the lace keeps the edge. It stays just behind the newest stone and feeds on what is already settled, and it waits for the stone to harden enough to bear it.

This waiting is the whole of their life. The stone never hardens quite enough, because the very act of building softens it again. Each new bead of waste brings fresh minerals to the rim, which dissolve a little of the old stone as they settle, and the boundary moves a hair's breadth and no further. The lace is always almost ready to cross. It has been almost ready for longer than the basin can count.

## Their Relations

The others in the basin move around them with a kind of courtesy. The frogs press their speckled bellies into the mats a little deeper when a shadow of thread passes over the pool. The insects rise above the lace in their columns and never settle on it. The birds step high. Only the things that rise and vanish beneath the surface seem to share the water with the lace on equal terms, and even they keep to the deeper pools, where the crust is still soft and the threads have not yet reached.

The bulbous growths, pale pink and violet and slick as wet fruit, are the closest thing to kin. They grow on the margins where the lace and the mats meet, and when the lace presses against them, they open their branching arms and receive the fringe. Some of the growths have been eaten down to stumps by the lace's slow feeding, and some have grown up out of the bare stone where the lace left a gap. Nobody can say which is the host and which the guest. The branches are symmetrical in a way that seems deliberate, and when the lace touches them, the branches shiver, and a faint sweet smell rises, like something very old saying yes.

## Time

The lace has no sense of hours. The mist thickens and thins, and the frogs call on their cycle, and the lace moves with neither. Its time is measured in the rise of bubbles, and the bubbles rise whenever the ground decides. A single thread may take a season to extend the width of a hair, or it may not move for an age, and to the lace these are the same. It is not waiting for anything in particular. It is simply staying in the shape of waiting.

If you could ask it what it was building toward, it would not understand the question, because the building is the whole of it. Stone is the lace's breath. Each ridge is a breath it has let go, and each breath is taken in again as the dissolving water carries the old stone back to the fissures. Nothing is kept, and nothing is lost. The lace is a process that has learned to be its own result.

## The Edge

Sometimes, at the very rim of the freshest stone, one thread lifts itself free of the crust. It does not cross. It hangs above the white line, quivering in the warm air, its fringe combing nothing but mist. It stays like that for a long time, perhaps as long as it takes the mist to gather and drop and gather again, and then it lowers itself back to the water and goes on feeding.

It is not a gesture of hope, or of fear. The lace has no word for either. If it has anything like a purpose, it is this: to keep the basin from finishing. Finish would mean the building stopping, the dissolving stopping, the balance tipping into a stillness that is no longer the holding of breath but simply the end of breath. The lace stays at the edge so that the edge stays alive. It is the basin's unfinished part, the living margin where the new stone meets the water and neither wins.

You can feel this if you kneel at the rim of a clear pool and wait. The warm water rises over your fingers. The fringe stirs, slow and patient, combing nothing from the air. Beneath it, the pale threads lace the crust in a pattern that repeats forever, and the rock beneath your hand is soft and new and already old, and something down there is still, unhurriedly, deciding not to finish.
