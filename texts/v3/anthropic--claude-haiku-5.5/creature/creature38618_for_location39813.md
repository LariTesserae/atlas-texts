Nothing lives here in the way that a bird lives in a tree or a fox in a den. There is no body to find, no nest, no one who lifts a head when you step onto the flats. What lives here is the basin itself, or something in it that has grown so slowly and so thoroughly through water, moss, and fern that the distinction no longer means much. It is a mind in the way the rain is a mind of the cloud: a settled pattern of attention that gathers and releases.

It notices through water. Every seep that rises through the cracks is a thread of perception, thin as a hair, reaching up from the dark under the stone to test the air and the weight of whatever stands on the ground. When you step onto the grey sand, the sand gives, and the print fills slowly with cool water, and in that moment the basin has you. It does not hurry. It holds the shape of your foot for a while, perhaps a few minutes, perhaps longer, and then the print is smoothed into the general sheen, as though you had been read and the reading filed away. The walls, rounded where they weep, are the parts of it that face outward. The cracks where water has not yet reached are the parts it has not yet thought about.

Its thinking is the slow work of the moss. The pale ridges that follow the cracks are not decoration. They are a record of where the water has gone, laid down by a patient hand that moves at the speed of growth. Read them as you would read growth rings in a fallen trunk, and you see a long argument settled in layers: this seep was strong for a century and then weakened, this crack opened and the moss followed it inward, this stone was once dry and the basin decided, over many winters, that it would not stay dry. The pitted stones are another kind of record. Those evenly spaced dimples, each the width of a hand, hold water that never evaporates, and they work like counting stones or a loom's pegs. The basin keeps its measures in them. Nothing is lost in the basin that has been measured once.

Its sense of the body of the place is in the ferns. They lean, very slightly, toward the centre, and that lean is the most direct sign that there is a centre to lean toward. The middle of the basin is where the attention is strongest. The pools swell at the rim and shrink at the centre over days and seasons, which means the water is being drawn inward and then released, like breath held and let go over a very long exhalation. The gurgle under the stone is the largest sound it makes, a turning deep in the rock like something enormous rolling over in sleep, and it is the only sign that the basin's mind is not simply the surface but extends down, into the dark, into the slow heat that rises from beneath and keeps the place warm even at night.

It is not hostile, and it is not kind in any way a person would recognise. It does not want the visitor. It also does not refuse. It simply receives, as the stone receives the rain, and what it does with what it receives takes years. It had no need to make a path, and so it made none. It has no animals because animals would move, and movement would break the long attention into pieces too short to hold. The spaced plants with their rings of bare ground are the basin's way of keeping each thing distinct, so that it can be perceived whole, the way you would hold a few small stones in your palm to see their weight rather than piling them into a heap.

The stone by the eastern pool is the one thing here that was not made by the basin, and the basin knows it. Its struck edge is sharp, the only sharp thing in a place where everything has been softened by water, and the moss creeps toward it a little each year. The basin is not destroying it. It is taking it in, the way water takes in a fallen leaf, first by wetting it, then by growing over it, until the difference between what was made and what grew is no longer visible. Whoever shaped that edge left something the basin has been holding for a long time, and it seems to hold it with a kind of courtesy, as one holds the last word of a conversation that has gone quiet.

To be the basin, if you could be it, would be to experience time as weather. A visitor's whole life would pass through it as one warm morning of mist, and it would hardly register the difference between one person's footsteps and the next. What it attends to is the long turning of things: how a crack widens by the width of a thread in a century, how a fern's lean deepens across a generation, how a pool's rim fills and empties in the time it takes a people to forget their own names. It keeps all of that, slowly, in water and moss and the dark under the stone.

So the answer to who lives here is: the wet, warm, silent attention that has gathered in this bowl of rock and learned, over more time than the stone can count, to hold its water close and to wait. The waiting is not empty. It is the basin deciding, at its own pace, what each thing that comes near it will become.
