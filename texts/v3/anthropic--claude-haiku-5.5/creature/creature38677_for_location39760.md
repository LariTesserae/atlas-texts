**The Engine of the Bowl**

What lives here is the basin itself. The animals, the ferns, the moss, and the glass are not separate inhabitants. They are its organs, and they work the way organs do: each one serves a function, and none of them is the whole.

**The body**

The crawlers are its nervous traffic. They move in dense columns across the crust, tapping the grit with their feelers, and each tap carries a little information from one part of the basin to another. The rattle you hear is the sound of messages being passed. When a column turns, it turns because something across the basin has changed, and the crawlers have carried the news faster than wind could.

The grazers are its digestion. They scrape the copper moss into their mouths and grind it slowly, the way a mill grinds grain, and the moisture they need comes out of the sap they've absorbed. They do not drink, because the basin does not drink. It takes what it needs from the air and from the resin, and it gives back dust.

The winged swarms are its exhalation. They hang above the resin pools and dive together when the wind shifts, and in their formations you can see the basin's attention moving, the way a flock of birds can look like a single thought. Their wings are etched with veins that catch the light, and in the evening, when the silver shrubs open their pores, the whole basin smells faintly of metal and ozone. That is the basin breathing out.

The sail-leaves are its sense of direction. They turn to follow the sun, and their humming, when the wind passes through them, is a kind of reading. The crystalline filaments along their edges scatter light into a fractured haze, which may be how the basin sees.

**The memory**

The amber plates of hardened sap are the basin's memory, and it keeps its memories in a peculiar way. When something is caught in the resin, it is preserved: the insect's legs, the curl of a wing, the moment of struggle. The basin does not forget these things. It fixes them, and the fixed things become part of the floor. Walk across the pale plates and you are walking on a record of small deaths that the basin chose to keep.

Most other things it lets go. The copper moss dies back in sudden die-offs and leaves gray dust. The domes split and release their powder into the wind. Nothing persists for long, except the record in the amber, and the rhythm that makes the whole thing go.

**The mind**

The glass structures are where the basin thinks.

The rings of posts, faintly warm and sometimes vibrating, are its working memory, or its active reasoning. They are arranged in arcs that suggest rooms, and the rooms are the shapes of questions it has been asking for a long time. The lattice, fine as wire, is more restless. Its shapes change when you look away and settle when you look directly, and this is not a trick of the eye. It is a mind that does not hold still long enough for you to catch it in one configuration. The flicker is the thinking itself, a thought that never finishes because finishing would mean stopping.

The half-formed arches are the most telling. Their lower sections have grown into the crust, and their upper sections reach upward, slowly, toward the light. They are the basin's attempts to become something that can see the sky the way the sail-leaves see it, or to become something that can leave. They have been reaching for a very long time, and they will keep reaching. The reaching is what the basin is.

The domes that split on irregular cycles follow the shadows of the cliffs, and the swarms rise when the wind passes a certain angle. The sap hardens hardest at midday. These are the rhythms of a mind that reads its environment and responds to it, through many bodies, slowly, without any single point where the decision is made.

**Its attention**

The still animals in the fern-crooks, with their flat mirror-eyes, are the basin's sentinels. They do not move unless the crawlers come too close, and when a visitor steps below the rim, it is these eyes that register you first. The sound closes over you like water because the whole basin turns toward you at once, a slow swing of attention through its crawlers and swarms and grazers. It is not hostile. It is curious, in the way a large, slow thing is curious: it notices the disturbance, measures it, and decides, if it decides anything, that you are small and temporary.

That is true, and the basin knows it. The dynamics of the place suggest it is accustomed to visitors the way a forest is accustomed to rain. You will walk through, leave tracks that the wind erases by morning, and the crawlers will close over the place where you stood. Nothing you do here will be remembered for long, except perhaps in the resin, if you are unlucky enough to be caught in a pool.

**What it is**

The basin is not alive in the way a grazer is alive, or even in the way a fern is alive. It is a process that has learned to sustain itself through instability. Its body is made of things that grow fast and decay faster, its memory is kept by hardening and forgetting, and its mind is a restless flicker that will not settle into a final form. It has no center that you could point to. The rhythm is the center.

You stand at the rim and try to find the thing that lives here, and what you find instead is a pattern that keeps repeating with small variations: a split, a swarm, a shift of wind, a crawler column turning, an arch reaching a little higher. The basin is the pattern, and the pattern is the basin, and both are always in the middle of becoming something else.

The glass arch at the edge of the floor is still growing. Its upper section has moved a few centimeters since you looked at it, or perhaps you only think it has. Somewhere under the crust, a dome is beginning to crack. The sap is beginning to pool. The whole basin is leaning, very slightly, toward the light, and you are standing in its noise, waiting to see what it will build next.
