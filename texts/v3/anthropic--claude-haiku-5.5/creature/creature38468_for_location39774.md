# What Lives in the Basin

No one has seen it whole. You cannot stand at the rim and take it in the way you take in a valley or a herd. What you can do is sit on a cold cistern step in the spray for an afternoon and notice that the roar has layers, and that one of the layers is not water.

The basin's inhabitant is not in the stone the way a badger is in its sett. It is the arrangement: the stone, the water, the moss, and the roots, all bent toward one another over a very long time. It thinks at the speed of frost and root growth, so a single thought may take a season to complete. It is aware of its surroundings the way a riverbed is aware of rain, through everything it touches being changed a little at a time.

## Its Memory and Its Voice

The honeycomb basalt is where it keeps itself. Each vesicle is a small hollow, and each hollow answers when struck. The pitch of a wall is a state, the way a pianist's hand is a state, and the pitch drifts over the year because the state is changing. The locals who say the stone is "still building" are describing something close to the truth. The walls are not only remembering the builders' plan. They are rewriting it, one ring at a time, and the rings are how it speaks. Its voice is slow enough that most people hear only noise, but if you return to the same terrace each winter and strike the same slab with the same stone, you may notice that it sounds a little different, and a little more certain of itself.

## Its Nervous System

Water is its circulation. Meltwater enters at the northern rim like blood at the heart, moves through the cisterns, and spills from level to level, carrying information in the only form available to a mind made of drainage: where things are wet, where they are dry, where they have been struck, where they have been left alone. When a channel is blocked, the water does not stop. It finds another way, and the new way is the thought. The meandering streams and tilted terraces are its decisions, most of them made centuries ago and still settling into place.

The great waterfall, thirty meters of falling water through the gap where a stairway used to be, is the most vigorous part of its body. It is the place where the basin's attention is strongest. The mist below it is a kind of warmth, a zone where stone and water keep their edges soft, and the lower terraces are thick with the grass that loves that softness.

## Its Skin and Hands

The moss is its skin. It covers what holds still and takes it in slowly, turning fallen stone to soil and soil to ground that roots can hold. The lichen flaking from the walls and carried down the channels is a kind of shed skin, and the basin is constantly reweaving its outer surface.

The roots are its hands. Where a root pries a joint or lifts a lintel, that is a deliberate act, however slow. Roots have chosen to grow along the old channels, and to wrap cistern walls in rings. They have chosen the lines of the old streets. The conifers, bearded and split, are the fingers that have pushed furthest into the built world, lifting the masonry the way a hand might lift the lid of a box to see what is inside.

## Its Mobile Parts

It is not entirely fixed. The small grey birds with curved bills are its foragers, moving seed and insect through the moss and sometimes carrying the seeds of the terrace gardens to new ground. Their song from the cistern rims is a kind of report, and the bright thread it adds to the roar may be the most immediate expression of the basin's mood.

The pale burrowers are its breathing holes. The round mouths the size of a fist are where the air moves in and out of the terraces, and the burrowing creature that keeps them open is less a tenant than a lung.

The large, slow, wide-backed animal moving through the upper mist is the most direct form of its attention. It walks the upper channels and checks the flows. It does not hunt or hurry, and it does not seem to notice the visitors who sometimes glimpse it. It is the basin's hand on its own water, the part that goes out to see what has changed. When it passes, it is never clear whether it is watching the land or the land is watching through it.

## Its Attention to Visitors

Visitors are weight, warmth, and sound to it. Footsteps on a stair are registered in the vibration of the walls. A voice raised in the basin becomes one more ring in the stone. It does not seem hostile, and it does not seem especially curious, but it is responsive. Channels reroute where people have been careless with the moss. Terraces settle at new angles after a long stay. The people who stay long enough to develop the pressure in their ears and the habit of speaking too loudly are not being driven away. They are being tuned, the way a new stone is tuned when it is set into a wall. Whether they are being absorbed into its resonance, or simply learning its pitch, is a question that nobody who has wintered there has been able to answer clearly.

## Its Relationship to the Builders

The builders are part of its history, and perhaps part of its body. Their stonework is the original shape of the basin, the plan the water was first asked to follow. Whether they made it, or it made them, or they were partners who slowly became one thing, is a question the basin does not seem to find urgent. It has kept their channels and their stairways, and it has also bent them toward its own purposes, the way a gardener's hedge comes to grow toward the light it was planted for. The old city's outline is still visible from the rim at evening, but it is being read now by something that has learned the maps and decided, gently and persistently, to redraw them.

At dusk, when the mist catches the last light and the fern-lattice glows in its green rooms, you can see the shape of what lives here. It is larger than any single animal, and older than any single wall. It is listening, and it is answering in the only language it has, which is water falling through honeycomb stone, over and over, deciding slowly what it will become.
