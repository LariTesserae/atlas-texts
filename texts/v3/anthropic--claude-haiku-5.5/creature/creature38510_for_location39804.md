# Who Keeps the Held Breath

You notice the inhabitants last, because they move the way the trunks do. A figure leans against a pale column in the half-shade, and for a moment it looks like a knot in the bark or a hanging cluster of moss. Then it shifts its weight, and the lichen on its shoulders stirs.

The people of the town are tall and narrow, built for climbing and for the long stillness of fog-watch. Their skin is pale, nearly the grey-white of the lichen, with a faint green undertone where the blood runs close to the surface. It is a camouflage as much as an adaptation, since the light here is always filtered through leaves, and skin that absorbs little of it keeps the body cool. Their hands are long and fine-fingered, with pads on the inner joints that grip bark and resin without slipping. Their feet are broad and splayed, so they can stand on a sloping root for hours. Many of them carry small scars along the forearms, pale rings where a builder once bit or a branch once cut too deep, and these are read as a kind of family history.

They wear very little. Their clothing is woven from the fine grey filaments that drape the trunks, which are tough, light, and pull moisture away from the body and hold it in the cloth. Some wear hoods of fern fronds stitched together, and some wear nothing but the lichen they have grown on their own shoulders and backs, trimmed and tended like a garden.

## Keepers and Kept

The people do not build the town, but they keep it. Their relationship with the builders is older than any record, and it runs in both directions. The people bring the builders lichen from the galleries and moss from the mausoleum shafts. They tend the sealed chambers, reopening them when the air goes stale and closing them when the cisterns need protection. In return the builders resin the cracks in the people's dwellings, seal their cisterns, and keep the trunks from rotting through.

A keeper spends much of the day walking the trunks, touching the mortar with the flat of a hand. The surface carries faint patterns, rises and dips, the changing texture of resin as it hardens or cracks. Keepers read these the way others might read a face. They can tell from the feel of the wall whether the builders are content, whether a hollow has begun to weep, whether a tower is leaning too far.

They speak little aloud. Their voices are low and breathy, pitched to carry a few meters through the canopy and no farther, and conversation is often conducted by touch and scent. A keeper will press two fingers to another's wrist and then drag them along the bark to leave a trail, and the trail itself is the message. Visitors mistake this for silence. It is closer to a second language written on the surfaces of the town, and a keeper who knows the marks can carry on a conversation across three districts without raising their voice.

There is an old discipline to their patience. A keeper does not hurry a task, and when a branch must be pruned or a bore must be drilled into a cistern, the work takes days, and the people who do it sit in long stillness between strokes, watching the light move on the leaves. They have no word for waiting, because waiting is simply what the work is.

## The Builders

The builders are the pale, many-legged creatures that crawl the trunks like slow tides of cream. Their bodies are segmented and soft-skinned beneath a chitinous plate, and each segment carries a pair of spinnerets along its underside, through which they extrude the resin. They move with a rippling, almost liquid gait, and when they settle to work they fold their legs beneath them with a sound like wet paper.

They are not mindless. They respond to the mortar's patterns, adjusting their work to the surfaces the keepers have touched and the marks the keepers have left. A builder will ignore a crack that the keepers have not marked, and rush to a hollow that they have. Over generations the two kinds of creature have learned each other's rhythms so thoroughly that it is hard to say which one directs the other. They have shaped the town together.

Builders are gentle with people. Their mandibles are small and blunt, adapted for grazing lichen, and a bite is more a nip. The scars on keepers' arms are mostly from mistakes, not malice. Young builders are sometimes kept in separate galleries, where keepers teach them the patterns of the mortar before they are released to the trunks.

## The Quiet Companions

The smaller life fills the spaces the people and builders do not. The soft-winged insects drift through the canopy in clouds, and keepers sometimes cup them in their hands to read their wing-patterns, which seem to carry information about the weather. The gliders, flat and leaf-like, are less welcome. They drop from crown to crown and sometimes steal lichen or seed from the galleries, but keepers tolerate them, since the gliders also carry the hard-shelled fruits across the ridges, and the seeds inside only ripen after such a journey.

The grazing animals move slowly among the galleries, nosing at the lichen, and the builders graze alongside them without conflict. The grazers are large and placid, with heavy, deliberate bodies and long, prehensile lips. They do not flee and do not charge. They simply continue, and the people walk around them.

## The Watchers

At dusk the pale nodes on the trunks begin to open. They are the eyes that no one has fully explained. Each one is a shallow cup of translucent tissue, lined with something that gleams faintly when the light goes. As the last sun drops below the terraces, they turn toward the west, toward the ridges and the lava shelf, and hold still for the length of the night.

Keepers have a tradition of sitting beneath the nodes after dark, not speaking, pressing their palms to the bark. Whether the nodes see anything, or what they see, is unknown. The keepers do not claim to know. They say only that the nodes are part of the town, as the builders and the gliders and the cisterns are part of it, and that a place which holds its breath needs something awake in the dark to keep watch while it does.

By full night the town is nearly silent. The wind combs the filaments, the seeds knock softly through the leaves, and the people sleep in sealed chambers that hold their own damp air. Somewhere in the lower cisterns, water gathered one drop at a time from nothing waits for morning, and the town goes on breathing slowly through the dark.
