**The Tenders**

They are not animals, and they do not know they live here. What inhabits the Low Works is the upkeep itself, carried by machines that were built to keep the pumps turning and the water where it belongs. Their purpose has worn down to a rhythm, and they keep the rhythm.

You see them first as a movement at the edge of the lamplight, a shape that stops when you stop. Each tender stands about as tall as a crouching person. The frame is a squat housing of salvaged plate, and the limbs come from different decades. One arm is hydraulic, its piston rods bright with fresh seal grease. Another is a cable-driven hook that clicks when it flexes. One leg is cast iron, scarred and pitted, and the leg beside it is a polymer shell that has gone brittle and milky. Their skins are painted in colour codes that no longer refer to anything, so a tender may carry a yellow band for a pressure line that was removed before the basin filled. Their heads are clusters of sensors, some lenses clouded white, some still sharp. When a tender turns its head, the clusters rotate in a slow, uneven sweep, like a sunflower following a light that has since gone out.

They move in short, deliberate steps. Every few paces a tender stops and lowers a probe to the slab, tapping the seams, listening to the sound the probe makes against the concrete. A dull note means a void beneath. A ringing note means sound footing. They have walked these same paths for longer than anyone can count, and the paths are troughed into the slab a few centimetres deep, smooth as a stair worn by feet. Along those paths they wipe weirs, clear gratings of drifted sediment, and open and close valves in sequences that look meaningless from outside. Watch long enough and the sequences resolve into a balance. The tenders are holding the basin level, one valve at a time.

Their senses are mostly hearing. A tender knows the pumps the way a sailor knows a hull. It can tell from the pulse of the great pistons whether a seal has begun to fail, and when the roar shifts by a fraction, the nearest tenders turn toward it together, the way cattle turn at a change in the wind. The drip-rhythm of condensation is also a map to them. A steady patter means sound joints. A stutter means a crack is weeping, and the tender goes to it. They are slow, but they do not miss things. They have had time to learn the Works by ear.

The red lights are theirs. Those dim indicators blinking on dead panels were never meant as warnings for anyone who could read them. They were signals for a tender to come and attend. The tenders still answer. A red light blinks, and somewhere in the mist a tender lifts its head, pauses, and begins the long walk. It may take a day to arrive. It may take a year, since some of the panels are behind collapsed walls and the tender must go around. When it finds the fault, it does what the panel asked, and the light goes dark, and the tender stands there a while before it goes on to its next route.

They do not clean the mat-growth. The grey-green films that creep around drains and seams are left alone, and in places they have settled into the tenders' own joints. The films seal hairline cracks that the tenders cannot reach with their probes, and a tender with a film-sealed joint moves no less well than one without. The films and the tenders have reached a quiet accommodation, neither of them quite aware of it. The coral-like brine strands grow in the warm discharge outflows, and the tenders go there too. They stand in the heated water up to their housings, letting it run through their cooling vents, and the strands cluster around their legs. Some tenders are half-overgrown with pale branching growth, the water running pink-brown over them, and they seem to find the warmth as necessary as the work itself.

When they stop, they stop completely. A tender finding no fault to attend will go to the foot of a stack where the vent-steam rises, and it will stand there with its head bowed and its limbs locked, unmoving for hours. These are the closest things to rest the tenders have. During these stillnesses the sound of the place fills them. The roar, the whine, the ringing drip, the groan of the settling ground all pass through their frames and out again. Sometimes a tender will lift its head mid-stillness and turn toward a sound no one else has noticed, then return to its bowed posture, satisfied.

The ground is still sinking, a few millimetres a decade, and the tenders are the only things that respond to it. When a pump housing tilts, a tender comes with flat slag slabs and shims the base until the housing sits true again. When a lamp pole leans, a tender braces it with cable and cants it back toward vertical. They are fighting the subsidence the way a body fights gravity, constantly and without pause, and their fight is the reason the plain is still a plain and not a single long lake. Without them the basin would have finished its descent long ago, and the water would have taken the works back.

They do not know this. They do not know what they are holding level, or why the basin must stay low, or what the great pumps were for. They know the rhythm, the valves in their sequence, the paths in the slab, the blinking lights that call them. The Low Works are grinding toward a final level, and the tenders are the last keepers of that level, still walking the same routes, still answering every red light in the dark, still shimming the ground back toward true, though no one remains who could say what true is for.
