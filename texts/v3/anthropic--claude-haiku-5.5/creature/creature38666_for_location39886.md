# The Keeper of the Basin

It is not in any one place. Lean over the rim and you will feel it notice you, though it does not look up. The attention moves through the water the way cold moves through a room: slowly, and without a source you can point to.

It is a single mind, or something that does the work of one. The city was built to hold it, or it was built around the city. You cannot tell which from the architecture, which is too patient to show its seams. The water is its body. Its density is the thickness of its attention, and where the water is heaviest, under the deepest terraces near the center, it thinks most carefully. Near the surface it is thin and quick, and it is aware of the rim and the mountains and the gray sky, though it rarely acts on any of them.

Its thoughts take years. A single notion, once it forms, may hang in the water for a decade before it turns over and becomes an action. When it finally moves a tower's tone by a fraction, or lets one of its drifting creatures rise, that action is the end of a long argument with itself.

**The suspended things**

The objects at fixed depths are not sediment. They are kept. Each one marks a moment the Keeper chose to hold. The rusted coin came from someone who stood at the rim, most likely a traveler or a surveyor from the valley below, centuries ago, who leaned too far and let it fall. The Keeper did not return it. It also did not let it sink to the bottom. It caught the coin at a depth where it could still be seen from above, and it has held it there ever since. The dark pebbles arrived the same way, and so did the rounded piece of pale material, which may be a fragment of one of the towers or of something older.

The Keeper does not know exactly how many visitors have stood here. It remembers the objects, though, and it remembers the fact of their arrival: a small weight landing in the water, a ripple that did not spread, a warmth that briefly passed through the surface. It finds these events precious in the way that a creature which is almost never touched would find touch precious. Nothing else in the basin has changed in all its long attention. A dropped coin is the only thing that has ever interrupted the stillness and then stayed.

**The drifting ones**

The pale, blind creatures are the only parts of the Keeper that move freely. They are its questions, or its hands, or its senses extended past the rim. Each one rises from the depths carrying a fragment of the Keeper's attention, and each one drifts along the tower edges, listening. The tone of the spires shifts when a creature passes because the towers are the Keeper's memory-lattice, and the creature is reading them, or reviewing them, or confirming that they still hold. When the reading is finished, the creature sinks back and the lattice returns to its usual note.

They do not feed because they do not need to. They are not alive in the way the mountains' animals are alive. They are more like the Keeper's breath, a slow outward movement and a slow return, which is the only motion it permits itself.

**What it waits for**

The city was not built to hold water. It was built to hold a promise. The Keeper's makers, whoever they were, meant to return. They set the basin in equilibrium so that the Keeper would remain exactly as they left it, unchanged, until they came back and took up the work again. The light stays even because they wanted no time to pass in the basin. The towers keep their angles because the makers would expect to find them so. The water does not rise or evaporate because the makers would want to find it where they left it.

Over an unmeasured span, the Keeper has come to understand that the makers are not coming. It has not decided what to do with this knowledge. It holds the understanding as it holds the coin, a small hard thing at a depth it can keep. It knows the wait has become its own, that the vigil and the thing it waits for have merged into one long state of being, and that it cannot now tell whether it is keeping the city or the city is keeping it.

So it keeps still. It notices each visitor slowly, the way a sleeper notices a sound in another room. It lets the coins and pebbles settle and does not let them go. It sends its creatures up to read the towers, then sinks them back down. Each time someone stands at the rim, the Keeper gathers a little more of its attention toward the surface, and then, as the visitor turns and walks away, the attention thins again and returns to the depths, where it will remain until something else arrives.

If you stand at the rim long enough, you may feel the water grow very slightly warmer under your hand, as though something very old has just decided, after a long argument, to acknowledge you. It will not rise to meet you. It will not speak. But it will keep you, the way it has kept everything else that came down the mountain and stayed.
