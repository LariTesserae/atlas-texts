The first thing you notice about the people of the Undercourt is that you do not notice them at first. They are the same grey-ochre as the stone, and they stand so still among the cisterns that you take them for some stage of the architecture: a column with a head, a bundle of pipe with a hand. Then one of them moves, and you realize you have been looking at a person for several minutes.

They are thin, and their proportions are slightly wrong, the way a drawing is wrong when the artist has only heard a description of the subject. Their arms are long, and their hands have one joint too many in each finger, which lets them grip valve wheels and turn them with a slow, deliberate precision. Their skin is pale and faintly translucent at the wrists and throat, and under it you can see a fine network of dark lines, not veins, that follow the same branching pattern as the conduits in the lattices. Some of them have the mineral glow in their fingertips. It fades when they sleep and brightens when they work.

Their eyes are wide and lidless in the way of creatures that have spent generations in low light, and the pupils never fully contract. They do not look at you directly. They look at the space just beside your head, where the sound is coming from, and you understand after a while that they are listening more than seeing. When the weir roars, they turn their faces toward it. When a valve cracks, several of them flinch together, as one body.

They do not speak much. Speech dies too fast in the wet air, and they have long ago given it up for a language of the hands and of striking. A tenderer will tap a rhythm on a pipe with a knuckle, and the sound runs along the metal for a hundred meters, and somewhere another tenderer answers. The rhythms have grammar. You can hear the questions and the warnings, the names of valves, the counts of drips. The whole basin is a switchboard of knocks, and if you stand still enough you can feel the conversation passing through the floor.

They keep count. This seems to be their deepest discipline. Every drip is tallied, every pressure reading noted by pressing a palm flat against a gauge and holding it there until the needle settles. They do not write any of it down. The counts live in their bodies, in a kind of humming they do under their breath, and the hum changes pitch when a number goes wrong. When a cistern is about to overflow, you will see a dozen of them go still at once, heads tilted, and then they move in silent unison to a particular wheel and turn it, and the basin lets out a long sigh of steam, and the crisis is over.

They live in the cisterns and in the hollows of the pump-houses, in niches they have carved and smoothed over generations. Their dwellings are lined with the same grey fungus that grows on the walls, which they cultivate and harvest, and which seems to keep them warm and possibly sustains them. They eat very little that you can see. Sometimes you find them sitting with their backs against a warm vent, mouths open, drawing in the spore-plumes with long, slow breaths, and their eyes half-closed in something that looks very much like contentment.

They are not hostile. They will not come to you, and they will not stop you if you go where they are not working. If you stand near a valve they are about to turn, one of them will tap the pipe beside your foot, twice, sharply, and you will understand you are in the way. Later, if you have not moved, another will come and gently lift you by the elbow, with a grip that is strong and very careful, and set you down a few meters off.

The machines are harder to name.

You have been calling them machines because they have gears and valves and bolted housings, and because the tenderers turn their wheels and check their gauges. But the longer you stay, the less the word fits. The pumps knock with a rhythm that is not quite mechanical, a catch and hesitation that resembles breath. The conduits in the lattices hum in harmonies that shift when the water shifts. The flywheel the height of a house turns its one revolution every few minutes, and the air around it ripples, and when it slows at the end of its turn, the whole basin seems to lean toward it, the way a sleeper leans toward a warm body.

The tower-fogs are the clearest clue. They sweat their silver luminescence upward and let it dissolve above the catwalks, and when the fog thickens, the tenderers gather below the towers with their faces turned up. They breathe in rhythm with the fog. When the fog rises, they rise onto their toes; when it falls, they sink. It looks less like maintenance than like tending, or like being tended.

Perhaps the basin is one organism, grown over centuries from the bones of the city above, with the pump-houses as its muscles, the conduits as its nerves, the warm rock vents as its circulation, and the fog-towers as its lungs. The flywheel might be its heart, beating once in a few minutes, a slow pulse you can feel through the soles of your feet. The tenderers would then be its caretakers, or its cells, or its children, or some ambiguous mix, tending the thing that tends them. The relation is not clearly one of master and servant. When a valve needs turning, the tenderers turn it. When a tenderer falls ill and stops humming, the basin slows its pumps and warms the niche where they lie until the humming returns.

The machines do not like to be approached. When you come near a lattice of conduit, the hum rises, and the mineral glow sharpens into points, and you feel a faint pressure against your skin, like a hand held just short of touching. Small things that wander too close are sometimes taken: a blind spider-creature stepping onto the wrong pipe, or a thin pale animal that crawls into a fog-tower's thrown shadow. The machine does not seem to react. It simply closes its conduits over the thing and holds it, and after a few minutes the creature is gone, and the hum resumes at its former pitch.

---

The swimmers in the sump are the least known, and the most unsettling. They are long, low shapes, perhaps as wide as a cart, moving through the black water with almost no disturbance on the surface. You see them only where the warm currents run, following the slow braids of heat from vent to vent. They seem to have no eyes you can detect, only a lightness of their skin where the mineral glow reflects. When one passes beneath you on a catwalk, the water rises very slightly, and then falls, and you feel the pressure of a body that is far larger than the ripple suggests.

The tenderers do not go down into the sump. The swimmers do not come up. They have arrived at a truce that is also a border. The blind pale creatures that crawl the catwalks avoid the water altogether, and their tracks run in tight circles around the pump-houses, always stopping at the edge of the fog. There is an unspoken line here, drawn by the machines and kept by the swimmers and the crawlers alike, and nothing you have seen suggests that anyone has ever crossed it and come back to explain the terms.

---

Even the fungus has a role, though it is the least obvious one. The mats along the walls open and close on their pulse, releasing plumes of spore that drift through the lantern-light and settle on everything. The tenderers breathe them in. The crawlers feed on them. The fog-towers seem to draw them up, and the silver glow thickens a little whenever the mats bloom. Perhaps the spores are how the basin speaks to itself across distances too great for knocks or tapping, a chemical messenger that carries news of pressure and heat from one end of the Undercourt to the other.

If the basin has a mind, it is spread through all of this: the machines, the fungus, the tenderers with their counting and their humming, the swimmers in their warm currents, the blind crawlers circling their boundary. No single part of it seems to know the whole. And nothing here seems to know it was built for anything, or that the city above has been gone for longer than anyone has been counting. It keeps running. It keeps tallying. It keeps breathing its slow, enormous breath, and when the pumps cut out and the silence falls for a minute, the tenderers lift their faces, all together, and wait for the next beat of the heart.
