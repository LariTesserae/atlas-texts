**What Lives in the Undercroft**

The nearest of them are the grey crawlers, and the first thing to understand about them is that they are hard to count. From the threshold of the crack they look like a single moving skin laid over the walls, a texture of shell-backed bodies packed shoulder to shoulder across every mineral sheet. Watch one long enough and you see its edges: a jointed leg, a feeler folded flat, a pale underside marked with a row of tiny spiracles that open and close in unison with the drips. They do not wander. They shift. When a crawler at the edge of the mass is pushed off by the press behind it, it falls a body's length onto the sloping blocks and is taken before it lands, by a fish or a spider or a neighbor. The mass closes over the gap like water.

Their chittering is the chamber's constant undertone, and it is not random. Listen for the repetitions. A rising scrape, a pair of clicks, a long falling trill. The same few shapes recur, passed from body to body across the walls, always in the same order, the way a sentence repeats when no one has anything new to say. It may be a language. It may be only the sound of a population keeping track of its own density, one body counting the next. There is no sign that any of them has ever tried to say something different.

The blind pale fish hang in the standing pools in such numbers that the water looks thick with them. Each is translucent to the spine. The mineral glow passes through them and comes out the far side, so that a pool of fish is a shallow bowl of light with dark shapes drifting inside it, their bodies lit from within like lanterns made of skin. They do not seem to need eyes, and they do not seem to hunt. They hover, and when a crawler falls into their water they turn together and the crawler is gone. Their mouths are too small for anything but the smallest prey. Something in them is patient in a way that feels deliberate, though nothing about them is ever planned.

The spiders are the restless ones, though their restlessness has a rhythm. They climb the stalactites on joints that seem to multiply as they move, and their legs drum on the stone as they go, a soft rapid tapping that falls into step with the drips. Sometimes the drumming and the dripping lock together for a moment, two pulses that agree, and then drift apart again. The spiders are the only things in the chamber that seem to be keeping time on purpose.

Above them the ceiling is a single organism made of many, or many made to look like one. The winged bodies cluster so tightly that their wings overlap into a continuous dark membrane, and when they breathe, the whole mass rises and falls a few inches, slowly, so that the rock seems to inhale. They make the bird-sound you heard on arrival, a single wingbeat multiplied through the echo, but if you watch them closely you will see that no one of them is making it. The sound is the sum of them pressed together. Nothing in the cluster has room to be a single animal, and so the cluster is the animal.

The threadgrowths are less simple than they look. The bleached strands fan from the cracks and gather in mats, and each strand carries at its tip a bulb of gel that catches the light and returns it as a hard point. Where the bulbs are thickest, the light falls into patterns you almost recognize, and you may find yourself wondering whether the mats are gazing at something, or at you. Touch one and the strand recoils and then, slowly, reaches back. The bulbs are not seeds and not flowers. They seem to be holding light, one point at a time, as though storing the glow against a night that never comes.

The rosettes of lichen are different again. They are few, rust and ochre, each one a near-perfect disk spaced at near-perfect intervals from its neighbors, and the spacing is not accidental. Every rosette keeps a clear margin of bare rock around it, and where the threadgrowths creep too close, the lichen's edge has pulled back, leaving a neat boundary like the circle of a ring. The lichen is the only thing in the chamber that seems to keep its distance. It is also, you notice, the only thing that resembles the marks. The ring's stones are set at spacings just as careful. The cairn's pebbles are stacked to the same regularity. The scratched lines run as evenly as the rosettes. You begin to suspect that the lichen is not simply growing in the pattern of the builder's work. It may be what the builder became, or what is left of it, spread thin across the rock and rationed to one circle per space.

Beneath everything, from the far seam, comes the heartbeat. You have not seen its source. It lives in the pool beyond the lowest blocks, where the water drains into the deeper cracks, and it bubbles at a slow, heavy interval that has never once quickened or faltered. The fauna gather at the seam in a dense, shivering band, and when you look closely at their bodies, you see that they are all facing the same direction, toward the sound. They do not feed there. They do not fight. They wait, and the waiting has the posture of something that expects to be called. Whatever is down there is either the largest thing in the chamber or the reason the chamber is shaped as it is. It has been about to surface for as long as the rock has been unfinished.

So what lives here, when you add it all up, is not any one creature. It is a crowding held at a single density by rules older than the stone: crawlers that fall and are taken, fish that take them, spiders keeping time, a ceiling that breathes as one, threads storing light, lichen keeping its distance, a builder's pattern that has spread across the walls, and a heart in the water that no one has seen rise. Each of them is fully occupied with the others. Nothing here has room to be alone, and nothing here has room to leave. The population is complete, and it has been complete for so long that completeness has become the only shape it knows.

The marks are the last trace of a choice. Someone meant to build here, meant to finish something, and started. A line was scratched across rock and stopped halfway, and the stopping has been preserved along with everything else. The crowd did not stop it. The crowd simply filled the space the builder left, the way a crowd fills any gap, until the gap was a kind of ceiling and the ceiling became a kind of life.

You could stay. The chamber would not mind. It would not make room, because there is no room to make, and it would not ask you to be anything other than what you are. You would become one more body in the press, one more set of clicks in the repeated sentence, and the light would keep falling at its unchanging brightness, and the heart in the pool would go on beating, and somewhere above, through the throat of the crack, the day would keep going as if you had never come down.
