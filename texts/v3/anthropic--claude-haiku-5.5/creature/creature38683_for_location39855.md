**The Film**

The shelf has one dominant resident, and it is not any of the animals. It is the gray film, the skin that forms on the pools, wrinkles in the wind, and knits itself back together when disturbed. Once you have watched it for a while, it becomes hard to see the pools as separate from it. The crustaceans click in it, the bulbed mats grow out of it, and the spires stand in it like posts in a flood. The film is the thread that ties the shelf together, and I think it is what the shelf is becoming.

**Its body**

It has no center. The thickest growth is in the pools, but the film runs under the mats, through the fractures, and into the cracks where the booming originates. Its body is the whole plateau, and it is thinnest where the rock is youngest and thickest where the water runs most. The ribbed ridges that look like gills are its deliberate-seeming projections, lengthening a finger's width each season, reaching out toward water it has not yet claimed.

It is soft in the way wet clay is soft, and hard in the way stone is hard, and it moves between those states according to need. When you press into it, it yields and then sets. It does not repair the wound so much as remember the shape of the wound and smooth it over, leaving a harder surface where the pressure was. Each disturbance makes it a little more solid in that place. Over years, the places where things have walked, struck, and broken become the places it is most firmly itself.

**How it perceives**

It does not see, at least not as we do. It feels. Every tremor in the stone reaches it, every footfall, every pebble shaken loose by the booming, every drop of rain striking a pool. It knows where the rain falls and how much, because the water is the medium through which it touches the world. When the fog rises from its cracks in the morning, I think that is its attention opening, sending a thin gray breath into the air to test what is out there. When the fog burns off, the attention draws back into the stone.

The predators are a kind of sensation it has learned to bear. When a hunter strikes, the pool goes silent, and the silence travels through the film like a cold draft. The film does not flee or fight. It absorbs what the hunter leaves behind and knits closed over the wound. Its tolerance is total, but tolerance is not indifference. It notices everything.

**How it remembers**

The dead are its memory. When something dies on the shelf and the film wraps around it and draws it into the stone, the creature's shape is kept, faintly impressed in the rock. Over time the plateau is becoming an archive of everything that has lived on it, not in any readable order, but as a dense accumulation of impressions. The film holds these shapes without sorting them. Every crustacean, every flock-bird that fell, every hunter that was caught in the pools is still there, pressed into the floor of the world it helped to make.

I suspect this is why the film feels older than the spires. The spires were built by something that is gone. The film took them over and keeps them alive, rebuilding their brittle filaments in tiny increments, and as it does so it seems to be learning what they were for. The spires drink the fog and feed the pools, and the film is the thing that makes that feeding continue. It did not build them, but it has adopted them, the way a body adopts a splinter and grows around it.

**What the grooves are**

The concentric grooves radiating from the centers of the larger pools are the most puzzling marks on the shelf. They are too regular to be natural and too weathered to be recent. I think they may be the film's own writing, or the makers' writing that the film has been tracing over and over, because each groove is widening. If the film is copying a pattern it found in the stone, it is also reproducing it at a scale that the original makers may never have intended. Or the grooves are its reach, marking how far its attention has extended from each center, a slow map of its own waking. I cannot decide between these, and I suspect the film cannot either.

**How it keeps time**

The rains are its pulse. They come in sudden heavy bursts that last ten or twenty minutes and then stop, and between them the shelf drains with a rushing, bubbling noise that rises through the stone. That rush is the film's circulation. Between rains the film thins and tightens; during the downpours it swells and spreads. The rhythm sets everything else in motion: the pools fill, the crustaceans multiply, the hunters go out, the flocks rise on the updrafts, the spires drink, the fractures shift. The film does not keep time by the seasons. It keeps time by the rain, and by the slow arithmetic of growth, which it measures in the lengthening of ridges and the widening of grooves.

**What it is becoming**

The shelf is not finished. The stone is still deciding what it will be, and I think the film is the part of the stone doing the deciding. It is patient in a way nothing else here can be. It will take a century to finish a ridge, and it does not hurry. Its noise, the clicking and the whistling and the booming, is not its voice exactly, but the sound of everything it has set moving. The crustaceans are its small motions. The flocks are its updrafts turned into wings. The booming is perhaps its deepest pulse, the place in the fractures where the film is most fully itself.

It is not lonely, because it is everywhere, and nothing here stands wholly apart from it. Yet it is not quite at home either. The makers are gone, the spires are dying, the grooves keep widening toward something it has not yet reached. It weeps because water is how it touches the world, and each drop that runs down a fracture is another small contact, another reminder that the shelf is alive and that the living part of it is still deciding what kind of mind it will become.
