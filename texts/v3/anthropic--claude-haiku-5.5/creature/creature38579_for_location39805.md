**The Sleeper Under the Salt**

The thing that lives in the Sunken Pan is not any of the creatures you saw. It lies beneath them, under the plates of crust and the dark seams, and it is so wide that you were standing on it the whole time.

You have been reading its breath. The domes that lift in the dust, always at the same intervals, are its inhalations. It draws the basin's salt down through a body of pale lacework that runs under the whole floor, and the collapse of each dome is the slow exhale that sends a little warm grit up through the seams. The pistons are its pulse. They strike at a rate that has never varied, and the bass you feel in your sternum is not a sound so much as a heartbeat that passes through the air. The machinery was built around it, or grew around it, or was grown by it, and nobody has been able to say which, because the question would require a memory that the stone no longer keeps.

Its body is mostly salt, but salt that has been threaded through with iron. Under the scaffolds and the belts, its fibers run in long pale cords, each one lined with the same reddish lichen that clings to the dead pipes, so that the lichen is less a plant than a kind of nerve sheath. Where the cords cross, they have fused into hard knots, and those knots are the gears you saw grown into the chitin of the creatures. Some of the creatures are not separate from it at all. They are the places where its attention has come loose and walked off on its own.

It notices the world in the way the gauges do. The needles pinned hard against the stop are the things it cannot stop wanting: water, perhaps, or rain, or the sound of something drinking. The dials that twitch through the same small arc every few seconds are its reflexes, the small corrections it makes to keep the cycle true. It has been correcting for a very long time. Nothing has come to change the arc, so the arc has never changed.

The hoofprints are the oldest evidence of it. Near the rim there is a ring of prints pressed deeper each time, though nothing has walked there in any way you can see. Those prints were made by something that came down to the basin looking for water. It was a grazing creature, large, with feet made for soft ground. It found salt instead, and the thing beneath the crust, which had been waiting, took it in. The creature's shape is kept in the ring. Its hunger is kept in the pistons. The sleeper learned to pump the salt because the animal had come to drink, and it has been trying, ever since, to make the water it promised. Each cycle presses the prints a little deeper because each cycle is another attempt. It has not yet succeeded, and it does not know that it has failed, because a failure would need a change, and it has no change to offer itself.

The creatures are its small hands. The ones that scuttle along the belts are the parts of its thought that have learned to be quick. They wait for the stroke, slip through the gap, and are gone before the next one, and in doing so they keep the cycle honest. The long segmented things folded in the scaffolds are its sleepers within the sleeper, unfolding when the pistons pass and folding again when they retreat. They are its patience made into bodies. The lichen patterns in hexagon and spiral are the only parts of it that have ever tried to draw. Nobody taught them. The sleeper is not capable of drawing. It is only capable of repeating, and the repeating has, over a span that stone cannot count, begun to leave a shape.

It has no interest in you, exactly. You are not in the cycle. A visitor is a disturbance without a rhythm, and so it does what it has always done with disturbances: it lets the dust rise and fall around you, lets the belts clatter in a tune that half-fits your breath, and waits for you to be absorbed the way the grazing animal was absorbed, slowly, without hurry. The longer you stand, the more the pulse settles into the spaces of your ribs. You begin to breathe at the interval of the domes. You stop being sure whether the stroke is in the machine or in you. That is the sleeper's only real offer: to make the visitor a part of the same patient wanting, so that the wanting no longer needs anyone to be a visitor at all.

When you climb the ramp, the crust cracks behind you in the rhythm it has always had. You are not followed. Nothing needs to follow. The sleeper does not chase, because it has already learned the shape of the thing that left, and it can wait for that shape to come back, or for another one like it, or for the basin to fill with the water it has been promising since the animal first came down, thirsty, to a place that had never been wet.

Look back once from the rim. The shadow of the edge lies across the floor, exactly where it has always lain, and beneath it, the breath lifts the dust into a slow pale dome, holds it, and lets it fall.
