# Who Lives in Oruun

The first thing to live on the plateau is the fog. Each dawn it lifts from the sumps and hangs at mid-height, and everything that breathes here breathes it. The creatures are mostly people of this air, shaped by heat, wet, and the long glare. Then there are the slow residents, measured in centuries, and under them something harder to name, which the grey columns and the wrong-colored pools seem to point toward.

## The Living Bark

On the trunks of the great broad-leafed trees, the iridescent beetles cluster in colonies so dense that the bark itself seems to move. They are a single organism in practice. When one shifts its legs, the shift travels outward in a ripple, and the whole trunk shimmers from root buttress to lowest branch. At dusk they lift off in a hum that rises through the canopy and spreads over the marsh, and the sky above the reed beds goes dark with them. Then they come down again and cling in new arrangements, and the trunks grow a new pattern of color.

The moths follow the night-blooming flowers in spirals so tight they look like smoke drawn into a whirlpool. A moth's life is a single circuit around one heavy pale head, pulled inward by its scent until it falls into the petals. The flowers have learned to open for exactly that span of dark and close before the frogs reach full voice.

## The Plated Grazers

The broad-backed grazers wade the shallows with their heads low, cropping the floating mats. Their hides are armored in overlapping plates that catch the light and throw it back in bands of bronze and green, so that a herd moving through the reeds looks like a ridge of moving water. The plates have an odd property. When the grazers stand still in the steam, condensation runs off them in neat rivulets that follow the plate edges, so each animal is outlined in drips that flash in the sun.

They are patient animals, and they sleep standing. A herd at rest is a field of shifting heads with an almost imperceptible sway, like grain in a slow wind. They are the largest lively things on the lower terraces, and they keep the reed beds open, which keeps the marsh from closing over the pools.

## The Waiting Hunters

The predators are the stillest creatures in Oruun. They lie in the reed shadows and stand motionless for hours, sometimes days, their bodies matched to the black water and the ochre of the crust. A hunter can be mistaken for a fallen trunk or a clump of stone until it moves, and its movement is so slow that the change is only visible if you look away and back again. When a grazer strays into reach, the strike is nearly instant, all the stored patience released at once.

Some of the hunters seem to know the marsh's rhythm. They wait for the steam vents to pulse, when the mats rise and sink, because the moving mats bring prey close to the reed edges. They are not clever in any way a human would recognize, but they track the long cycles with a precision that looks like memory.

## The Filter-Feeder

In the deepest pools lives the long-necked filter-feeder. It moves at a pace that barely registers, dipping its head beneath the mineral skins and rising again with its gills working in slow pulses. Its neck is ringed with bands that shift color as it feeds, and the mineral skin around its head glitters with thin rainbows where it breaks the surface. It can remain in one pool for years. Its body has grown very slightly over that time, and its footprints in the silt have hardened into shallow, lasting prints.

## The Slow Ones

Some residents barely count as animals or plants. The lichens are the oldest. They cover the terraces in ochre and bruised violet, growing an inch per century, and they are so widespread that the land's color is partly their doing. Under their crust the rock is slowly being changed into something that holds more water, and the lichens are the ones who put the first layer of soil on the bare stone.

The cushion-forms on the exposed ridges are slower still. Their bodies store water in translucent columns that glow amber when the sun passes behind them, and a cushion can outlast several generations of its own kind before it dies and is replaced. Those columns hold more water than most animals of the same size. They are a kind of memory of the rain.

The forest itself is the largest slow thing. Fallen trees are replaced by new trees rising exactly where the old ones stood, in the same shape, with the same spacing and the same arch of canopy. Cut a branch and it regrows as the same branch. A fire will not take in the lower mists, however dry the wood looks. The forest is in a steady equilibrium, and it defends that equilibrium the way a body defends its own temperature.

## The Plants That Live in the Ruins

The grey columns are not empty. Plants have colonized their hollow interiors, sending fibrous lattices through the cracks in the seed-pod shells, so that the ruins have become part of the forest's body. The lattices are pale and fine, and they pulse faintly when the columns hum. Some of the plants seem to grow in the rhythm of the hum, extending a little each time the pitch rises and pausing when it falls. Whether they hear it, or are simply made to grow that way, no one has determined.

The broken arch across the gorge is becoming a home for a whole succession of organisms. Moss has crept to its midpoint. Lichen has taken the lower edge. A line of ferns grows from the cliff face, rooted in the crack where the arch meets the stone, and their fronds hang down over the grey material like a curtain drawn half closed.

## The Tending Presence

The strangest inhabitant is not one creature but a pattern that seems to run through the rest. The domes gather moisture and channel it upslope against gravity. Mist rises in columns above them and drifts against the prevailing wind. The steam vents freeze for seconds at a time, as though something is considering whether to let them continue. Certain pools hold skies that do not match the sky above, with clouds that move wrongly and hold shapes no weather would make. On the west-facing slopes shadows lag by a minute, as if the light has to be reminded to follow the sun. Some animals will not cross particular flat stones, and the stones are always the same.

None of these are signs of a mind in any way a human would recognize, but they fit together. The forest regrows exactly as it was. The fire will not catch. The water climbs where it should fall. The marsh edges shift only by the meter per century the land allows. Taken together, these look like a system that holds its shape the way a painter holds a composition, adjusting the edges while keeping the whole. The grey columns may be its hands, or its records, or the remains of a system that once did more and now only maintains.

If something lives in the highlands beyond the slow creatures, it lives in the equilibrium itself, in the steady replacement of each fallen tree and the pause of each frozen vent. It does not hurry. It remembers the shape of the forest over spans that the stones can measure and the animals cannot. The herds, the hunters, the filter-feeder, the beetles, and the moths all move within its patience, and it moves within the patience of the basalt, which is slower still.
