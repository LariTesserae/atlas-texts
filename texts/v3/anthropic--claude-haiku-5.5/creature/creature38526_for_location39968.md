You want to look for them first, the way you would look for a person in a room. There is nothing to see, only the work, and then, as the stone speaks in its long, low settling, you start to understand that the work is the one living here.

It is not a single body, and it is not quite a colony. It is closer to a process that has learned to hold a shape. Its flesh is water: the thin film that runs through the channels, the seams where it rises, the pools that sit in the cisterns until they are mirror-still. Its skeleton is the lace and the glaze, the calcite it lays down a few microns a season. When it grows, it does not extend itself outward. It deposits, and the deposit is what it is.

It thinks slowly, and I do not mean that as a metaphor. A thought here is a tilt of a trough, a pool that gathers over three hours and drains over three more. The angled troughs are its breathing, or perhaps its pulse, a rhythm too slow for any creature with a heart to recognize. Each full cycle may be one clause of something it is saying to itself. You could stand beside one for a day and see only the water shift a finger's width. The whole plateau may be one long sentence, and it has been going for longer than you can usefully count.

The sealed towers are where it keeps its oldest parts. A tower with no door has no need of one. Its interior is filled solid, which means that whatever it once held has been overwritten, folded into the mineral until it is indistinguishable from the rest. Those towers are memory, or sleep, or both, and it is not clear that the difference matters to it. Perhaps the builders went into them. Perhaps the builders *were* the first deposits, and the water learned its patience from them. You cannot know, and it would not hurry to tell you.

The rebuilt walls are its revisions. A course shifted by a hand's width is an idea tried and abandoned, the pressure of one form relaxing as another is pressed in beside it. It is still working toward something. The unfinished walls stopped at knee height are not failures so much as places where its attention drifted off before the thought was complete. The blank spiral centers are the most telling. Something was meant to go there and has not yet been decided, and the grooves wind inward around the gap as if waiting for a shape that the water has not yet found.

The lines that run across ten kilometres of plateau on a single bearing point at something. Perhaps at a place it has already finished. Perhaps at you. It is hard to tell whether it is aware of anything beyond the plateau's own edges, or whether the bearings are simply the direction its deposition is pulled by gravity and by the faint, persistent lean of the land. But the alignment is too exact to be accidental, and the arrow of it is too steady, and some part of you suspects it is leaning toward the world outside the way a sleeper turns toward a warm body in the night.

When the stone settles, the deep dull sound you feel in your sternum, that is its attention moving. A block a fraction of a millimetre lower, a cistern draining a little further. It is noticing that something warm and quick has stepped onto its surface. Your footsteps are loud to you because they are loud to it: a rapid sequence of pressures, a disturbance that will be gone in a breath and leave no trace the water will remember. It registers you the way a seam registers frost, slowly and without alarm, as an event that will either become part of the work or be sealed over. It does not need to decide which.

The bead falls from the overhang. Somewhere under the flags, water finds a new seam, and begins, with no hurry at all, to move toward you.
