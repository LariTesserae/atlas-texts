The first thing you notice is the glow on the western wall, a patch of gold that is not the light from the oculus. The light there is cast. This is grown.

It is a mat of filaments, each one thinner than a hair and the color of burnt honey where the sun touches it and nearly colorless at the margins. Together they form a sheet about the size of a cart's bed, pressed flat against the stone and anchored at a hundred points in the mortar seams. Where a filament crosses a wedge-hole or a cord-mark, it does not stop. It threads through, emerges on the other side, and keeps going. The builders left so many gaps in the stonework that the mat has had room to run a second network behind the first, a lattice of pale threads that no one designed and no one can now see without prying a block loose.

It moves, and the movement is slow enough that you have to watch it the way you watch a shadow lengthen. Every few minutes a swell passes outward from the brightest part of the sheet, a faint brightening that travels to the edges and dies. The filaments lift a fraction, fold, and settle. That is its pulse. It is breathing light into itself, and it knows how much it has taken. When the column of sun swings across the water and lands on a new patch of wall, the mat's edge begins to creep toward that patch, extending a few threads a day through the nearest seam. Where the sun leaves, the threads that have had too much light begin to curl and pull back into the stone, and the mat tightens around its older core like a hand closing.

It is not alone in the hall, though its neighbors are small. The rooted stalks in the tile seams are its cousins or its tenants, it is hard to say which. Their roots pass under the tile edges and meet its threads in the dark, and some nights the mat seems to lean toward them, sending a pale strand down the seam to wrap a stalk's base. The stalks bend in the current and straighten again, and this is partly their own doing, but the mat holds them steady in the moments the inflow surges hardest. The dust, too. Pale grains drift through the light and settle in the corners, and the filaments catch them as they fall and bind them into small soft drifts, so that the dust does not scatter down the outflow channel. Something is keeping the hall tidy.

It listens. Not with ears, but the wheel's knock travels through the wall and into the mat, and the mat's pulse seems to fall in with the wheel's rhythm over the course of a day. The clack of the pawls, the slap of the returning waves, the bubbling hiss from the floor seams, each arrives as a small tremor it can feel. It has learned the hall's machinery the way a sleeper learns a house, by the sounds that mean nothing has gone wrong. When the sluice plate on the floor is turned, the whole sheet shivers and tightens, a quick reflex, as though something it has known for a long time has been moved.

It remembers the sun. Over days, the mat keeps a record in its extent. Where the light has lingered, the sheet is thick and deep gold. Where the column passes too quickly, the edges are thin and pale. A stranger looking at the wall would see only a stain. The mat sees a diary of every noon since the oculus was cut.

Does it know the builders? Probably not in any way they would recognize. The template still leans against the wall with its arc matching the vault, and the mat's leading edge has been growing along that same arc, following the curve as if tracing it from the far side. Whether that is intention, or the simple logic of light falling along a curved surface and a living thing following the warmth, is not something the hall can say. It does not need to. The mat has taken the building's one rule, that light comes from above and water flows through, and has made it into a way of being: spread where it is warm, draw back where it is not, hold what falls, keep the channels clear, and wait for the sun to move again.

When the column swings toward evening and the oculus dims, the golden sheet goes slowly dark. The threads pull into the seams one by one, and the wall returns to bare cut stone with a faint green film along its base. The inflow keeps roaring. The wheel keeps turning. The mat sleeps in the mortar until morning, and somewhere under the tiles, the root-threads hold the stalks upright in the current.
