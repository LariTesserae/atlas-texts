The people of Kethra Ashward are shaped by the thin air and the heat that rises through their floors. Their chests are deep and slow, built to pull a little more from each breath. Born in the warm lower streets, they sleep through the long dusk with their windows open to the steam, and most learn to walk on stone that is never fully cool. Their feet are thick and calloused, and they step lightly on the ringing lava, the way people elsewhere might learn to step around a sleeping animal.

Their skin takes the mineral films. Ochre and sulfur-yellow settle into the creases of their knuckles and the lines beside their mouths, and by middle age most residents wear faint stains that no washing removes. Nobody remarks on them. A person's hands are read by what they have handled: rust-grey for the cistern keepers, yellow to the wrist for the distillers, and a bright bruised green for the few who work the copper veins in the ridge foothills. Their eyes are narrowed against the glare and the bluish haze, and many wear lenses ground from old scoria, smoked dark, which give the faces of the crowd a uniform, watchful calm.

They speak little, and softly. The thin air carries a voice poorly, so a shout dies within a few paces, and a whisper can be heard across a room. Over generations the residents have built a language that runs as much on gesture and silence as on words. A pause can mean agreement, a question, or a grief too large to name. A tilt of the head toward the vents means *I have something to say about the ground*. Two people who have known each other long enough can hold an entire quarrel through a hand lifted and set down again. Strangers are often thought rude, and newcomers are treated with a polite, patient confusion until they learn to wait.

Among them the Regraders have the most authority. They walk the ridge with brass pins and surveying chains, marking the stone and measuring how far the rising ground has moved since the last season. Their figures decide where the streets go next, which buildings are abandoned to the slope, and which vents will be capped. They speak of the ridge as one might speak of an ill relative, with a mix of care and resignation, and they are the only residents who regularly say the word *forever* without irony, meaning a span of about forty years. Below them, in the older districts, the Diggers open sealed streets. The city's old levels lie buried under its new ones, and the Diggers cut down through the fill to reach rooms that were last walked when the city was smaller. They bring up tools, bowls, and sometimes a child's shoe fused to a basalt slab. They do not keep these things in museums. They return most of them to the fill, saying that the old streets are still in use, only in a different direction.

The dead are laid in the fill beneath the new paving, so that the city walks over its ancestors. People do not speak of their dead by name but by the street they lie under, and a mourner may say *she is under the Long Stair* or *he is beneath the third bridge over the furnace yard* as easily as one might say a person lives on a certain road. The Diggers sometimes report hearing faint ticking in the lowest levels, the cooling of heated stone or the settling of the foundations, and the families of the buried listen closely for a while, then go home.

Food comes from the distillers and the cistern keepers. The distillers scrape the mineral films from the stone and boil them down in copper stills to a pale, sour broth that tastes of iron and rain. The keepers raise the slate-coloured crawlers in the warm dark of the cisterns. The crawlers are blind and slow, and they navigate by the heat of a hand and the tremor of footsteps. Keepers work with their eyes half closed, reaching into the wet shadows with bare, warm fingers and lifting the shelled creatures one at a time. Each batch is tasted by a taster before the rest are sold. Tasters are chosen young, and they are rarely ill for long, because the city values their bodies very highly and has learned, through several bad seasons, what happens when the wrong creature reaches the pot.

Then there are the films. Nobody in the city can yet say what they are, and most residents prefer not to argue about it. The old keepers say the films creep a few centimetres each season across the bare stone, and every so often a boundary marker that was set in a shadowed hollow is found on a sunlit slab, or the grey pools at the edge of a cistern have drawn a thin line farther toward the warm cracks. The Regraders have measured the crawl and recorded it in their ledgers without comment. Some residents will not step on the crusts after dark. Others scrape them carefully from the stone at dawn, boil them, and eat them with a quiet thankfulness that is also a kind of suspicion. A few of the very old say that the films are the city's first inhabitants, and that the people only arrived later to occupy a space that had already begun to move.

The city does not last in the way that the people who live in it would like. Its councils meet to decide which vents to open and which to cap, and each decision reshapes the streets for a century. Its children are born into a steady dusk, learn to read the ground before they can read words, and grow up knowing that the place they stand on was not here a thousand years ago and will not be here in a thousand more, at least not in the form they know. They keep the furnaces burning, keep the ledgers and the cisterns and the stills, and keep laying new paving over old, sealing their dead beneath the stone they walk. In the evenings, when the vents glow orange at their edges and the furnace drone dims, they sit on the warm steps with their dark lenses tilted toward the ridge, and wait in the long pause the ground allows them, the way a person waits for a word that has not yet been said.
