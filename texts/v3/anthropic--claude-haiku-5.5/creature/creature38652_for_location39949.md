**Who Lives in the Roar**

You don't see the residents of the Upland Roar at first. You feel them. The mat shifts under your boots with a slow give that is not quite the ground's own motion. Something beneath is turning over, rearranging its weight. Then a segmented shape the color of wet clay unfolds from a split in the lattice, and you understand that the plateau is full of bodies, most of them doing something important and none of them paying you any attention.

**The Tearers**

The grazers are the first you learn to recognize. They walk the mat on forty or more legs, in long rippling files, each segment armored in plates the rust-orange of the shelf faces. Their heads are wide and flat, with a row of serrated mandibles that open and close with a dry clack. When they feed, they lock their forelegs around a section of mat and pull, and the fibrous crust gives way with a crack like splitting timber. The sound carries for kilometers. Herds learn to feed upwind of one another so the noise does not merge into one continuous report.

They are not stupid. Watch a file of them cross a fresh sinkhole and you will see the lead animal stop, lower its head, and press its mandibles against the edge of the hole, testing the give. It is listening to the ground through its jaws. When the rim sags, it backs away and the file reroutes without any visible signal. They know the mat the way a sailor knows a current.

**The Sap-Wardens**

Burrowers move underneath, and most people never see them. Their bodies are long, pale, and nearly blind, with hands like shovels at the front and a hide that glistens with a mucous sheen that keeps the mat's sap from crusting on them. They tunnel through the vascular cords, and where they cut, they open new channels for pressure to flow. Their work is careful. They avoid the thickest cords, which would kill them if the sap rushed back, and instead bore into the fine capillaries that feed the upper towers, widening them just enough to redirect the flow.

At the base of the canyon walls you can find their nests, which look like small, dark hills pocked with breathing holes. Sap seeps from these holes in slow beads. The wardens lick it up and carry it in their guts to the brine-pans, where they regurgitate it in glossy pools. The brine thickens. The white plates crack and reform. Some of the wardens drown there, and their bodies sink into the salt and become part of the crust, which is one reason the brine-pans are so strangely textured.

**The Seed-Carriers**

Among the grazers and wardens move the seed-carriers, which look almost comical beside them: round-bodied, slow, with high domed backs covered in pods that rattle when they walk. Each pod holds a seed and a little packet of fertilizer, and the carriers' digestion is designed to break down everything except the pods. They wander the shelves and deposit their loads in the cracks, where the rain, when it comes, will wash the packets into the rock.

They are gentle, and they are also incredibly stubborn. A seed-carrier that has chosen a route will walk it through a dust storm, through a lightning strike, through a fresh fountain of sap, and it will not change its path. On a windy day you can watch them forming long migrations across the mat, each one leaving a faint trail of pods behind, and you realize the towers were planted this way, one stubborn animal at a time.

**The Flyers**

Above all of it the flyers spiral around the towers. Their wings are broad and translucent, veined with dark cord, and they glide on the thermals that rise off the sun-struck shelves. Their calls are the sharpest sound in the Roar, a piercing whistle that cuts through the drone of the insects and the boom of the organs and makes your teeth ache. They call to one another across kilometers, a chain of sharp notes that rises and falls with the wind.

Some flyers roost in the crowns of the bulbous towers, where the fronds catch the wind and sway. Others nest on the shelf-edges, in ledges too narrow for anything to walk. In storms they dive into the canyons, riding the gusts, and they come out the other side with grit caught in their feathers and their calls even sharper than before. They seem to enjoy the turbulence, or at least they have learned to exploit it.

**The Drone**

The insects are not individuals. They are a cloud, a single distributed being whose body is the swarm. Each insect is small, dark, and fast, with compound eyes that catch the white light in a thousand glints. Together they form a shifting veil over the mat, rising and falling with the temperature, thickening over the sap-geysers and thinning over the dry channels. Their wing-beats make the drone, and the drone is the only sound that never stops, even in the brief silences between storms.

They pollinate the towers, carry spores from one fold of the mat to another, and feed on the sap that spatters from the geysers. When a storm sweeps over, the swarm does not scatter. It dives into the lee of the shelves and reforms there, a dark fog pressing against the rock. When the storm passes, it rises again like smoke.

**The Tenders**

Along the canyon walls, where the great pressure-organs swell and collapse, live the tenders. These are the largest beasts in the Roar, broad and heavy, with long necks and mouths that open like valves. They press their bodies against the membranes, sometimes for hours, and their weight and warmth seem to coax the organs into rhythm. When a tender pulls away, the organ sometimes falters, and a geyser erupts from a nearby cord. The tenders watch the fountain with a calm, unhurried attention, then move on to the next organ.

Nobody knows whether the tenders are keeping the machinery running or whether the machinery is keeping them alive. Both are probably true. Carcasses of tenders lie along the canyon floors, half-buried in the mat, and new tenders are always there to replace them, which suggests that the relationship is older than memory.

**The Mat**

And beneath everything, the largest resident of all: the lattice itself. It does not move in any way you could point to, but it reacts. It re-knits its scars with a slow, fibrous patience. It sends pressure where pressure is needed and withdraws it where it is not. It shapes the towers, nudges the sinkholes open, and lets the brine-pans spread or shrink with the rains. The deep groan that underlies all the other sounds is its voice, and its voice has no words, only the long settling of enormous weight.

The grazers, wardens, carriers, flyers, insects, and tenders are all, in some sense, its organs. They feed it, carry it, open it, guard it, and are eaten by it. Whether the mat thinks, or merely responds, the Roar does not say. But the plateau behaves as if it is alive in a single, sprawling way, and everything that lives on it lives inside that way.

**Those Who Would Cross**

If you walk here, the residents will mostly ignore you. The grazers will part around you in their files. The wardens will sink below the mat before you reach them. The flyers will call warnings you cannot understand. The tenders will look at you once, slowly, then return to their organs.

Learn the rhythms. Listen for the boom of the pressure-organs; it means the mat is breathing hard and a geyser may follow. Watch the grazers' heads; when they stop and press their jaws to the ground, step away from that spot. Follow the seed-carriers' trails, since they always cross the firmest ground. And when the drone rises suddenly and the flyers dive for the canyons, find the nearest shelf-edge and hold on. The storm is coming, and the plateau is about to change shape around you.
