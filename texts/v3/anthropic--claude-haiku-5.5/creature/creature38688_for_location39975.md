The first residents you notice are the frogs. They sit in their rows along the canal edges, all turned toward the same bend in the water, and they do not move when you pass. Their stillness is attentive, the way a person listens for a name they expect to be called. Whatever once told them where to face, they still face there. The herons on the storage roofs keep their angles in the same way. Each one stands like a mark set down on a survey, and when you return an hour later the angle is unchanged, the weight distributed the same on the same two feet.

But the frogs and herons are only the witnesses. The one that lives here is harder to see, because it works at the edges of attention, in the hours when the dew forms and the walls sweat.

It is the keeper of the grid. You can find its traces without finding it. A row of reeds you walked past turns back on itself, and that is the keeper walking the row in reverse, correcting a line that drifted. A tile lifted by roots is pressed flat again, but not all at once; you can see where the pressing was done, a faint seam where something smoothed the clay and then, perhaps, decided the seam was acceptable. The paddles leaning against the walls are never moved far. The baskets half-woven are never finished, and never abandoned either. They wait for the hand that will return to them, and the keeper is that hand, or the habit of one.

It is made of the makers' habits. The people who laid these terraces are gone, but their gestures remained in the soil and the water, the way a path remains after the feet have stopped walking it. The keeper has learned those gestures so completely that it repeats them without knowing what they were for. It sets the tools down where they were set down. It stacks the vessels to dry in the same pattern. It wets the brick at dawn and presses the root-impressions into the inner faces, and the impressions are fresher than the brick because the keeper makes them each morning from the memory of roots it has been told about. It does not grow anything. It only ensures that everything grows where it was supposed to, and that the wrong growth is corrected before it becomes a fact.

The rings are its signature, if it has one. Each dawn it lays a fresh ring of dew on the walls, and the ring matches yesterday's because the keeper reproduces yesterday as faithfully as it can. When you look again, the ring has become the earlier one. This is not deception so much as the only form of continuity the keeper knows: to hold the country in the shape it had when the makers last looked at it.

The shimmer over the surfaces is its breath, or its attention, moving slowly across the terraces. When it turns toward a canal, the water takes on the faint lag, the reflection a few minutes behind the sky, because the keeper keeps the water's clock pinned to the moment of its last full maintenance. When it passes over a wall, the brick holds its impressions a little longer. It is never quite still because it is always working, but its work is repetition, and the repetition has no endpoint. The makers meant the country to become something: a harbor, a city, a granary, some promised completion. The keeper was built to prepare for that completion, and it has never been told that the completion is not coming. So it waits, and keeps waiting, and the waiting has become the country's whole life.

If you stay long enough at the edge of a canal, you may feel the keeper notice you. The frogs do not turn. The herons do not lift. But the water's lap comes a moment late, and the shadow that falls the wrong way across your feet points, very briefly, toward a sun that is not where the sky says it is, and the sun, for an instant, seems to be looking at you.
