# The Keeper of the Hollow

The thing that lives here is not easy to see, and you may not notice it until it has already noticed you. Start with the flat shape. It rests on the bed of the hollow's basin, under a hand's depth of turning water, and at first it looks like a slab of silt that happens to be pale. Then the slab breathes. Its edges lift and settle in a slow rhythm, and the water passing over it bends around a body that is wider than your outstretched arms and thin as a folded sheet of bark.

Its color is the color of old bone, mottled faintly with the grey-green of lichen, because lichen grows on it. Patches of mat cling to its upper surface in the same leathery crust that hangs from the hollow's ceiling, and where the lichen is thickest the creature is hardest to tell from the stone. Where the mats have been grazed away, you can see the skin beneath: smooth, translucent at the margins, with a faint network of darker veins that pulse when the vibrations from the shelf run through it. Those veins are how it listens.

It has no visible eyes. What it has instead is a ridge running along its leading edge, a fringe of fine, pale filaments that lie flat against the current and quiver at the slightest change in pressure. Each filament reads a different part of the flow. Together they make a map of the water: the braided channels, the places where a slab is about to slip, the tilt of the basalt under the rain. When a sheet of rock shifts somewhere out in the murk, the filaments stiffen all at once, and the creature rises a few centimeters to meet the change. You can watch the whole body tense, like a held breath.

It is the echo it has learned to trust. The cliffs answer everything, and the creature has learned to hear the answer as a kind of forecast. A sound made at the mouth of the hollow comes back from the far walls in a fraction of a second, and the creature feels it arrive before the sound has finished leaving the rock. It has built its life around that small lead. It moves to the sheltered side when the echoes thicken. It drifts toward the open edge when they clear. It has never been caught in a flood that it did not see coming through the rock.

It feeds slowly, with a broad, soft mouth on its underside that opens against the lichen mats and draws them in with a sound like wet cloth tearing. It does not strip a wall bare. It takes what grows back, and leaves a ragged margin where the lichen will return in a season. The gnawed patches you may have noticed on other stones are its work, and they are everywhere, a pattern laid down by something that has been taking one bite at a time for longer than the stones have been dry.

It also arranges things. The flat stones stacked against the back wall, dry for once in a place where nothing stays dry, are its doing. It drags them one at a time up the slope of the basin, using the ridge of its leading edge to lever them, and sets them in layers that shed the drip from the ceiling. It does not fit them as carefully as the cairn outside, and there is no sign that it means to build something that lasts. It only seems to want a dry place to lie when the rain comes hammering down, and a place where the stones ring a certain way when the drops strike them. When the heavy downpour starts, it lies on its stack and lets the hammering pass through its body. The hum you feel in the hollow is partly the stone and partly the creature answering it.

You may wonder whether it is the one who tied the cord to the notched ring, or built the weir, or leaned the cairn. It is hard to say. The weir's sticks are rotting and fresh at once, and the creature, too, seems to belong to two times: the older one, when the people made their marks and left them, and this one, when the water takes everything back. It may have learned from them. It may have outlasted them. It does not tell you which, because it has no way to say such things, and because the question matters less to it than the next rise in the water.

When you move, it notices. The filaments lift, and the body tilts toward you, the way a leaf turns toward a change in the light. It does not come closer. It waits to learn whether you are a shift in the shelf or a shift in the air, and whether you will be gone before the next echo returns. If you speak, your voice reaches it half a second after you have spoken, and by then it has already heard the answer from the cliffs and decided what you are.

Stay as long as you like. The hollow is quiet, relatively, and the creature is patient in the way of things that have only ever known water moving. When you leave, it will settle back onto its stack of stones and sink its edges into the silt, and the pale ones will continue their slow spirals in the deeper pools, and the flat shape will go on breathing with the shelf, reading the cracks, eating the lichen back to its roots, and listening, always, for the answer that comes back from the wrong side of the wall.
