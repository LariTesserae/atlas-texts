**The Ones Who Build**

The first thing you see of the people here is their hands. They are on the faces of the walls, pressing clay into joints, scraping mortar smooth, resting flat against a course that has only just set. You see the hands before you see the people, and the hands seem sure of what they are doing in a way that the people, when they turn around, do not quite match.

They are not hurried. In the lower terraces you may find a worker sitting on a block with a gourd between their knees, watching the mist lift off the rim. Their clothes are the undyed grey-brown of the clay, their hair pulled back with cord. Their faces are weathered, but not evenly: the brow may be deeply lined while the cheeks are smooth, as if they had been made from two different ages and fitted together. When they nod to you, the nod arrives a moment before their head moves. You have the impression they were already nodding before you came into view, and that you are catching up to a gesture that has been going on for some time.

They do not seem to have families in any sense you would recognize. You see no children, though you hear, sometimes, a small voice counting from somewhere behind a wall, a few numbers and then silence. You do see the same faces repeated at different heights. A woman carrying a basket on a pole down the third step has the same chin and the same scar across one palm as a man kneeling at the mortar trough two terraces below. You decide they are related, and then you decide they are not, and then you stop deciding, because the question seems to belong to a different way of counting.

**The counters**

The tallies on the walls were made by someone, and the someone is not hard to find once you know where to look. Every few hundred meters there is a worker sitting with a notched bone stylus and a slab of fresh clay, marking dots in rows. They are always at the same task. They do not look up when you approach. If you watch their hands you see that they are counting the courses of the wall beside them, touching each block with the stylus as they go, and when they reach the top they start again from the bottom. Their lips move. They never seem to reach the end of the count.

Ask one how many terraces there are and they will answer, quite calmly, with a number. Then they will look at the wall and frown and begin counting again. It is not that they are careless. It is that the wall is not finished, and each new course changes the total, and the old courses, having set and settled, change it again. The counters appear to know this. They keep counting anyway, because counting is what they do while the building goes on. Perhaps the tallies are not meant to agree. Perhaps the difference between one count and the next is the only record of how much has been done.

**The ones who stand still**

Some of the figures in the terraces do not work at all. They sit or lean against the walls in the late afternoon, hands loose in their laps, faces turned toward the bowl's center. They do not drink from the troughs. When the mist comes up through the vines they do not move aside, and you may walk right through the edge of one before you realize it is there, the mist folding around a shape that is slightly more solid than the air, and then slightly less.

These are, perhaps, the ones who have finished. They have set into the walls the way mortar sets, and they are no longer fully separate from the stone. You can see faint lines in their skin that echo the chisel marks on the blocks beside them. Their eyes are open, and they are very still, and when the wind moves the vines their shadows lie a little behind them on the stone, as though their outline has not yet agreed to stay where they are.

**The birds and lizards**

The birds nest in the gaps where the workers have not yet closed the wall, and they are the most restless things here. They come and go with a clatter of wings, and each one carries a pale thread or a single seed up into the vines. You may notice that some of them, when they perch on the rim, have shadows that fall on the wrong side of the stone, or that arrive a few seconds after the bird does. These seem to be the newest arrivals, or perhaps the ones carrying the most of the count, slightly out of step with the light.

The lizards are older in their manner. They lie along the top courses with their pale bellies to the warm stone and their eyes half closed, and when you look at them they freeze, but not as animals usually freeze. It is closer to the stillness of the counters, a holding of the body in a position that has been chosen for a reason. If one begins to cross a block, you may lose track of its legs, and then find that it has crossed without your seeing how. I think they may be keeping their own tally, though I have never seen one touch the stone to mark it.

**The silent moths**

At dusk the long-winged things come through the vines. They are soft and dark and make no sound at all, and they carry the sweet resinous smell that hangs over the whole bowl. They move slowly from terrace to terrace, and when they settle on the millet heads, the grain seems to thicken and swell. You suspect they are the reason the fields are so dense, that they are the ones who carried the soil here, one dusting at a time, over more seasons than the terraces have had.

In the last light, one of them may settle on a sleeping worker's shoulder and stay there, wings folded, until the mist covers them both.

**What they are building**

The people here are building the terraces, and the terraces are building them back. Each course of clay is set into a place in a shape that has not yet fully formed, and each worker, as they age and settle into the stone, becomes one more fixed point in the count. The disagreement of the steps, the shifting doorways, the shadows that fall toward the center: these are not errors in the building. They are what happens when the counters are themselves part of what they count, when the number of steps depends on how many people have finished standing and how many are still arriving.

If you ask them what the finished shape will be, they will look at the wall, and then at you, and then they will take up the stylus and begin the count again from the bottom. Their lips will move without sound. Somewhere below, one of the terraces will be a little higher than it was, and a worker who was kneeling at its foot will have, without anyone quite noticing, become a little more still.
