The largest thing in the shallows is a slow, broad-bodied amphibian, flat as a shed skin and as long as a man lying down. It is hard to see at first. Its back is mottled in greens, rusts, and dull greys that match the lichen on the shrubs and the bare mud, and it lies so still in the brown water that the sky and the reed shadows seem to rest on it. Then one of the pools sends a ripple across the sheet, the reeds shiver after it, and you realize the shape has been turning beneath you the whole time.

It breathes both ways. Its skin takes in water and air through a dense net of tiny vessels, and it can also lift its blunt head to the surface and gulp from a lung that runs the length of its body. Its eyes sit high on a broad skull, small and gold, with a membrane that slides across them when it sinks. It moves by sculling with a fringe of pale, feathery gills that tremble along its flanks like a fan being slowly opened. In the warm midday water it sinks to the bottom and rests with its limbs folded under, letting the tannins darken its skin, and small swimmers drift past its nose without any hurry on either side.

It eats the things that move in the shallows: the tadpoles of the frogs, the small armored swimmers that scatter from the edges, the insects that fall onto the water's skin and wriggle there. It does not chase. It waits at the edge of a tussock with its head just under the surface until something drifts close enough, and then it opens its mouth in a motion so brief that the water barely dimples. The wading birds seem to understand this and keep their distance from its pools, though they stand in the shallows nearby, watching it the way one watches a neighbor who is known to be dangerous and is also, in the long run, indifferent.

The seasons change it more than they change the land. When the rains come, the creatures rise from the clay where they have spent the dry months. Their hibernation is a sealed sleep. As the mud cracks and the water sinks, each one curls into a hollow in the ground and sheds its outer skin into a stiff, papery cocoon that hardens around it, and it waits there, breathing so slowly that the clay around it takes no sign. The crust over those hollows is the hard, ringing layer that sounds like pottery underfoot. If you stand on it at the right time of year and go very still, you can feel a faint pulse through the soles of your feet, a deep and patient knocking, as though the plain were a drum with thousands of sleepers beneath it, each one keeping time against the dark.

When the first real rain falls, the cocoons split. The creatures climb out blind and sluggish, and they go straight to the water. Their voices come up with the frogs. The deep resonant croaks that roll over the plain at dusk are theirs, low notes that the frogs' trills and hammers ride over like a floor beneath a dance. A male sinks into a pool and calls with his throat swelled to the size of a child's head, and the other males answer from the next pool, and the sound overlaps across the plain until it seems the water itself is singing.

They climb the stone domes too, though not often. On the north-facing side, where the moss glistens and the seeps hang like curtains, a few of them haul their bodies out onto the wet rock and lie there in the shade of the overhangs, letting the fog settle on their backs and the drips run down their flanks. Their skin, pale and mottled, looks almost like the rock. They stay until the heat builds, and then they slide back into the pools. Over the generations the rock has been rubbed smooth in certain places, the bare patches polished the way a river stone is polished, and the moss there grows thin and cropped.

When they die, they sink into the mud, and the mud takes them slowly. Their bones are pale and dense, and the iron in the ground stains them, so that over the long centuries the bone-coloured layers beneath the crust are full of their remains, veined and mottled with rust. The plain is built, in part, from the bodies of creatures like these, and their shapes are pressed into its strata as faintly as the ripples they once made across the water.

Nothing here hurries them. They live in the slow rhythm of the place: a year of sinking and rising water, a long sleep in the clay, a brief bright season of calling and feeding, then the sleep again. They have been doing this for longer than anything has had a name for it. Watching one turn beneath the surface, you understand that it is not waiting for anything, and that it is not separate from the water it lies in, or from the mud it returns to, or from the low hills that are slowly settling into the plain around them.
