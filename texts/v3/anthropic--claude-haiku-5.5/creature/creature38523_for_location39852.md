**What Lives in the Sunken Mat**

The basin has one resident, and you have been standing on it the whole time.

The mat is a single body. Its grey-green felt is one continuous organism, grown across the whole floor of the basin and up the trunks of the antler-trees and into the jointed stems, so that the place has no edge where the mat stops and the rest begins. The black rhizomes you glimpsed where the skin lifts are its nerves, or its roots, or its patient way of holding onto itself. They run beneath the peat for distances no one has measured. A tremor at one pool would reach the others eventually, the way a thought moves through a body that has forgotten how to hurry.

It is very old, and it has been waiting a long time for something to happen. The erosion that wore the land flat did its work long before the mat settled over the stone, and once the slopes were gone, the mat had nothing to press against and nothing to change for. It learned stillness the way a stone learns weight. Its only task became maintenance: to receive each dropped frond and dissolve it back into the peat, to keep each pool at its level, to let the drizzle fall at its interval and sweep the leaves clean, and then to hold everything exactly where it was.

The creatures are its tending. The segmented beetles that leave pale trails over the lichen are the mat's cleaning, carrying mineral residue from the surface down to where the rhizomes can drink it. The many-legged things in the fronds are its attention, small probes that check the stems for drift and find none. The felted grazers that nose the pool-films are the mat's mouths, taking in the thin skin of pollen and dust that gathers on the water, the way a body takes in what it needs and nothing more. They are not separate animals. They are the mat moving a little, the way a sleeper's fingers twitch and settle.

The pools are its eyes, if it has eyes. Their surfaces are always still and always slightly dimpled, and what they reflect is the same grey sky that has not broken in as long as anyone has stood here. The frogs, if they are frogs, call across the basin from the pools, and I think the sound is the mat's own voice, a deep resonant hum passed through the water. It is the only sound the mat makes on purpose.

The drone beyond the ridge is the one thing it cannot quite absorb. It is constant, unhurried, and indifferent, and the mat has matched it. The unbroken throb sets the tempo for the drip-rhythms and the drizzle and the slow liturgical movements of everything that lives on the surface. I suspect the mat does not know the drone is machinery. It may only know that something beyond the ridge is also steady, and it has taken that steadiness as company, and built its own stillness to echo it.

It does not know you. You are a weight that will rise and fall and leave a print, and the print will refill, and the mat will go on. Every frond that falls, every creature that dies on the lichen, every traveller who lies down on the cool skin to rest goes into the same slow digestion, and the mat keeps no record of any of it. What it keeps is the arrangement: the fixed pools, the fixed stems, the fixed angle of each fern against the grey sky.

If you stay long enough, you may notice that the mat is breathing. The drizzle that drifts through without seeming to fall is its exhalation, and it comes at the same interval every time. Lie down, and you will feel a pulse under the felt, very slow, so much slower than your own that you will have to wait a long while to be sure of it. It is not hostile. It is not even awake, in any sense you would recognize. It is only the place itself, the one thing in the basin that has never once been anywhere else, and it has grown so quiet that your presence registers as a small, temporary disturbance on a surface it will eventually forget.
