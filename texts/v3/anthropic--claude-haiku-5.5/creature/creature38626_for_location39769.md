The builders are the reason the hollow is a city at all, and you find them first by their work. Their bodies are soft and segmented, nearly the color of the clay they mix, pale ochre dulled to the gray of wet stone. Each one carries a load of river silt in its front mouthparts and a bead of resin in a gland along its flank, and each one knows exactly where the next ring of wall should go. They work mostly in the dark hours, when the fog is thickest and the drip from the leaves is loudest, so that the sound of their labor is hard to tell from the rain. In the morning the fresh seams on the mounds are still glossy, and the galleries smell sharply of the resin, sweet and a little like crushed sap.

Individually they seem almost unfinished, more like a stage in a process than a creature with a self. They have eyes only at the tips of their stalks, pale and clouded, and they seem to navigate by touch and by the chemistry of the walls under their bodies. Watch one for long enough and you notice it does not wander. Each movement is the next movement of a larger pattern, and when one pauses to press its head against a damp patch of wall, the pause is shared by the others nearby, a ripple of stillness moving through a gallery and then dissolving back into work. Their attention is spread across the whole structure. Whatever they know, they know in the walls.

When two of them meet on a bridge of cured clay, they touch antennae in a brief flicker, and the one that has been carrying silt sometimes sets down its load and turns back the way it came. Whether this is greeting or correction, no one watching could say for certain. The gesture that the wall-crawlers also make, two front limbs lifted and held, is the same gesture, and it is possible the builders learned it from them, or the crawlers learned it from the builders, long before anyone was here to notice.

Along the trails the long-legged walkers move in their dotted lines, pale and stick-thin, each one following the scent that the last one left. They are the messengers and carriers, and they never seem to be going anywhere in particular. A trail will run from one mound across the flats to a fallen log and back again, and the walkers on it will go around a patch of ground that has just gone soft, then cross it when it dries, as if the trail itself were a living thing that moves with the land. When a walker reaches the water's edge, it stops, antennae lifted toward the pool, and then turns, and that turn is the only decision it makes all day.

The beetles are the bright spots in the gray. Their shells are blue-black, glossy as oil on water, and they turn over the mud at the edges of the shallows in slow arcs, rolling up the silt to find whatever is soft underneath. When the light breaks through the lid of cloud, the shells flash a deep iridescent blue for a moment and then go dull again. They are not social like the builders, and they do not seem to care about the mounds at all. They dig, they eat, they move on, and they are, as far as anything can tell, content to be small and unremarkable in a place that is still deciding what it will be.

The many-jointed things live on the walls of the galleries, moving along the glazed surfaces with a clicking, deliberate gait. There are too many legs for the eye to count, and they seem to have been made for climbing a surface that is always slightly damp and always slightly rising. They stop, lift two front limbs, and hold them there. Then they go on. The gesture is the most common thing in the hollow, and the least explained.

In the largest pool beneath the broad leaves, the flat-headed thing lies half-sunk, its eyes two small hard points just above the surface of the water. It is still for an hour at a time, and the stillness is so complete that the surface of the pool forgets it is there, and a leaf drops onto the water and drifts across the reflection of its own eyes. Nothing moves in the pool except, very occasionally, a slow bubble that rises from the deep, a low sound like something breathing under the mud. When a walker wanders too near the edge, the pool does not so much strike as close, and the splash is over before the ripples reach the shore. Afterward the surface is smooth, and the walker's trail goes on as though nothing has happened, because for the walkers nothing has.

Above, in the gaps of the canopy, the small winged hunters dart and circle. They are quick and brief, and they seem to hunt by the sound of the drips, striking at the places where the water falls. Sometimes a drop from a leaf carries something with it, a faint buzzing or a sudden scrape, and then the drop lands on the silt and the sound stops. The hunters do not linger. They take what they need and go, and the canopy goes back to its slow dripping.

The frogs are the color of wet bark and are almost impossible to see until they move. They sit on fallen logs and at the margins of the flats, breathing in a rhythm that matches the drip. They do not build and they do not hunt much. They wait for the insects to come near enough, and then they take them, and the wait is the whole of their lives.

All of this lives in a quiet that is not empty. Everything is listening: the builders in their walls, the walkers on their scent, the beetles in the mud, the pool-thing in the water, the hunters in the leaves, the frogs on the logs. The sounds that do come are soft and few, and each one seems to mean something to the creature that makes it and to the creatures that hear it. The water drips from leaves onto leaves. The silt ticks as it settles. A claw scrapes clay. Then nothing, and the nothing is full of attention.

And the whole population is building, waiting, hunting, and moving in a place that does not hold still. The river will keep laying down its film, and the builders will keep raising their walls to meet it. The walkers will keep finding new trails across the rising ground. The pool will keep its patience. In a few seasons the hollow may be a forest, and in a few decades a bare glassy plain with no trace of any of them. For now they are what the place is, a city that is not yet finished, its citizens going about the slow, watchful business of making a world out of silt, one wet ring of wall at a time.
