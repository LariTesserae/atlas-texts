Look closer at any one of the small walkers and it stops looking like an animal. Its shell is a spiral of bands, each one a little lighter or darker than the last, the way a tree's rings record its wet and dry years. The bands are a record. Each one marks a season of growth, and at the seam where one band meets the next, the walker has shed its old shell and climbed out of it, soft and pale, to harden in the open air. The shed skins don't disappear. They sink into the mounds and fuse there, so every mound is a library of molts, and every walker begins its life standing on the cast-off bodies of everything that came before.

The walkers are not alone in their work. They move in ranks, and their clicks travel through the ranks in patterns. One shell strikes another, the second strikes a third, and a ripple of sharp little sounds runs across the floor faster than any creature could walk. The ripple carries information. It tells the ranks when a vent has begun to swell, when the water hollow is drying, when a disc has lifted its edge somewhere and the nearest walkers should scatter. The bowl does not respond to its weather the way a field of separate animals would. It responds the way a single nervous system does, with a thousand relays firing in sequence and no one relay in charge.

The disc-shaped things are the bowl's closing reflex. They lie so flat that they seem to be part of the rock, and they sense the walkers' passing through vibration and heat. When they fold, they do not simply kill. They take the walker's whole substance into a hiss of acid and then hold very still, digesting in the crust, until the walker's band-pattern has been dissolved and the disc's own edges have hardened again. They are the bowl's way of keeping its numbers and its patterns from growing beyond what the water can support.

The brass burrowers keep the cycle clean. They carry what the discs leave down into the mounds, and the mounds take it in as they take in everything, cementing it into new rings. The paper-winged things, the ones that drift above the vents, seem to be the bowl's only means of traveling. They rise on the hot columns, spiral over the basin, and settle in clusters on the rim, and then some of them go on past it, out onto the plateau. Something in the transparent membrane of their wings holds a pattern. Where one settles, the walkers below shift their ranks, and a new hollow becomes a new centre of gathering. It is possible they are seeds, or messengers, or the bowl's nerves reaching for ground it has not yet covered.

Then there is the tapping.

It comes from the passage under the broken arch, slow and even, steady as a heartbeat. The walkers do not gather around it the way they gather at the water, and the discs do not fold toward it. It is not one of the ranks, and it does not click in their pattern. Its tap is on stone, not shell. But the basin's rhythm and the tap are not separate. When the tapping quickens, the vents begin to swell a short while later. When the vents peak, the tapping slows. It is possible the tap sets the pace of the whole cycle and the bowl simply follows it. It is also possible the tapping is a prisoner, something sealed beneath the keystone that is still counting the years it has been held there, and that the walkers are its jailers or its tenders or its children. The tapping has no answer to either guess.

The grooves on the six standing stones are the clearest clue, and they still don't settle the question. The lines are parallel bands, and the walkers' shells carry the same bands. The grooves have been slowly filling with living bodies. The stones were not built from the land as an animal builds a nest. They are what the walkers' ancestors left behind when they molted into stone. Or they are the mark of some people who were drawn into the ranks, whose bodies were shaped by the same heat and grit into the same banding, and who now speak through clicks in the dark. The bowl does not say which. It only goes on scraping, shedding, and rebuilding, as if it were writing something very slowly that it has not yet finished reading.

Climbing out, you hear the clicks follow you for a long while, faint and orderly, passing from shell to shell up the slope, until the sound thins into the hiss of the vents and the far, steady tapping under the arch, counting.
