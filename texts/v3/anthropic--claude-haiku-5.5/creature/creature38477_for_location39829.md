The first thing you notice is that some of the heads in the water are not still.

They sit in the cisterns of the third and fourth terraces, where the water is shallow and warm enough to hold the mist. Each head rests just above the surface, with the crown bare and the eyes half-closed or wholly open, depending on the individual. Their skin is the colour of wet slate and has taken on the texture of the calcareous moss that lines the basins, so that at a distance they read as outcrops, as knots in the stone. Up close, the skin has a faint sheen, like the inside of a shell. Gills, long since sealed into folds, sit under the jaw. Their hands lie flat on the basin floor, fingers spread, and over years the fingers have grown fused to the moss, so that a watcher cannot lift a hand without pulling away a little of the terrace.

They do not breathe in any way you can see. Watch one for an hour and the only change is the slow rise of its eyelids, which open at the same moment the frogs in the nearest pool begin a new phrase. This is the first clue to what they are doing.

The frogs keep time. Their chorus in the lower pools is not random. It runs in intervals of roughly nine hundred heartbeats, and each cycle has a shape: a rising swell, a held silence, a descending call that ends in a single sharp note. The watchers track the cycle. Their eyes open at the sharp note and close at the swell. Between those moments they are, as far as anyone can tell, listening to the water.

It is tempting to call this waiting, but waiting implies an end. Nothing here is coming, and the watchers seem to know it. What they attend to is the surface of their own cistern, which is perfectly level and holds one reflection that never changes, the grey underside of the canopy sixty meters above. Some of them have spent so long looking at it that the basin walls have grown a faint depression under each chin, a shallow seat, worn by the patient friction of a body that does not move.

There are older ones too, deeper in the terraces, where the water is cold and black. These have stopped opening their eyes at all. Their heads are half-sunk, and the moss has climbed over their brows and collected in the wrinkles of their necks, and small ferns grow from their ears. Birds perch on them. The birds seem to regard them as furniture, and the watchers seem to regard the birds as weather. You might pass one and believe it is only an unusual rock until it breathes, slowly, and a film of water runs off its nose.

Between the cisterns, something walks.

You will hear it before you see it, a heavy, deliberate thud carried through the root-floor, and the watchers do not turn toward it, but they do change. Their hands lift an inch from the moss. The frogs fall briefly silent. The thing comes into view on the terrace path, a quadruped the size of a house, with a hide that is more bark than skin and a broad flat head that bends low to the ground. It moves along the same line it has always moved along, a path worn to a groove in the root-stone. At each cistern it stops and lowers its head into the channel where the water runs in, and it pulls out a mat of silt and dead leaf with its teeth and lays it on the bank, and then it packs new moss into the cracks in the basin wall with a pad of its forelimb, and then it moves on. It does not look at the watchers. The watchers do not look at it. But when it passes, the water in each cistern ripples once, outward from the wall it has touched, and then settles, and the reflection of the canopy comes back exactly as it was.

This is the maintainer. It has no name that anyone has given it. It does not appear to need food, or rest, or anything except the repetition of its route. Where it stops, the water runs clear. Where it does not reach, the channels clog slowly and a terrace drowns a few centimetres deeper, and the watchers in that cistern sink by a fraction, and the frogs in that pool change their phrase by a beat. Everything here is adjusted, constantly, by something that does not seem to know it is adjusting anything.

The birds are the only things that move without purpose. They tear across the open air between the canopy walls in loose flocks, calling in long descending runs, and they land on the watchers and on the fallen trunks and on each other, and they drink from the cisterns, and sometimes one of them stays too long and its feathers grow dull and wet, and then it is still too, and it becomes part of the stone.

Here is what you might eventually understand, if you sit at the edge of a cistern long enough and let the rain soak your hair flat against your skull. The terraces were not built for anyone. They were grown, and the watchers were among the things that grew them, or perhaps among the things that were grown into them, the difference blurred by ages of water. They climbed up through the cloud once, long ago, the way you did, and let the water take them. Some of them chose the basins. Some were chosen by the moss. What is left is a population that has finished becoming architecture, that holds the terraces level from inside their own bodies, and that goes on looking at a reflection that no longer changes because there is nothing left in them that wants it to.

When the frogs sound their sharp note, one of the watchers in the nearest cistern opens its eyes fully. For a moment the grey light catches in them, and the surface of the water trembles, very faintly, as though something beneath it has shifted its weight.

Then the swell begins again, the eyes close, and the rain goes on falling at the same angle it always has.
