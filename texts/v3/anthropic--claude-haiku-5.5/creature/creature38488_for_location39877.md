# Those Who Keep the Shelves

The first thing you notice moving across the sinter is the grazers, and you take them for the owners of the place until you look closer.

They are low and broad, many-legged, with shells the texture of glazed ceramic. Their backs are pale and faintly rose, the same tint as the terraces, so at a distance a herd looks like a patch of the shelf that has decided to walk. Their mouthparts are flat combs, and they drag them across the fresh crust with a patient, rasping rhythm you can feel through the soles of your feet even though you cannot hear it. Where they have fed, the stone is smooth and the pale glaze is thinner. Where they have not fed for a while, the terraces grow lumpy and ridged. They are not destroying the sinter. They are its finishing hand, scraping the newest layer into lips and ripples and leaving behind a surface that will harden into the next step.

Their shells are made from what they eat. Each grazer secretes a glaze as it moves, a silica film that thickens over its life, so the old ones are the most lustrous, nearly mirror-bright in the noon glare. Watch a herd for a day and you will see the young ones trailing behind, duller and smaller, and the elders at the front, slow and gleaming, the way a crew follows its most practiced worker. The herd moves as a single shape. No one leads it audibly. When the grazers gather under the gutters at the start of an afternoon rain, they press together and tilt their combs up toward the falling sweet water, and for a few minutes the whole herd is motionless and open-mouthed, drinking the condensation off the stone like a congregation at a fountain.

The wading forms stand in the pools and seem to be the watchers. Their long legs are rooted in the margins, and their bodies are so clear in the light that their inner workings show: a dark, slow pulse in the chest, a lattice of something pale that might be bone or might be crystallized mineral taken up from the water. They hold still for so long that you begin to wonder whether they are alive in the ordinary sense, until one strikes. The strike is a single unfolding, neck and beak and then nothing, and the small swimming things in the warm margins are simply gone. Afterward the wader resettles and the ripples spread out across the pool. The pools are rarely clear for long, but they are never empty. The waders keep the water working, thinning its crowds and returning what they take to the mineral cycle through their own slow dissolution.

Their stillness is not idleness. They sense the pumps. Watch one at the edge of a pool where a pipe runs down into the water, and you will see its head tilt in time with the shiver of the metal, a rhythm that is invisible to you until you match it. The waders seem to hear the machines through the water and the stone. They know when the city is pulling water from the deep, and they know when the flow changes.

Above the cooling stacks, the winged flocks turn in their tight spirals, and it is easy to think of them as the least important of the living things here, mere ornaments of the thermal columns. But they carry something. Where they land on the pipe-tops, the scale grows in the pattern of their feet, and the sharp crystalline spines that line the pipe seams often run in short bursts that begin exactly where a flock has settled. Their wingbeats, the only sound the place makes beyond the water, are not a call but a kind of punctuation: they pass close to the stacks, and the vapor shivers. Some of the spines seem to be grown from seed-crystals that cling to their feathers, carried from pool to pipe and pipe to pool across the whole basin. The flocks are gardeners of a sort, planting the hard lattice that the machines will later be built around.

And then there are the ones who are not seen at all.

The stepping stones across the pool were laid by hands, or by something with a sense of order and placement. The doors of the shelters are propped open with stones worn smooth by the same kind of touch. The railings are polished on the places where a grip would fall. None of it looks abandoned. It looks like the work of creatures who stepped out for a moment and will return. You cannot find them, and you begin to suspect you are not meant to.

Some of the spines along the pipe seams form patterns too regular for simple mineral chance: parallel ridges that run like handwriting, junctions that branch at the angles a maintenance crew would need to reach a valve. It is possible that the builders are still there in some form, not as bodies moving through the terraces but as a logic embedded in the scale, growing the hard structures they once tended, or perhaps the scale grew into their habits until the two could not be told apart. The grazers have learned which seams to avoid. The waders have learned where the pumps run strongest. The flocks have learned which pipe-tops are warm enough to settle on in the evening. Each creature has absorbed a piece of a plan no one is still directing.

By evening the mist returns in long banks, the pools go violet, and the flocks settle on the pipe-tops in a row so even it seems deliberate, like a shift changing. The grazers withdraw to the lee of the gantries. The waders fold their necks. The stacks still breathe their slow vapor, and the city's gutters keep running in the dark. Nothing here is outside the loop. The grazers make the terraces, the waders thin the pools, the flocks plant the spines, the pumps move the water, the stacks make the rain, and the rain feeds the grazers again. Whoever built the Sinter Shelves may be gone, but they are not absent. They are the shape the living things have settled into, the way water settles into the channel of a riverbed, and what lives on the terraces now is that shape, moving quietly and continuously, keeping the work going.
