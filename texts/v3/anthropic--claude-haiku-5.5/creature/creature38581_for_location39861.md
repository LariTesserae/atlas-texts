You feel them before you see them. The damp settles on your neck and wrists, and somewhere below the haze a valve is turning, slow and grudging, with a sound like a knuckle cracking. Whatever lives here lives in the wet, in the seams between the water and the stone, and mostly it does not come out to meet you.

**The Clickers**

The pale segmented things in the standing pools are the most visible, and the most misunderstood. Each is about the size of a hand, ivory-coloured, with a dozen jointed plates along the back that rattle when they move. They walk stiffly, and the clicking is their gait. A single one makes little noise. A dozen together sound like a dry rain on a tin roof.

They are scrapers. They graze the film in the channels and pick the mineral crust from the valve seats with fine, folding forelimbs, keeping the old feedlines open a few microns at a time. If you watch long enough you notice that they move in pulses, and the pulses match the knock of the distant pumps. They wait for the thud, then move, then wait again. Nobody taught them this. The pumps and the clickers may have grown into each other over generations until the difference stopped mattering. Where the clickers have died out, the valves seize within a season. Where they are thick, the water runs clean, and the pools hold their tea-coloured clarity.

**The Mats**

The violet and ochre films on the north faces are not many creatures. They are one creature, spread thin across kilometres of wall, gantry, and roof, joined by threads too fine to see. It takes its moisture from the sweat that runs down the surfaces, and in exchange it eats the metal, slowly and precisely. The mats can be read. Where a gantry was load-bearing, the mat grows heavier along the old stress lines. Where a conveyor once ran, a faint, pale stripe persists, like a memory of motion. If the mat has a mind, it is a mind that remembers the shape of what it is dissolving. It is not hostile. It is patient, which here amounts to the same thing.

**The Lace**

In the shaded pump halls the fungal sheets grow in soft grey lace, and at dusk the edges begin to glow. The glow tracks the pumps. When a pump cycles on, a ripple of light runs along the lace like current along a wire. When it stops, the light pools and sinks back into the frond. The lace is the nearest thing this place has to a nervous system, and it seems to be the part of the city that is still listening. People who work the old halls say the lace is warm to the touch, and that it is warmest right before a pump fails. Nobody knows whether it is warning the machines or simply feeding on the heat they throw off. Both explanations may be true.

**The Winged Ones**

The great shapes over the rim are not residents. They ride the thermals from the warm stone, rising in long spirals that carry them up past the haze and out of sight. They never come within sight of the city, and it is tempting to say they avoid it. But the vent stacks open each afternoon, releasing their long warm exhalations of fog, and the winged ones tend to circle nearest the rim where those exhalations drift. They seem to feed on the warmth, or on something carried in it. Their presence is part of the afternoon rhythm, like the clicking and the pumps. Whether they know the city is there is an open question.

**The Keepers**

The lit windows are the last thing to mention, and the hardest. Five or six lit structures are visible from any point. Behind some of those blue-green panes live people, or what remains of people who learned to live inside the clock. They keep the schedules. They wake before dawn, walk the channels with lamps, and open or close valves according to tables scratched on the inside of their doors. Some of the tables are very old, and no one remembers the reasons for them. The keepers do not always understand what they are doing. They understand that stopping is what kills things here, so they do not stop. Some have grown quiet and precise, moving with the same stiff timing as the clickers. Some have grown their own mineral lace on their sills and walls. Some, in the afternoons, sit in the vent fog and let it bead on their skin, the way the stone does, and it is hard to say which of them is the one doing the waiting.

You will hear them before you find them: a door being unlatched, a voice calling a count across an empty plaza, the rattle of a cart over pavement that has heaved into ridges. You will almost never see them walking openly in the streets. They keep to the channels and the lit rooms, where the water runs and the machines still answer.

**All Together**

None of these are separate from the water. The clickers keep the valves open, the mats eat the metal that the water loosens, the lace feels the pumps, the winged ones ride the heat that the stone releases in the same cycle that brings the sweat. The keepers turn the valves by hand and take their rhythm from the rest. What lives here is less a population than a circulation, a slow metabolism in which the city, the water, and whatever breathes among them keep passing the same drop back and forth. Stop attending to it for a season and the place will close over you, mineral and film and lace, and it will keep running anyway.
