# The People of the Shelf

The people who live on the Sallow Shelf call themselves **the Tended**, though the word has changed meaning several times. Once it meant those cared for by the condensers. Now it means those who care for them, and it also means those who have been let into the water-ways and the moss. Lowlanders call them *drips* or *fogfolk*. The Tended don't mind, mostly. They have more pressing things to think about.

## Bodies and Senses

Generations on the plateau have changed them, though slowly and not uniformly. Their skin tends toward a pale, cool tone and stays damp in the way of things kept in shade. They rarely sweat, because the air never gets warm enough to need it. Their eyes have adapted to the flat, diffuse light: they read texture and motion well in gray conditions and are often uneasy in direct sun, which few of them see anyway.

Their hearing matters most. Sound behaves strangely in fog, so the Tended listen the way sailors watch the sea. They can tell the note of a clogged intake from a clean one. They can locate a leaking joint by its ticking, and they know a pump is failing before the gauge moves. Many can hear the hum of the condensers as a set of separate voices, each with its own pitch, and when one goes quiet they wake from sleep. Silence in the night means something has broken.

They speak softly. Voices carry in ways that make shouting unreliable, so a whisper across a terrace is often clearer than a call. Children learn early to speak close and to lean in.

## Work and Roles

Most of the Tended work for the water, and their roles are old and specific.

**Lattice-tenders** climb the condensers in harnesses of waxed cord, scraping the frames, replacing mesh, and welding patches over rust. They are the city's most respected workers and its most often injured. Their hands are scarred from the metal and their calves are thick from the climbs.

**Channel-readers** walk the braided rivers each morning. They judge the water by color, sediment, and the sound of the current over stone, and they decide where new cuts must be made and which old beds to block. A good channel-reader's advice can decide whether a terrace stays dry through the wet season.

**Moss-wardens** hold the green back. They cut, burn, and salt the advancing growth along the city's edges, and they clear the pipes that the moss has swallowed. Some wardens do the opposite and cultivate: they graft certain lichens and mosses into the walls of houses for insulation and for the cool, springy bedding they provide. This is an argument within the Tended that has never been settled.

**Pool-keepers** tend the tank-plant ponds, the most closely guarded water on the Shelf. They skim larvae, count the translucent crustaceans, and keep rainwater from spilling into the wrong basin. Their work is quiet and patient, and they are often the first to notice that a pond has changed.

**Pitcher-rousers** harvest the digestive fluid from the hanging plants at dusk, when the lids open. The fluid cleans wounds and preserves salt-fish and is traded down to the lowlands for metal. It takes steady hands, because a rouser who is slow to close a lid can be dosed by the plant's own juices.

## Households and Terraces

The Tended live in households of six to twenty, usually grouped around one condenser. The household that tends a condenser owns its water, and this makes them the most powerful unit in the city, though the power is quiet. Disputes between households are fought over pools, over channel rights, and over the dry-week contracts, which set how much water each terrace may draw when the rains fail. These contracts are sworn at the change of each season, and they are recited aloud by the eldest of each house. A broken contract is one of the few things the Tended treat as a crime.

Houses are low, thick-walled, and sheathed in moss on the north faces. The Tended sleep close to the warmth of their lamps and use oiled wool, waxed cloth, and a rubber made from tree sap, which they harvest in long strips from the broad-leaved trees. Their clothing is layered and dark. Bright colors are rare and valued. A red scrap sewn to a cloak is a mark of a pool-keeper's family or of a household that has lost someone to the Lattice.

## Death and Return

When someone dies, the Tended do not burn or bury them. They are laid at the edge of the moss, wrapped in cloth and left for the green. The moss takes them over a season or two. Families visit the growth as it spreads over the body and say the names of the dead while they watch. It is considered an honor, and an obligation, that the living maintain the patch of moss afterward and do not cut it back.

Some find this strange. The Tended find the alternative stranger. They say the Shelf has been taking things back for longer than there have been people, and that it would be rude to refuse.

## The Ones Not Seen

The Tended share their land with more than the plants and the salamanders. Their oldest customs acknowledge a presence they rarely name.

Work crews sometimes find pipes rerouted overnight, their joints clean and perfectly fitted, in a way no one in the city can replicate. Pools that had been left to dry are found refilled, their edges cleared of debris. Once in a while, at the edge of a fern field in fog, someone sees a shape: a bulk moving low, unhurried, too dim to name. The Tended set out bowls of fresh water at the boundaries of the moss, and they do not take the bowls back in. The bowls are gone by morning.

Pool-keepers say that whatever moves through the fog counts the ponds. Lattice-tenders say it listens to the condensers, and that it is the reason a failing machine sometimes restarts on its own. Channel-readers say the braided rivers follow its paths more than the ground's slope. No one has proven any of this. No one has proven the opposite either.

## What They Are

The Tended are not masters of the Shelf and do not pretend to be. They are more like a crew that has shipped onto a vessel too large to steer, learning its creaks and currents, bailing when they must and mending when they can. Their lives are a constant bargain with water, growth, and the dim presence beneath the fog. They have adapted their bodies, their speech, their rituals, and their architecture to a place that never stops moving. In exchange, the Shelf lets them stay, and the fog, each morning, settles softly onto the roofs of their city as though it had never meant to leave.
