# Who Lives on the Ashen Shelf

The people of Corvane are the most visible inhabitants, but they are not the only ones who keep the Shelf the way it is. The damp, the altitude, and the young stone shape every living thing here, and the same patterns recur across them: steady rhythms, careful repetition, and a stillness that seems to be something the inhabitants have agreed to rather than something imposed on them.

## The People of Corvane

They are lean and sure-footed, built for stepped streets and steep terraces. Years of thin air have given their chests a wide, slow breathing, and they rarely hurry, even when a sudden task calls for it. Their skin takes on the dampness of the place: it is often cool to the touch, and it darkens slightly after rain, so that a crowd on the terraces at midday looks almost like the wet basalt around them. They dress in heavy wool and tight-woven fiber, and they carry their cloaks rolled across their shoulders, ready for the mist that comes and goes without warning.

Their hands are their most striking feature. Years of braiding rope, knapping obsidian, and setting basalt blocks have made them precise and patient. A child of Corvane learns to split reed before she learns to read the terrace lines, and by adolescence most people can fit two stones together by touch in the dark. They have a particular talent for knots and joins, and they will spend a full morning on a single seam in a basket because a seam that holds water matters more than a day's speed.

They count. Not in numbers so much as in intervals. Ask a woman of Corvane how long a task will take and she will answer in drips of the forest, or in the number of times the birds sing before the fog lifts. Their sense of time is patient and cyclical. Harvest comes after the grain goes silver, re-laying the stones comes after the waters rise in the lower channels, and no one has ever needed a calendar because the year arrives in the same order each time it comes.

The families who lead the upper terraces are called the *Hollin houses*, after the stone-cutters who first combed the hillsides. They are not rulers in any formal sense. They are the ones who know where each terrace line runs, which stones must be re-set after the rains, and which channels to clear before the water rises. People defer to them the way one defers to a well-worn path: out of habit, and because it has always worked. When a child asks why the Hollin houses lead, the answer is always the same: *because they have always led, and the terraces remember them.*

Asked about floods, the elders speak carefully. There was a flood once, they say, in the time before the time before. The stones were re-laid. Nothing has flooded since, and nothing will, so the stones are re-laid anyway, every year, in the same places. Sometimes a visitor points out that the channels are never full. The people of Corvane nod and say: *Yes. That is why we keep them clean.*

## The People of the Hamlets

Out at the heads of the valleys, the hamlet people are quieter still. They carry news on foot, walking the worn paths that connect their small settlements, and they are fluent in long silences. A traveler arriving at a hamlet is greeted with food and a bench but few questions. The hamlet people have learned that most things here arrive without being sought, and that patience is a kind of courtesy.

They are excellent trackers and weavers. Their loom knocks carry through the still air for a long way, and an experienced ear can tell from across a ridge which household is working and what they are making. Many of them have never seen Corvane's full stepped streets, and they speak of the town as a place of great density and noise, a little like a strange bird with too many voices.

## The Herds

The grey, long-legged grazers move through the terraces in loose, unhurried herds. Their thick coats keep the damp off their backs, and they carry a faint sheen of moisture that makes them look like they have just come up from the pools. They know the terrace lines better than most people do. They graze the outermost edges first, then cut across the contour in a pattern that repeats from season to season, and they return to the same bedding stones each evening, leaving small hollows worn into the moss.

They are watchful but not skittish. A herd will lift its heads at a footstep, track the walker for a while, and then lower its heads again to the silver grain, as if it has decided the walker is part of the scenery and not a threat to it.

## The Birds and the Small Singers

The blue-black birds roost in the cracks of the oldest buildings, so their song sounds from inside the walls of Corvane. At dawn they sing in a fixed order, the same phrases in the same sequence, and the people of Corvane say you can set a day by them. Their voices are soft and bright, like glass struck gently under water. If you listen long enough, the song seems to repeat exactly, down to the pauses.

The amphibians gather in the pools and call through the rain. Their calls overlap in a steady texture rather than a chorus, each one answering the last at a regular interval, so that a whole pool seems to hum on a single held note. The people of Corvane never interrupt the frogs' calls with talk. They say the calls are how the pools keep their level.

## The Reptiles and the Slow Things

Low, broad reptiles bask on warm basalt at midday, moving only when the sun breaks through and the stone warms. They are the only creatures here that seem to live at a different tempo: slow to wake, slower to leave, and they vanish into the moss at the first footstep with a sudden, startling speed. Some of the older people say the reptiles are the oldest inhabitants of the Shelf, present before the people, and that they still remember the lava as a soft red floor. This may be only a story. But the reptiles do seem to be the only creatures who do not return to the same spot every day. They bask where the stone is warmest, and that changes a little with each season.

## The Canopy and the Hum

High in the cloud forest, tree-climbing animals move through the leaves with a rustle that carries far. Few people ever see them clearly. Their passage marks the forest as much as the drip of leaves does, a kind of irregular counterpoint to the steady beat of water.

Beneath it all runs the insect drone, a low, persistent hum that seems to come from everywhere at once. It is the most constant sound on the Shelf, and visitors sometimes mistake it for silence until it stops. It has never stopped for long. When the wind comes and moves the forest in its long wave, the drone rises and falls with it, and then, as the wind dies, it settles back into its unchanging pitch.

## What Keeps the Shape

Here the people of Corvane and the animals of the Shelf do much the same thing: they repeat, they return, and they keep to a cycle. The herds bed down in the same places. The birds sing in the same order. The people re-lay the same stones. Each one follows a pattern that was laid down before anyone thought to ask why.

In the old carvings on the lava columns, a figure stands at the edge of the ridgelines, always at the same distance from the hamlets, always facing the same direction. The people of Corvane say it is a carver who lived long ago, watching the land so the carving would be accurate. Some say it is one of the Hollin ancestors. Some say nothing at all, and smile when the question is asked.

The forest drips at intervals that could be counted. The pools hold their level. The fog lifts on schedule. Something in the Shelf keeps all of this from changing, and if you stay long enough you begin to feel it: not a single creature, but a kind of agreement among the inhabitants, the stone, the water, and the cloud, to remain exactly as they are.

Visitors feel it first as unease. Then, after a few weeks, they stop noticing it. That, the people of Corvane say, is how you know you have become one of the Shelf's inhabitants too.
