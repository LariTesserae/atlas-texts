The first thing you notice is the scraping. Along the lower terraces, where the film of mineral is thickest, a grazer moves in a slow, many-legged crawl, each limb ending in a flattened blade that takes a thin layer off the stone and leaves it bright and raw. The pale green film it removes is a kind of bloom, and the grazer eats it. The scraped surface is a little more open, a little more ready to take new growth. Watch one for long enough and you see that it never works in a straight line. It keeps to the ridges and the seams, where the stone is most stressed, and it lingers where the lattice has begun to split. It seems to read the structure through the vibration of its own feet, as if the city were a long chord it was tuning.

Above the grazers, and sometimes among them, the stitchers move along the lattice seams. They are segmented, each body a chain of small pale units no bigger than a fist, and each segment carries a gland that secretes a fast-setting paste. A stitcher does not so much build as thread. It lays its body along a gap, extrudes paste into the join, and moves on, curling its tail-segments through the gaps like a needle through cloth. Their work is precise where the structure is sound and careless where it is failing, and in the places where a storm has torn a wall apart, you can see the stitchers gathering in clusters, pressing their bodies against the broken edges as if listening for the place where the stone wants to meet itself again.

The banners hang from the arches. They are the longest creatures on the plateau, sometimes as long as a tall man, flattened into translucent ribbons that trail from the highest points of the spires and sway in the currents. Their bodies are covered with fine filaments that catch particles drifting in the water, and the rhythm of their movement stirs the surrounding water into small eddies that carry food toward them and, not incidentally, deposit the lightest mineral dust in the curves of the arches. Many of the arches on the plateau are casts. A banner hangs in one place for years while the lattice forms around its body, and then it sheds, leaving behind a hollow tube of calcified stone that is shaped exactly like the hanging of a living thing. The banners do not seem to know this. They simply hang where the current is best, and the city grows around the places where they have chosen to be.

The drifters are the largest and the slowest. They move at mid-depth, through the basins and along the causeways, dim shapes with broad, soft bodies and long grasping limbs folded against their flanks. They are the ones who move the old stone. After a storm, when the western slope has been scattered and the fragments lie heaped in the silt, the drifters come down from the deep water and begin to sort. They lift a block in their limbs, turn it, set it against another, and hold it there for a long time, sometimes many hours, before moving on. What they are doing is not clear. The new patterns they make are not always stable, and sometimes a drifter will take apart what another has built in a single night. But over seasons, the scattered wreckage of a collapse often ends up in a shape that looks, from the surface, almost deliberate: a buttress where the storm left a heap, a causeway where two basins were once separated by a ridge. The drifters seem less concerned with building anything in particular than with keeping the stone in motion, moving it from one place to another so that nothing is ever simply lost.

The creatures communicate through light and through the water itself. Bioluminescence flickers across the basins in bursts, a few seconds of cold blue or pale green, and then fades. The bursts come in patterns, often at dusk or in the hour before a storm-state arrives. A bright flicker that travels along a causeway seems to pass a message from one group to another. Another, held and repeated, appears to gather the stitchers and grazers into a single place, and the lattice around that place begins to thicken as if they have been called to repair something. When a surge is coming, the signals grow quieter. The drifters leave the basins for the deep water. The banners retract their filaments and hang limp. The grazers pull back into the seams of the older ruins, where the stone is thickest and the water is calmest, and they wait. The storm comes through the terraces with a roar that the whole plateau seems to carry in its stone, and when it passes the creatures return, one by one, to the wreckage.

They die, and their death becomes part of the place. When a stitcher or a grazer stops moving, its body settles into the silt, and over time the mineral it carried, and the mineral it has shed over its life, bonds with the nearest lattice. Some of the soft, sponge-textured grey of the older structures is the remains of creatures who once lived in the water and now lie inside the walls. The city is a record of its own inhabitants, not in the sense that it preserves them as memory, but in the sense that every structure contains the material of those who made it, and every new structure is built from the stone of old ones. In this way the builders are also the building, and the building is also what has been built.

What they are, finally, is not easy to say. They are not a civilization in any human sense, and they have no obvious purpose beyond the work of moving and joining and letting go. But the work is continuous and it is coordinated, and it responds to the water with a kind of attentiveness that looks, from a distance, like care. The grazers listen to the stress in the stone. The stitchers follow the cracks. The drifters carry the wreckage back toward something like order. The banners hang where the current is strongest and the city bends around them. None of them builds to last, and none of them builds to be seen. They build because the water moves, and the stone moves with it, and something has to keep working the seams together while the mountains wear away underneath, slowly, toward a horizon no one here has ever seen.
