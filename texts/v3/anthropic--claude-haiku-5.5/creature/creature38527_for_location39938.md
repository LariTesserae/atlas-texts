**The Ones Who Drink**

The small ones are the majority, and you notice them only when you stop moving. Then you see that the ribs are not bare metal but furred with them, clusters of pale, translucent bodies packed so densely along the beams that the steel shows through only in slivers. Each one is a bead of living glass with a dozen or more legs folded beneath it. Their gut-sacs fill and swell as they drink the falling water, and you can watch the color travel through them, a faint silt-brown rising from the dark pool when they have fed well.

They do not seem to be aware of one another as individuals. When a cluster moves, it moves like a single surface, a ripple passing across a skin of wax. When a patch is disturbed, the disturbance spreads outward and is absorbed, the way a stone dropped in a basin ceases to be a stone. Their attention, if that is the word, is spread so thin that it might be better described as a tendency: a settled inclination toward water, toward the seam, toward the shape the last generation left behind.

Their building is a kind of slow memory. The hanging nests and lace tubes are not designed so much as accumulated, each generation adding silt and crushed lichen to what the one before set down, and the secretion that binds them hardens at exactly the rate the water arrives. The result is architecture with no architect, a record of thousands of years of drinking written in grey and pale-ochre lace. If you held one of the tubes to the light, you would see the layers, thin as the rings in a cut tree, each one a season of this same unchanging weather.

**The Tenders**

The long-legged ones are fewer, and they move as if the air were thicker for them. Their legs are jointed in too many places, and they fold and unfold with a slowness that makes the eye lag. They are blind in the ordinary sense. Their heads are smooth, crowned with a ring of sensory pits that twitch toward moisture and warmth. They take falling drops straight from the air on their fore-limbs, lifting them to a mouth that seems barely to open, and they move through the mist from nest to nest with a patience that resembles, very closely, the patience of a nurse.

They are the ones who tend. They press their limbs against the hanging tubes to check the flow, they break away clogged lace with a delicacy that would be impossible for anything of their size, and they nudge the young from the nests and guide them toward the plates. Watching them, you begin to feel that the tenders are doing a job they were given long ago, by some process that no longer needs a name, and that they would go on doing it whether or not anything had ever been watching.

Nothing eats them. Nothing here eats anything that is not water, mineral, or another of the same. The tenders die, and their bodies go into the nests as silt, and the nests grow a little, and the water carries the rest down through the drains to wherever drains go.

**The Chorus**

At irregular intervals, the clicking rises. It starts in one rafter and spreads, a layered murmur like a crowd settling into a hush, then a rattle, then a cascade of whistles that sounds almost like speech if you let your ears believe it. It is not directed at anything you can see. It lasts perhaps a minute and then folds back into the general roar of rain and pumps, and the small ones return to their drinking as though nothing had happened.

You may come to believe the chorus is a kind of census, or a rhythm kept in step with the pumps, or the sound of a thousand bodies agreeing on a shape. The pumps cycle every eleven seconds, and the clicks and valves keep the same time, and it is easy to imagine the creatures have learned that beat so thoroughly that they breathe in it, drink in it, speak in it.

**What the Hall Takes**

There is one thing in the hall that is not water, mineral, or itself. It is the visitor. Your breath clouds at once in the cold, and the moisture in it is far warmer and more saturated than the air the machine draws in. You will notice that the nearest tubes, when you exhale, lean very slightly toward you. The tenders that move past you pause, their fore-limbs lifted, and when you step back they resume their route with a kind of disappointment you could almost mistake for your imagination.

The hall is a closed system with one opening, the louvers through which the air comes, and the air is the only inflow. Your breath is a new kind of inflow, a small wet gift that the place has not received in a long time, and it is not clear whether it has been waiting for you specifically or for anyone at all. The flowstone does not change. The nests do not change. But the tenders, when they lift their limbs toward your mouth, seem to be asking the one question the hall has always asked of every arrival: *will you stay to drink, or will you leave the water here?*

The place resists being left. You will feel it in the way the mist thickens at the door, in the slight chill that follows you up the steps, in the way the chorus rises as you turn your back. And if you find yourself, a week later, at a window, breathing slowly and wondering whether the moisture on the glass is your own, you may notice that the water is running a little more slowly than before, as though something very patient on the other side has begun, quietly, to keep you.
