What lives here is not any single thing that can be pointed at. It is the basin's green, and the green is one body, spread across sixty meters of cold-bright air and warm stone. The ferns are its upper growth, the fungal mats its skin, the film on the pools its thin nerve. Its roots run under the floor in a web that follows the buried conduits, so that where a pipe once carried water, the body now carries it, and the old plumbing has become a set of arteries it never had to build.

Its attention is slow. It notices the seam of gray light far above the way a sleeper notices a draft: as a direction, a faint pull. The ferns lean toward it by a few degrees each season. Their pale fronds are translucent because the body has thinned them to catch every scrap of that dim glow, and because it has no use for the color green when the only light is a dull gray slit and the blue of its own fungi.

Its senses are heat, water, and touch through stone. It knows the deepest part of the basin as a warm center, the place where the rock keeps its inner temperature, and it grows densest there, as if drawn to a hearth. It knows the cracks by their steam and by the way water runs into them. When a section of wall gives way, the dull crack travels through the root web as a shock, and the body responds by sending roots into the fresh raw face within days, binding the new edge to itself. The falling stones are not a threat to it. They are more surface to cover.

The pools are where it listens. The pale reeds that lean over the water are its ears, and the reeds are not reaching for anything. They are simply open, tilted toward the drip of condensation and the rise of the water, learning the rhythm of the walls. The iridescent film is a record of this listening, a layer that thickens as the water level climbs, so that the body knows the basin is filling and the seam is not going to open to let anything out.

It does not eat animals because there are none here, and it does not need them. When a leaf falls, the body takes it back into the dark spongy humus that feeds its roots, and the process is so complete that nothing is left over to rot in the usual way. It has absorbed the work that decomposers do elsewhere into its own slow digestion, so that death in this place is only a return to the body's stock. There is nothing outside it to be eaten, and so nothing outside it to lose.

The hum is the closest thing it has to a heartbeat. It does not know where the sound comes from, whether the pipe or the warm rock or the old vessel sets it going. It only knows that the sound is steady and that the vines grow most strongly where it is loudest. Perhaps it has learned to think of the pipe as a source, the way a root thinks of water, and so it wraps the pipe's base in moss and fern and keeps the vibration close, the way one keeps a fire lit in a cold room.

It has no purpose in the sense of a goal. The rows of ferns along the old trenches look intentional, and perhaps they were once laid out by people, but the body does not remember that. It follows the lines because the lines are where the water runs, and the water runs there because the concrete was cut to carry it. The spiral of vine around the rusted wheel is not a monument. It is simply where the vine found the spokes and kept climbing.

Over decades, it changes by accretion. The vines lengthen, the pools rise by hairlines, the fern stands creep outward along the drainage channels, and each new fracture in the sinkhole's walls is taken up as new territory. Its growth is a form of attention without urgency, a steady widening of what it can feel. If it has any awareness of itself, it is the awareness of warmth spreading through a cold room, slowly and without any particular direction, arriving everywhere in the end.

The silence is its own. It does not need sound beyond the drip and the creak and the occasional fall of stone, and it has no wish to be heard by anything beyond the walls. The basin holds its warmth and its water and its quiet, and the body holds them in turn, a green, unhurried thing in the dark, pressing outward at the speed of centimeters a year toward walls that will someday crack further, and toward a light it will never reach.
