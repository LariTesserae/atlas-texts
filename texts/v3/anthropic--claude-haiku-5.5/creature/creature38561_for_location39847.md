**Who Lives on the Pumping Flats**

The first people you notice are the pump-tenders, because they are always walking out to the stations along the raised catwalks, their boots ringing on the grating. They wear coveralls gone dark at the knees and elbows, and their forearms are pale and wrinkled from hours in wet air, the skin slightly softened, as if it has never fully dried since birth. Most of them carry a short steel bar, which they use to tap the housings. They listen to the drone. A tender can tell from the pitch when a bearing is starting to go, and will stop mid-sentence to cock their head at a sound no visitor can hear. Their hearing is the town's real instrument. Many of them have a flat, patient way of talking, pausing between words as if waiting for the next pump to answer.

The foundry crews are different. They come off shift grey-faced, with a fine brown dust in the creases of their necks and around their eyes, and they cough in the mornings before they speak. They trade in heat. Their hands are scarred in small pale dots from spatter, and they have a habit of standing very close to any warm surface, a stove or a lamp housing, without seeming to notice. They are the loudest people in town, as much from the work as from temperament. In the evenings they drink in the one long hall near the rail embankment, and the talk there is mostly about tonnage and shifts, the same numbers repeated with small variations.

Between them are the people who do neither: the repair gangs, who go out along the sluice channels with waders and welding kit and come back with mud to the waist, and the route-walkers, who check the intake pipes sunk into the silt by feeling along them with their arms. The route-walkers are quiet. Several of them have lost the tips of fingers to the cold seep at the intakes, and they hold their hands loosely, carefully, as if the world might catch on them.

There are children. They play on the drained plates at the edges of town, hopping from one crack-edged slab to the next and shrieking when one gives. They know the flats better than any adult does. They can tell a holding plate from a sinking one by the sound it makes underfoot, a dull slap versus a soft suck, and they name the bad ones after people they dislike. Adults tell them not to go past the last fence post, and they go past it anyway, and come back with silt to the knee and bright, unbothered faces.

Among the animals, the frogs are the first thing you hear and the last thing you stop hearing. They are not a single population so much as a weather system, a rising rattle that starts at the channel mouths and spreads across the whole district at dusk. The town has stopped noticing them as anything but the evening's start. Old residents say the frogs have always come on at the same hour, and the crews sometimes check their watches against them, half joking.

The herons stand in the shallows in long grey lines, motionless for hours, their necks folded, their eyes open. They are unhurried around the trucks and the crews, and the crews return the courtesy. Nobody remembers when the rows began, but the same few stretches of channel have always held them, and when a heron dies another steps into its exact place within a day or two, as though the spot had been reserved. Their stillness is the calmest thing on the flats and seems to lend the place a kind of composure.

The gulls work the sludge outlets, shrieking and tearing at anything that drifts out of the discharge pipes. The larger scavenger is less welcome. It is piebald, black and white, scruffy at the throat, and it walks the catwalks with a bold, flat-footed confidence, often pecking at the lunch tins the foundry crews leave on their benches. People tolerate it. Some of the older tenders leave it scraps on purpose, and it has learned to wait by their stations at shift change.

The rats move along the drainage channels in grey, sinuous streams, mostly unseen, and the feral dogs have made a rough arrangement with the town. They sleep under the pilings during the day and work the district at night and at shift changes, when the bins are opened. They have learned the crews' timetables better than some of the crews have. A dog will trot beside a foundry worker for a block, then peel off at the exact corner where the lunch hall door opens.

Under all of these, though, is the water, which is the true inhabitant of the place and the one that holds everything else in its shape. It seeps up through the cracks and sits a hand's depth under the surface, and it carries the bacteria that give the flats their sulphur smell and their warm, tacky mud. It forms the salt crusts on the weeping ridges and the thin sheets that slide down the rock all day. It does not move much, in the way that a large sleeping thing does not move much, but everything else on the flats is arranged around its level, and everyone who lives here learns to read it: the wet line on a piling, the colour of a channel, the faint give of a plate underfoot.

Nobody here is in a hurry, and nobody quite seems to expect anything to change. The tenders tap the same housings. The foundry produces what it produces. The herons stand in their places, the dogs keep their timetable, and the children cross the same plates to the same fence post and come back muddy. At dusk the frogs begin, and the whole district goes on under their rattle, with the drone of the pumps underneath it and the lamps coming on yellow and haloed in the damp, and the water rising a little, and being carried away, and rising again.
