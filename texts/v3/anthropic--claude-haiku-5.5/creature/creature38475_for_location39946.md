# The People of the Cradle

The people here are few, perhaps a few hundred, and they call themselves by the name of the basin, or sometimes just *those who keep the walls*. They are the descendants of a small group who climbed up from the valley generations ago, carrying iron wedges, seed stock, and the mountain animals in woven baskets. The first ones probably came for the springs or the clear grey clay, or because they were fleeing something below. The stories disagree, and the disagreement is itself a custom: each family tells its own arrival, and no one corrects anyone.

## Bodies

They are compact and broad through the chest, shaped by thin air and steep stairs. Their legs are strong and their hands are heavy-knuckled from decades of wedge-driving and adze work. Their skin is pale and fine-grained, often beaded with mist, and it has a faint sheen that the valley people find unsettling. Hair is worn long and tied back, and it holds moisture so well that it often darkens and curls in the damp. Many of them have a slight stoop from the lanes' steep grades, and their gait is careful, placing each foot on the moss as if testing whether the ground will hold.

Their bones are dense. Old people sometimes say their skeletons feel like the walls: heavy, slowly settling, a little out of true. Injuries heal slowly. A broken wrist may take a year to feel right again. Nobody is surprised by this. A tooth that is lost is rarely replaced, but the gums toughen and the remaining teeth grow long and worn in a way that the people accept as the price of chewing hard dried fern and tough mountain meat.

Their eyes adjust quickly to the grey-blue dusk and the violet cast of evening, and they can read the cliffs' faint colors long after the valley folk have gone blind. Their hearing is sharp, tuned to the falls. They talk at a low pitch that carries under the roar, and they can pick out a child's voice from three terraces down while ignoring a dozen conversations nearby.

## Dress and Adornment

Clothing is layered wool and fiber, dyed with bark and dried fern, rubbed with resin to shed the damp. Cloaks are heavy and hooded, and the best ones are waterproofed with a thick smear of pine resin that makes them smell sharp and clean. Shoes are soled with split horsetail stalks bound in rope, which grip the moss and dry quickly.

Ornament is sparse and practical. People wear pale stone beads cut from the cliff chips, and the more important ones carry the white cairn stones on thongs, a sign that they have been allowed near the sweet springs. Tattoos are rare, but some adults have fine blue-green lichen-patterns pressed into their forearms with ash and needle, a habit that the older families say recalls the first rings of colonization on the cliffs.

## Speech and Sound

They speak low and economically. Long speeches are unusual, and silence is comfortable. Because the falls drown out most shouting, they have developed a habit of touch and gesture across distance: a raised palm, a tap on a wall, a pattern knocked on a cistern lid that carries through the stone. Children learn these signals before they learn to read the cliffs.

The evening hum is part of their music. Their songs are sung in long, sustained notes that hover near the frequency of the cliffs, and when the hum is loud the singing grows slower and more urgent, a way of answering the stone. A quiet night is an unsettling thing for them, not because of the scree but because the falls seem to have stopped speaking.

## Work and Time

They do not hurry. A house is a generational project, and a new family member is often told that their work is to lay the course that their grandchild will finish. Stones are split, dressed, and fitted with a patience that outsiders find almost maddening, but the people experience it as a kind of conversation with the rock. They read the fractures the way others read faces.

Time is measured in springs, not in seasons or years. Every spring, when the cloud lifts for a few days and the sun is warm, the families walk their walls with plumb lines and levels, marking any shift with a notch in the stone and a small knot of rope tied to the doorframe. These knots are kept, and the older families have long strings of them hung in their houses, a record of how far the ground has risen and how much they have chased it.

## Customs

The plumb-line walk is the great rite. It is done in silence, and no one is allowed to speak until the last notch is cut. Families who have neglected it are not shamed openly, but their children are quietly given the worst terraces to work on.

Feast days are marked by bathing. Children are carried down to the cairned springs, where the iron-sweet water runs warm even in the cold. They wade in up to the waist and are kept there until their lips and cheeks flush, and then they are wrapped in wool and sat by the fire. Afterward they are quiet for a long time. They describe the taste as sugar and iron, and say that the water hums in the bones, very faintly, like the cliffs at dusk. Adults who bathe in it do not talk about it. Some of them have grown calm in a way that outsiders find eerie, their faces smoothed and their movements slower. Nobody forces it. Nobody refuses it either.

The dogs are their constant companions. The hardy woolly animals move among the terraces silently, and they seem to understand the signals of the people, appearing at the right doorway before they are called. Some families believe the dogs are the first to know when the ground will shift.

## What They Are Becoming

The people are not separate from the basin. The stone that is still deciding what it will be seems to shape them as they shape it. They have grown dense and patient, their bodies settling like the walls. They read the fractures and the falls the way they read each other, slowly and with care. They sing to the hum, and they wait for the cliffs to finish their work.

When the families walk the walls each spring, they are not only checking the stone. They are checking themselves: whether they are still level with the ground beneath them, whether the house they are building is still true to the one before it. Nobody has ever torn anything down completely. The people carry the old courses inside them as well as in their walls, and they are slowly becoming the place they have built.
