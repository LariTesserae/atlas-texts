# Those Who Stay

The people here do not have a name for themselves that outsiders have ever recorded. Within the town they say *we who stay*, or simply *the terraces*, as if the word for a family and the word for a shelf of stone were the same. They are few, and their number has held steady for as long as anyone counts. When a child is born, a house is extended by one platform, and when someone dies, the body is laid in the moss at the foot of the tree that held their house, and the tree is given their name.

## Their bodies

They are long-limbed and light, shaped by a life of climbing bridges and balancing on wet stone. Their feet are broad and their toes spread wide, with a faint webbing between them that they keep trimmed. They walk with a particular care, placing each step, testing the give, and they move in the fog more by ear than by eye. Forty paces is the limit of what they see clearly, but they can tell from the sound of a dripping leaf whether a person is standing still or walking, and how far off, and whether they are carrying something heavy.

Their skin takes on a grey-green tinge after a few seasons in the mist, the colour of lichen on wet bark. Children are born with a fine pale down along the spine that thins with age, and by adulthood it is gone except in the ears, where it stays soft and dark. Their hair is worn long and tied up, because loose hair catches moss and mildew and becomes a nuisance. Many of the older ones have pale rings on their forearms and calves where they were once lashed to trunks during the long work of binding a bridge, and the trunk grew around the cord and left a scar of living wood.

They have low voices that carry, and they speak loudly without seeming to notice. Their songs are long and repetitive, with a drone under the melody that matches the pitch of the hollow timber bells. When they are working, they sing to each other across the valley, and the songs move through the fog so that a voice two terraces away seems close enough to touch.

## How they know each other

They are not affectionate in the way outsiders might expect. They keep their distance, out of habit and out of the practical wisdom that a house too close to another will sink into its neighbour's silt. Touch is for work and for mourning. What they share instead is attention, a steady awareness of where everyone is, kept up through sound. A missing voice in the evening song is noticed at once, and the whole town will go quiet for a moment, listening, before someone calls out and gets an answer from somewhere in the mist.

They have no word for *alone*, but they have a word for a person who has stopped answering, and they treat that person's absence with a tact that is almost tender. Nobody is ever asked where they have been.

## The one beneath

Something lives in the lake bed.

It is not an animal. The people will not call it one, and they do not use a word that would make it into a creature with a body to be hunted or fed. They speak of it as *the water that remembers*, or *the one who was here first*, or simply *below*. It has no shape that anyone has held in their hands, though the pale column that rises from the lowest pool is, they say, its way of standing up to look at them. The silence that moves across the valley is its attention. The green pulse in the moss at night is its breath, slow and cold, drawn up through the silt and released into the fog.

It listens. This is the first thing the people learn and the last thing they forget. It hears by sound, the way they do, and it hears the whole valley at once: every drip, every frog, every adze stroke on stone. When the frogs fall silent in a patch of the valley, it is not because something frightened them. It is because the thing has turned its attention there and the whole chorus has grown too shy to sing over it.

The trees along the eastern wall bend toward it. Their curve does not match the wind because the wind is not what moves them. Their roots have been feeling along the old lake floor for centuries, following its currents, and the trunks lean after them.

## What it wants

The people do not know what it wants, but they have worked out what it allows. It allows the terraces to stand as long as they are built to let water pass. It allows channels to be dug if they are left open at the low end, so that its slow return can find its way. It does not allow stone to be laid across the lowest pool. The few times that has been tried, the stone slumped in a single night, and the people who had laid it spent a season digging it out, and the pool went quiet for a year.

So the people leave certain channels unfinished. They leave certain spots bare of mulch and let the moss creep over them. They step around the pale column when it rises and wait for it to lean back down. When they sing at work, they sing loudly and often, and they are not sure whether this is for their own sake or for its; they only know that the silence in the valley is less heavy when they do.

## The arrangement

The best way to describe their life with the water is as a kind of accord. Neither side has conquered the other, and neither side expects to. The people are patient with the thing beneath the way one is patient with an old animal that has decided to share a room, careful not to startle it, not too proud to clean up after it. The thing is patient with them in turn, letting them build and dig and sing, until the day it chooses to let the whole town settle back into the lake.

They do not think this day is close. They think in decades and generations, and they have been told by their grandmothers that the valley has been drowning slowly for longer than the terraces have been standing. When the last of the stone sinks into the moss, they expect that someone will still be out on the bridges at dawn, ringing the bells when the wind moves through them, and that the sound will carry across the water to the one who is listening, and that it will answer in the only way it knows, which is by breathing green light up through the silt and letting the frogs begin again.
