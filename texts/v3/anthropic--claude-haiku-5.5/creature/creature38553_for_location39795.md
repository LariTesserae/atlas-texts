The thing that lives in the Settling Basin is not any one of its animals. It is the arrangement they make together, a slow, distributed organism that builds its body out of salt and silt and then lives inside that body. The clicking, the wings, the predators waiting in the crust are its parts. You can learn to see the whole only by watching the parts for a long time.

**The builders**

Start at the mounds. Each is a mouth, a chimney, and a tomb all at once. The builders lay down their cement in thin, deliberate courses, drawing salt and silt up from the pan and binding them with the dark fluid that comes out of their own bodies. Each course hardens before the next is laid, so every mound carries the marks of its making in fine horizontal rings, like the growth rings of a stump. Break one open and you find not a nest but an archive: the old caps and columns of earlier generations fused beneath the new, the lattice of galleries threading through all of it, each layer a decision taken and then sealed.

Below the crust, the lattice is one continuous structure. Air moves through it in slow pulses, drawn in at the low vents and pushed out through the caps, so the whole network breathes with the rhythm of the crowd. The builders keep it open. When a gallery silts shut, they dig it clear with their forelimbs and bind the walls again. When the brine rises too high, they redirect it, channel by channel, the way a body shifts blood away from a wound. They do not know they are doing this. Or perhaps knowing is the wrong word for what the lattice does with itself.

**The walkers**

The pale segmented ones move in the gaps between mounds like cells in a tissue. Each is small and unremarkable. Each carries the same armour, the same pincers, the same click. But the clicking is never random. It swells when the feeding columns form and drops when they disperse. When the wind lifts the salt veils, the whole pan of walkers falls silent at once, a silence that passes from mound to mound across the basin in a few seconds, as if a single message had been relayed. Their individual bodies are short-lived. What persists is the rhythm they keep, passed down through the galleries as the young are carried in.

**The fliers**

The ash-coloured fliers are the connective tissue. They ride the afternoon breath of the mounds up to the rim, then fall back down to the pan in dense carpets, carrying dead matter, scraps, and something else, a kind of traffic between the floor and the open air. Their papery wings make the sound of a sheet shaken and shaken again, and when a flock lifts all at once, the basin seems to exhale. They are the part of the organism that reaches beyond its walls. Nothing else here leaves the bowl for long.

**The predators**

The long-backed, salt-crusted ones are the most solitary and the most patient. They lie motionless among the mounds, so crusted with mineral that they read as stone until they move. They hunt by waiting. The basin has learned from them that a body which does not move can still take what passes close. Of everything that lives here, they seem least like the lattice and most like individuals, each one a separate will sitting in a shared field. Yet they depend on the colonies that feed the walkers that feed them, and their stillness is part of the same rhythm, the long pause in the cycle of consumption.

**The tangle**

The strangest part of the organism is its relation to the old works. The builders have taken up the brine circulation the machines once managed. Their channels follow the drainage lines exactly, carrying the same slurry the settling tanks once carried, now carried to the galleries instead. The pump house hums, and its intake has been wrapped in a fringe of cement, so that the machine draws its air and brine through the creatures' bodies. Whether the colonies are using the machine or the machine is using the colonies has stopped being a question with an answer. The gauges tick. The needles tremble. Something beneath clanks and sighs every few minutes, and the basin has begun to speak in that rhythm too.

**What it is**

If the basin has a self, it lives in the rhythms: the pulse of the galleries, the clicking that rises and falls, the pause that passes across the pan when the wind builds, the long rests of the predators, the sudden loosing of the fliers into the sky. It is a mind without a head and a body without an edge, built one course at a time from the mineral and the dead. It remembers in its architecture. It acts through its many small movements. It does not seem to be trying to reach anything.

And yet it is never still. The erosion that shaped the bowl finished its work long ago, but the thing inside has not finished its own. Each generation builds on the last and adjusts the lattice a little, rebinds a gallery, channels the brine differently, takes up another piece of the old machinery and makes it its own. The organism is still forming the shape it will hold. It is dense, patient, and crowded, and it moves in every direction at once, and it is still deciding what it will become.
