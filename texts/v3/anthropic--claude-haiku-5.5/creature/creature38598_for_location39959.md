The first thing that lives in Sennoch Reach is the sound. It is not a creature, but it behaves like one. It breathes in with the wind and out with the rain, it swells when a bank fails, and it dips for a heartbeat before rising again as though something has turned over in its sleep. You stop noticing it after an hour and start noticing it again only when it changes.

The people live inside that sound. They are lean, with the sinewy, low-slung build of those who spend their lives balancing on moving reed. Their hands are the most striking thing about them. The palms are broad and calloused from rope, and the fingers are often bound with cord or stained dark from tannin. In conversation their hands never stop. Through generations of shouting across channels they have built a language of fingers, wrists, and flat-palmed pushes, and it is fast and dense. A woman on a raft will flick two fingers toward the water, close her fist, and tap her collarbone, and a man forty meters off will answer with a sweep of the forearm that means *the bank is going, take the children to the upper line*. Children learn the signs before they learn to swim. They also learn touch: a squeeze on the wrist for *stay close*, a double pat between the shoulder blades for *move now*. In the storm-state, when the sound swallows speech, a whole community can turn as one body.

They dress for wet. Oiled cloth wraps the torso, and leggings of woven reed-fiber are knotted at the calf. Many carry a waterproof pack on the back at all times, a bundle that holds what they cannot afford to lose: a knife, a few dried roots, a bundle of cord, a carved token from someone who did not survive a flood. The packs are rebuilt and refilled constantly. They are the closest thing these people have to a home that moves with them.

Their eyes are narrowed against spray and tuned to low light. They read the world by the shape of water. Still water with a thin skin of film means a channel is silting shut. A dimpled surface far out means something large is rising. The sound of a stream changing its voice from gurgle to hiss means the gradient is steepening, and in an hour the path you walked this morning will be a torrent. They do not say they predict floods. They say the water is *getting ideas*, and they go where the ideas are not.

Around them moves the wading population. The tall grey birds with long reaching legs stand in the shallows like posts that have decided to watch. They are patient to the point of stillness, and then in a single movement they strike, and the reed bed trembles. Their calls are rare and piercing, nothing like the tearing-cloth cry of the great flocks. The flocks themselves are the weather's own animals. A flock of several thousand pours over the flats, pulls into a single dark sheet, folds along an invisible seam, and tilts its whole mass toward the hum, as if listening. Where the sound is rising the flocks gather. Where the sound is falling they scatter. The people have long since stopped being surprised that the birds seem to know the land is about to change. They only watch which way the birds go.

The broad-winged hunters hang on the updrafts above the channels, circling so slowly that they seem fixed to the grey lid of the sky. They drop without warning. What they take is mostly the small pelted runners, the quick low shapes that flicker through the undergrowth with their thick coats slicked dark by rain. These runners are the loudest small things here, not in voice but in motion, a constant rustle of passing fur. They seem to have no fear of the people, only of the hunters and of the ground that suddenly gives way. Their eyes shine in the dusk, and the children chase them for sport, though the runners are always faster.

Beneath the surface of the deep channels lives the slow thing. No one has seen all of it. It shows itself as long smooth swells that lift the water in a wide, unbroken curve, and as an occasional ridge of dark back that breaks the surface and sinks again without a splash. It does not come close to the stilt-towns. When a raft drifts over a deep channel, the children are pulled in tight and the adults go quiet, not in fear exactly, but in the way one goes quiet near a sleeping animal much larger than oneself. Some of the older people say it follows the tuning, that it moves toward the new channels before they open, and that the lakes it leaves behind are where the fish die. They say this as a fact, and they do not say it with any particular resentment.

The frogs fill the flooded margins with a chorus that rises and falls with the rain. It is the closest thing in the Reach to music, and it has a pattern that the people have learned to read: when the chorus stops entirely, something is about to happen, and everyone climbs. When it swells past its usual height, the water is high and the ground will soon be soft. The frogs are small and the color of wet clay, and they are rarely seen, only heard. A person who stands in the margins at dusk can hear the whole flat breathe with them.

The moss, the reeds, and the waxy shrubs are a kind of population too, one that renews itself faster than anything else. The sphagnum drinks the water and holds it, which is why the paths sink under the feet and why the people walk on poles and rope. The reeds rattle in a wind that never really stops. The shrubs shed water in bright beads, so that every leaf sparkles faintly under the lid of cloud. The buttressed trees on the higher ground are old by the standards of the Reach, though no tree here is truly old, since every century or so the land picks up and moves. The beards of moss on their branches drip without pause, and the lichen on their bark is pale as bone.

Then there are the towers and the panels, which are not alive in the way the birds and frogs are alive, though the people do not always draw a clean line between them. The panels slide a centimeter at a time, following a current that the engineers have mapped only partially. They are warm where the hum is strongest. Some people lay their palms on them in the mornings, the way a farmer might touch a sleeping animal, and say they can feel the pitch in their wrists.

The spire in the lagoon still hums, and its hum is the deepest voice in the Reach. The people can feel it in their chests at night. It does not care whether they are there. It sings to the silt, the silt rises and falls, the channels open and close, and the people rebuild their rafts and move their children and tap their collarbones and watch the birds.

Nothing here has had time to become old, and so nothing here is quite finished. The people are the most unfinished of all. They have learned to live as the land does, always partly in motion, carrying their homes on their backs and speaking mostly with their hands. They are good at leaving. They are good at arriving. And when the rain eases and the sound shifts, they lift their heads like the birds do, listening for the pitch to change, waiting to find out which way the Reach will bend next.
