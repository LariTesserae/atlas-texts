**Who Keeps the Shelf**

You do not see them at first. You see a flicker at the edge of a pool, a point of light that moves when nothing else does, and you look toward it and find only crust and water. Then one of the pools seems to have a second surface, a reflection standing a little too straight, and you realise the reflection is not of the sky or the wall. It belongs to something standing in the water.

They are low and upright, about the height of a seated person, and they stand very still for long periods. Their bodies look like the rock that surrounds them: pale grey at the core, smoky and translucent at the edges, with bands that run in the same curves as the grit ripples. Trapped bubbles show in their limbs like seeds. Where the skin has thickened, it is glassy and conchoidal, and it breaks along the same clean curves as the stones. When the light is right, they throw sparks off their shoulders and crowns, which is how you find them. Their eyes are flat and dark and polished, like the old glass flakes, and they hold reflections more faithfully than the water does.

They do not move much, and when they do, the movement is slow and deliberate, like the leaning of the crystal clusters. Each one seems to wait for a particular angle of sun before it will shift its weight. At dawn they stand in the shallows with their arms held a little away from the body, and the rime forms on them in feathery lattices exactly as it forms on the rock faces. This is how they feed. The frost draws salts out of the water and into the surface of the body, and as the rime melts from them, the salts stay behind in a thin crust. The crust is then taken into the body again in the next cold, and some of it, they leave on the ledges. This is part of why the knobs grow. The people of the shelf do not separate eating from weather. Their bodies and the frost trade the same material back and forth, and nobody could say where one stops.

Their work is maintenance. They reset the cairns, lifting fallen stones and fitting them to the courses below, testing each one by striking it with a second stone until it gives back a clean, true note. Some of the fitting is done by pressure alone: they lean on a stone with the whole weight of the body until it settles. They chip new flakes from the broken glass, and they chip them slowly, with the care of people who have made the same tool a thousand times and expect to make it a thousand more. Their knapping is the only sound on the shelf that is not water, wind, or cooling crystal. It comes in short, measured sequences, two or three strikes, then a pause, then another. Sometimes it is answered from across the shelf, a fraction later, the way one drip answers another from a different ledge.

That answering is how they seem to speak. They do not use breath, or not much. They strike stone against stone and listen, and the notes travel through the rock more clearly than through the thin air. A cairn that rings true tells the others that its course has been reset. A cracked note means a stone has shifted and needs attention. Over a season, a whole hollow may be rung and re-rung until the sound of it settles into a pattern, and the pattern is what you hear when you walk into the shelf and think it is silent.

They do not seem to sleep as we do. At night, when the sun leaves the crystal, they stand in the pools and cool with it, and as they cool, they tick. The sound is so close to the sound of the prisms that it takes a long time to tell them apart. In the deepest cold, the ones in the larger pools go still under the ice, and their reflections fade. In the morning the ice cracks, and the reflections return, a little later than the sun, as if they were coming up after it.

The living ones are few. You will count eight or nine on a good day, though you may count more, and some of the figures you count may be the same one standing in two pools. They do not seem to mind being counted. They do not seem to mind much at all, except that the cairns be kept and the salts be taken and the knocking from the alcove be answered.

That knocking is the thing they attend to most. It comes from the far western end, under the white crust, slow and regular. The living ones go to the alcove at dusk, when the air there is warm enough to hold them, and they stand at its edge with their arms lowered and their heads tilted toward the floor. Some of them carry salt from the pools in cupped hands and set it on the plates, and the crust there cracks a little where they set it, and the knocking, for a time, quickens. Then it slows again.

No one has gone under the crust. No one has seen what is making the sound, or whether the knocking is a heartbeat, or a tool being struck, or the slow falling of stones in a very deep fracture. The living ones do not go past the edge of the crust. When the knocking changes its rhythm, they strike their own stones, quickly, twice, and the note travels back across the shelf, and the cairns ring, and the others come to the water's edge one by one, standing in the light, reflecting.

Some of the old cairns, the ones that have fallen furthest, have no living ones near them, and their stones are settling into the grit, lowering themselves a fraction of a finger each season toward the rock beneath. The living ones do not reset these. They leave them to the frost, and the frost takes them, and what is left of the pattern is overwritten by the next. It is possible that the cairns are not for the living ones at all, but for the sound, so that somewhere in the deep crust something can hear, across the whole shelf, that it has not been forgotten.

Then the fog comes up the eastern side, and the hiss crosses the rock, and every living reflection goes soft at once. For a moment there is nothing on the shelf but water, dripping from each ledge in its own time, and the sound of each drip seems separate and deliberate, and you cannot tell whether you are alone. Then the fog lifts, the sun finds the crystal, and somewhere in the shallows a figure turns its face toward the light and begins, slowly, to shine.
