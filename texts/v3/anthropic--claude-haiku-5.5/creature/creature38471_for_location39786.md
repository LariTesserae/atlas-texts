# Who Stays in the Sump Town

Not many. The town is too large for its people, and most of it is given over to the water. Those who remain live high, in the upper galleries of the mill halls, in the attic rooms of the worker housing where the roofs still hold, and on the flat leads of the foundry stacks where the wind, rare as it is, reaches them first. They call themselves **the Tenders**, though no one remembers who first used the word or what it was meant to cover. It is a word for people who keep something running that no longer has an owner.

They are thin, and their skin has gone the color of the old mortar, with pale mineral bands across the forearms and throat where the brine has crusted into them over years of climbing. Their eyes are large and slow to adjust, and they seldom look directly at a light. They move with great economy, because every ladder, gutter, and rope in the town is slick with silt and every mistake ends in the flood. They do not swim well. Most have never learned, and those who did have learned that the deep layers take a swimmer's warmth and hold it, then take the swimmer.

**Their speech** is mostly tapping. A Tender lays a knuckle or a wrench against an iron pipe and the sound runs along the metal for hundreds of meters, clear where the air would have swallowed it. The grammar is rhythmic and patient. A double knock with a pause means *are you there*. A slow falling series means *the lower brine is turning* and carries the direction of the gas front. Three quick taps followed by a long silence is the sign for a name, and the name is always followed by a pitch, high or low, that tells the listener whether the person is living or has gone into the sediment. The Tenders talk across the whole basin this way, and a night in the high galleries is full of a sparse, unhurried percussion that seems to come from the iron itself.

Writing is rare. Paper dissolves, and ink runs within a season. What they do write, they cut into iron: the names of pumps, the depth marks on stanchions, the dates of the last turnover. A name cut into a girder is the only permanence the town offers, and the Tenders treat those marks with a seriousness that outsiders find strange until they see how quickly everything else goes.

**Their work** is the pumps. Somewhere under the lowest districts, in a chamber no Tender has seen, the machinery knocks and sucks and lifts, and the Tenders keep it alive as far as they can. They clear the intake grates of drowned rope and the bodies of crustaceans that colonize the screens. They listen to the rhythm of the knocking and know when a bearing is failing a month before it fails. They read the water by touch and smell, dipping a hand into the upper skin to feel its warmth and tasting the brine for iron or sweetness, so that they know which layer is rising before the bubbles come. Their knowledge is practical and specific and almost entirely unwritten. It lives in the hands of the older ones and passes down the way the town's ladders pass from one roof to the next.

They do not fully understand the pumps. Their grandparents' grandparents knew the names of the men who built them and the purpose the town had when the water first began to rise. Now there is only the work, and a belief, held more as a habit of the hands than a doctrine, that if the pumps stop, the water will take the town faster than it has ever taken anything. Some Tenders suspect the pumps are not really lifting water for the town's sake. The lowest intake is warmer than it should be, and sometimes, when a Tender lays an ear against the casing, the knocking has a pattern that repeats in answer to their taps.

**The dead** go into the water. There is no other way. A body laid on a leaded roof is gone within a season, and the Tenders do not fight it. They fold the dead into a shroud of waxed cloth weighted with an iron ring, and they lower the bundle through the stairwell into the upper skin. The water takes it down through the layers, and over the years it is absorbed into the silt beneath the streets. The Tenders believe the dead become part of the floor the living walk on, and so the town gets lower and the living climb higher, and no one is buried anywhere they can be visited. Their names are cut into iron and the iron is left in place until it too gives way.

**Their children** learn the taps before they learn to speak. A child is taught to knock along a pipe from the time her hands can hold a wrench, and she is taught to climb before she can read the depth marks. They are usually born in the high galleries and rarely go down. The lower districts belong to the water, and the Tenders' children grow up with a healthy fear of the stairwells and the warm, heavy layers below them, and a quiet, unspoken fascination with the bioluminescence that rises up the old lamp posts on the nights it blooms.

**What they are**, in the end, is hard to pin down. They are not the town's rulers, nor its ghosts. They are closer to its nerves: the thin, attentive part of a body that is still trying to hold itself up. When a boat drifts through a dark channel and leaves its glowing wake, a Tender somewhere on a stack roof will see it and tap twice on the nearest pipe. The sound runs along the iron through the whole flooded district, and somewhere far off another Tender answers, and then another, until the message has passed from roof to roof around the basin and back again.

On nights when the bioluminescence is strong and the whole town glows faintly from below, the Tenders sit in the open galleries and listen to the knocking under the water. They do not say much. Sometimes one of them lays a hand on the iron and holds it there, feeling for the rhythm, and waits to see whether the rhythm will change.
