**Who Lives on the Sponge Plateau**

The first thing to live here is the water, and the second is the ground that holds it. Everything else arrived later and is still arriving. If you want to know who inhabits the plateau, you have to start with the mat, because the mat is the largest creature on the high country, and it is the only one that has never once stopped moving.

It does not have a head. It has pressure. Where a hummock swells, something has been decided. Where a seep opens, a thought has been finished and set down in the stone. The mat grows toward warmth and away from weight, it weaves its fibrous strands into the shape of a passage it has already used, and it remembers, in the slow way of things that measure time in seasons, which hollows drained and which ridges held. Its pale hair-roots run for kilometres beneath the surface and touch each other in the dark, and where they meet they exchange water, sugar, and something older that has no name in any language the people here speak. When a dome splits and slides into a valley, that is the mat changing its mind. When a pool migrates a kilometre, that is the mat walking.

Under the mat, in the cold layers near the stone, live the burrowers. Nobody has seen them whole. They are heard in the thumping and scraping that rolls up through the ground at dusk, and felt as a faint tremor through the soles of boots. Some of the people believe they are the source of the sudden hollows that open without warning. Others believe the burrowers are the mat's own nerves, the slow pulses that carry word from one end of the plateau to the other. The burrowers do not seem to mind which story is told, and they have not come up to correct anyone.

Above them, in the damp channels that the mat leaves open between its swells, run the brown many-legged things, the runners. They are quick and low and never stop moving, and where they pass the mat is clean and the channels stay open. Their bodies are soft and their legs are a blur. They eat the fallen spores, the dead fronds, and the small pale grubs that ride the seeps. If the runners were to disappear, the channels would close within a season and the water would have nowhere to go, and the whole country would drown quietly in its own patience. The people know this and leave them alone, and they say the runners are the reason the streams still run where they should.

The grazers are the heavy ones. They move through the hollows in long, unhurried lines, shouldering the big fronds aside with their broad, mossy backs. They are the color of the mat itself, and at a distance they are hard to tell from the ground except that the ground does not follow you with its eyes. Behind each grazer the frond beds it has crushed fill slowly with water, so that its trail becomes a small stream within a day, and a grazer that has walked the same loop for a few seasons has cut a river into the country. They do not hurry. The grazers are, in a sense, the plateau's plumbers and surveyors, and the people say that when a grazer stops using a trail, the trail fills, and a new stream is pushed out elsewhere. The place is always being redrawn, and the grazers hold the pen.

Above the seeps, the winged things dip and circle. They are not birds, and they are not quite insects. Their wings are thin and translucent and shiver with a frequency that makes the air above the pools ripple. They hunt the drifting spore-storms, catching the bright green particles in the hairs along their bellies, and when they tire they come down to rest on the mat. Here is the strangest thing about them: they sink. Each one lands on the mat and settles, sometimes by half its body, and the mat takes a little of its weight and holds it. Over the course of a day the wings fold and the body softens, and by evening there is no sign of the creature on the surface. Something has been received. Where the spores in their bellies fall, new growth appears, and it is never quite the growth that was there before. The people do not know whether the winged things are seeders or offerings. They suspect that the distinction does not matter here.

The grey long-necked birds live in the hollows and call in unison at dusk. Their voices carry across the plateau like a single chord struck by a hundred throats, and when they call, the water in the nearest pools seems to tremble in time. They stand very still on the mat between calls, heads raised, and some people say they are waiting to be answered. Nobody has answered yet, except the turbines, whose low hum rises a half-beat under the birds' voices on windy nights, as if in reply. The birds seem content with this. They hold their necks at the same angle as the turbine blades when the wind turns them, and they never move until the blades have stopped.

The predators are rarely seen. What is seen is the sudden silence in the grazers' paths, the runners that vanish mid-channel, a scatter of winged things lifting off the seep all at once. Something lives on the grazers, something lives in the dark under the swells, and the people say you know it is near when the sound of the water changes. They do not hunt it. They have learned to walk on the high ridges when the cloud is thick and to keep their fires small and low.

And then there are the people, who are the most recent arrivals and the least certain of their place. They live in the lee of the old trunks, in houses built from fallen branches that the mat has already begun to cover, so that every dwelling seems to rise out of the ground as if it had always been there. Their skin is pale and damp, their hair fine and pale, and in the young ones, at the very tips, there is a faint lime-green glow, which the elders say fades with age. They walk barefoot on the mat, because boots sink and trap water, and they have learned to step lightly, to test the ground before they shift their weight, to feel for the moment when a swell is deciding to move. They drink from the seeps without boiling, because the water comes up warm from deep in the stone and tastes of iron and something sweeter underneath.

They keep the turbines. Each morning a few of the people climb the ridge and sit by the iron, laying their palms against the cold pistons and listening for the faint hiss of valves responding to the pressure in the mat. They do not repair the machines, because the machines do not need repairing. They keep them company, and in return the machines lift and settle in a rhythm that the people have learned to read, the way one learns the moods of a neighbour. When a lattice tower creaks, they say the mat is changing shape beneath it. When a stack's vines shiver in still air, they say a burrower has moved. When the north glow brightens in the slow rhythm, they stop what they are doing and look up, and they do not speak until it fades.

The people do not think of themselves as owners of the plateau. They think of themselves as guests who have stayed a long time, and they are honest about how little they understand of their hosts. Some of them go up to the western scarp to sit with the old cliffs, which are the only stone left that still holds an edge. The elders say the cliffs remember the mountains, and that the mat has been trying for a long time to soften them too. Nobody is sure what will happen when the last sharp thing has become a shoulder. The mat seems in no hurry to find out.

So the plateau is inhabited by water, and by the ground that gathers it, and by the small, patient things that tunnel and run and graze and sink, and by the grey birds calling in their chord, and by the iron that has learned to listen. The people are among them, living lightly, learning the ground's moods, feeling for the moment when the swell is about to move. Everything here is becoming something else, and everything here is part of the becoming. If you walk the plateau long enough, you will find that you are not walking across it so much as being carried, slowly and without apology, toward whatever it has decided to be next.
