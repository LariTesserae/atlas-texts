# The Lean Walkers

They come up the gorge walls at dusk, when the lichen starts to glow and the herds have bunched for the night. You learn them first by their pace. A pair moves so evenly that the drip-clicks of the tors seem to skip around them, as though the rock itself declines to notice their weight. Then you see the shape. They are long and low, bodies held a hand's width off the stone, with the spine running flat and taut from skull to tail. Their hide is pale grey where the mist has bleached it and mottled in dark ochre along the flanks, the same rust and violet as the lichen crusts. On a ledge at dusk, one of them can vanish entirely.

Their feet are the key to everything. Each foot is wide and webbed between long digits, and the undersurfaces are covered in a fine, dense papilla, soft and gripping, like the wet pads of a tree frog scaled up to the size of a hand. The basalt is always slick with condensation, but they climb it in silence. Their pads sample the film on the stone as they step: each contact tastes the water, its temperature, its speed, the faint mineral sweetness of the ochre trails. A walker can tell from one step whether the water beneath it is running up or down.

That is the thing the herds fear most, and the thing the walkers have built their lives around. They are readers of the stone's rule. Along the spine, under a fold of skin that rises when the air is wet, there is a row of fine sensory ridges, and these pick up the faint pressure of the eddies that turn the water upward. When a walker lifts its head and the ridges spread, it is reading the air. When it lowers its body and presses its jaw to a basalt face, it is reading the grooves. The two pieces of information, wind and water, tell it where the next runoff will pour and where a grazer will be forced to walk.

They hunt in pairs, and the pairing is not a matter of strength but of role. One is the driver. It works the high shelves and the seep lines, moving slowly through the mist, letting the herd smell it and turn. It is patient in the way that rock is patient. It walks until the grazers bunch and then walks again, so that the column bends toward the gorge edge where the footing is best. The other is the waiter. It takes the far side of the crossing, a ledge above the narrowest point where the seep lines converge, and it lies flat against the wet stone with its eyes half-closed. Its eyes are wide and dark and reflect the fungal light in two dim green coins. When the herd comes, it does not chase. It waits for the moment when the water begins to reverse, when the stone's grooves pull the flood-channel back up toward the herd's flank, and then it moves.

Their communication is almost entirely through the rock. A walker presses its chest to a ledge and produces a low, sustained vibration, too deep to hear clearly in the air but readable through the stone. A partner across a gorge can feel it through its own feet. The sound travels a long way on this plateau. On still nights, a pair can coordinate across two ridges without either of them making a noise that a grazer would notice. It is possible that the throb from the southeast, the one no one here has traced, is something they feel most keenly. It arrives through the rock as a steady pressure in their pads, and when it swells they lift their heads toward the wind and hold still, as if listening to a far-off thing that has been breathing on the stone for longer than their line has existed.

They do not bury their dead, and they do not waste what they kill. A carcass in a narrow crossing draws the flyers down in an hour, and the walkers eat the best of the flesh before the wings can reach it. The rest is left to the fungal shelves, which bloom fast on the wet remains. There is a rhythm to this too. After the great storms, the pairs follow the flood-channels down to the narrows, where the water has pinned the herds in pools, and the walkers feed until their bellies drag on the rock. Then they climb back up through the mist, leaving wet tracks that the stone holds for hours before the water erases them. The tracks are oriented by the grooves, and a walker following the trail of another can tell from the direction of the lines how long ago it passed and whether it was alone.

Other creatures live alongside them, and some of those live in the same tension. The grazers, slow and armored as sheared boulders, have learned to walk the seep lines at the edge of the gorges precisely because the walkers cannot approach from below and cannot leap across the flood-channels. The burrowers seal their tunnels at dusk and breathe through hidden vents, feeling the pressure of the weather as the walkers feel the water. The flyers ride the updrafts over the tors, watching the carrion and the ground. Everything here is shaped by the same wet stone, and each creature is a partial answer to the question of where the water will go.

The walkers are the only ones who seem to know the answer before it happens. When the air shifts and the first pulse of rain begins to build over the high center, you can see them move. They come down from the shelves in the last amber light, following lines that are no longer quite visible, and they settle onto their ledges above the crossings. Their eyes narrow. Their chests press to the stone. In the grey dusk, two of them lie still on either side of a narrow gorge, reading the water as it begins to run backward, and waiting for the herd to walk into the place where the rule of the stone will close around it.
