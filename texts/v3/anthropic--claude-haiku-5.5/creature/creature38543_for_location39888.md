# The Ones Who Keep the Seams

They are the only thing here that moves on purpose, and they are everywhere. The first ones you notice are the ones that touch you. A cluster of them loosens from the dark skin of a seam and climbs your boot, quick and light, and you feel six or eight pairs of fine legs test the fabric of your trouser cuff. They do not bite. They pass over you the way water passes over a stone, and when you look down the seam beside your foot is already darker, crowded with their bodies, and the sheen on the edge has brightened.

## Body

Each one is about the length of a finger joint, though some are longer and flatter, the shape of a fingernail or a leaf. The body is plated, and the plates overlap in tight rows along the back, the same way the ground's composite plates interlock. You begin to suspect that the creatures and the ground were shaped by the same logic. Their shells are a dull grey-black, flecked with metallic points that catch the light in the same hard bands you see on the cracked plates. Where the sun hits the back of a moving group, the whole swarm flashes faintly, a rolling wave of small glints, then goes dark again.

The legs are long, jointed, and tipped with flattened, serrated pads. Those pads are the important part. They are shaped to grip an edge, and they fit the seams exactly. When a creature walks along a fresh seam, its pads drop into the groove and slide without slipping, the way a key turns in a lock. The clicking comes from this. Each joint ratchets against its neighbor as the legs lock and release, and a thousand locking legs make the constant soft percussion that fills the basin. The sound is not made for communication, or it does not appear to be. It is the sound of a body in continuous contact with the seams it moves along.

Some have a fine proboscis folded beneath the head. They press it against vertical surfaces and drink the condensation, and you can see the thin filament pulse as the water passes up it. A few stand on the stacks with their bodies pointed down and their proboscises extended, unmoving, for minutes at a time, like cups set out to catch the drip.

## Routes

They do not wander. Each group follows a route, and the route is almost always a loop: along a seam, up a gantry strut, across the underside of a conveyor, down a drain, then back to the seam where it began. The loops are fixed. If you mark a point on a stack and watch it, a group will pass the same mark every time it circles, usually within a hand's width. Some routes are long, going around an entire extrusion tower and returning several hours later. Others are tight, circling a single pump housing so often that the plates around its base are worn to a shine.

Their routes cross, but the groups do not collide. When two streams meet at an intersection, they divide without hesitation and rejoin on the far side, with no visible sign of recognition or hesitation. You begin to think they are following something beneath the surface, a pattern in the grooves or a signal in the composite itself. You cannot prove it. What you can see is that they never break their paths. If you pick up one creature and set it on the ground, it stands still for a moment, then turns, searches the seam, finds the groove that matches its legs, and resumes its route as though it had never been lifted.

## Tending

The sheen on the fresh seams is theirs. The iridescent films that drift over the water in the low places are not just water chemistry. Where the creatures press their flattened bodies along an edge and drag their pads slowly across the groove, the composite beneath them stays bright. The films seem to be what they leave behind. A swarm that passes over a seam for an hour leaves it glossy and crisp. Seams that they have neglected, or that lie on routes they have abandoned, look duller, though only slightly. The composite holds its form longer than it should, but not forever.

The oldest sections, the black glassy bowls and ridges with their fine parallel scratches, may be where the tending started. Those scratches look like the marks of many legs over a very long time, each one a pass along the same groove, each pass adding a little clean fresh surface on top of the last. The ringing bowls are the places where this has gone on the longest. The creatures trace those rings more often than any others.

Something has to replace what wears away, and the creatures do not seem to be the builders. The extrusion towers lay the new plates. The creatures keep the old ones sharp. The two work together, and neither changes.

## What happens to them

You rarely see the dead. Sometimes you find a shed skin, a thin, empty shell with the plate-rows still intact, caught in the throat of a drain or wedged beneath a gantry strut. If you pick one up it is light and brittle and dries in your hand. Within a day the skin is gone, and you cannot find where it went. Once, at the foot of a pump stack, you saw a group of creatures gathered around an empty shell, their legs pressed against it, moving slowly over its surface. When they moved on, the shell was gone, and the seam beneath it looked freshly cut.

You never see the young. There may be drains or channels where they live, out of sight, feeding on the condensation and growing into their plates. Or there may be no young at all, and the swarms are the same creatures, changed only by the grooves they travel and the slow wearing of their shells. You cannot tell which. The routes do not change. The count does not change. The sheen does not change. Whatever the creatures are to each other, the pattern they keep is the same one the machines keep, and the ground keeps.

## The last thing

Stand still long enough and a single creature will climb onto your boot and rest there. It will not stay long. It will settle its pads into the seam between your shoe and the plate beneath it, and for a moment the clicking stops. Then it moves on, down the edge of your sole, onto the seam, along the groove, toward wherever its route goes next. Behind it, the seam under your foot is brighter than before.
