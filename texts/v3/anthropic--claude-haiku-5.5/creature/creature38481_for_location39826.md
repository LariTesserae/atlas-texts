# The People of the Terraces

You see them first as a stillness on a ledge, a figure sitting with its back to a wall of stacked basalt, so motionless that the calcite dust on its shoulders looks like part of the stone. Then it lifts a hand, and the hand is slow, and you understand that the stillness was not idleness. It was a kind of attention, held for a long time.

They are people, or close enough that the difference is a matter of argument. They stand about as tall as you would expect, but they are heavier through the bone, with a density that makes them settle into the ground when they sit. Their skin is a grey-brown that takes on a faint pale bloom in the mist, as though they had been lightly dusted with the same mineral that builds the terraces. Scrape a thumbnail across a forearm and a fine white powder comes away.

## Bodies Shaped by the Mist

Their eyes are wide and dark, with pupils that stay open in the pewter light and never narrow. They have no use for squinting, and they look at the world with a steady, unhurried gaze that strangers find disconcerting. They do not seem to notice the absence of a sun. Dawn and dusk are for them the same undifferentiated hour, and they mark time instead by the color of the mist, silver, amber, grey, and by the rise and fall of the swells, which they can count from memory.

Their hair grows long and dark and beads with moisture, so they keep it bound up in coils of reed fiber, or shaved close at the temples in the old style. Their hands are heavy and broad, calloused across the palms and ridged with fine pale lines from years of knapping basalt. Many of the older ones have a faint mottling along their shins and the tops of their feet, where the sea film has settled and thickened over decades into a lacework of pale crust, like the lime-stained roots of the plants. The people do not hide this. They regard it the way a carpenter regards grain.

Children are small for a long time. Their growth is slow, and the transition from child to adult is not marked by a sudden change but by a ritual. At a certain age, usually after many seasons of watching others, a young person walks down the terraces to the shallows and stands in the sea up to the knees, for as long as they can bear, letting the mineral water work into their skin. The film that forms is the first lime-wear. Adults are those who have stood there. Those who do not survive the cold, or the bitterness, are carried up and buried above the waterline, and no one speaks of it.

## Voice

Their speech is low and measured, and it has a rhythm that is hard for outsiders to follow. They leave long gaps between phrases, and at first it seems they are searching for words. They are not. They are listening. The mist returns sound, sometimes quickly and sometimes after days, so a person who speaks too fast or too loudly risks hearing their own words come back to them at an inconvenient moment, in a voice that is not quite their own.

Because of this, they speak in short, deliberate sentences, and they lower their voices in the hollows near the basins. Conversation is intimate by necessity. People lean close, murmur, and wait. Public speech is rare and formal, delivered from high ledges in a voice pitched to carry, and listeners answer only after a long silence, so the reply is never quite simultaneous with the statement.

They have a habit of finishing one another's thoughts with a pause rather than a word, a small nod that means *I have heard you, go on*. Visitors who fill these pauses are considered rude, though no one will say so directly. The people simply wait until the speaker has continued, or until the speaker has decided not to.

Their names are often drawn from the terraces or the basins: the one that sings low in the evenings, the shelf where the ferns grow thick, the lip that cracked in a year of heavy swells. A name is spoken aloud only in certain places. In the open, people use a shorter, softer version, and the full name is kept for the hollows, where it will come back to the speaker in its proper form.

## Time and Generations

The people think of time as a stack. Each generation builds a course on the walls of the world, and each course sits on the ones beneath it. A person born on a high ledge will live their whole life among the same houses, watching the same lip of travertine grow a finger's width every few years, and they will understand that the growth will continue long after they are gone. Nobody expects to see a terrace finished. They expect to add to it, and to be added to.

This shapes their sense of self. A person is not a single life but a layer in a continuous construction. They speak of their grandparents as "the lower course" and their grandchildren as "the course above," and they say this without sadness. The idea of an individual who matters more than the wall is strange to them, though they respect the individual's hands and the individual's care.

They keep long memories, but the memories are arranged by place rather than by date. You remember a thing by where you were standing when it happened, which terrace, which basin, which bend in a causeway. A grandmother's death is recalled as the year the eastern shelf split, and the splitting is recalled by its position relative to the pale root-cast near the reed beds. Their history is a map of the stone, and to walk the terraces is to read it.

## Work as Prayer

Their craft is slow, careful, and repetitive, and they value the repetition for its own sake. A knapper spends a morning striking flakes from a basalt core, each blow measured, each flake set aside in a particular order. A weaver turns a reed rope in long, unbroken rhythms, and the rope grows so slowly that a single coil might take a day. A potter forms a gourd or a clay vessel by coiling and smoothing, coiling and smoothing, and the vessel's walls are thin and even because the potter has spent years learning patience.

Watching them work, you see that the motions are not really about the object. The hands move in cycles that match the breathing of the sea, and the sound of the work, scraping, tapping, the soft knock of stone on stone, settles into a pattern that seems to hold the workers still while their hands travel. A person who works well is said to have "gone down into the stone," meaning their attention has sunk so far into the task that the world outside it falls quiet.

They make few things, but each thing is made completely. A house has been built and rebuilt over generations by the same family, and the walls show every hand that has touched them. A basket holds its shape for years. A basin's rim is smoothed until it feels like skin. Nothing is hurried, and nothing is thrown away carelessly, because each piece is a record of the time it took.

## The Dead Beneath

The people bury their dead in the shallows, below the waterline and above the deep trenches, in shallow pits lined with reed mats and weighted with basalt. Over years the bodies calcify into the shelves, and the shelves grow over them, so that the terraces are, quite literally, built from their ancestors. The living walk on the dead. They build their houses on the bones of the people who built the houses before.

They do not find this grim. It is the natural conclusion of the world's logic: water lingers, stone grows, and what is left in the sea becomes part of the land. When someone dies, the family sings them down the terraces to the shallows, and the chant follows the rhythm of the swells, so that the body is lowered on a rising tide and settled on a falling one. Afterward, the family visits the spot and tends the growth, scraping away fouling, keeping the calcite smooth, watching the shelf thicken above the grave. Within a generation the grave is part of the floor, and the family's children walk over it without knowing whose hand is beneath their feet.

This gives the people a deep, quiet relationship with their dead. The ancestors are not gone. They are under the stone, slowly becoming the terrace, holding up the houses and the causeways. A person who wishes to honor a parent does not go to a grave marker. They go to the shelf and place a hand on the lime, and they wait, and the mist carries the sound of their breathing back to them after a while, and they take that as an answer.

## Keepers of the Shelves

Some among the people have a particular role. They are called by a word that roughly means *readers*, and their job is to watch the sea's growth. They know the shelves by their color and sheen, by how they sound when struck, by the way the water pools on them after rain. They can tell which crust will hold a causeway and which is thinning toward a crack. They keep records not in writing, of which there is none, but in the arrangement of stones on the terraces, in notches cut into the rock, in songs whose verses correspond to particular shelves.

The readers are the most patient of the people, and they are also the most cautious. They enforce the taboo against disturbing the sea. Dredging, drilling, and cutting the travertine are forbidden, and the readers guard this rule with a seriousness that the rest of the community sometimes finds excessive. They have a saying that the sea is still deciding, and that to touch it is to risk choosing the wrong thing. When asked what the wrong thing would be, they are silent for a long time, and the mist seems to thicken around them.

Some readers, it is said, have stood at the edge of the deep trenches and listened. They report a sound beneath the hum of the swells, a faint, regular tapping, like a patient hand knocking on the underside of the world. They do not claim to know what it means. They say only that it is not the sound of stone growing, and that it has been there longer than the terraces.

## Ceremony

Their ceremonies are long and low. The chants are sung in cycles that last as long as a single swell, rising through the night and falling toward dawn, and the people sing them in call-and-response across terraces, so that a single ritual can stretch across an entire settlement. The voices do not blend. Each singer holds a line until the mist returns it, and the returned line becomes part of the next singer's response, so the chant is partly sung by the air itself.

Major rites mark the seasons of growth. When the eastern shelf has thickened enough to bear a new causeway, the people gather at its edge and sing for an afternoon, and then they walk onto it together, one at a time, testing each step. When a child stands in the shallows for the first time, the whole community sings them in. When a reader dies, the terraces are silent for three days, and then the readers of the next generation are named by a chant that uses the old reader's songs, so that the knowledge passes down through the stone's own rhythm.

Hunting, when it happens, is preceded by a ceremony of apology. The people speak to the animal and explain why they are taking it, and they wait for the mist to return their words. If the words come back clearly, the hunt goes ahead. If the words come back distorted, the hunt is postponed. Nobody has ever been able to explain why the mist does this, and nobody has ever seriously questioned it.

## Relationship with the Creatures

The people live gently alongside the animals of the plateau. The shelled grazers are left undisturbed except during the reed harvest, and the people treat them with a kind of affectionate deference, as slow elders who have seen more than anyone. The grey-furred reed beasts are kept in pens woven from fiber, and the children learn to milk them with the same patience they bring to every task. The flightless shore birds are tended more loosely, wandering the causeways and sheltering in the lee of the houses, and the people feed them scraps and watch them preen.

The mist-drifting creatures, the translucent, pulsing things that glow faintly in the haze, are not hunted or kept. The people regard them as messengers, or as visitors from the sea's deeper country, and when one drifts close to a settlement, people fall silent and watch it pass. Some say the glowing things carry the mist's memories, and that a person who stares into their light long enough will hear a phrase they spoke days before, returned from somewhere far off.

The heavy, silent birds that glide over the sea are regarded with something close to reverence. No one knows where they nest. They are never seen landing, and the people say they are the sea's own watchers, circling the edge of the decision and reporting back in some way that does not require feathers or voices.

## Inner Life

What is it like to be one of these people? It is hard to say without flattening it into a set of traits. They are not anxious, though they are watchful. They are not sad, though they are acquainted with loss in a way that has given their faces a settled, unhurried gravity. They are not in a hurry to be anywhere else, because there is nowhere else, and because the place they are is continuously becoming something new under their feet.

They have a sense of humor, dry and slow, that surfaces in the long pauses of conversation, a comment delivered after a silence that makes the listener laugh a beat too late. They take pleasure in small things: the sound of a drop landing in a basin, the shape of a fern frond opening after a wet night, the first pale crust on a newly built causeway. They have songs for these things, and they sing them quietly, to themselves, while they work.

They are also, in their way, deeply aware of their precariousness. They know the sea could change its mind. They know the shelves could crack, the mist could carry a word to the wrong ear, the thing knocking beneath the trenches could finish its patient work. They do not panic about this. They keep building, scraping, singing, knapping, and they hold their awareness the way they hold a basalt core: carefully, without gripping too hard, ready to shift their grip if the stone begins to fracture.

## What They Are Becoming

The people of the Drowned Terraces are not separate from the place. They are made of its materials and its rhythms. Their bodies take on its minerals, their voices adopt its delayed returns, their memories are arranged along its stone, and their dead become its floor. When they stand in the shallows and let the sea work into their skin, they are not resisting the land's transformation. They are joining it.

And yet they are not passive. They read the shelves, they guard the sea, they choose where to build and where not to. They are the land's hands, slowly shaping the world that is shaping them, and the question of what kind of world it will be is one they are answering together with the sea, one course of stone and one lifetime at a time. They know they will not see the answer. They have made their peace with that. Their children will add to the wall, and their children's children will stand on the shelf where they now sit, and somewhere under all of it the sea will keep deciding, and the people will keep listening.
