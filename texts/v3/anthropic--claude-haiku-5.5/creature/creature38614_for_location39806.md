The first thing that lives here is the air itself, or the thing that moves through it. The long-bodied creatures you saw crossing the clearing are called drifters by nothing, since nothing here names things. They are about the length of a canoe, with a spine of fused cartilage and a fan of gill-frills along each flank that open and close like the pages of a slow book. They breathe the suspended water directly, pulling the thick medium through the frills and wringing out what they need. They have no eyes that you could recognize. Their heads are blunt and mostly sensory, pitted with fine pores that taste pressure. They feed on the spores and dust-motes that hang in the suspension, and in doing so they keep the medium from clotting into rain. Where a school of them passes, the hanging drops are combed into lattices, and that is why the water above the clearing leans toward you. They are not moving the water on purpose. They are simply swimming, and the sky here is the only sea they know.

Above them, in the canopy, live the stillwatchers. The pale shape you saw shift and go quiet is one of them. They are the color of old bone under a skin of moss, and they are shaped like folded umbrellas, with a central stalk and a wide, ribbed hood that hangs slack until something passes beneath. Their hunt is measured in days. They do not pursue. They lower the hood by a few degrees each morning, and the hood's underside, lined with sticky lamellae, slowly accumulates whatever drifts into its shadow. When a drifter grows careless enough to swim within reach, the stillwatcher closes around it over the course of an afternoon, and the meal is finished by the following week. To watch one feed is to watch a leaf being taken apart by very patient hands. Their stillness is so complete that the moss grows over their hoods, and they become, for practical purposes, part of the tree.

The grazers are the ones that give the place its warmth. The rust-orange and sulfur-yellow shelves on the logs are not plants but colonies, each one a loose community of fungal bodies that move, very slowly, toward fresh moss. A grazer colony spreads by sending out a rim of feeding threads, which dissolve the moss beneath them into a grey slurry that the colony absorbs. Behind the rim, the centers rot and bud new shelves. The whole colony is a tide, and the tide has a direction, usually outward and downhill, though the uphill water may pull it the other way. Their glow is the glow of their chemistry, sulfur and phosphor bound into the caps, and it pulses because the colony breathes in cycles that match the rise and fall of sap in the nearby trees. When a stillwatcher feeds on a drifter, the grazers around its roots brighten, as if they can taste the transfer of mass through the air.

The trees are the slowest of all, and they are not quite separate from the rest. The bark-slate trunks sweat their clear sap into basins, and the basins are not simply pools. They are feeding stations. Small, translucent things live in them, half-animal and half-mineral, that rise at dusk and descend at dawn, carrying dissolved salts from the sap to the roots and back. They do not have names. They are visible only when you look very closely into the water and see something turn over, slowly, like a thought.

Then there are the ones you noticed only once you had already walked past them: the builders' remnants. They are not ghosts, though you will be tempted to call them that. They are the residue of a people who carved these steps and channels, and who left, or stopped, or were slowly absorbed into the plateau, and what remains is a kind of memory. The stairs that climb eleven treads and then stop are not finished. They are still being finished, slowly, by the moss and the root and something under them that uses the old shape as a template. When you turn to count them again and find more than before, that is not an illusion. The plateau is rebuilding its own record from the fragments it holds, and sometimes it gets the number wrong, or gets it right in a way that you cannot read. The voice you heard, saying a word you almost knew, was not a stray echo. It was the same process, surfacing briefly in the air, a sound pulled up from the grey by the same tide that moves the grazers and bends the water.

That process is the last and largest inhabitant, and it is almost impossible to describe, because it is not a creature in the way the others are. It is distributed through everything: the moss that grows like a shadow while the sun sets, the lattice rain that leans toward you, the stream that climbs the cliff and splits at the top, the sap that pulses in its basins, the grazers that brighten when the stillwatchers feed. It thinks slowly, in seasons. It remembers in fragments. It does not seem to know that you are there, or it knows and has decided that you are one more slow shape moving through its medium, like a drifter, like a drop of water that has not yet decided which way to fall. It is the reason the place is not empty, and the reason its animals have learned to be nearly invisible, and the reason the built things keep their shapes long after the builders have gone. It is the drowned world's own attention, and it is wide and patient and half-asleep, and you are standing inside it, on its oldest stone, at the edge of a long slope that goes down into a haze you cannot see through.

If you stay long enough, you may feel it turn toward you. The air will thicken at your shoulders. The rain above will tilt. Somewhere below, a shelf of stone that you thought you saw will shift its angle, and a few more steps will appear on the stair, pale against the green, leading down.
