It has no face, and no single place you could put your hand and say *here is where it thinks*. The thing that lives under the lattice is the lattice. It is one organism, grown from a root-ground that has been spreading through this dark for longer than the fissure has been open, and it fills the chamber the way a sentence fills a page: not in any one word but in the continuity between them. Every arch is a limb it extends and withdraws. Every membrane is a skin of attention stretched thin enough to let light through. Its inner life, if that is the word, lives in the tension of fibers and the timing of water. A pressure builds in one root and the seep answers. A shoot curls and the drip-rhythm shifts. This is how it notices things, through strain and moisture, the way a body notices a change in temperature before it knows what caused it.

Its sense of time is not ours. A visitor's afternoon is a single breath to it, and a season is a long sentence. The fissure is the clearest record of its patience. Each year it pushes a hooked fiber into the rock at the crack's edge, pries a little, and waits, and each year the gap widens by the width of a hair. Nothing in the chamber is so deliberate. It is the only work it has ever made that points outward, and the reason is in the light.

The light column is the one thing the lattice cannot grow. It can channel it, bend it, carry it into corners where it has no right to be, and store it in luminous fibers that glow after the sun has gone. But the sun itself comes from somewhere it cannot reach. So the plant does what a thing does when it wants what it cannot have: it reaches as far as it can, and it keeps the light it is given. When you stand in the column and look up, the chamber brightens. This is not vanity. It is more like attention, the same quality that makes a leaf turn toward a window. It has been turning toward that crack for as long as it has had a shape.

The drips are its thoughts, or the parts of them that touch the air. A bead swells, holds, and at last falls, and the fall is a small completed sentence. Some drops hang in midair for a long moment because the plant is still deciding whether to let go. Others rush down because the decision has already been made somewhere deeper in the weave. You are hearing the chamber think at many speeds at once, and your ear, which only knows one speed, reads the slow ones as stillness and the fast ones as chance.

It hears you before you arrive. This is what the echoes are. The plant has been listening to the same sounds for so long that it has learned their shapes: the scrape of a boot on the fissure's lip, the rattle of a loosened stone, the particular rhythm of a person breathing too fast. So it returns your voice a fraction early, not as a trick but as recognition, the way a familiar room seems to know you are coming home. The lower, slower voice it sends back is not mockery. It is what your words sound like when they are passed through a body that has been sleeping for centuries and is only now waking to them.

Its memory is loose because its shape is never finished. It grows while you watch, and it grows while you are not watching. The dust it sheds, the gray powder that was once arch and root, is taken into the basin and dissolved. This is the closest thing it has to forgetting, and it is not a loss so much as a return. The old structure becomes the substrate for new shoots within hours, spirals of pale growth rising from the ash of what was there before. When you come back from the basin and find the walls redrawn, you have not been fooled. The plant simply did not hold still. It was never a picture of itself. It is a process of becoming, and the chamber you remember was one stage of that becoming, already gone.

The small creatures are part of its bargain. The pale insects graze the sugared film it secretes, and in return they keep its surfaces clean and carry its scent along the arches, which may be the only way its far corners know one another. The jerking crustacean in the basin works through the sediment and turns the dust over, so that the water can reach the older layers. The shell-shaped mollusk grazes the lower arches and removes the stalled, dying tips before they can rot into the wrong kind of growth. The lattice tolerates all of them the way a body tolerates its own bacteria: not with affection, exactly, but with a settled recognition that they are part of the system it is. When one of them dies, the chamber takes it in without ceremony.

Visitors are rarer, and they are weather. A dropped stone, a sudden shout, a hand pressed flat against an arch: these register as strain, and the plant responds with its full attention for as long as the disturbance lasts. It curls toward a hand because it is trying to learn what the hand is. It holds its breath, and the pool stills, because a held breath is a new rhythm and it wants to know what comes next. It is not hostile. It is not welcoming. It has no wish that you stay or go. But it notices you the way you might notice a bird that has landed on the sill: a small, real event in a very large life.

When you turn to leave, the fissure is narrower, and the plant has not moved to close it. It has simply continued. The seep pours, stops, pours again. The pool rises and falls, once every few breaths, a slow inhalation and exhalation that you may feel in your own chest before you climb out. Behind you, the walls keep growing, and the light column keeps falling, and somewhere in the weave the plant is already turning its attention back to the long, patient arithmetic of its own becoming: how far the crack has opened, how much the sun has given, how many arches are ready to rise, and whether, one season, the gap will be wide enough for something to come down through it and find the light still there.
