**Those Who Keep the Water**

They come down the walkways at the change of the light, one at a time, each stepping onto the pilings as if testing whether the wood still remembers its load. Most are thin in the way of people who eat carefully and climb often. Their skin has the bleached, faintly translucent quality of the willow leaves, a pallor from living in the shade of the bowl. They do not tan. Children born here have eyes that take a long time to adjust when they are carried into open sun, and they squint at the sky the way visitors squint at the dark.

There is no single leader. There is a rota, chalked on a slate by the headframe, and a set of duties that passes from household to household like a debt. Someone sits at the pump-house through each shift and listens. The pumps have a rhythm, and the keepers know it the way a mother knows her infant's cough. When a pump slows, they lay a hand on its housing, feel for the change in its pulse, and go down into the flooded lower rooms with a lamp and a length of cord. They do not hurry. Hurrying is what drowns people here.

The old ones are the bell-listeners. They sit on the upper terraces with their backs against warm-holding stone and their eyes half closed, and they track the notes the cables give off. They can tell a rise from a fall by the timbre alone. They can tell when the deep has drawn a long breath, and they say so quietly to the others, who nod and do not look up from their work. Some of them have lost most of their hearing and still sit there, because the bells are felt through the ground as well as the air, and the old ones say the rock hums louder when they are near it.

The children are the namers. They know every pale salamander by the scar on its gill-fan, every frog by the pitch of its throat-glow, every cistern shrimp by the way it folds when a stone drops. They have names for things that the adults will not repeat in front of visitors, and when the children are asked why, they only shrug and say the names are for the creatures to answer to, and that a creature who hears its real name might come up the walkway to see who called it. This is said as a joke. Nobody laughs, but nobody corrects it.

They are careful with the fauna. The salamanders at the pilings are fed in the cold months by lowering strips of cured fish on cord, and the keepers do this with a particular stillness, almost reverence. During the thaw they leave the pilings alone entirely, and they will walk a long way around a house to avoid casting a shadow across the water where the big ones are feeding. The frogs they barely acknowledge, except at dusk, when everyone who can stand on a balcony does, and they watch the throats brighten in the reeds and count the notes of the chorus under their breath. They do not know whether the frogs are keeping time or following it.

They drink boiled water and rain. They eat what grows in the cracks and what comes up on the lines from the lower pools. They rarely swim. Nobody here learns to swim well. The water is dark, and it does not let go of what it has taken.

What they feel toward the deep is hard to name, and they do not name it often. It is not fear, exactly, though there is fear in it. It is closer to the attention one pays to a sleeping animal that shares one's bed: an awareness that the thing beneath is large, that its breathing sets the terms of the night, and that the arrangement between you has been negotiated long before you were born and is kept mostly out of courtesy. Each household ties a cord to a piling at the start of the year and knots it at the level of the water. The cords hang in a long fringe along the eastern walk, and visitors sometimes take them for decoration. They are a record. Anyone who wants to know how the deep has moved over the last century need only climb along the piling-line and read the knots, and the knots are not evenly spaced.

When the water passes the mark on the eastern wall, the household doors open all at once. People climb with bedding and small animals and the old ones carried in chairs, and they sit on the upper terraces in rows, facing the water, and they wait. Nobody talks much. A few sing, low and without words. Some of the younger keepers pace the terrace edge and watch the bells. When the level falls back, the doors close again, and the town resumes its ordinary slow work, and the knots are tied in their new positions, and nobody remarks on how long the wait was.

There is a woman who lives in the topmost house on the western rim, in a room with no windows at all, because she says she has heard enough of the deep through the walls and wants a quiet place to sleep. Her name is Ila, or something close to it. Her hands are always cold. She is sometimes seen at the water's edge at night, not fishing, not drawing water, only kneeling with her palms flat on the surface as though she were feeling for a pulse through a sheet. When people ask her what she is doing, she says she is listening for the place where the bell and the water agree. She says she has almost found it. She has said this for many years.

The people of Harrow Deep are not waiting for the deep to exhale, exactly. They are the part of the place that breathes in time with it, the lungs and the valves and the knocking pumps, and they have learned the timing so thoroughly that they can no longer tell whether they are keeping the water still or the water is keeping them. The bells ring when the surface moves. The keepers feel it in their chests before the sound arrives. Nobody sleeps through the change anymore, though most of them would say that they do.
