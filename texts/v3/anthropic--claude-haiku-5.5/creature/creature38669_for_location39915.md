# Who Lives Under the Mat

The first thing to live here is the mat. It is one body, though it looks like many things: moss, liverwort, rootlace, the black peat under them, the seep threads running through the dark. It has no visible center and no edge. It breathes in the slow swells of the floor and sighs when you step on it. It drinks the drip from the canopy and passes the water along, and it takes back whatever stays still too long. Everything else in the basin is a tenant, a guest, or a meal.

The people are the most visible of its tenants, and they are few. They are pale in the way things grown in shade are pale, their skin the color of peeled bark, with a faint greenish cast at the throat and wrists where the damp settles. Their eyes are wide and slow to adjust, and they squint at bright light and turn their faces from it. Most of them move with a careful economy, placing each foot where the mat has firmed and shifting their weight before they commit. Long habit has taught them that the ground will give way if you hurry. They speak low, because sound carries strangely under the canopy and because the bats hunt by ear. Their voices have a soft, rounded quality, as if the air had worn the edges off them.

They are harvesters and tenders, and their lives are organized around the mat's cycles. They cut living blocks from the cutting grounds and lay them in patterns, reading the grain of the moss the way other peoples read the weather. They know which wounds close clean and which turn to rot, and they keep a memory of the cutting rotation that passes from parent to child by hand and by song. A person's worth is measured less by what they take than by how well they leave the ground. The old ones who have harvested for fifty years can tell by touch whether a patch will heal. Their hands are thick at the fingertips and gray-green in the creases.

They are also a little strange to the outside eye, though the town would not call itself strange. They do not gather in crowds. They sit alone or in pairs in the spaces between houses, where the mat grows thick, and they seem comfortable with long silences. When they speak, they often turn toward the nearest curtain of moss, and few of them notice they are doing it. Children learn to sing before they learn to speak clearly, and the songs are slow and low, pitched to carry through wet air without going far. The town has a word for the particular quiet that follows a song in the basin, a word that means something like *the moment the ground has heard you*.

Around them, the fauna moves in its own dense economy. Pale segmented crawlers thread the mat in long files. Glass-shelled beetles graze the moss in silence, and whatever eats the beetles eats them in turn. Blind white things run the peat channels with their bodies pressed flat to the walls. Under the surface a soft, uncountable population presses and shifts, and when you kneel and lay a palm on the mat you can feel it, a faint tremor like a held breath. The bats come out at dusk in long wavering streams and fill the air with their high clicks. They are the sharpest sound in the basin, and most people hear them the way they hear their own pulse, without listening. The seep-fish, eyeless and flushed with faint pink, sway in their pools with their mouths open, feeding on algae and larvae. The pale frogs sit in the moss without calling. The large grey burrower surfaces only at the thaw, and for a few weeks the whole basin seems to be made of its movement.

Then there is the canopy, which nobody has seen the top of. The stalks are pale and enormous, and they lean together overhead, close enough to shelter the basin and never quite closing. Their trunks disappear into the grey before they reach anything like a crown. The people do not climb them, and do not much talk about them, except to say that the stalks are older than the town and the town is older than anyone's memory. At dusk, sometimes, a long low note comes down through the dark. It sounds like wood bending under a weight too slow to see. The people stop what they are doing when they hear it, and then the bats come out, and the drip begins again.

The basin is a single living arrangement, and each of its inhabitants is a part of the same slow process, eating and being eaten, cutting and healing, singing and being heard. The people believe the mat listens to them. The rings of heavy moss around the lamps, the curtains that lean toward the voices, the grooves worn into the cutting grounds by generations of the same songs: they take these as evidence of a conversation, though none of them could say who speaks first. The mat does not answer in words. It answers in growth, in where it thickens and where it thins, in which cuts close and which go dark.

If you ask who lives under the mat, the people will tell you their names, and the bats will tell you where the insects are, and the seep-fish will drift toward you in the dark. But the one who has the most say in the matter is the ground itself, which lets everyone else stay, and which is listening.
