**The Sunken Pan Is Awake, and Slowly Thinking**

You might take the first things you notice for the inhabitants: the grazers spiralling on the mud, the translucent drifter in the deep pool, the lizards flattening into the plate-gaps. They are the obvious residents. But the basin does not seem to notice them as separate lives. Watch the water over a few days and the pattern shows. When the rain comes, the creatures all move toward the deep pool, and the pool answers by swelling. When the pockets dry, the grazers gather into crusted clusters on the last wet mud, and the crust thickens around them. Nothing here is alone in the way an animal is alone. Each creature is a place where the basin's attention happens to touch down.

Consider the floor first. The plates lift a few millimetres after rain and settle again. The centre rises, the edges fall, and the lowest terrace tilts toward its middle. This is a movement that takes decades to register, and in that time it has the rhythm of a breath held and released. The plates are its skin, salted and clean, and nothing may root in them. Anything that grows here must send its roots out sideways, reaching a dozen metres for a single seep, so the shrubs are not standing on the basin so much as feeling for it, the way a sleeper's hand feels for the edge of the bed.

The water is its blood, though it moves more like a mood moves. It gathers in the hollows where it is needed, sits in tarns and threads, and withdraws when the body has had enough. The deep pool at the centre is the heart or the memory, depending on how you count. It is green-black and very still, and when something breaks its surface the sound is almost nothing. The creatures that drift there feed on the orange and blue crust that grows over everything, and their waste feeds the crust in turn. The basin feeds itself through them, the way a mind feeds on its own thoughts.

The cliffs are its bones, and the hollow concretions are its nerves. Narrow channels wind through the strata, and when the air is still and rain is coming, wind is forced through them. The whistle is faint, so faint that it is noticed only in the stillness before a storm. That is the closest thing this place has to speech. It is the sound of the basin registering weather, a sensation carried through the rock over a distance too long for any single moment to hold.

The rings in the mud along the pool are its memory. A ridge a few centimetres high accumulates every few years, and the older ones are worn soft. You could read them the way one reads a dream that has been half forgotten, each ring a wrinkle in a long attention, the outer ones eroded by time into something close to forgetting. A lifetime of watching would show you almost nothing change. A thousand lifetimes would show you a mind slowly drawing its own outline in silt.

And then there are the stones.

A few dozen flaked tools lie half-buried in the silt of the lower terraces. They are dark, fine-grained, worked by someone who knew what they were doing, and they have lain long enough to be crusted over. No hearth, no refuse, no path marks the place where they were made. They have simply been taken in. The basin has not thrown them out, and it has not cleared them away. It is slowly drawing them down into its floor, the crust growing over each one, the plates rising and falling around it, the way the cliffs are slowly dissolving into the sky.

It keeps them. That seems to be its nature. It keeps what is set down on it, not as a collector keeps things but as a body keeps a scar: without remark, without distinguishing between the injury and the self that carries it. The tools are not a relic the basin is guarding. They are one more thing it has absorbed into the long process of being itself.

So when you stand on the rim in the early morning and watch the cliffs catch fire, and the pools blaze like pieces of sky laid on the ground, do not think you are looking at a landscape. You are looking at something that has been awake for longer than the word "awake" has meant anything, and that thinks, if it thinks at all, in plates and rings and rainfall, in the slow arithmetic of centuries. It is not hostile. It has no interest in you. It is simply very, very patient, and it has the rain for company, and it has time enough to notice, eventually, everything that has ever stood on it.
