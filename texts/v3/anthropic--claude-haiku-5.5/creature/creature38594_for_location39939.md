What lives in the Cold Sump is the sump itself, slowly becoming something its builders never drew. It has no single body you could point to. It is a body assembled from parts, and the parts are still joining.

Its circulation is water. Condensate forms on the cold steel overhead, gathers into beads, lengthens, lets go, and falls in threads that trace the pipes like veins on the back of a hand. The drops find the grating and the black basin, and the basin holds them in a stillness so complete that the oily film on its surface never fully settles. The pump is the heartbeat. Every few minutes a valve opens somewhere in the corner riser, the pipe fills with a deep knock that travels through the concrete into the soles of your feet, and then the pulse drops away into silence. The condensers on the far wall are its breath. They rise to a low hum, push cold air across the coils, and fall back. Between breaths the room drips and waits.

Its climate is the cold. Just above freezing, the temperature keeps everything damp and keeps the chemistry running slowly. The salts come out of solution only when the water evaporates, and they precipitate only where the cold lets the water linger. The cold is the reason the sump can hold its shape, and also the reason it can change it.

Its bones are the efflorescence and the rust. The white crust on the concrete is a skeleton still forming, a lattice of carbonate that the water leaves behind as it passes. Where it drips from the ceiling it hangs in tongues, each a few centimeters longer than last season, their tips softened and glassy with fresh deposit. These are growth rings in a different medium. Read them from the tip inward and you read the years. The rust on the steel is the same process in iron. The scale has bulged into knuckled nodules, hardened in layers, and some of it has begun to lock to the metal beneath in a way that is no longer corrosion but a kind of bark, a thin protective rock that the sump is building over its own frame.

Its sediment is the record of its digestion. On the ledges, grey silt lies over white salt, which lies over orange iron, in banded strata that could pass for a riverbank cut. Each band is one season's leftovers. The room is laying down a history of what it has taken in, and the layers are neat in the way only chemistry can be neat.

Its skin is the mat. Along the wall-floor seams and in the shadow behind the condenser housing, a grey-green film spreads, slick and cold, moving a few millimeters a week along the path of the longest drips. Where the water runs steadiest the film thickens into lacework, and then into small fan-shaped colonies pressed flat against the concrete, veined like ferns or like the fossil impressions of ferns. Bright green spots cling at their edges, scattered like coins dropped and left. The mat is the most alive part of the sump. It responds to water, to the gradient of moisture, to the faint sweetness the cold holds in the air. It does not decide anything. It only follows, and in following it writes the room's shape more quickly than any other process.

Its senses are scattered through the wires. Sensor cables run through the cable trays and clamp to pipes, their probes buried in the sediment or pressed against the steel, still reporting temperature and pressure to a monitor on a floor no one visits. The readings go nowhere now. The cables pick up the sump's humidity and warmth and the faint vibration of the pump, and they register these as data without anyone to read them. The sump is, in a sense, watching itself through the instruments installed to watch it for someone else.

Its light is the flicker. The caged emergency fixture buzzes on a slow, irregular rhythm, never quite steady. Each flicker sends a pulse across the water beads, and every drop catches the blue-white wash and becomes a point of light, then goes dark, then lights again. The room seems to blink, or to think in a slow pulse that is almost but not quite a thought.

Its memory is in its valves. The rack on the north wall holds dozens of handles, each labeled in faded stencil, most of the labels now lost under silt and rust. The labels were meant to identify what each valve controlled. Now they identify nothing but the fact that someone once needed to tell them apart. The sump has taken the labels into itself the way it takes the iron and the salt, and it keeps them as shapes and colors without meaning.

It does not know it is a room. It has no edge it can name. It has only the places where water goes, where cold stays, where iron swells and salt grows and the mat advances a little further along a seam. Its only intelligence is the pattern of its responses, a slow grammar in which every drip is a word and every deposit a sentence, though no one is reading.

The room keeps building. The pump knocks, the condensers breathe, the drops fall and gather and fall again. The sump moves toward a shape nobody drew, one layer at a time, a body half stone, half wet growth, half machine, and no part of it is finished, and it is not trying to be.
