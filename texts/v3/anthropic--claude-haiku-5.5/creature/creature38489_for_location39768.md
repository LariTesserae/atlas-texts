They live in the water, and the water runs through the whole city, so they are harder to count than to find. Where the cisterns overflow into the gutters, where the moss rings the drips, where the reeds stand in the seep, you will find them if you are patient, and you will usually find them only after they have decided you are harmless.

They are low and flat-bodied, roughly the length of a forearm, and they move the way water moves over stone: a slow, spreading push, many short limbs working in turn, the body barely lifting. Their skin is olive-grey and mottled with rust, the same colors as the lichen on the basalt, and it takes up moisture so readily that after rain they seem to swell and darken, then pale as the day dries. In fog they are nearly invisible. Their eyes sit in a ridge along the top of the head, small and clear, and they catch the diffuse light like wet glass. They have no fur, no scales, and no bright markings. Everything about them is camouflage in a city that has no color to spare.

They do not build. Whoever cut the basins, laid the walls, and shaped the spouts is gone, and the cistern-folk live in the ruins the way the moss does, without repairing anything. What they do is maintain the water. Each morning, before the fog lifts, they work the channels with their broad flat forelimbs, scraping silt from the gutters and clearing the spout mouths of leaves and grit so the overflow keeps moving. The cascade has stayed open this long largely because of them. A cistern they neglect silts up in a season. A channel they abandon dries out or chokes with reed, and the district above it goes quiet. Where they have been most active, the stone is cleanest, and the streets run with bright, fast sheets of water that catch the light when the cloud breaks.

Their reproduction follows the water. Eggs are laid in the reed seep at the highest cisterns, in clusters of clear jelly that hang from the stems like beads. The hatchlings are tiny and drift downward with the overflow, tier by tier, through the carved spouts and along the gutters, feeding on the lichen film and fine silt that collects in each basin as they pass. By the time they reach the lowest pools they have grown enough to crawl out and climb the walls, and they spend their adult years working their way back up the cascade, one district at a time. The whole settlement is, in this sense, a single long nursery, and the stepped basins are not so much storage as a route. An adult who has grown up in the low pools will remember the way to the high ones. Many do not return. The Shelf does not feed many mouths, and it is careful about which ones it feeds.

They talk little, and not loudly. Their language is made mostly of touch, long flat presses of a forelimb against wet stone that carry down the basin walls and out along the gutters, faster than a voice would reach in this damp air. Listen at dusk and you will hear a patterned tapping that does not match the drip, a rhythm that goes around a courtyard and comes back. Some of the drops that fall under the overhangs were not made by the rock. The breathing note in the empty doorways is also partly theirs, though that is harder to hear: a low hum they make in their throats when they settle under a ledge for the night, pressed flat against the moss, so that the sound seems to come out of the wall itself.

They are old in a way the stone is not. Individuals live a long time, moving slowly through the same few streets, and the knowledge they carry is mostly in the channels. Which spouts crack in a freeze, which cisterns leak at the seam, which gutter holds water longest in a dry year. None of it is written. It is only the shape of the flow, kept open, one scraped channel at a time.

When a block slips and the wall comes down with its short dull thud, they do not hurry toward it. They wait until the echo has faded, then go to see whether the water has found a new way through, and if it has, they follow it down.
