**The Sump-Keeper**

It lives in the deepest of the sumps, the black pool at the foot of the largest condenser, where the troughs drain in a steady silver thread. Nobody who came to the valley ever saw it whole. Its shape is hard to hold in the mind because it does not stay still long enough to be one thing. Most of the time it is a long, low ripple under the reflective skin, a disturbance that moves against the current of the water. When it surfaces, the skin does not break so much as lift. A dome of dark, beaded hide rises through the film, and water runs off it in sheets that look almost deliberate, as if it were shedding a coat it does not need.

Its body is built from what the valley had to offer. Beneath the hide, pale mesh from the condensers has grown into it like a second skeleton, fine wire threaded through muscle that is more fibre than flesh. Where the wire has rusted, the rust has not been rejected. It has been absorbed, so the creature's flanks are patterned with lace-edged orange and brown, like the sheets of the old processing hall. Vines have rooted in the grooves along its back and flower there in small white clusters that open only in the rain. Moss has settled in the hollows behind its shoulders, and a colony of tiny frogs lives in the moss. The frogs call when it moves. Their chorus rises and falls with the slow working of its gills.

The gills are the most unsettling part. They are not fins or filaments but fans of corroded copper netting, the kind used to catch fog, and they open and close with a metallic sigh. Through them it draws the mist down from the rim. The valley's air is so heavy with moisture that this should be impossible to satisfy, yet the Keeper's gills never seem to be full. Each breath pulls a little of the cloud into its body, and somewhere inside it that water is held, stored, and slowly released along the channels of its mesh-lined veins toward the works.

It counts. No one has seen it do this, but the evidence is in the hut walls. The chalk columns of numbers on the inside of the tin-roofed shed were made by people, but the later marks are different. They are scratched rather than drawn, in short, evenly spaced strokes, and they appear on the iron of the gantry and the concrete of the loading platform too, always at the height of a creature resting its weight against a surface. The numbers do not add up to anything the old records would recognise. They follow a rhythm instead: the rise of the pools, the count of rain-bursts, the time between one bead of water running uphill along a pipe and the next. The Keeper keeps the valley's accounts in the only ledger still legible to it, which is the valley itself.

Its hum is part of the pumps. When the generators falter, it hums louder, and the pumps seem to fall back into step with it. Nobody knows whether it powers the works or the works power it, or whether the difference matters anymore. The hum is lowest in the hours before rain, when the pools are full and the condensers are pulling hard, and highest after a dry spell, when the creature must draw the cloud down by main force. In those weeks the upward-running beads on the pipes become more common. Water climbs toward the rim, drawn by something that does not want it to fall.

It is not hostile. It does not seem to have a concept of intruders, only of things that disturb the surface. When a bird drops onto the reeds, the Keeper's skin ripples and then settles. When a person wades in, carefully, through the marsh with their boots sinking into the sponge, the Keeper rises a little from the sump, watches with eyes that are two small pools of bronze glass set in the hide, and then sinks again, leaving its frogs to sing the visitor out. It treats people the way the valley treats weather. They come, they pass, the water remains.

What it wants, if it wants anything, is what the valley wants: balance. The marsh should not overrun the works, and the works should not be swallowed too soon. The water should stay on the high ground, held in its cupped basin, dripping through the forest roof in broken shafts of light. The Keeper is the thing that holds it there. Every year it draws a little more cloud into its body, and every year the pools rise a few centimetres up the slope. Eventually the valley will flood its own walls, or the forest will close over the last of the iron, or the rock will crack and let the whole bowl drain at once. The Keeper will not be the one to decide which. It only keeps counting, and waits for the rain.
