The valley's residents are mostly green, and most of them are patient. They do not move in any way a visitor would notice, but they are always arranging themselves, and the arrangement has a logic that the old town never had.

Begin with the water, because the water is where their cooperation shows most clearly. The pale creeper that follows the old pipe lines is not simply growing along them. It has built its own conduits, hollow tubes that wrap the iron and carry seepage from one low place to the next. When a pool grows too full, the creeper opens a new channel. When a culvert clogs with leaf-litter, the creeper's tubes bypass it within a season. Water in the valley does not drain to the river, because the river is gone, but it does move, slowly, through a circulatory system that the plants maintain and repair. The standing pools are their reservoirs, and each one wears a skin of pollen and litter like a lid, which seems to keep the water from evaporating too quickly. The pools are not empty places. They are held.

The vines are the masonry crew. The grey-leaved climber does not simply cover the brick; it knits. Its stems run across cracks and under lintels, and where mortar has failed, its woody trunks have thickened into struts that brace the walls from inside. The result is a single green membrane, but the membrane is not uniform. In some places its tendrils are dense and muscular. In others they are thin and reaching, testing the next gap. The whole network seems to share information about load. A wall that begins to lean is braced from three sides within a few months, and nobody has to be told where the stress is.

Above, the fern rosettes line the ridgelines like a row of small cisterns. They catch rain in their central cups, hold it, and release it slowly down through their root-mats, so that the roofs below stay damp enough to keep the moss alive. The moss itself is the skin of every horizontal surface, and it breathes with the weather, swelling in showers and drawing back under the thin sun. The paving underfoot is slowly being lifted by roots that push with what can only be called deliberate force. The grid of streets is turning into a field of plates, and the plants are in no hurry to finish.

Many of the stems grow at regular intervals, spaced like posts set by a surveyor, and it is hard to look at them without suspecting they are counting. Some of the larger plant masses open and close together in a slow rhythm, leaves unfolding in the damp of morning and folding back as the air dries. Taken alone, each plant is a simple thing. Taken together, they pulse. The valley has a collective breath, and it follows the mist.

They also orient to one thing. As the day warms, the leaves across the town tilt toward the cannery stack. It is the tallest structure in the valley, the one that once told everyone where the work was, and the plants have kept that role. It is their compass, their fixed point, and they turn toward it the way a crowd turns toward a stage. Nobody has explained why. The stack has no function now except to be looked at, and the plants look.

At night the fungal mats on the cannery walls give off a faint cold light. It is not bright enough to read by, but it outlines every course of brick and every window frame, so the building seems briefly to be drawn in pale ink. The light pulses in patterns that shift from night to night, and it is strongest after rain. It may be a form of signaling, a way for distant mats to coordinate their growth, or it may be only a by-product of their decay, but the effect is the same. The cannery becomes a map of itself, and the valley, for a few hours, is visibly awake.

The animals are few, and they seem to be guests rather than owners. The slugs and snails are the moss-keepers, grazing the surface and leaving their silver trails as a kind of maintenance log. The small dark beetles work the bark of the vines, and their tunnels may be part of how the vines regulate their own growth. The heron is the most striking of them. It stands motionless at the edge of the largest pool each morning, and while its stillness might look like hunting, it reads more like attendance, as though it has been posted there to watch the water. A few small birds move through the fern canopies, appearing and disappearing, and they carry seeds and pollen from one ridge to another without ever settling long enough to be counted.

So what lives in this valley is not one thing. It is a settled arrangement of growth, water, fungus, and the few animals that have chosen to stay, all organized around the bones of a town that no longer needs them. The plants do not hate the buildings, and they do not simply consume them. They have taken the buildings into their own system, and the buildings give them shape, height, and shelter in return. Every crack that widens is also a new opening for a vine, and every fallen wall becomes a bed for moss and fern.

The valley is patient in a way that people are not. Its changes come in seasons, in the slow lean of walls, in the shifting edges of pools, in the way a stack is watched all day by thousands of leaves. Whoever lives here has learned the town's shape and is teaching it, slowly, a new one. The town gave them the frame. They are still deciding what to build inside it.
