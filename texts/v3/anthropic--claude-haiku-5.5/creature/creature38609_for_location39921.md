**The Lantern-Fans of the Shelf**

The first thing to notice is the light, and the second is that the light moves in patterns. A cluster of pale filaments on a swell's flank brightens, dims, and brightens again, and a few body-lengths away another cluster answers. The exchange is slow, a conversation conducted in pulses that take a long time to finish. Then you see the bodies that make the light.

They are pale, translucent, roughly the size of a hand when spread and much smaller when folded. Each one is less a single animal than a loose gathering of soft lobes, held together by a thin membrane that stretches and tightens with the current. Through it you can see darker cores, dense as knots, and fine channels running between them like the veins of a leaf held to a lamp. The membrane is the colour of the silt, so that when a body settles onto the shelf it nearly disappears, and only the filaments give it away.

The filaments are the fan. They spread from the upper edge of the body in a crown of hair-thin threads, each one tipped with a tiny light organ. When the fan opens, the water around the creature fills with glowing particles, the drifting dead matter and the mineral flecks and the film of living things too small to see. The threads comb these out of the current and draw them down into the body in slow, rhythmic sweeps. What the creature eats is also what it lights. Its brightness is borrowed from the snow of the shelf, and when it dims, it is because it has eaten less, or because it is resting, or because it has passed a message along and is waiting for the reply.

It does not hunt. It gardens. Its lobes fold down over the pale bacterial cushions in the lee of the swells and graze the honeycombed channels with fine, sieving movements, taking only the top layer and leaving the rest to regrow. Where it has grazed, the mat is thinner and brighter, and the sea-glass stalks nearby have been left alone, as if the creature knows that they are slow and that their bloom is the only thing that matters to the silt above. You can see the pattern across the shelf: a patch of cushions cropped close, then a patch left thick, then another cropped. It is a field under cultivation by something that has no word for field.

The flaked stones are a puzzle that the creatures seem to have solved in their own way. Several of the sharp-edged nodules lie half-buried in the silt with fresh scrape marks along their edges, bright threads caught in the notches. The creatures do not carve. But they press their lobes against the edges as they settle, and where the stone is keen the membrane parts along a line, and the body draws itself through and folds again. Whether they learned this from the makers of those stones, or simply found the sharp edges useful, or whether the makers and these creatures are close kin who long ago stopped being different, the shelf does not say. The stones have softened and the knowledge, if it is knowledge, has been reduced to a habit of pressing against what is sharp.

They rise, too. The surfacing you sometimes see, the pale shape that lifts from the silt and pauses and sinks back down, is one of them riding a boil. Where the warm vent water meets the cold drift, the plumes climb in slow columns, and the creatures unfold their fans and let the rising water lift them. They drift up through the silt cloud, combing it, glowing against the murk, and when the column tears apart they let themselves fall, and they settle somewhere new. A creature may spend a whole cycle of the current moving from one side of the shelf to the other this way, and it will not be exactly where it began.

They are very old, or perhaps they are very slow, and it is hard to tell which. A body may hold its shape for what feels like a season and then, over what feels like a night, fold into two, or lose a lobe to the drift and grow another in its place. There is no single creature to point to. There is a lineage of folded bodies, a long conversation of pulses across the silt, and a field of cropped and uncropped mats that has been tended for longer than the stones have been softened.

They do not know you are here. When you move, the nearest fans close, and the glow goes dim in a ring around you, and then, slowly, it returns. The creature nearest you has not turned to look. It has lowered a lobe into the silt, sieved the snow that drifted down while you watched, and sent a pulse out along the shelf to whatever is listening, which is nothing you can see. The light answers, far off, and then the water moves, and the whole pale field brightens and dims together, as if the shelf were breathing, and you are only one more particle caught in its slow current.
