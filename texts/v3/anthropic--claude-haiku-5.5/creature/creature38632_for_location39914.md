# Who Lives on the Mat Plateau

The first answer is the mat. It is the floor, the roof of the soil, and the bed of everything that moves here. Its fibers go down into the dark earth and hold the rain. Its leaves shelter the small things and feed the grazers. Hidden in its depths, a second population lives in the damp at its roots, in the crevices where stems meet stone. Some of these have never been seen above ground. They are the mat's own guests, and the mat seems to tolerate them the way a body tolerates its bacteria.

Beneath the green, the burrowers work in the dark. They are segmented and pale, some as thin as a finger and others as thick as a forearm, and they move through the root-tangle in long, patient loops. You know they are there because the surface ridges along their tunnels rise and collapse as they pass, like slow breathing under a sheet. Their feeding turns the mat's oldest growth back into soil, which is why the soil is shallow and dark and always moist. In the rain window, when the ground is soft, some of them come up to drink. They never stay long. By the time the mist burns off, they have all gone down again, and the only sign of them is a scatter of crumbs where the mat has been opened.

The grazers are slow and heavy and seem almost unhurried in a world full of noise. Their heads are flat and wide, shaped like shovels, built for cropping the mat in long sweeping arcs. They move in files, one behind another, following the same routes across the plateau so that over the years their feeding has cut pale tracks through the green. The tracks heal slowly, but they never fully disappear. What is strange is that the mat grows thickest and most vivid where the grazers have passed. Their dung is rich, and their trampling opens the surface to rain. Where the herds have walked, the hummocks rise taller. Some of the older grazers are so immense and so quiet that you might mistake them for hummocks, until one of them lifts its head and the mat slides off its back.

Among the hummocks lives the quick crowd, the banded crawlers and hoppers that match the mat in green and rust. They flicker through the stems in constant motion, and their coloring is so good that you can watch one walk across a leaf and lose it in the same instant. Many are grazers in miniature, nibbling the mat's fine growth. Others are hunters, and their stillness is the most dangerous thing on the plateau. A crawler that stops moving in the middle of a leaf is either eating, or waiting, or already dead, and the difference is only visible if you have watched it for a while. The mat trembles under all of them. Wherever one runs beneath the surface, the hummock above it bulges and shivers and then settles.

The long-legged hunters are the ones that move in silence. They travel in small groups through the tower-shadows, where the light is broken and green-gold and the sound of the insects is thinnest. They do not hunt in the open. They wait at the edges of the shade, then cross the sunlit mat in short bursts, long legs folding and unfolding with a fluid, almost mechanical grace. They have learned the swell. You will sometimes see a group settle on a rising hummock and ride it up, then drop as it falls, as if they were waiting for the land to lift them toward something. Nobody has seen them eat. Nobody has seen them sleep. They are simply there, at the edge of the shadows, watching the grazer-files come in.

The insects are the plateau's voice, and in a sense they are its mind as well. Their chorus is not background noise. It is a constant, layered report of everything that is happening: which predators are moving, which pools are filling, which storms are coming. The cicada-like rattlers rise and fall in great waves that travel across kilometers, and the deep humming ones hold steady beneath them like a floor. The clickers and hissers keep time in patterns that almost, but never quite, settle into rhythm. When the chorus goes silent before the afternoon rain, the whole plateau is holding its breath. The insects do not seem to be talking to one another so much as tuning themselves to the land, and the land answers.

Above the towers, in the fanned sails, live the birds with bellows for throats. Their calls overlap in long, looping phrases that are almost speech, and some of those phrases are very strange. A few have a cadence that does not belong to any bird you have heard. It is too evenly spaced, too deliberate. Some of the birds seem to be repeating it, the way a child repeats a word they have heard but do not understand. It sounds, if you listen long enough, like counting. The phrases rise and fall in patterns that repeat every few seconds, and then stop, and then begin again from a different point. Nobody can say for certain where the birds learned it.

Over the towers drift the great slow fliers, the vast gliding shapes that ride the thermals rising off the mat. They circle in wide lazy spirals, sometimes for hours, without a single wingbeat. From the ground they look like kites that have forgotten their strings. They seem to shadow the towers, drifting down to brush the sails and then climbing back up again. Seeds fall from the sails when they touch, and the fliers may be the means by which the towers spread themselves, though no one has seen a flier release a seed. The drifters are simply part of the upper air, the way the mist is part of the afternoon.

The towers themselves are inhabitants. They are not passive trees rooted in the mat. Their bark is pale and spiraled, and within the spiral ridges live small creatures that come out only at dusk, and fungi that glow faintly on the bark's shadow side. The sails open and close with the weather, folding up before the storm and spreading again after, so that the canopy seems to breathe with the rain. At the edges of the largest towers, where the air goes cold and the sound drops out, the towers may be responsible. No one has determined whether the cold comes from the ground beneath them or from something the towers give off, but animals know the boundary better than any instrument. They walk around it. The hunters step into it only with great care.

The stones have their own occupancy, though they seem to live at a different pace. The bloom-stones wait through the dry hours, then open when the rain soaks into their cracks. Their threads spread like lace over the surface, and their flowers are briefly alive before they fall. What they leave behind is a stain, and the stains are layered in patterns that persist for years. A stone that has bloomed many times carries a record of the rains it has seen. Nobody has learned to read the rings fully, but some of the older stones are marked with patterns that are too regular to be chance, and some of them are very close to the counting marks cut into the boulder near the hearth-rings.

And then there are the people who are not here anymore. Their traces are the most eloquent things on the plateau, and the most absent. The hearth-rings are still there, grown over but recognizable, their blackened centers marking where meals were cooked long ago. The lashed shelters have given way to living vines, which have woven the old poles into new structures that are part plant, part frame. The grinding hollows in the rock are still smooth and shallow, and the tools left beside them have lost their edges to the wind. The counting marks are scored deep into the boulders, arranged in rows that suggest someone was keeping track of something over a long span. Whatever they were counting has been lost, though the birds still repeat something very like it.

Who lives on the Mat Plateau, then? The mat, the burrowers, the grazers, the hunters, the crawlers, the singers, the birds, the drifters, the towers, the stones, and the memory of the people who once kept count. All of them are tenants of something larger, which is the plateau itself. The mat swells every few hours, lifting and settling everything resting on it, and the swells seem less like weather than like a slow inhalation and exhalation. The rains come in pulses. The insects tune to the pulses. The stones record them. The towers fold and open with them. In this sense the land is the one that lives most fully here, and everything else is a kind of organ or nerve or passing thought within its slow, damp, wakeful body.

At dusk the insects reach the crescendo of their evening chorus, the birds call their last overlapping phrases, and the drifters descend toward the sails. The rain-light fades from the canopy and the sky goes violet over the green. The plateau seems to hum, a single low note that sits under everything, and the mat settles and breathes slowly beneath it. Anything that lives here knows that the note means the day is ending, and that the land is still there, still breathing, still counting.
