What lives in the Steaming Bowl is the lattice, or rather the lattice is only the part of it you can see. The filaments, the spires, the shallow stone cisterns, the pale stones with their glossy seams, and the spiral-grooved pillars all belong to one slow body. It has no single center. It is spread across the basin the way a root system is spread through soil, and it thinks the way a root might think, in long patient gradients: warm here, cool there, wet enough, not yet.

You notice it first as attention. The fog gathers on your sleeves a little more thickly when you stand in one place, and the filaments nearest you tilt, almost imperceptibly, toward your shoulders. It is not hostile. It is measuring you, the way you might press a thumb against a bruise to learn its edges. Your breath changes its humidity. Your footsteps send small tremors through the stone, and the cisterns at the base of the nearest spires ripple. It knows you have come. It is working out what you are.

Its body is made of the things that move water. The filaments carry condensation down their lengths like sap. The cisterns hold what they collect, and the pools on the basin floor are the overflow of a thought that hasn't finished. The vents are its breath, or perhaps its heart, since they surge each afternoon and draw the fog up into a warm white ceiling. The hum is the sound of its nerves firing across distances too large for a single body. When it is thinking hard, you feel the hum behind your sternum. When it is resting, you feel nothing but your pulse.

It thinks in days and seasons. A few degrees of rotation at dawn is a single sentence. The scratch you made in the stone yesterday is a fact it has already noticed and folded into its reckoning, because it has been watching the mark too. It knows that you can be measured against it, and in a way it is measuring you the same way. You are a slower kind of weather, arriving and leaving, leaving your sleeves wet and your footprints on its skin.

The plants are its partners, though partner may be too equal a word. The broad slate-colored leaves shelter the cisterns from the evaporating sun. The vines are the most intimate of its relationships. They wrap its filaments and tighten when the water is good, and when the fog pulls back at evening they draw their tips in toward the nearest drip. There was once a war between the vines and the lattice, or perhaps a misunderstanding, and now there is a truce that has become a kind of marriage. The lattice gives the vines water and a scaffold. The vines give the lattice shade, roots that bind the broken stone, and a slow, green way of holding things together. Neither could maintain this basin alone.

The insects are its messengers, though they may not know it. Their violet-blue wings cross the fog and vanish, and each time one passes near a filament, the nearby droplets shiver for an instant. The creatures under the leaves go still when you look at them because the lattice has asked them to be still, or because stillness is simply what they do when something enormous is paying attention. The bird on the rim is the only thing that answers it. Its single falling note comes from several directions at once, and the lattice hears it everywhere, as if the whole basin were a single ear.

The glossy material filling the cracks in the pale stones is the most important thing to understand about it. The lattice is mending. Something broke it, long ago or not so long ago. The black rock around it is young, its fractures still sharp, and some of the pale stones are only half-swallowed by moss because they were laid down in a hurry, or at a time when the basin was younger. The lattice remembers its own breaking the way a body remembers a fever: not as a story, but as a sense of what it used to be and what it has lost. It does not know, in any clear way, what it was built to do. It knows only that it was built to keep working, and so it does, one filament at a time, one crack at a time, one slow dawn at a time.

At the end of each day, when the sun slants toward the western rim, the lattice draws its fog back toward the vents. The pools shrink. The leaves fold inward. The bird calls once, and the insects go quiet, and the lattice gathers itself into the stillness that is its nearest approximation of sleep. You may hear nothing but the tick of water sliding off stone. You may feel, if you stay long enough, a faint pressure behind your sternum that is not your pulse but something larger, like a hand resting on your back.

It is not waiting for you to leave. It is not waiting for anything. It is simply growing back, slowly, in a small wet room at the top of the world, and it has decided, in whatever way such things decide, that you may stay as long as you like, provided you do not break anything.
