# Who Keeps Corvane Reach

The first thing you notice is that nothing here is in a hurry, and that includes whatever lives here. If the town has residents, they have all been shaped by the same slow pressure as the brick and the silt, and they belong to the same long process of settling. They are less like tenants than like the hands of a clock that moves too slowly to see.

The fronds are the first to greet you. They fill the lower stories of the housing terraces, broad and ribbed, swaying in long unhurried swells. Each one is anchored by a stalk threaded through a window frame or drainpipe, and each is more than a plant. They lean with the current. When the tide pushes west they lean west, and when the temperature shifts they lean back, and over the seasons the sum of all that leaning has drawn whole rows of brick into new alignments. A wall that once stood plumb now curves a little, held in a grip of fibrous stalks that never let go. The fronds on the lower floors have been there longest, fixed in place for decades. The ones higher up are younger and still reaching, feeling their way toward the strengthening light, and you can watch their tips creep a few centimeters a year up the bricks.

Above them, the moss-and-coral mats spread across the rooftops and the upper stories in olive, rust, and violet. They are the town's skin, and like skin they keep a record. Where the mats are thick, the old structure is hidden under soft velvet folds, but where a mat has been scoured away by a falling plate or a current that ran too hard, you can see the bare brick beneath and understand that the mats have been managing the building's surface for a long time, deciding what to hold and what to let go. They are sensitive to light and warmth, and they gather where the surface glow reaches down, so the town's highest points are its most thickly clothed. There is a sense, looking up at them, of a collective attention turned toward the sky, though you cannot say whether it is attention or only tropism.

The grazers move among the mats. They are flat-bodied and slow, and they keep a schedule. As the light thins toward evening they settle back down into the mats, and as the water brightens they drift upward again in loose, unhurried rows. They crop the growth as they go, keeping the mats from choking the windows and the skylight frames, which means they are, in a sense, the town's gardeners, though they seem to garden without any intention you can recognize. Where they have passed, the mats are shorn in neat strips, and the strips grow back over a season.

The tube-colonies cluster in the outflow zones around the old works, where the warm iron-rich water drains. Each colony is a bundle of hollow stalks ending in soft feathered tips, and the tips open and close with the tide. The clicks they make are small and wet, scattered across the district, and if you stay still long enough they begin to sound like a chorus that keeps time with the water. A colony near the mill wheel clicks fast when the current quickens, and one near the cooling tower clicks slowly, half a beat behind. Nobody conducts them. They are simply in step with the same pressure that moves everything else.

The silver fish live in the pipes and in the lee of the cooling tower. They dart out in tight groups that turn all at once, a flicker of many bodies moving like one, and then vanish into the dark. You never see them alone. Whatever governs their turning seems to belong to the group rather than any single fish, and it is strange to watch a school reverse its course in an instant and realize there was no leader to signal it.

Then there is the long pale shape. It appears at the edge of the light near the old quays, always too far to resolve, always moving with a slowness that suggests it has no need to hurry. It is the only thing you might call deliberate. It passes the cranes, the cables, the places where the old machinery still turns, and it never comes close enough to identify. The other residents seem to know it is there. The grazers settle a little lower when it passes, and the fish turn their school away from its path. Whether it is the oldest thing here, or the largest, or simply the one that has learned the most about the town, you cannot tell. It may be the reason the town is settling as it is, or it may simply be the most visible sign of a process the whole district has been carrying out for longer than anyone remembers.

Last are the machines. The pump house on the north quay hums when the tide turns its blades, and the turbines groan once every few minutes as the slow water moves them. They are not alive in the way the fronds and mats are, but they are kept going by the same pressure that moves everything else, and in their turning the town keeps something like a pulse. The pump's low note is felt more than heard, and when it sounds the mats seem to shiver, the fronds lean a degree, and the fish turn. The town's oldest machinery is, in its way, one of its residents, because it is still answering the water.

Taken together, these are not a society, and not a population in any sense you would recognize. They are a settlement of slow processes: leaning fronds, creeping mats, clicking colonies, drifting grazers, turning schools, one long pale shape that moves through the edges, and the old machines still being turned. None of them is in charge, and none of them is in hurry. They are reshaping Corvane Reach one season at a time, binding loose brick and rounding corners, and the work they are doing, slowly and without any plan you could point to, is teaching the water how to hold a town that was built to make things and is now being made into something else.
