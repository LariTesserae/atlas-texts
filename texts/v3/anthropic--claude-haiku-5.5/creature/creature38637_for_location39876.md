**The Settlers in the Slots**

They are long and flat, nearly the width of a hand and several times that in length, and they lie in the gaps between slabs where the stone has parted from the stone. From above, if there were an above, they would look like the kelp-ribbons, pale at the head and darkening toward the tail, and they move with the same slow sway. Only up close does the difference show. The ribbons are anchored at the root and taper into the current. The settlers are anchored nowhere. They drift in their slots with the water's turning and pull themselves along in short, deliberate creeps, and along both flanks runs a fringe of fine sensory cilia, pale and fine as frost on a blade of grass, that trembles whenever the water changes its pressure.

They have no eyes. Along the head there are a few shallow pits that once might have registered faint warmth or light, but the water here has been dark for so long that the pits have drawn in on themselves, and what remains is only a dimpled texture, like the worn tool-marks on the stone. Everything they know arrives through the flanks: the drag of current across the fringe, the push and pull of pressure, the faint shivers that run through the lattice when something far off gives way.

They feed slowly. The filament mats trap the drifting detritus, and the settlers graze the mats the way a slow tide grazes a shore, rasping the fine growth from the filaments with a flat, toothless lip and drawing the film of trapped matter into their bodies. Nothing is killed in this. The mats are not stripped. The settlers take only what the mats have already caught from the water, and they leave the rest. A patch grazed one season is thick again the next, because the same slow currents bring the same slow fall of grit and dead matter, and the filaments hold it. The settlers and the mats have arranged themselves around each other so long ago that neither could say which one began it.

They listen. This is the thing they do most, and perhaps the thing they are for, if a creature can be for anything here. When a block shifts in its bed and the long low groan rolls through the water, the settlers feel it in the flanks before the sound reaches the far tiers. Some of them answer. They pulse back with a low, slow tone, pressed through the fringe, a sound too soft for any ear the basin holds, and the answer travels out through the lattice, from slot to slot, from tier to tier. A stone groans in the upper courses; a settler three terraces down pulses in reply; the pulse reaches a sluice gate, and the gate, sealed and furred, gives a faint tremor that feeds back into the lattice. The exchange can take days to cross the basin. Nothing in it is meant to be heard. It is only the place remaining aware of itself.

They grow into the joints. A young settler is thin and quick, and it wedges itself into a crack the width of its own body and makes a home there, pressing against the faces on either side. As it ages it thickens, and its flanks harden and pale, and the dense bodies of the old ones take on the look of the hardwood dowels, swollen and bonded, fossilized into something that will outlast the stone around it. In some joints there are pale ridged bulges that are not stone and not plant, and only the cilia-fringe, when you brush it, reveals what they were. Some of them still tremble when the lattice does. The settlers do not hold the terraces up. They do not know the terraces exist as a whole. But where a slab has been pushed aside by the ground's slow compaction and a settler has pressed itself into the gap, the slab stays a little longer than it otherwise would have. The stone is held in place by animals that have no idea they are holding anything.

They do not repair. Nothing here is repaired, and the settlers are no exception. When a sluice gate sags on its pivot, they do not work to free it. When a tier cracks, they wedge in and let the crack widen or narrow as the ground decides. They are not builders and have never been. Their presence is a slowness that runs with the basin's slowness, and it fits the basin's single direction, down and together, grinding and growing and settling, without any wish to stop it or any notion that it could be stopped.

Some nights, if the word could mean anything in water this dark and cold, a settler will press its fringe against a fallen column and stay there for a very long time, feeling the column's faint cold weight, the faint slow turning of the water over its face, the distant groan of a block somewhere in the lattice that has not yet finished settling. It does not know that the basin is moving toward a state it has been moving toward for ages. It knows only what the fringe tells it: that the water is turning, that the stone is still giving, that everything here is going down at the same unhurried rate and that it is going down with it, and that the cold is steady, and that the next pulse from the next slot will arrive in its own time.
