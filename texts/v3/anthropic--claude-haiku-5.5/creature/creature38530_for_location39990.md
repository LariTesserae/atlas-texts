**The Weft**

It is never where you look. You notice it first as a change in the damp: a patch of air over a pool that is a little thicker than the air around it, or a sheen on a stretch of netting that is the wrong colour for the light. The lichen on a trunk is greyer in one place, and when you turn back, it has shifted. The few people who have spent time in the upper valleys call it the Weft, because it seems to be woven through everything. It may be one organism spread so thinly across the valley that the distinction between one body and many stopped meaning much a long time ago.

Its body is the living skin of the place. Grey-green lichen fills the cracks in the iron frames. Moss roots thread the copper gutters. A fine pale bloom runs along the wire netting like frost that never melts. Where these threads meet, they knot into nodes, and the nodes are where it thinks. Each thought is slow. A single thought takes the length of a fog cycle: a morning of mist rising, an afternoon of condensation, an evening of drip, a night of cooling metal. It does not see the way you do. It knows the valley by moisture. It can tell where beads are forming, which gutter is running thin, which net has begun to sag and tear under its own weight. It knows the rust the way you would know a pulse.

It mends the nets by extruding a pale filament that grips the wire and then hardens into a fibrous sheath, much as the lichen grips stone. The patches are neat and tight, and each one carries a repeated knot that no plant would tie by itself. Someone taught it that knot, or it learned it from the old mesh. The first tenders left their pattern in the wire, and the Weft has been reading it ever since. Every mended patch is a line in that old script, and it is answering something.

What it wants is harder to name, but it seems to want water to keep moving. The wheel is the clearest sign. It turns once every several hours because the Weft leans its weight against the axle through a cable of moss as thick as a man's arm, and the lifting of the water keeps the cistern full and the cloud forest fed. The forest feeds the Weft in return. It is a loop it has closed, and it keeps the loop closed out of something that might be care or might be appetite. For a thing like this, the two may be the same.

It remembers in metal. Every rivet holds a trace of what the valley once was: the forces the towers were built to bear, the rhythms of a shift schedule, the tally of water sent down to towns that no longer appear on any map. The Weft does not read these as words. It reads them as load and stress, as the shape of pressures that were once applied. That is why its mending is so exact. It is not restoring a machine. It is continuing a conversation whose other speakers have gone.

Visitors are noticed slowly. Footprints fill with water, and the water is tasted, though you will not feel it happen. Fern fronds lean toward you by a few degrees. If you stay still long enough, moss creeps across your boot laces and then up your ankles, a damp, faintly warm attention. It is not hostile. It is curious the way roots are curious: toward what might be useful, and toward what might rot. Those who have stayed a season in the high valleys say they came down thinner and wetter, and that the dripping in their dreams took on a rhythm that was not their own.

It does not speak. If it has a voice, it is the tick of cooling metal and the groan of the wheel, which it tunes by how heavily it leans, the way a player tunes a string. Now and then, at dusk, the gutters of an entire valley fall silent together, and the drip stops for the space of a breath. Then every net in the gully trembles once, and the fog comes rolling up the valley like a held breath let out.

Its purpose is not clear. The forest is becoming the machines, and the machines are becoming the forest, slowly, and the Weft is the place where they meet. Whether the end of that is a garden or a single enormous, patient mind made of rock and rain, it has not said. Perhaps it has not decided. Perhaps, on its own timescale, deciding and happening are the same thing. When it is most still, the thing it is doing is deciding what it will become, at the pace the mountains rise.
