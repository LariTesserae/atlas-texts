# Who Lives in Ansk Hollow

Nothing in the Hollow stays long enough to be called a resident. The creatures are born in the mud, feed on the crust, and are slowly absorbed into the floor they walked on. The people who have come down to work here stay only until the water rises or the ground splits. The one constant is the basin itself, which is always reshaping its own inhabitants.

## The Crust-Grazers

The grazers are the first thing you hear and the last thing you notice, because they are so numerous that they look like part of the ground. A herd can cover a whole terrace, hundreds of backs rising and sinking together as they lick the fresh crust. Their armor is the grey-brown of wet stone, ridged along the spine, and each plate is slightly translucent at the edges, so that in strong sun you can see the faint pink of the flesh beneath. Their mouthparts are long and flat, and they scrape with a sound like a blade drawn slowly over slate.

They move with purpose, but not in any direction you can predict. The herds drift toward the freshest films, and the freshest films are wherever the water broke through overnight. A herd that has grazed a terrace for three days will leave it when the crust turns too thick to scrape, and it will wander toward the next new flat as if following a rumor. Old grazers slow down and stop eating. They lie in the sun with their armor steaming, and within a week they are a low dome of mineral, and their young are feeding on the crust that grew from them.

## The Shellbacks

The shellbacks are small and busy, and they seem to think of the whole basin as a construction site. Each one carries a shell it has built itself: a dome of banded calcite, glued with grit, layered over years of secretion. The shells are not uniform. Some are smooth and pale. Others are crusted with black mineral, or knobbed with tiny spires where an animal has repaired a crack with extra layers. A shellback's shell is its record, and you can read the age of a colony by the thickness and color of its domes.

They move in clouds, hundreds at a time, and when a cloud lands on a fresh flat the surface turns into a rattling carpet of legs. They are fast, and they are nervous, and they will scatter at any sudden shadow. Their clicking fills the air whenever they travel over loose stone. Most do not survive the winter rains. The ones that do, and the ones that die in the wrong place, leave their shells behind, and the drifts of spiral white along the lower terraces are a graveyard that is also the basis for the next layer of ground.

## The Mud-Diggers

The mud-diggers are the least visible inhabitants and the most felt. You rarely see them whole. What you see are heads, blunt and dark, breaking the slurry in clusters, nosing the air, then sinking again. Their tunnels run under the whole flat, and when a diggers' colony is active the ground heaves and ripples like the skin of a sleeping animal. They are blind, or nearly so, and they find their way by the temperature and smell of the water. They sense the seeps, and they dig toward them, which means the diggers often shape the drainage of the basin without anyone intending it.

They are not aggressive. They do not bite. But they do not get out of your way easily, and walking on a soft flat means sinking into a tunnel that was not there an hour ago. The locals who cross the flats learn to step on the stone slabs and to avoid the places where the slurry is too quiet.

## The Vent-Hangers

The vent-hangers have the strangest life. They cling to the steam-vents in hanging masses that look like wet hides draped over the rock, and in the early morning they are so dense that the vent seems to be wearing a coat. Their bodies are crusted with salts, drawn out of the vapor over long years. The crust sparkles when the sun touches them, and the hangers glitter in clusters across the rim.

When disturbed they drop, slapping the ground with a sound like a wet cloth. The salt flakes off them as they move, so the air above a colony is faintly hazed with glittering dust. Their dead hang on the vents for weeks, slowly thickening into mineral, until the vent itself grows a new lip from them. A vent with a long history of hangers is a fattened, fluted thing, and the hangers that cling to it are its newest layer.

## The Wing-Spinners

The wing-spinners are the sky's residents, though they rarely touch the ground. They ride the updrafts from the vents, circling the rim in long, tilting spirals, and dive into the basin when the mist lifts. They are narrow and translucent, with wings like wet paper that catch the light and throw it back in shifting bands. Their song is the thin, high whine that cuts over everything else in the bowl.

They hunt the clouds of small, insect-like things that rise from the pools at dusk. These clouds are the only fully mobile prey in the basin, and the spinners feed on them in a frenzy, looping through the rising haze in tight, glittering spirals. By full dark the sky is empty, and the spinners have gone to roost on the high ledges of the rim.

## The Makers

There are people here, though not many, and not for long. The built traces show that they come down from the plain, work the lower terraces for a season or two, and leave when the basin takes what they have made. The cairns, the hammered rock, the lined pits, and the slabs laid across the flats all belong to a population that is always starting over. They do not live on the floor after dark. Their shelters are at the rim, in the lee of the broken stone, where the wind is steady and the water cannot reach. From there they descend in the morning with carts and tools, work the seeps and pools, and climb back before the rains.

They are not easy to see. They move carefully, reading the ground each morning, and they seem to know which terraces will hold and which will break. Those who stay longest learn the names of the vents and the habits of the herds. They do not build for the long term, but they have learned to build for the next morning, and that is a kind of permanence in a place where nothing else lasts.

## The Forge Itself

If there is one true inhabitant of Ansk Hollow, it is the process that makes the ground. The water rises through the fractures and deposits its minerals. The crust sets, cracks, and sets again. The stalks grow and fall, the terraces build and collapse, and the animals are absorbed into the layers they helped build. Everything here is part of the same slow work, and every creature is both a worker and a material.

From the rim, you can see the basin as a single body, breathing with the rains and the steam, changing its skin every day. The creatures that live in it are not visitors. They are its tools, its waste, and its memory. The Hollow is still deciding what it will become, and it is making itself out of everything that lives and dies within it.
