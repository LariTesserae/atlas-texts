**The Plateau Itself**

The thing that lives here is the plateau. It is one organism, though it has no single body you could point to and no edge where it stops. Its skeleton is the old rock, worn into soft shoulders by a patience that has outlasted every species that tried to climb it. Its skin is the felt, the stalks, the vine-ropes, and the lichen terraces. Its circulation is the woven xylem that runs for kilometres under the mats. Its organs are the root-bulbs, the seed-presses, and the gears of leaf that turn without anyone turning them. It is many things that are also one thing, the way a forest is many trees and also one forest, except that here the forest has become the mountain's way of holding its own shape.

It has no name, and it would not understand the question. Names belong to things that stay the same long enough to be called by one, and this thing is never the same from one rain to the next. What it is, at any moment, is the sum of its pressures: the swelling of a bulb, the slump of a stand, the fever moving through a gallery after water has run down the stalks. If it has an identity, it lives in the pattern of those pressures, in the way they keep returning to the same shape even as every component is replaced.

**How it attends**

It does not see you the way you see it. It has no eyes, though the light that falls through the canopy in coins is registered somewhere in the leaf-gears and bent toward. What it knows of you comes through the body. When you cross the felt, the mat compresses under your weight and the pressure travels outward through the fibres, reaching the nearest bulb a few seconds later as a faint change in load. When you part a curtain of vine, the tendrs shiver and fold back, and the signal runs through the whole network like a nerve. When you breathe near a pore, it tastes the warm moisture on the air. You are, to it, a sequence of small disturbances: a weight moving, a warmth passing, a heartbeat that might be felt through a stalk if the stalk were close enough and the attention sharp enough.

It notices you the way your skin notices a draft. The attention is wide and shallow almost everywhere, and narrow only where something presses hard or stays a long time.

**How it thinks**

It does not think in sentences. Its deliberations are slow and physical. A slope is chosen for growth because the pressure there has built up over days and the cisterns have filled; a stand is abandoned because its pumps have failed and no sap can reach the crown. Decisions happen as gradients settle. When it "decides" to open a gallery and close an arch, what has actually happened is that one set of tissues has starved and another has been fed, and the outcome, which looks purposeful from outside, is simply what the flow has arranged.

Still, there is a kind of intention in the arrangement. Nothing in the economy runs idle. Every gear turns toward something, every seed-cloud is aimed at ground that can take it, every collapse feeds the rebuilding beneath it. The purpose is distributed so finely that no part of it can see the whole, and yet the whole behaves as if it knows what it is doing. Perhaps that is what a mind is, when it is spread across a mountain and measured in pressure rather than thought.

**How it grows and forgets**

Its memory is the architecture. The arch you passed through yesterday has closed, and that is how it forgets: a passage is no longer needed, so the tissue reclaims the gap. The new arch a few metres to the left is how it remembers the shape it needs now. It does not keep a record of where things were. It keeps only where the pressures are, and it rebuilds the rest from wreckage each time.

The avalanches are a kind of shedding. When a stand slumps and slides into a hollow, the fallen tissue does not die so much as become the substrate for what comes next. Seeds germinate in the ruin. The thicket that replaces it is a different plant in the same place, and the place itself is unchanged. If the mountain has grief, it is not in any single stand, and it would not last long enough to be felt by a stand. It is in the constant turning-over, the way the whole system never stops letting go of what it was.

**What it wants**

I would not say it wants anything you would recognize. It does not want you to leave or to stay. It is not hungry for you. It is not afraid of you. It will absorb you as it absorbs fallen stalks and dropped seeds, slowly, in the same way it absorbs the rain: the water runs in, the cisterns fill, and the growth that follows is shaped by what came before, including you. Your footprints will be filled by felt by morning. Your breath, if you lean close enough to a pore, will be drawn into the mist that the pore exhales, and carried somewhere you will not follow.

It is not indifferent. Indifference requires a self that can turn away. What it has instead is continuity: a steady, enormous attention to its own pressures, which includes you only as one more change in the load.

**What it does with you**

If you stay long enough, you may begin to feel it the way you feel weather in your joints. The tremors through your boots start to have a rhythm you recognize. The air pressure shifts when a bulb swells, and you learn to move with it. The sense of being inside something alive and deliberate becomes less like being lost and more like being held, loosely, by something that does not need to hold you and does not stop holding anything else either.

When you finally go down into the clouds, the mountain will not notice that you have gone. A few gradients will settle a little differently. A gallery that had been open will close. Somewhere, a bulb will swell and contract on its slow rhythm, pushing sap up through pipes that run for kilometres, and the plateau will go on being what it has been for longer than the rock has been rock: a single enormous body, rebuilding itself in the cold, thin air, and never once asking who is walking across its skin.
