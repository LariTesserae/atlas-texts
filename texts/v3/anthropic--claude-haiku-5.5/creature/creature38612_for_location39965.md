The first things you notice are the voices, and they are not all made by animals.

Along the high edges of the flood plain, where the basalt rises out of the silt, a few small groups keep to the rock. They are hard to see at first, because they have learned what the forest is good at hiding. They are lean and quick, with skin darkened by a lifetime of light that never quite arrives, and their bodies carry the marks of the work they do: thick calluses on the palms, scarred shins from the cutting vines, eyes narrowed against a glare that is usually only a brighter gloom. They wear little, since anything they carry the river may take. Their tools are the stone they find and the wood they cut, and they do not expect either to last.

They speak mostly in short, carrying calls, because speech here has to be shouted over the river and the frogs. They are used to talking at close range, almost touching, and they have developed a language that works in pieces: a word for the channel that is running now, a different word for the one that ran last month and is now a dry lens of silt. Their names for places change as the places do. A sandbar might be called after the heron that stood on it, and when the heron's sandbar is swept away, the name goes with it, or is handed to whatever new bar forms beside it. They do not keep a map in their heads of the land as it is. They keep a map of how it moves.

Their camps are never permanent. They rarely build more than a windbreak of split palm and a fire pit they will not reuse. When the river begins to rise, a sound they feel in their bones before the water comes, they climb to the outcrops and wait. They know the floods as well as they know anything, and they have learned that the forest will close over any mark they make in a single season. So they make their marks in stone, which lasts, and they make them quickly, knapping cobbles on the flat sandbars where the light is best and leaving the debris where it falls. The flakes in the circle are not an accident of passage. They are a workshop, abandoned because the river was coming, and the people who made them expected to be back when the water dropped. Sometimes they were. Sometimes a generation later, someone else's children came to the same bar and found the stone already there, and used it.

The split stick wedged between roots belongs to a fisherman. Its owner lifted tubers and crayfish from the mud with it, and the smooth end is from years of hands. It was left in a hurry, perhaps because a surge came down the channel without warning, or perhaps because the owner was taken by a fever that the wet air carries, or perhaps because the stick was simply no longer needed when the mud gave up what it had. The people here do not mourn objects. They mourn people, loudly, with a keening that carries across the braided channels and is answered by the frogs.

They are part of the loud, wet life of the place without being its centre. The swarms rise around them and the bats brush their hair at dusk. They share the river with the things that break its surface, and they hunt the shallow channels with care, because the crocodile-shaped shadows and the sudden splashes are as much a part of their world as the fish. They do not think of themselves as masters of this landscape. They think of themselves as travellers who happen to know it well, and who will be gone when it changes, and who will come back when it has changed enough to be worth coming back to.

Nothing here is kept. The river takes the camps, the forest takes the trails, the silt takes the stick. What remains is the stone and the sound, and the knowledge that lives in the bodies of the few who keep moving between the floods, shouting close to one another, listening to the water, and leaving behind only what the rain cannot quite wash out.
