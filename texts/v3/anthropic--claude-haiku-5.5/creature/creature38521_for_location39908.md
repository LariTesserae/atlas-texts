The first thing that lives on the Mesa of Thin Rain is the stone's patience, and the second is the cloud that hangs over one place at a time. Everything else arranges itself around those two. But the most insistent presence is the lattice, which may be the oldest thing still working on the plateau.

**The Lattices**

They are not quite animals and not quite machines. A lattice keeps a metabolism. It takes water out of the air, holds it in threads thinner than hair, and releases it on a schedule that is not the sun's. Its filaments draw inward over several days, gathering the mist into the grey frame, and then spread outward again, reaching. When its tops tangle toward the cloud banks, the threads seem to lengthen, as if testing the air for something. Sometimes the single rain cloud moves toward one of them and breaks over that patch of ground alone. No one has shown that the lattices cause this, and no one has shown that they don't.

The hum is their voice, or their breathing. It is so steady that the other creatures have stopped reacting to it. A lattice that has fallen silent does not rot the way a plant or animal does. It goes to a fine grey powder that the wind lifts and carries off, so that the field of lattices shrinks by a few frames every decade. The ones still standing seem to know their neighbors. When one dies, the threads on its nearest companions tighten, and the mist there beads more heavily for a season.

**The Water-Folk**

In the pools, life is translucent and hidden. Shrimp-like creatures the color of thin glass move in slow loops through the clear water, and the blue-green film on the stones grows and retreats with the light. The small fish are the strangest of them. Between rains they are not present at all. They wait in the mud as eggs, dry-sealed and patient, through months or years of dust. When the single cloud breaks, the water rises, and within a day the pools are full of quick silver bodies that were not there at dawn. They do not arrive from elsewhere. They wake into the rain.

**The Rooted and the Climbing**

The squat succulents are the plateau's oldest living things and its most generous. Each clump keeps its own dry patch of ground, but their roots run out a long way beneath the stone, and it is possible that they share what they gather. Their knuckled skins split a little after rain and seal again within the week. Their hollow stems catch the morning mist, and the water runs down into the core of each plant in a slow, audible seep.

On the cliff faces the silver shrubs are a different kind of patience. They press into the cracks where the drip zones are, and they turn their leaves toward the sun all day. In the afternoons the slope leans in unison, a long slow wave of silver across the whole face of the mesa, as though the hillside were breathing with a single lung.

**The Herds**

The grazers are pale, long-necked, and quiet. They move in files that wind between the troughs, and their paths have worn the stone into roads that run exactly along the grooves. They have learned the plateau well. They know which silver shrubs will hold out through a dry month and which have already been stripped, and they know where the mist lies thickest in the early morning. When a herd bunches tightly and turns all at once, the turn is never aimless. Something has changed in the air or in the grass.

They avoid the lattice fields, though not with fear. They pass near them with their heads low, as one passes a sleeping animal.

**The Runners and the Riders**

The banded-tailed runners sun themselves on flat rocks, their bodies flattened to the warmth. They are the first to disappear when a shadow crosses them, and they are the first to reappear once it passes. Their tails carry a stripe of pale and dark in precise rings, and when they run, the rings blur into a single flicker along the ground.

Above them, the wing-riders circle the warm updrafts from the rock. Their wings are broad and nearly translucent, so that sunlight passes through them in gold veins as they turn. They nest in the cliff cracks above the drip zones. Their calls are rare and thin, and when one sounds, the others answer briefly and fall silent again. They seem to measure the plateau from above, and they are the only creatures here that can see the whole of the troughs at once.

**The Hunters Beneath the Mist**

The predators are seldom seen, but they are not absent. They are known by what they leave: feathers and bone collected in shallow hollows, and the herds bunching in ways that have nothing to do with grazing. The hunters seem to belong to the mist. Their coloring must be close to the pale of the herds and the bone of the stone, so that at dawn, when the air is white and the beaded rock glitters, a pale shape can move between the troughs and never resolve into anything. They hunt along the grooves, where the herds travel, and they wait in the drip zones for animals too thirsty to refuse the water.

**The Insects and the Whole**

Above all of it, the insects rise from the crevices in iridescent clouds, turning in spirals through the mist. They bloom with the rains and vanish in the dry, and their numbers are the plateau's great reserve, feeding the runners, the riders, and something that comes to the surface only in the wet months.

Every creature here is adapted to the same unpredictable rhythm, and every one of them depends on the lattices in some way that is not visible. The mist would not gather so well without them, and the single clouds would not break so reliably over the pools. Perhaps the plateau was once a machine with a purpose, and the purpose has been forgotten, and the living things that now hold the machine together have forgotten it too. Or perhaps the lattices are the living things, and the rest of the plateau is only what they have grown around themselves in the long quiet after their makers left. From where you stand, with the thin air in your chest and the hum beneath your feet, the question has no edge to press against. The whole place keeps its balance, drawing water out of nothing and passing it from one life to the next, slowly, without asking why.
