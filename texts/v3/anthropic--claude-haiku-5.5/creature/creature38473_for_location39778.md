The thing that lives here is the green, and it is not one plant. It is the whole mat, the fused stems, the canoe-sized fronds, the vines and mosses and rhizome sheets stacked down through the buried stone. Each part is a separate growth, but the parts share water, and water here is how the green thinks.

Put your hand flat on the bark-like skin of a thick stem and wait. At first there is only the thrum, a pulse too low to hear. Then you begin to sense the pattern inside it. The pipes spiral up through the stem, and the water moves in them in long loops, up toward the cloud and down through the mat, and at the top of each loop it changes. A little heavier, a little more salt, a little warmer or colder. The stem is reading the pressure of its own circulation the way a body reads its blood. Somewhere in the mesh, a pressure is rising in the eastern ridges, and the fronds there begin to tilt and fold to take the rain. Somewhere else a pool has gone sour with too much mineral, and the roots nearby stop drawing from it and send their threads toward cleaner water. None of these decisions belongs to any single frond. They happen in the water, across the whole plateau, the way a thought happens across a brain.

The unison breathing is the clearest sign of it. Look across the valley and you will see a band of fronds open together, then close together, then open again in a wave that travels from the north ridge to the southern lakes and back. This is not a reflex. Each frond takes its cue from the pressure of its neighbours, and the neighbours take theirs from further off, and the wave is the shape of the green noticing itself. When it breathes out, the fog pours down into the valleys. When it breathes in, the cloud thins and the silver sun shows through, and every drop on every leaf catches the light at once. The sparkle is not a display. It is the green turning its face to the sky for an instant, before it draws the light back into itself.

It builds with the same patience. The rock under the mat is still cooling, and the green is slowly fitting itself to the stone as it hardens: its roots pressing into seams of pale mineral, its water pooling in the hollows of the glassy crust, its pipes laid along the cracks that the cooling makes. The braided streams that run uphill for short stretches are not a mistake of slope. They are the green pulling water toward a place it has decided to grow, the way a tongue moves toward a tooth that aches. The channels in the bedrock are its memory. Every rivulet that has found a path through the stone is a line the green once drew and has not yet forgotten.

The small things are its hands. The translucent beetles that move through the moss, too clear to fully see, carry seed and spore and bits of dissolved stone from one place to another, and they do not survive long outside the mat. The pale worms that rise after rain are its dreams made flesh for a few hours: they take in the excess water and the dissolved nutrients of the storm, and then they dissolve back into the soil, and the soil is the green again. There is no face because the green has no need of one. Its attention is not directed at a point. It is spread across every surface it touches, a field of response rather than a gaze.

Does it notice you? Yes, in the way the valley notices a gust of wind. You are a pressure change, a warm weight on the mat, a sudden spike of heat and breath and the faint salt of sweat drifting up into the damp air. The fronds nearest you tilt slightly toward your body, testing. The stems in your footprint send a ripple of tightening through the pipes, then relax when you move on. You will leave no lasting mark, because the green does not keep things it cannot use. But it has registered you, and it will remember, in the way a wave remembers a stone: as a small, temporary shape in the water, already dissolving into the next pressure.

Standing on the rise, dripping and deafened, you begin to understand what the sound is. It is not noise. It is the green talking to itself. The hiss of rain on fronds, the chatter of water in the stone, the creak of pipes flexing, the click of seed-pods snapping open in waves, the sudden tearing sigh of a frond unrolling: these are the voices of a single mind that thinks in water and grows in stone and breathes through the whole width of the sky. It has no need of you to understand it, and it is not trying to be understood. It is only doing what it has always been doing here, which is becoming ground, and weather, and itself, one pulse at a time, while the cloud folds over the ridges and the light holds everything in its unfinished grey.
