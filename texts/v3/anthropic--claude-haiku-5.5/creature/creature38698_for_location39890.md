The ones who live on Sohl Ardan are the plants, and they are not a single kind. They are a crowd of strategies pressed together on young stone, and what they build is the result of their arguing with each other, the heat, and the water.

The bone-stemmed builders are the oldest-looking and the most patient. Each one begins as a single green tip pushing out of a crack in the basalt, no thicker than a finger, and for weeks it does almost nothing but grow. Its stem is hollow and carries water and sugar up from the root zone, and wherever a thermal seep warms the stem, the plant secretes silica into its own fibers. The silica hardens into a pale crust that works like enamel. What the tip does next depends on where it finds itself. If the footing is firm, it arcs outward and sends a rib across a gap. If the footing is unstable, it grows low and thick, a sort of crouching root-mass that holds the ground together until it can risk more height. The lower stem dies back behind the advancing tip, bleaching and drying, becoming the frame that the next generation will climb. A single lattice may be the body of one organism that has been dying and extending for three hundred years, or it may be a dozen separate plants that grew into one another and fused their silica so completely that their boundaries are lost. You cannot tell from the outside. The plants themselves do not seem to care to tell.

The mats are the soft tissue. They spread under the scree and along the seams between the frames, dark and leathery at the surface, fernlike where they catch the mist. They are thick enough to hold a footstep and yet they drip constantly, saturated, their fronds weighted with water the way a sponge is. The mats are the ones that fill the gaps, and they move slowly, sending runners underground that surface in the warm pools and then throw up new fronds on the far side. They do not build walls. They fill what the walls leave open, and where a lattice collapses, the mats are the first to reach the wreckage and drape it in green, pulling the rubble toward the next structure.

Then there are the volley plants, the restless ones. Their stems are short and stiff, and their seed pods form in tight clusters along the upper nodes, dry and papery, packed tight enough to rattle when the wind passes through. Their strategy is not to hold ground but to scatter. When a pod splits, it does so in a sequence so fast that the sound is a single crack, and the husks go up in a cloud that takes minutes to settle. Most of them land on bare stone and die. A few land in the spring runoff, or in the damp rubble of a fallen frame, and those few sprout within days. The volley plants are not wise, or they are wise in the way a gamble is wise: they accept that the ground will fail under almost everything, and they throw enough of themselves across the plateau that something will find a place.

Below the visible growth, in the warm pools, there is another life that you can only see as color. The shallow edges of the boiling pools are rimmed with films of thermophilic cells, bands of rust and ochre and a deep green that deepens in the afternoon. These films are the reason the water is so clear in the still pools and so cloudy in the streams: they sort minerals, they pull silica out of solution, they make the crust that the bone-stemmed plants later use. At dusk, when the steam catches the sun, the amber glow comes partly from the light and partly from these films, which lift their pigments toward the surface as the air cools. They are the first builders, and they never appear in any of the architecture, only in the stain they leave on it.

What binds them together is not a shared mind, or not one you could point to. It is the way each kind's failures become another kind's material. The bone-stemmed frame falls, and the mats creep in and bind its ribs, and the volley plants land in the mat and sprout, and the thermophiles coat the broken silica and begin to harden it again. No part of this process is designed. But it has a direction, slow and uneven, toward denser growth in the corners where water runs warm and steady, and toward retreat in the places where the vents shift and the ground keeps opening.

When the wind comes down the terraces and moves through the hollow ribs, the whole place sounds. Each frame has a different pitch depending on its thickness and age, and the young growth rings higher than the old, so the plateau at any moment is an organ with its stops pulled in different directions. The sound is the closest thing Sohl Ardan has to a voice, and it is made entirely by plants that are, in the most literal sense, building the room they speak in, and building it without knowing what a room is for.
