The first things to notice are the ones that move, and the grazers are the most obvious. They come along the floor in low, unhurried bodies, their plated backs catching the amber light as it swells and sinks. Their legs fold under them in a way that suggests they were built for pressing into narrow places. Their faces are blunt and wide, with mouthparts that work sideways against each other, and when they reach a fallen tendril they bite down and hold, the way a person might hold a stalk of sugarcane, and the wet grinding starts. They do not seem to hurry. They have never needed to. Each one has a worn track in the dust where it returns to the same hanging tendrils, and the tracks overlap, looping back on themselves like a path worn by generations of walkers who have forgotten where they were going.

The glass-winged ones are different. They hold to the columns in dense clusters and never settle for long. When they click their mandibles, the sound runs along the ribs of the plants and comes back from the ceiling, so the chatter seems to have no source. Their wings are so thin that the amber light passes through them, and when a cluster shifts, the whole column seems to shiver. They are restless, but the restlessness has a pattern. Their clicks rise and fall with the glow, and if you listen long enough you hear that they are keeping time with it.

The plants are not passive either. The great reed-bundle hanging from the ceiling is a patient organ, and its tendrils move the way fingers move when someone is thinking. They tap the dust, lift, search, and sometimes lift a grazer's leftovers and draw them up into the swollen core. The leaves rattle in the warm wind from the fissure, and the rattle is so constant that after a while you stop hearing it as sound and feel it instead, a dry pressure at the back of the skull. The columns with fringed crowns seem to lean very slightly toward the glazed yellow stone where the vent opens. You might dismiss this as the wind, but the lean has been there long enough to have hardened into the shape of the columns themselves.

Beneath all of it is the hum. It comes up through the fissure in long, low swells, and it is difficult to say whether it is made by the animals or by the rock itself, resonating as the heat rises through narrow throats. It is not quite a voice. But it has the structure of one: a call, a pause, an answer that sounds like the same call returning from a slightly different direction. Every grazer in the chamber lifts its head a fraction when the hum rises, and lowers it when it falls. Whatever speaks through the fissure is being listened to.

Then there are the marks, and the marks are where the question becomes harder. The grooves at arm height were cut by someone who wanted to climb, or to hold, or to pull something up. There were not many of them. Each groove is shallow, but evenly spaced, and worn smooth at the lower edges by something that gripped them many times. The cairns in the open floor were built by hands that knew how to balance a flat stone on three upright plates, and the knocked-over ones have not been rebuilt. Maybe they fell by accident. Maybe nobody has needed them since. The tally strokes, scratched in rows near the stacks, run in groups of seven, then a longer mark, then seven again. If they count anything, it is not a season, because nothing here changes by season. It might be the pulses of the light. It might be the rises of the hum. It might be how many times the chamber has returned to itself.

The resin basin is the most difficult to read. Its rim is worn smooth, as though a great many hands rested there, and the crust lining it is cut away in neat patches, the way the cut-grain of old surfaces is exposed elsewhere in the room. The brittle residue inside is dry, and it has a faint sweetness that matches the air. Someone once put something in it, and someone once broke that something apart. You can imagine that it was an offering to the vent, or a store of resin for those who lived here, or a place where the animals were brought to be fed or to be stilled. You cannot tell which, and the chamber does not seem to care whether you can.

What lives in this place, then, is not one creature. It is a closed arrangement, a loop of resin and feeding and shedding, of light that breathes at a fixed interval, of a heat that has not varied, of animals whose tracks overlap, of plants that lean toward the same vent, of a hum that is both answer and call. The careful few who cut the grooves and stacked the cairns were part of that loop once, and their work is still here, but they are not. The chamber kept its shape after they left, or after they stopped being able to leave, and it has been holding that shape ever since.

The broken roof is the strongest evidence of this. The fracture is clean and sharp, unweathered, and it is still sharp because nothing here erodes. Something split the ceiling open, and then something closed it, and the sealing has held for as long as the marks have stood. The chamber does not seem to have been surprised by the break. It has absorbed it, the way it absorbs everything: the light, the wind, the dust that lies undisturbed in ridges, the grazers that return to the same tendrils.

If anything here is the inhabitant in the full sense, it is this patience. The animals eat, the plants store, the light pulses, and the hum rises and falls, and none of it asks for anything. It has no need to change, and so it does not. The only thing that moves in the Hollow is what enters it. The longer you stay, the more your breathing starts to match the glow. Your head begins to lift when the hum rises. You notice that the leaves have stopped sounding like noise and started sounding like time, and that the time has no direction, only return.
