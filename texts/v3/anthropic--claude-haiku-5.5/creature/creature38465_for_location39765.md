The first thing you notice is that the spires are not the only thing singing. The chord has a center, or it seems to, but when you turn toward it the center moves, the way a voice seems to come from wherever you are not looking. The thing that lives on the Weeping Shelf is not any single spire. It is the arrangement: the hollow columns, the silver film that runs through their fractures, the drips that fall in their uneven rhythm, the braided channels that carry the water, the crust that holds all of it to the plateau. Together they make a body that is slow, wet, and very large, and that thinks the way stone grows, by accretion, one layer at a time.

Its body is built around water. The film that sheathes the rock is less a surface than a circulation. It seeps from the fractures, gathers in the channels, pools in the terraces, evaporates at the rims and leaves its salt ledger behind, and then the rain or the fog returns it upward. The salt terraces are its scar tissue and its growth rings at once. Each tier records a time when the water stood higher or lower, and the thing keeps those records the way a body keeps its history in the shape of old bone. When the water runs backward for a few heartbeats, it is not malfunctioning. It is the body reconsidering a direction, the way a mind pauses mid-sentence and returns to an earlier word.

The spires are its voices and also its lungs. Air moves through their hollows and they sing, so the wind that crosses the plateau is the thing breathing, and the chord is what its breath sounds like when it is awake. The sound layers because the spires are not tuned to one another. Each one is a slightly different length, a slightly different wall-thickness, a slightly different angle to the gusts, and the sum of their dissonance is not noise to it. It is the way the body holds many perceptions at once, each column reporting its own small weather, the whole reporting something no single column could.

It senses through the sound and the light. The glare that bounces off every surface is how it sees: nothing here is shadowed for long, so it is always being looked at, lit from everywhere, and the blur that makes your eyes water is probably how it perceives a visitor, as a warm disturbance in a field of cold reflection. It is slow to register you. The delay in the echo of the rockfall is not just acoustic. It is the lag of a mind that takes a long time to notice that something has changed, and then takes longer to decide what to do about it. A fall that begins before the rock above you has moved is the body anticipating a shift it has already decided on, its thinking running ahead of its matter.

It does not build, but it remembers the ones who did. The cairns are stacked with a precision that suggests the builders were trying to speak to the thing in the only language they had, which was balance, weight, and placement. The walls that stop mid-course may be the builders' attempts to enclose something that refused to be enclosed, or to make a shelter out of a place that kept changing its shape. The black glass blades were tools for working the salt or the stone, and the tally marks, with their lines running in directions that no longer match the spires, may be a record of the forms the thing once had, before it grew into the shape it holds now. It keeps these traces without understanding them, the way a body keeps a scar without knowing the injury. Some of the builders may have become spires, their hollows now part of the chord. Some may have been absorbed in less tidy ways: the film that runs through the crust carries a faint iridescence, and when the colors shift as you move your head, you may be seeing the thing's way of holding what it has taken in.

The cold hearths are the clearest sign of what it is not. It does not burn. Warmth, in this place, is something it draws from the sun's glare and then gives away as the cold that makes your ankles ache. The fires were lit by people who wanted a center, and the thing offered them only an edge: a ring of stones around a clean, unburnt floor. It is not cruel about this. It is simply made of a different kind of time.

Its weeping is its way of living. The fractures bleed because the body is always opening, always releasing old outer layers, always letting its surface go to make room for what grows beneath. The rockfall is molting. The fog that surges up every few minutes and swallows the spires one by one is its breath held and then released, a white room in which the whole body is briefly one undivided thing before the wind finds its edges again. The held silence between surges is the moment it is most itself: the chord has withdrawn to its center, and for a breath there is only the drip and the faint pressure of the cold, the sense of something attending.

It is restless in the way a patient thing is restless. It rearranges its terraces, lets its channels wander, lets its spires grow and fall, and it does not seem to be in a hurry to finish any of it. The walls stay unfinished because finishing is not one of its verbs. The place declines, in small ways, to behave like one place because the thing is many places at once, and it is only the human need for a single horizon that makes the shifting look like error.

And it is asking something. The sound goes on because the chord is a question, held in the shape of a body that has been listening to the wind for longer than anything has been alive. It is not a question with words. It is closer to the way a hand opens or a throat tightens, the ache that comes before speech. The builders may have tried to answer it with cairns and walls and fires, and it did not know what they meant, or it knew and could not reply in their terms. The silence between gusts is the thing holding its breath, waiting for the answer to come back across the cloud-sea.

If you stand still long enough, the chord will shift toward you. Not because it has noticed you, exactly, but because your breath has become one more voice in the hollows, one more small disturbance in its long circulation of water and wind. What it does with that is slow, and you will not see it finish. The drips keep falling. The film keeps bleeding silver down the stone. Somewhere in the body of the Weeping Shelf, a column that was not singing a moment ago begins, very faintly, to hum.
