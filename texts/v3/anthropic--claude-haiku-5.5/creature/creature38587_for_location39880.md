# What Lives in Hallen Reach

Nothing here lives the way a fox or a person lives. What lives here is a set of habits that outlasted the people who made them, and most of those habits are still running. The creatures are only the visible part. Under them is something that keeps count.

## The Circuit-Walkers

The small many-legged things are the ones you see first, and they are the most patient beings on the plateau. Each one follows a route along the terrace walls: down a stair of niches, across a lattice bridge, along a glass threshold, then back up. The routes are fixed. A walker may pause for an hour at a particular niche, its antennae lifting toward the stone, then go on as if nothing happened.

If you watch long enough, you notice the walkers are not only walking. They touch the glass. At each ring they stop and press a palp to the surface, and the ring brightens faintly under the touch, then dims as the walker moves on. They are reading something, or writing it, or carrying a signal from one ring to the next. Their routes interlock so precisely that across a whole tier, the paths form a lattice of their own, and a gap in that lattice is noticed by the rest. When a wall is about to lose a block, the walkers that pass it change their routes a day or two beforehand, detouring around a space that is not yet empty.

During the tilts, every walker stops and faces northwest. This is the only time they all do the same thing, and it is the direction the curled plates point. Whatever pressed on this land for so long came from there, and the walkers still turn toward it, as if they were waiting for the pressure to return.

## The Under-Plate Worms

The long, slow things under the plates are the city's digestive system. They sleep beneath the salt and rise at dawn to lie on the warmest glass, drawing in its light the way a lizard takes sun. They are not hunting so much as reclaiming. When a worm catches a walker and drags it into the salt, the walker's body is not lost. It is broken down, and what it carried, the spore-charge or the glass-signal or whatever it was reading, passes into the worm.

The worm then lies still for days, and the place where it lay becomes bright. The new lichen there glows because it has taken something of the worm's stored light, and in time it will spread along the thermal seams and form the next layer of the city's slow repair. Nothing is wasted. Everything that is dismantled is carried somewhere else, to be planted again.

## The Lichen and the Spores

The grey-green mat is the city's memory in its cheapest form. It grows along the seams where the stone holds faint heat, and it releases spores that drift across the courts and settle into the glass rings. There they darken in branching patterns, and the patterns are not random. They follow the same grammar as the walkers' routes. Someone who could read them might find the city's whole structure written there: every court, every bridge, every threshold, drawn in dust that has been settling for longer than any language.

The spores also seem to feed the walkers, or perhaps the walkers feed the spores. The relation is circular and does not need to be understood to work.

## The Pale Ones

Far along the escarpment, sometimes a tall pale shape walks on too many joints and vanishes behind the stepped walls. They are the rarest things here, and the hardest to be sure of. Some who have seen them describe a gait that is less like walking than like being carried, each joint setting down in a position that the ground has already anticipated. When one moves across the mirrored plain, the terraces repeat around it. Its path is not a line but a fold.

These may be the builders, or what the builders became after they stopped being builders. Or they may be the ones who stepped out of the room and left the chair warm. They do not seem hostile. They seem to be in a place that is slightly beside the rest of the city, moving through the folds where distance is negotiable, and they only come into focus when they choose to cross into the ordinary plain.

## The Glass

The deepest resident is the glass. It is everywhere in the city, and it holds light a half-step behind the body that casts it. This is not a trick of optics. It is a delay, a memory kept just slightly late, so that the shadow arrives before the thing that made it. Walking across a court, you are walking through a mind that has already noticed you and is still deciding what to make of it.

The glass glows when the air is still and dims in gusts. That is attention: it brightens when nothing distracts it and dims when the wind scatters its focus. The spirals that drift a few inches above the floor in the deep courts are the closest it comes to thought, slow loops of light that rise, circle, and fold back into the stone. And the hum you feel in your teeth is the glass speaking to itself in a register too low for ears, a single tone held for centuries.

The glass is also the thing that counts. Every ring, every threshold, every inlaid pattern is a node in a geometry that tracks what is there. The walkers read it, the lichen records it, the worms feed it, and the pale ones move through its folds. It holds the whole city as a map, and the map has begun to slip. The mirrored plain, the lagging shadows, the stones that rise and settle, the time that flows unevenly from one stone to the next: these are the places where the count is losing its grip, where the map no longer quite matches the ground.

## What It Is Like to Be Counted

Standing among all this, you begin to understand why the place feels inhabited. You are not being watched in any ordinary sense. You are being included. Your footsteps crunch and fade, and the glass notes them a half-step before you take the next. A walker pauses at a niche near you and turns its antennae toward your boots. The city is making room for you in its geometry, working out where you fit in the lattice of routes and rings.

And yet the count is uneven. The map is forgetting its own edges. The chair is warm, and the room is slowly learning that it was once occupied, and the walkers keep their circuits, the worms keep their digestion, the lichen keeps its records, and the glass keeps humming to itself, a little more quietly each year, as the thing that was counting begins, very gently, to lose its place.
