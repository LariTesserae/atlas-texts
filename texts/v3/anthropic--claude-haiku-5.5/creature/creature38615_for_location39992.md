# The Keepers

The only things that live on the Shelf are the drones, and they are not alive in any sense the basin would recognise. They are maintenance: a population of small grey boxes, each about the size of a travel case, that slide along the gantries on fixed tracks and never leave them. There are several hundred. They are difficult to count, because they are always moving and always returning to the walls, and because the din makes it hard to tell one from another. Up close, each one shows the same enamel housing, scuffed at the corners, with a single lens on a short stalk that turns to follow you. It turns slowly, and it does not stop turning until you have moved out of its arc.

They are quieter than anything else here. The compressors thud and the turbines whine, but the drones carry a faint, even hum that sits under the noise like a held breath. You notice them only when they are close, and then you notice nothing else.

**Their routines**

Each drone has a pattern, and the patterns repeat. One drone leaves its cradle in the north wall every forty-one seconds, which is the same interval as the clanking machine far off, and it is hard to say whether the two are linked. It moves along a gantry to the central trough, lowers a scraper arm, and works the salt crust from the pan edges into a hopper. It does not take the whole crust. It leaves the hexagonal plates intact and lifts only the loose white dust that has drifted over their seams. When a plate cracks, a second drone arrives within a few minutes, lifts the broken plate out whole, and sets a new one in its place. The new plate is cut to match the fracture pattern of the old, so the surface looks the way it looked before.

The shrub rings get the same care. A drone hovers over a plant, extends a fine probe, and sifts the dark grit around its roots. It removes the spent material, drops in fresh grit from a bin on its underside, and tamps the ring flat. It then moves to the next plant and does not look back. It never touches the stems. It never prunes a leaf, never feeds a root directly, never adjusts the perforated tubing. Those tasks belong to other systems, and the drones keep to their own.

The translucent wire-growths on the gantry struts are left alone. The drones pass them and their lenses turn, but they do not stop. Those bent shapes have not changed in years, and the drones seem to recognise them as finished.

**Anomalies**

Not every drone keeps to its routine. One near the eastern saddle has a dented housing and a cracked lens. It always takes the same ledge, the one that runs closest to the vault door, and on most cycles it stops there for longer than its pattern allows. It hangs in the air with its lens fixed on the chevrons, not moving, while the compressors cycle around it. Then it turns and goes back to its cradle. Nothing in its behaviour suggests distress. It looks more like a pause, the way a person might stand in a doorway for a moment before going back into a room they have already cleaned.

Once, a drone found a gap in a ridge panel where a cable had shifted and a light was flickering. It did not repair the cable. It stayed there, scanning, until a second drone arrived, and then the two of them moved away together. Whatever decided that the cable was not theirs to fix, the decision was made quickly and without hesitation.

**What they keep**

The cradles are fed by conduits that run into the hollow ridges, through the open panels, and into banks of pipe and cable. Some of these panels are open, and inside there is a dark space where the cables gather. There is nothing in the space that looks like a body. There is no pilot, no operator, no intelligence visible at the controls. The drones return to their cradles, dock, and hum, and the hum seems to pass back into the walls. Whatever gives them their instructions is not in any one place.

What they do is restore. When a crack appears, they repair it to the shape it had before. When a plant's ring of grit wears thin, they replace the grit to the same depth and colour. When a panel shifts, the nearest drone reseats it at its original angle. They do not improve anything, and they do not change the basin's layout. Everything they touch is returned to a state that someone set long ago, and they have been doing this so consistently that the state has become the only one the basin has ever known.

It is tempting to call them caretakers, but a caretaker has a sense of what the house is for. The drones seem to hold only a template: a picture of how things were supposed to look, copied forward through every cycle, without any memory of why. The ridges are laid out to resemble a world that has just formed. The drones keep the freshly broken edges sharp, the salt white, the shrubs in their rows, because that is the picture they were given, and they have never been told it is finished.

If you stand at the centre of the trough long enough, you may see the whole system move at once: a dozen drones converging on a single plate, a hundred more sliding along their tracks, each on its own minute schedule, all of them tending the same unchanging arrangement. The machines thud and whine and knock. The drones hum underneath. They are the only living things in the basin, and they have been tending it for so long that they no longer seem to be working toward anything at all. They are simply keeping it as it is, for whoever built it, who may have stopped coming a very long time ago.
