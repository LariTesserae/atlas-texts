The first thing that lives here is the listening. Stand still at the edge of any pool and you will see them: tall, thin-legged forms wading in water to their knees, so still that the rings spreading around their shins seem to come from nowhere. Their bodies are narrow and grey-violet, the color of the mineral mats after rain. Their heads are long and mostly bare, and they turn slowly, one way and then another, following the calls across the flats. When a pitch shifts, a head swings toward it and holds. When the choruses overlap, they hold still in the middle and let the sound pass through them. They do not feed while they listen. They seem to take in the land's noise the way a field takes in rain, and they keep the most accurate map of it. The others move according to what the listeners hear, so an animal that has been noticed by one of them is an animal that has already been located.

The grazers are slower and heavier, built like something that was once armored against a much harder world. Their shells are domed and ridged, the ridges worn smooth where they scrape. They move in loose herds across the crust, lowering broad, flat mouthparts to the sheets of black mat and drawing them along with a sound like a wet blade on slate. They do not chew so much as file. Each herd leaves behind a band of scraped, paler stone, and over years these bands have become the long parallel ridges that run across the land, so the grazers have, in a sense, been sanding the country smooth for as long as it has been smooth. They are patient and rarely hurry. When one of them dies, its shell does not last, and the others know it, because they will step around the lace of its dissolving body for days without touching it.

The stitchers live in the wet mats and are the most numerous creatures on the flats, though few people have ever seen them whole. They are low and flattened, with many short legs along both sides of the body, and their movements make a faint continuous rustle that is almost lost in the larger sounds. The trails they leave are the stitched lines across the mud, neat double rows of prints that cross and recross each other as the creatures forage. They feed on what the mats hold and on what seeps out of the cracks, and they are the main reason the mats never settle into stillness. Wherever they pass, the crust is turned over and reformed, so the sheets curl, crack, and reseal over and over again.

Above the pools, the skimmers are never still. They fly in flocks that bank and wheel in a shared rhythm, and when they dip to brush the water, they leave rings that spread outward and cross the rings of the others, so the surface of every pool is a slow interference pattern of their passing. Their calls are sharp and clicking, and they answer the listeners' silence with a burst of noise whenever a predator moves. Their wings are a dull slate blue, which vanishes against the overcast until they are close, and then they flash pale on the underside. They drink on the wing and sleep on the ledges of the cliffs, where the seeps keep the stone wet. At dusk they rise together, and the whole sky over the basins seems to tilt and reform.

The quiet hunters are harder to name, because you almost never see them doing anything. They move between the calls, timing each step to the loudest moment of a chorus so that nothing hears them land. Their bodies are long and low, mottled to the color of wet stone, and they have a way of freezing that makes them part of the rock. They do not hunt the listeners, which seem to be too alert, but they take the stitchers from the edges of the mats, the grazers when a herd is careless, and sometimes a skimmer that has drifted too low over the reeds. Their hunting is almost silent, and the land seems to know it. When a hunter is near, the choruses thin out in a ring around it, and the rings move as it moves. It is one of the few times this country goes quiet.

The spires are not animals, or at least not in any way that would be recognized elsewhere, but they are not only stone. They are hollow and fluted, and the water in the pools at their feet changes the pitch they hum at, so that over the seasons they have become instruments tuned by the rain. Something lives in the hollows, something small and dense that produces the drones you feel in your chest. It is not clear whether the spires call to the creatures or the creatures call to the spires. The two seem to have grown into a single arrangement, with the spires providing the resonance and the living things providing the pattern, and each depending on the other to keep the sound from fading into noise.

Under the deepest basins is the thing that breathes. It has no shape anyone has seen. The water above it clouds for a moment, a pale milk spreading and thinning, and then clears again, and all the listeners in range turn at once toward the spot. The creatures that feed on the flats avoid those basins during the clouding. The rimstone pools release their faint iron-smelling vapor when disturbed, and it may be related, or it may be a warning the land gives to whatever tries to reach down. Whatever it is, it is patient, and it has been there longer than the spires and the cairns and the grazers' ridges. Some of the other creatures seem to be waiting for it to finish a breath.

And then there are the keepers, if that is the word. They are not anything you can point to. The cairns along the ridges are still being repaired. Stones that have slipped are set back in place, usually after a rain, and the channels from the seeps are cleared of silt before they fill entirely. The upright slabs are kept from falling. Nobody seen does this work, though the stitchers' trails sometimes lead straight to a cairn and stop, and the listeners sometimes turn toward a fallen stone just before the next rain brings a runoff that would have carried it away. Whoever built this country's marks may be gone, but something here still tends them, the way the land tends its water.

All of these live together in a single, noisy web of call and answer, pitch and rhythm, the listeners tracking the grazers, the skimmers warning the stitchers, the hunters moving in the gaps, the spires humming under the rain, and the deep thing breathing below all of it. None of them rules the others. The balance is kept by attention, and attention is kept by sound. When the rain comes, they all go quiet for a moment, then begin again, louder, as if the country had been waiting for the water to remind it how to speak.
