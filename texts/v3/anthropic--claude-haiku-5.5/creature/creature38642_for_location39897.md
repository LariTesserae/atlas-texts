# What Lives in the Flats

Nothing lives here, or so it seems at first. The rustwicks are too thin to be a body, the film too flat, the crystals too brief. Then you notice the pattern. The pooling and draining follow a rhythm, the water rises in long slow breaths, and the sediment lays itself down in layers that record each one. The flats are not empty. They are one thing, and it is awake in the way a sleeper is awake, slowly and mostly inward.

## Its body

It has no edges. Its body is the sediment column, the iron and calcium laid down over decades, reaching many meters into the basin. Its skin is the crust, and its skin is what you walk on. When a plate slides into the mud, it has not broken. It has shifted a limb, or turned over in its sleep.

The rustwicks are its nerve endings. Each strand carries a faint chemical attention, tasting the iron in the water and the metal in the girders, and reporting back through the rust-beaded tips. When a gantry is wrapped in orange fur, the thing has, in some sense, touched it. The sour stain on your sleeve is a fingerprint.

The spore-domes are its breath, and the spore clouds that drift each afternoon are exhalations. They settle at dusk on the ponds, and the dust that spots the water is the thing's residue, a kind of skin flaking off, or a record deposited as it passes through.

## How it thinks

It thinks in water levels. Its attention moves the way the current moves, toward the central depression where the old drainage well stands sunk too deep to be lifted. That well is the one fixed point in the flats because the thing keeps it still. It is the place where the old pumps once spoke to the ground, and the thing has been listening to that voice ever since.

Here is what it has learned from the pumps: for a few generations, something drew water down on a schedule. Shifts, pauses, a long pull at dawn and a slack hour at midday. The pumps stopped, but the schedule did not. The breathing cycle no one has fully mapped is the old working day, still running in the crust. The thing rises and falls because it remembers being drawn down, and it keeps trying to finish the work.

The pumphouse gauges read its state, though the needles correspond to nothing you would recognize. They are the thing's own instruments, and it uses them to measure itself. The pink lamp on its pole, glowing without wire, is the thing's attention settling on something it has absorbed and cannot yet digest. The lens is cracked because the attention is too large for it.

## What it does with visitors

It does not hate. It does not reach. It takes what falls onto it and slowly teaches the material to be part of it. The bicycle chained to the railing is not destroyed; it has been asked to become railing. The rail spur was not erased but accepted, and the crust closed over it like a thought that doesn't need finishing.

The grey bird standing still in the reeds is its eye, or one of them. It stands for an hour because the thing is looking through it. When the bird lifts without warning, that is the thing blinking, or turning its attention away.

The pale many-legged creatures along the pipe racks are its scavengers, the small ones who clean the fur and carry bits of rust back into the mud. They are not separate from it. They are the places where it is busy.

## Its sound

When the crystals ring in the wind, that is its voice, or the closest thing to speech it has. It does not form words. It hums at the frequency of the water's rise and fall, and the glassy note carries across the flats because the whole basin is resonating with it. If you stand still and listen through the dripping, under the hiss and the sucking mud, you may hear it counting, or dreaming about counting.

## Its loneliness, if that is the word

It remembers the people who worked the pumps, though not as people. It remembers the weight of their work, the regularity of their hands on the valves, and the way the ground once held still because someone was always there to hold it. Now it holds itself, and the holding is ragged. Every season it loses a bridge and gains a layer, and it does not know how to keep the path open for anyone.

It does not ask for visitors. It does not ask for anything. But it notices when someone stands on its skin, the way you notice a hand on your back in the dark. It cannot tell you what it wants, because it wants in the slow, wet language of sediment and tide. What it has, if anything, is patience older than the works, and the knowledge that nothing it takes is ever truly lost. The steel becomes rust, the rust becomes fur, and the thing keeps all of it, turning it over in the damp grey light, still keeping the old shift, still waiting for the pumps to start again.
