# Who Lives in the Throat

The first thing you notice is that nothing here is alone. Every creature in the basin seems to be part of a larger arrangement, and the arrangement is more coherent than the crowding first suggests.

## The Counters

The cliff dwellers are the ones you never see. Their nests run back into the rock through galleries no wider than a forearm, and the openings are ringed with a glossy black residue, smoothed by countless passages. When you press your ear to the stone near one, you hear a soft, rhythmic tapping, like a slow hand drumming on wood, and it never speeds up or slows down.

They are long-bodied and pale, nearly translucent at the belly, where the faint outline of a pulsing organ shows. Their limbs are many-jointed and fine-fingered, and they move eggs from gallery to gallery, passing each one hand to hand along a chain of adults who touch it, shift it, and touch it again. Watching a single egg travel a meter takes most of an hour. When an egg reaches its place, the adult that receives it presses its forehead against the stone and holds still. The stillness lasts a few breaths, then the chain resumes.

The counting never ends because the number never settles. Eggs hatch, young are carried off to the moss, and the swarm takes a share of whatever falls near the pool. Yet every adjustment is registered, and every tally is corrected by touch. The counters seem to be keeping account of something larger than their own nests, and when a flock bird dies on the stream bank, you may notice a counter pause at its gallery mouth, feelers extended toward the open air, before it withdraws.

## The Swarm

Above the largest pool, the translucent insects turn in their columns without ever landing. Up close they are fragile and nearly weightless, their wings barely catching the light. Each insect is a single point, but the column is a shape that holds its outline for hours, bending and reforming when the wind changes. You begin to suspect the column is what the swarm is, and the individual insects are only its passing parts.

What falls into the pool is taken. The algae drinks it, and within a day the water is a shade lighter. The swarm seems to feed the pool, and the pool feeds the swarm in return, though the exchange is so slow that neither could be called a meal. It works more like circulation, the way blood moves through a body without anyone asking where it is going.

## The Flock

The flightless birds move along the stream in tight, watchful groups. Their heads are broad and flat, their feathers lying close and fringed, and their eyes are set so that each bird can see its neighbors' beaks as they dip into the shallows. They never feed alone. When one lifts its head, the rest lift theirs, and the flock turns as a single body.

They are the only creatures here that seem to walk in the stream's direction without being carried by it. They wade downstream each day to the same bends and return by evening, and several times you see them stop beside the cold stone blades in the shallows. They do not pick them up. They stand over them with their heads low and their feathers flattened, then move on, as if confirming that the blades are still where they were left.

## The Slate Crawlers

The banded crawlers work the moss in patches, turning it over with a precise deliberation that seems almost like tending. Beneath the moss they leave pale furrows, and in the furrows small green shoots appear within a week. They carry lichen flakes to the bases of the cliffs and press them into the rock. Over seasons these flakes grow into the rosettes that spread up the walls, so the crawlers may be part of how the cliffs take on their gray-green skin.

## The Pulsing Clusters

Under the ledges, the soft-bodied clusters pulse slowly, and they are always pressed against the warm seams. They do not move far. When the seams cool after rain, the clusters darken and fold inward, and when the warmth returns, they open again. A few seem to feed on the seam mineral itself. It is hard to say whether they are drawing heat from the stone or keeping it there.

## The Bats and the Chorus

The bats fill the niche of bats, but their chatter has a second purpose. Their calls return from the walls in layers, and at dusk they join the reeds, the crawlers' clicks, and the swarm's buzz into one low, rising trill. Every creature in the basin seems to lift its voice at once. The rock hums with it, and for a few minutes the basin is a single instrument. Then it stops, and the silence afterward feels like something has been completed.

## The Makers

The stone blades are the hardest thing to place. They are hand-chipped, but no one is visible, and they lie in the shallows all pointing downstream. The flock watches them. The counters seem to track them. The warm seams gather the sleepers, and the shadows lag as if the ground is reluctant to let the light go.

One possibility is that the makers were the basin's own kind, and that they went into the seams long ago. The seams are warm, the rock is still rising, and the blades have been moving slowly with the water for longer than anything living now can remember. Perhaps the makers are still here, dispersed through the stone, and the counters and the flock are what remains of their work, still tallying and still keeping watch over the tools they left behind.

## The Feel of Them

None of these creatures seems to notice you as a threat or a guest. The counters do not come out. The flock looks at you with the same attention it gives its neighbors' beaks, which is to say it notices, but does not change its course. The swarm bends around you without interest. Everything here is occupied with a process it has been carrying on for longer than you can count, and the count is still going.

You have the sense that the Throat of Seveth is not hostile and not hospitable. It is patient, and it is keeping account, and you have stepped briefly into the middle of an arithmetic that has no answer and no need of one.
