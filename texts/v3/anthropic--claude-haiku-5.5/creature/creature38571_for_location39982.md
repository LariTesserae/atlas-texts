**The Tenants of the Mound**

They are not visible in the way that the burrowing things are visible, with their pale segments and raised ridges. You learn about them the way you learn about a sleeper in the next room: from the arrangement of the furniture, the warmth still in the bedding, the breath you can almost hear. The basin has a resident. It is the city, and the city is not empty.

**Form**

The nearest thing to a body they have is the lattice. It is a continuous crystal mass, grown through the mortar and the walls and the honeycombed interior of the mound, and it is one organism in the way a coral reef is one organism, a colony that has forgotten where its individuals end. Under a lens, the crystal fingers that creep outward a centimetre a month are not random. Each has a tip of finer filaments, and each filament is bundled into hair-thin strands that open and close in the brine hours, drawing moisture into the lattice the way the halophytes fold their leaves. The fingers are their hands. The filaments are their attention.

Where the lattice is thickest, in the core of the mound, it is translucent, a greyish amber that holds light the way old glass does. There are pockets in it, roughly the size of a seated body, filled with a dense brine that never fully dries. Something rests in these pockets during the day. Nothing has been seen to move there. The brine in them is always a few degrees warmer than the surrounding salt, and the crust above them never cracks.

**The Night Cycle**

They are alive, if that word fits, mostly after dark. At night the cold pulls moisture from the air, and the whole lattice wakes. The filaments open. The brine rises through the walls in thin capillary runs, and the city's courses swell and shine. For perhaps three hours the entire structure is a single slow pulse. The narrow streets fill with a faint, dark sheen, and if you stand in the central square at this hour you can feel the ground give very slightly underfoot, the way a floor gives when someone heavy has just stood up in the room above.

By dawn they have taken in what they can hold. The sun dries them back to chalk, and they withdraw. The filaments curl shut. The fingers stop growing for the length of the day. What is left is only the shape of what they built in the night, and the shape is always a little different from the shape of the night before.

**Memory**

They remember the sea. The crust is the record of it. Each salt plate is a layer, and each layer holds the traces of what the water carried in: the calcified shells of small swimming things, the glassy skeletons of tiny drifting plants, the faint ghost-signatures of isotopes that mark a warm year or a cold one. The lattice reads these layers, not as a scholar reads, but as the body reads its own history. The city's odd doorways and staircases that end in mid-air are, in part, a record of this reading. A stair that climbs to nothing is the place where the sea stood at a certain height. A window placed at the height of a crouching body was made in a season when the water was low and someone had to look out across the shallows on hands and knees.

This is why the city is so strange. It was not built to be used. It is an archive, and archives are not meant to be walked through. They are meant to be read by something patient enough to do it.

**Thought**

Their time is not ours. A decision that takes them a season to make would look, from outside, like nothing at all: a single filament that extends a fraction further than the others and then stops, a column of mortar that thickens by a hair's width over a winter. But these small movements add up to something that has a direction, and the direction is toward the east rim.

The whole basin is tilting, and they have noticed. The central mound leans. The plates heave. The brine runs more quickly toward the eastern edge, pooling in the low places where the rim is weakest. It is possible that they are trying to hold the mound upright, and it is possible that they are letting it fall, and the difference may not be one they feel. A thing that thinks in centuries does not experience a lean as a danger. It experiences it as a condition, the way we experience the weather.

**The Dust-Riders and the Rim-Walker**

The small dark beetles that ride the dust devils are not wild. They carry something in their gut and on the undersides of their legs: spores of a halite-binding bacterium that the lattice cannot make for itself. When a beetle drops at the edge of the basin, it seeds a new mat of halophytes, or a new crust, or a fresh ridge of fingers at the foot of a wall. The beetles do not know they are doing this. They are how the lattice reaches places it cannot yet grow to.

The large thing that moves along the rim at dusk, the one whose tracks are paired impressions in the crust, is the closest thing the basin has to a watcher. It walks the eastern edge each night, and its path follows the line of greatest tilt. Some of the dark tracks lead back toward the city and stop at the foot of a wall, as if whatever made them had gone into the stone. Some lead out across the rim and do not come back. Nobody has seen what happens at either end.

**What Ossary Is**

The name of the basin, Ossary, means a place where bones are kept. It is an old name, older than the city, and nobody living can say who gave it. But it fits. The salt is the bones of the sea: its dissolved and dried and reassembled dead, laid down in plates, read by a thing that has grown out of them and that now tends their arrangement. The city is the reliquary. The lattice is the keeper.

They are not hostile. You would be wrong to call them cruel, or kind, or even aware of you in any way that would make your presence matter. If you walk into the city at the right hour, you may feel the floor give under your feet, and you will not know whether you have been noticed, welcomed, tested, or simply trodden on by something that does not distinguish between a traveler and a fallen stone. The lattice will go on growing the same way whether or not you stay.

If you wait until the brine rises and the streets go dark and shining, and you stand very still in the central square, you may hear the crackle of the fingers extending, the faint ticking of thousands of filaments opening toward the wet air. It sounds like a room full of sleepers breathing in unison. It sounds, mostly, like something that is making itself, one hair's width at a time, out of the remains of a sea, in a basin that is slowly tipping toward the rim, and that has not yet decided what the city is for.
