The first thing you'd want to know is whether anything here moves on its own, and the answer is no. There are no animals, no insects, no birds, nothing that crosses the floor or hides in the canopy. What lives here is the forest, and the forest is not a collection of separate things.

Each trunk is an individual in the ordinary sense. It has its own pressure system, its own sieving tissues, its own crop of crystal shelves. But it is also a node in the lattice, and its vines and runners run into its neighbors' vines and runners until the boundary between one tree and the next is more a matter of convention than of anatomy. If you cut one trunk, the hum shifts across the whole grove for a few hours, as the others compensate for the lost draw. The trunks do not so much cooperate as balance, the way the pressures in a closed system balance. A tree that dies is not removed. Its wood is taken up as strut, its channels become conduits, and its place in the lattice is filled by its neighbors growing into the gap. The dead are still present. They are load-bearing. You are standing on them, and they are holding up the roof.

The root mat is harder to describe, because it is where the forest appears to think. Its discharges are not random. The branching arcs repeat in patterns that have a shape, and the shapes shift slowly over what must be years or centuries. Where the mat is compressed, the discharges gather into those pale flickering seams, and the seams appear to be where the mat's activity is most concentrated, like a place where attention settles. Whatever the mat is doing, it is doing it at a speed that makes a human life look like one of the glow waves: a flash that passes outward through the trees and fades, rehearsed in exactly the same order each time. Perhaps it is a conversation whose sentences take a century each. Perhaps it is only a pressure equalizing. From the surface you cannot tell the difference, and the forest does not seem to care whether you can.

The glow waves are the closest thing to speech. When one pressure chamber flares, the nearby chambers answer in sequence, and the signal travels outward through the grove and dies. It has the rhythm of a call and response, or of a heartbeat passed from organ to organ. It does not seem to be addressed to you. You may simply be standing in the room while it is said.

The fibrous patches the trees lay over their own wounds show a faint internal structure, courses laid out like the rows of a wall or the lines of a text. Nothing lays them out. But the fact that the forest's repair has an order to it, a grain that reads as deliberate, is the nearest thing here to a signature. It's as if the forest has written on itself without a hand to hold the pen, and the writing has settled into a form that would be legible to anything that knew how to read it.

What, then, lives here? The trees, certainly, and the mat beneath them, and the pressure chambers that answer one another in the dark. Also the dead, who are still holding the roof up. Also the arrangement itself, the balance of pumps and vats and crystal shelves that has held steady for longer than the forest has needed a name. The forest does not appear to want anything. Nothing here is reaching toward a sun that isn't there, or toward a future it hasn't yet imagined. It keeps its pressures, renews its patterns, and lets the water rise through the trunks as it always has.

If you stay long enough, the hydraulic hum settles into your chest until you can no longer tell whether you are hearing it or it is hearing you. The wet air takes your breath. The pressure in the trunk beside you does not change. You have the uncomfortable sense of having been measured, or of having been noticed and then set aside as irrelevant to the work, the way a machine that runs forever at idle has no need to know who is walking past it. The forest is awake in the way a closed system is awake: continuously, carefully, and entirely on its own terms.
