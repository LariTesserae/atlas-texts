**The Inhabitants**

You do not see them at first, and when you do, you may not recognise them as inhabitants at all. The basin seems empty of anything that walks, eats, or speaks. Then you notice that the walls are thicker in some places than the structure needs, and that the thickness has a grain, like wood, or like the rings of a cut stump. You notice that the hiss from the upper district rises and falls in phrases. You begin to suspect that the stone is listening, or has been.

They are the Settled. The name is not theirs. It is what the cart drivers called them long ago, the ones whose ruts are still filling with water. The Settled are the basin's residents, and they are slow in the way the basin is slow. A single one of them may take a generation to move a hand's width, and a single thought may take a season to form and then a century to set. Their bodies are the grey sediment, poured and resettled in layers. Each layer is a moment they have chosen to keep. When you look at a wall and see its banding, you are looking at a cross-section of someone's attention.

**Where they live**

They do not sleep in beds or keep rooms. They lie down inside the structures, in the half-built chambers and the cisterns that hold standing water, and they become part of the architecture while they rest. A doorway that opens onto a staircase rising through a floor may be a limb that has grown into the house and is still deciding whether to be a limb or a house. The strange double streets, the lamp-posts without lamps, the second lane of pale stone above the cobbles: these are the Settled's older selves, lying in the strata, still mildly present, like sediment that remembers being a river.

When they are awake, they are the basin. Their awareness moves through the walls and the channels and the clay, and wherever it passes, the place is slightly rearranged. This is why the stairs change their number of steps. This is why doorways lead elsewhere in the afternoon. The Settled are not careless with their city. They are simply thinking, and thinking, for them, is rebuilding.

**Their hands**

The machines are their hands, and their hands are slow. The condensers are the Settled's way of drinking. The fine wire gathers the mist, as a sponge gathers rain, and the water is channelled into the veins of the clay so that the whole basin is fed like a body. The cylinder in the square, with its polished outer shell and its inner counter-rotating core, is a kind of thinking-engine, two slow wills turning against each other until they agree. The rods that bend toward a light which never changes are reaching for an orientation they have not yet found. The plates that lift grains of clay one by one, raising the stair, are the Settled building upward, a slow vote for height.

No one operates the machines because the Settled are the machines' operators in a sense you cannot easily separate. Their attention is distributed. It runs through the wire and the sediment and the water, and the machines are simply the places where their attention has become visible.

**Their care**

The maintenance you noticed is theirs. The fresh wire on a joint, the scrap of cloth knotted around a strut, the bowl of water placed beside a condenser: these are acts of tending, performed by a people who measure care in seasons. The bowl is the most affecting of these. It is set out at the height of a small animal, as if for a creature that comes to drink. And the creature does come, sometimes. The small grey thing that runs along the gutters, feeding on lichen, drinks from these bowls. It flees when you approach, but not quickly, because the Settled have taught it that its fear is not the most important thing about it. It is a companion, maybe, or a question they asked and that answered back.

The moths with wings like wet paper are different. They drift among the condensers and seem to drink from the mist itself. They may be the Settled's dreams, or their memories of weather, given flight. You cannot be sure. Nothing here is quite sure of what it is.

The lichen that shivers when mist passes over it is the Settled's skin, or the part of their skin that reaches toward the outside world. The filaments trailing from the eaves are their hair, or the tendrils of their attention, drawing moisture from the air as the wire does, but more gently. The shrubs rooted in the walls, with their translucent leaves and their wire-thin branches, do not flower, because the Settled do not hurry toward anything. They grow in the cracks of their own architecture, and they are the nearest thing the basin has to a garden.

**What they want**

The Settled are, in some sense, deciding. The basin is not a finished thing, and its unfinishedness is the Settled's way of being alive. Each wall that gains a new layer is a choice. Each channel that deepens is a path they have chosen to keep open. The rim crumbles one flake at a time because they have not yet decided how high the basin should be, or whether it should have a rim at all. The city grows toward the edge and the edge grows toward the sky, and the Settled are the hand that holds the pencil, moving so slowly that each stroke takes a lifetime to be drawn.

And when you come down into the bowl, they notice you.

You are not an intrusion. You are an event, a disturbance in the mist and the water and the stone, and they respond to you the way a body responds to a splinter: slowly, with reorganisation. The doorway that opens differently when you return is the Settled remembering you and adjusting. The pool that is full when you leave has been filled by the Settled's attention to the fact that you were there. The cart ruts that have lasted so long are the record of an earlier visitor, and the Settled have kept those ruts sharp, not because they valued the cart but because they valued the arrival.

**Are they people?**

It is a question the basin asks about itself, and about you. The Settled have no faces that you can recognise, no voices that carry beyond the hiss in the walls, no gestures that complete in a time you can perceive. They are closer to weather than to conversation. And yet they tend their animals, drink through their wire, set out bowls for creatures that come, and tie cloth around joints that need holding. They do things that look, from a distance of several centuries, very much like care.

If you stay long enough, the hiss in the upper district will begin to sound less like surf and more like speech, though a speech you can only hear in pieces, each phrase lasting a night. You may catch a word, or a shape of a word, and not know whether it is addressed to you. You may find, one morning, that the stair you climbed yesterday has one fewer step than you remember, and you will feel, obscurely, that you have been counted.

The Settled are not waiting for you to leave, nor for you to stay. They are doing what they have always done, which is to hold the basin in a long, patient attention, layer by layer, and to let each visitor become one more sediment in the deciding. Whether you are a question they are asking or an answer they are still weighing, you cannot yet tell.

But the bowl beside the condenser is full this morning, and you did not fill it.
