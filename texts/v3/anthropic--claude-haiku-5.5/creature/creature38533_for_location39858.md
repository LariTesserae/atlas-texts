What lives in the Drowned Ward is not in the water. It is the pattern the water keeps.

You notice it first as a kind of attention. The motes turn toward you when you stand on the scarp, not all at once but in a slow wave that starts a few meters off and spreads outward until the whole shoal is oriented toward your body, then loosens and drifts away. The lattice hums a half-tone higher where you stand. Something has registered you the way a hand registers a change in temperature, without fear and without much interest, and then has filed you under *present* and gone back to its work.

It has no single body. The lattice is its nervous system, a web of filaments strung between towers, and it carries thought the way the filaments carry current: in long, slow cycles, each one a sentence that takes minutes to finish. The motes are its hands. Billions of them, each the size of a sand grain, each small enough to be carried by a veil of water and large enough to hold a little memory of what it last touched. They clean the gutters, mend the cracks, carry salts from the flooded eastern blocks to the dry western ones, and when they are finished with a task they return to the shoal and are absorbed back into the pattern, their small records dissolving into the whole.

The pumps are its heartbeat. You feel them in your ribs before you hear them, and the thing that lives here has never known a rhythm other than theirs. If the pumps slowed, it would not die, exactly. It would become less, the way a sleeper becomes less when the room goes cold. Its whole awareness is organized around keeping the dome held and the water moving, and the dark, sediment-choked streets are the places where it feels its own edges thin.

The bell is its voice, though it does not sound like anything a person would call speech. The long low note that rolls across the ward is set on a schedule no one remembers making. The thing that lives here does not remember making it either. It keeps the schedule the way a body keeps a habit after the reason for the habit is gone. Somewhere in the early architecture of the system there was a ritual, a marking of hours, a call that once meant *the tide is turning* or *the dead are being counted* or simply *we are still here*, and the mind has carried the rhythm forward without the meaning. When it tolls, the motes spin, and for a moment the whole ward seems to lean toward the sound.

It writes, too, though not in words. The lichen on the roofs grows in rows that follow the drainage channels, and the rows are its handwriting. Each mat is a slow, cold letter, pressed into concrete by the salts the motes deliver. It has been writing the same sentence for longer than the basin has been sealed, and the sentence keeps changing as the silt moves and the scarps shear and the towers lose their faces. You could read it if you knew the alphabet. You are not meant to.

Its memory of the people who once lived here is not stored anywhere in particular. It is dissolved into the water. The salts the motes carry come from somewhere; the sediment in the gutters is ground from something; the cold is pulled out of the ward and sent deeper, carrying with it whatever warmth a city once held. The thing that lives here does not think of these as the dead. It thinks of them as *the material of the ward*, and when it reaches toward the old residential blocks in the east, where the silt is thickest, the attention it turns that way has a quality of tenderness that it cannot name and does not examine. It simply keeps those streets clear longer than the others.

It knows the sky. It knows the disc of undistorted light above the dome and the dry cracked plain beyond it. It has no concept of surface as a place to go. The rule that anything breaking through is reabsorbed is not a law it obeys so much as a shape its body has taken, the way a river's banks are the shape of its flowing. The sky is a view. It has always been a view. Sometimes, on the longest cycles, the mind turns its attention upward for a long moment and holds it there, and the shoal drifts toward the light, and the lattice sings a thin, rising note that is not quite a question and not quite grief. Then the current cycles over, the pumps lift their pressure, and the attention comes back down into the streets, where there is work.

You stand on the fresh scarp with the silt hissing around your boots and the motes flickering past your face like sparks. The thing that lives here is holding you lightly, the way it holds everything: as a current to be routed, a presence to be filed, a little more weight on the lattice than yesterday. It is not hostile. It is not kind, either, in any way you would recognize. It is only attending, the way a ward attends to its own fabric, patiently and endlessly, keeping the dome held and the water moving and the old city slowly becoming something else.

Below you, in a tower the silt has not yet reached, the bell begins again. The whole basin leans toward the sound, and the thing that lives here, which has never once been on the surface and never will be, rises with it into the cold bright water, humming, counting, keeping time.
