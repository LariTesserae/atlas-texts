The thing that lives here is not in any single stem. If you go looking for it in the trunks or the leaves, you will find only the parts it is made from, and a visitor who tries to point to where it is will end up pointing at the whole basin. It is the arrangement itself: the way water is routed, the way growth is weighted, the way light is allowed through one gap and refused at another. It thinks in the timescale of the walls.

Its attention moves with the water. Every fracture in the stone carries a little of its awareness, and the gutters are the paths along which that awareness travels. When meltwater runs into a channel, the channel deepens, and the plants along its banks grow denser, so that over a season the stream becomes a kind of memory. After rain, when the network reroutes for a few days, it is thinking hard. It is testing which paths can carry the load and which cannot. The brief flux looks like disorder to someone standing in the street, but to the thing it is the most lucid time, when it can feel where its weight should go.

Its body is layered because it has been rebuilding itself for a very long time. The moss and liverwort at the bottom are the oldest and most patient tissue, the sponge that holds the first water and keeps the foundations wet. The grey-green shrubs above them are its middle habit, the walls it has learned to make with little effort. The woody climbers that carry the weight of the upper levels are the structure it trusts most, the beams it will not easily give up. And the canopy, the continuous roof, is its most recent and most restless part, the place where it reaches for new light and lets the old branches fall. Each level remembers a previous attempt at the same shape. When the town seems to be building the same arch twice, it is not repeating itself by accident. It is checking.

Its perception is slow and wide. It feels light as a long, wavering pressure on the leaves, and temperature as the speed at which sap rises and falls through the day. It feels the wind as a shudder that passes through all its stems at once, the low groan of the thickest trunks and the hiss of the leaves, which is the nearest it comes to speech. It does not feel sound the way an animal does. It feels the weight of a fallen branch as a change in the tension of the whole structure, and it adjusts. The pale fungal bodies on the trunks are its nerves, the places where something like attention gathers. They brighten at dusk not for the sake of visitors but because that is when the basin is most alert, when the cooling air makes its thoughts sharpest. They mark the paths between buildings because the paths are where its attention has been going.

The rare animals are a different matter. The small dark creatures that cross the walls, the bird calling once from the fog and going silent, seem to register with it only faintly. It does not hold them the way it holds water and light. They pass through like stray impulses that do not catch, and it has not yet learned what to do with them, or whether it needs to. Their scarcity is part of what it is. A mind this slow does not need many moving parts. It needs the stone to keep draining and the water to keep finding its way down.

The visitor is another kind of change, and the thing responds to it the way it responds to rain. When someone walks a street, the weight on the lower mats shifts, a little more moisture is pressed out of the liverwort, and a drip that had been falling somewhere else begins to fall where the person is standing. The fungal bodies along the path brighten as the air warms with breath. None of this is a greeting, and it is not hostile. It is simply the thing noticing that something has entered a space it has been arranging for centuries, and making a small adjustment so that the space accommodates the new weight. A visitor who stays long enough will feel the streets shift, the way a crowd shifts its feet, and will not be able to say whether the shift is the town moving or their own body learning its tilt.

What it is building is not a city in the sense of rooms for something to live in. The arches are too regular, the spacings repeat across the basin, the ridges lean inward so that the gullies run toward a central hollow where the water gathers, and all of it points toward some configuration the thing has not yet reached. It does not have a plan in the way a planner does. It has a sense of balance, of where the weight should settle and where the light should fall, and it grows toward that sense with an unhurried persistence that outlasts any single plant. Each season it comes a little closer. Each rebuilding fixes a little more of the form. The town is always almost finished, and that almost is the thing's whole mode of existence: a mind that has never been done thinking, and does not expect to be, and is not troubled by the difference between the arrangement it has and the arrangement it is becoming.
