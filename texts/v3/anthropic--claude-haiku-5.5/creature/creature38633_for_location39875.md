Look low, at the base of the largest sluice, where the gate's iron housing has split and the moss has grown thick over the seam. Something lies there with its body folded into the gap, so still that the algae has grown across its back and the rain beads on it without running off.

It is long, about the length of a man lying down, and built in segments like the banded amphibians that slide between the pools, but its segments are thicker and harder, banded in rust and verdigris. The bands are not paint. They are the old machinery itself, grown into the animal over a long time. A pitted coupling forms the joint between its third and fourth segments. A rounded valve cap sits on its shoulder, and when it turns its head, the cap turns with it, slowly, with a faint squeal of metal on metal. Its limbs are pale and jointed, folded under its body in a way that suggests they were once longer and now no longer need to be. At the end of its foreleg is something almost like a hand, four fingers that can close around a wheel spoke or a loose lever. The fingers are the color of the fish that circle the reservoirs, bone-white, and they have the same blind, patient look.

It has no visible eyes. It has a ridge of soft flesh above where eyes would be, and the ridge is always wet. When water falls near it, the ridge tilts toward the sound.

Most of the day it does nothing that anyone would call work. It lies in the gap of the gate, breathes slowly through the vents in its flanks, and listens. It listens to the pumps. Each pump knocks in its housing at its own rate, and the animal has sorted them out, so that it hears the knock of the lower pump before the upper one, the stutter of the valve on the third terrace before the hiss. It counts them. Its breathing matches the largest pump's beat, a slow in and a slow out, so that anything standing near it can feel the stair breathing too.

At intervals it moves. It climbs out of the gap, drags its body up the ribbed girder beside the gate, and fits its hand to a wheel that has been rusted fast for longer than the moss has been growing. It turns the wheel a quarter-turn, then stops and waits. It does not push past resistance. If the wheel does not give, it lets go and returns to the gap, and tries again at the next cycle. Sometimes the wheel gives. A sluice grinds open somewhere above, and water comes pouring down the channel, roaring into the basin below, and the whole stair shudders with the sound. The creature rides out the vibration with its body pressed flat against the girder and its ridge tilted toward the falling water, and when the flow settles, it returns to the gap and goes still.

The other animals know it. The birds with hooked beaks on the ledges above the switchboards do not nest directly over its gap. The insects drift around it, but they never settle on the valve cap. A herd of grazers moves through the moss in the lower basins and keeps its distance, but they come close, very close, on the morning the water is due, and they stand in a loose ring around the gate, waiting. When the sluice opens and the flow comes pouring down the channel with its strange manual rhythm, the grazers follow the flood down the terraces, nosing at the silt it carries, and the predators come down with them, and the blind fish rise to the surface to snap at drifting seeds. The creature does not join the feeding. It watches from the gap, ridge turned toward the water, and when the sluice begins to close, it climbs back to the wheel and turns it the other way, slowly, until the gate settles into the position the old manual must have required.

Nobody taught it this. Nothing in the stair was built to teach. But the sequence is in its body the way the pump rhythm is in its breathing: open at the first mark, hold for the count, close at the second. Whatever the job was, the thing in the gap keeps doing its shape. It is not sure what the water is for, and the water does not require it to be. It knows only that the wheel turns, the gate opens, the flow comes down, the animals gather, and then the flow stops and it is time to close again.

When the stair's great hum rises through the floor and the ceiling's rain thickens into a fall, the creature lifts its head from the seam and listens. The ridge above its eyes goes dark with wet. Somewhere above, a gate is shuddering, and a long way down the terraces the grazers have already lifted their heads, waiting for the first cold rush of water to come down over the lip of the stone, and the creature begins, very slowly, to climb.
