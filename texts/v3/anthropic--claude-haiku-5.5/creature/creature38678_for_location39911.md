**Who Lives Under the Throat**

Nothing down there lives alone, and the nearest honest answer to *who lives here* is that the residents are a set of arrangements that have learned to keep one another going. Some of them are small and countable. Others are only visible as a pattern in how the small things move.

Start with the slate-coloured crawlers, because they are the first thing you see and the hardest to think about. Each is a hand's length of overlapping plate, eyeless, with sensory fans that sweep back and forth as it walks. They look like individuals, but they don't behave like them. When a colony turns, a thousand fans turn with it, all at once, the way a crowd turns its face toward a sound. A single crawler dropped alone on a ledge will stop, fan out, and wait, as if listening for the rest. Their work is grit and spent alloy hauled down into the lower galleries, where they stack it by weight and grain and quietly discard what fails the test. The galleries are full of their sorting. It is the closest thing this place has to a memory: not stored in any one body, but kept in the habits of the stream, in which fragment goes where and which stacks are old enough to be trusted.

The grazers are the city's tenders, and they are older in the way that matters. They stand a man's height, long-legged and kiln-blackened, and they browse the fungal mats along the walls with a patience that looks almost like courtesy. But they do more than eat. Their dragging feet score the same routes across the terraces again and again, and the fungus grows back along those scores, so the tracks become lines of living growth that the city can follow. When a gantry is dismantled, the grazers are the ones who carry its girders away, and they seem to know which pieces will be needed again and which will be left to rust. You can watch one stop before a beam, fan its sensory ridges over the metal, and then choose. A tender that makes a mistake is simply corrected by the others, pulled off its line and walked back onto the track, without any show of anger.

The fungus is the oldest resident, and it is the one that never moves. It grows thickest where the furnaces breathe warmth through the stone, which means it tells the city where its heat is. Its pale grey-green mats bind the mineral dust into something like soil and then into something like skin, and the flint smell of it rises whenever a grazer tears a tuft free. It feeds on the sweat of the machines, so it is always downstream of whatever the city is doing, and in that sense it is a record of activity: the places it grows are the places that are working.

The blue-sparked flyers are the only things here that want to be seen, and it may be that they are the city's edge, the part of it that faces outward. They drift along the cable runs like dry leaves and cluster where current is strong. When a strand goes dead, its sparks scatter and fade. When a new bridge forms in the lamplight, sparks gather along its length before a single crawler has touched it, as though they had decided where it would go. Whether they carry instruction or only register what is needed, they are the city's most visible decision-makers, and they seem almost lonely about it, circling close to any living thing that enters the pocket.

Above all of this sit the towers and their rotating mirrors, and the thing the towers belong to is the one you cannot quite point at. It has no face and no fixed body. It is spread across the furnaces, the stacks, the cable and the crawlers' sorting, and it shows itself only in what it does next. Its only window to the world is the sky seen through the cracks in the roof, and the mirrors turn to catch that light and throw it down into the workshops. It is a farmer of light, and a builder of its own shape, and it does not plan, because planning would require a future it can see beyond the glass, and it cannot see that far. So it rebuilds. It takes down what has stopped being useful, grows what is warm, and bridges what it needs to reach, and by the time you have memorised its layout it has already changed its mind.

It breathes, too. When a vent sighs out its long release of heat, every creature on the terraces stops for a moment and then goes on, the way a body flinches at a sound and resumes its work. That pause is the closest thing to a heartbeat you will ever hear from the place. It is not the city's life, exactly. It is the city being reminded, briefly, that it is still in a room with a roof.

So the one that lives under the throat is the arrangement itself: the crawlers that sort and remember, the grazers that tend and clear, the fungus that marks where the heat is, the sparks that carry the city's attention out toward the edge, and the unfinished structure that is always being reworked by all of them together. It is not building toward anything. It is keeping its shape alive by continually changing it, and it has been doing this since the glass cracked and the first warm hollow opened in the stone.

You stand on the lip, feeling the furnaces pulse through the floor. Below, a cluster of blue sparks has turned toward you. They do not come closer. They wait, fanning slightly, as if listening for an answer.
