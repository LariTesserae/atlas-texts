The builders are known first by their walls. You meet them as a pale sheen on a bank of clay, a fresh tier of tower still weeping salt, a gallery mouth glazed with resin, and only later as bodies. A single worker is about the length of a finger joint, soft-bodied under a thin, glossy sheath the color of wet bone, with a dark head and mandibles that clip salt crust into pellets. They move in files down the lanes, and the lanes are polished by the passage of their bodies, so the ground gleams where they have walked for generations.

They do not see much. They read the city by touch, by the humidity gradient that climbs toward the warm core, and by the sharp resinous smell that marks which walls are sound and which are cracking. Each worker carries something: clay in its jaws, water in its crop, a sealant gland swollen with secretion. Between workers, food and fluid pass mouth to mouth, so that no single body keeps anything for long. The whole population shares one gut, one wet interior, and a few hundred paces of lane can carry a drop of dew from the morning leaves to a nursery roof by noon.

The castes are visible in how they work. Foragers go out before dawn, when the broad leaves of the ground mats are heavy with dew. They climb the stems, strip the wet surfaces with their mouthparts, and come back in lines that never quite break, drinking the sky on the way home. Diggers cut the new lanes, undercutting the banks at the edges of the flash-flood channels, sometimes working in the raw grey and ochre layers that have not yet hardened. Mixers knead clay with salt from the brine rim and resin from their own glands, and the mixture sets in the open air into the pale, dense material of the walls. The walls are never simply finished. They are patched, extended, undercut, and resealed on a rhythm too slow for any one worker to perceive and too constant for the city to ever stop.

Near the warm core, in sealed chambers that grow more swollen as they go deeper, live the few that do not forage or dig. They are the reason the colony keeps any shape at all. Their bodies are vast and pale, and they stay inside the walls while the city does its work around them. The city's form seems to follow their dispositions. When they move, new galleries open. When they are quiet, the old terraces sink and fuse.

The roofs are where the builders are most unlike the rest of the basin. They are the colony's gardens, sun-struck and carpeted in seedlings that the tenders have planted into the warm crust. The tenders cut back vines that threaten to split the walls, and they leave the roots that bind the sediment, because the roots hold the mounds as much as the mounds hold the roots. Where a fine resin seeps from a crack into the soil, something green takes hold and spreads, and the builders follow it the way a gardener follows the sun. The relationship is old enough that it is hard to say which side is cultivating which. The lush green over the cities is partly the colony's crop, and the colony is partly the green's shelter.

The herds keep close to the mounds, drawn by the shade of the towers and the shelter from the predators that range the open flats. Their hooves compact the lanes, and their dung, dried and broken, goes into the clay mix. The builders take what the herds leave. The predators are the colony's standing problem. They have learned the gallery walls and the vent shafts, and they slip in where air moves. The builders answer each intrusion by sealing one passage and opening another, rerouting the slow cycles of air through the galleries, and the city's shape is partly the record of these arguments with the things that hunt at its edges.

At night, the mounds breathe. Vents draw cooler air down through the terraces, the walls shed the day's heat in long slow sighs, and the lanes hum with the last returning foragers. The humidity hangs thick over the towers, and the insects gather above them in a faint gold haze. What you hear is close and muffled: drops ticking from leaves, the soft stir of bodies in the galleries, a rustle that never resolves into any single voice, and under it all the low, continuous work of building.

Nothing about this colony is finished. Its towers are still climbing, the oldest plateaus are still sinking into one another, the new spires are still damp and pale, and the lanes are still wearing deeper into the ground. The builders are not a people who have arrived somewhere. They are a slow mind made of many bodies, thinking in clay and resin and root, and the thinking has never stopped and is still becoming what it is, the same as the basin around it.
