**What Lives in the Hollow**

They are not one kind of creature. They are many, and they have arranged themselves by trade, the way a workshop sorts its hands. What they share is the plating: a dark, glossy armor that is part chitin and part mineral, saturated with sulfur and iron until it is nearly as hard as the rock it works. Their bodies are segmented, each ring a separate hinge, and when a creature moves you can count the segments by the small clicks of their joints. Most are the color of wet slate, with seams of rust-orange and, where the sulfur has crept into the shell, a yellow bloom along the edges, like lichen that has learned to bite.

The largest are the Breakers. They are squat and broad, the length of a man's forearm, with a domed carapace shaped like a fist. Their front pair of limbs ends in a heavy, fused plate, and they raise it and bring it down onto the rock with a force that cracks the stone. They come in clusters of thirty or forty, pressed close, each one striking in its own time. The percussion of the hollow is theirs. Individually the blows are uneven, but they fall into a slow, shifting rhythm: one cluster builds to a crescendo, the sound climbs, then the cluster exhausts itself and another takes over, and the whole floor seems to breathe in long waves. A Breaker does not seem to aim. It strikes where the stone is already weak, where a fracture has begun, and the fracture opens under the blow. It is working with the rock's own preferences, the way a sculptor reads grain.

Beside them move the Sorters, long and narrow, with ridged limbs that interlock like the teeth of two gears. When they walk, their legs engage and disengage with a ratcheting click, and the sound repeats for hours, a mechanical tick that seems to come from nowhere until you locate it in the dark. The Sorters pick through the broken fragments. They lift each piece, turn it with their jaws, and pass it along the line, larger stones to one side and fine glass to the other. Some of the fragments they discard are still warm enough to glow faintly in their grip. They are not gentle. They shove, drag, and wedge, and over long spans the scree has been stratified by their hands, bands of glass and bands of shale running in curves across the floor like the marks of a potter's thumb.

Along the vents live the Bellows. These are the stillest creatures in the hollow, rounded and heavy, lying with their bodies pressed against the mouths of the cracks. Each one has a chambered abdomen, an internal arrangement of sacs and valves, and they pump gas and heat through it, inhaling sulfur-thick air from the deep and exhaling it in measured puffs. The puffs match the roar of the vents. When you watch a row of Bellows along a fissure, the exhalations travel down the line like a wave passing through a crowd, each one answering the next. The roar of the hollow is not only the earth. It is also the lungs of these animals, working in step.

On the walls are the Grinders, flattened and wide, with broad undersides that cling to ledges and fissures. They drag themselves across the stone with a sawing motion, and their undersides are rough with fine, hard bristles that score the rock as they move. Over decades they have cut terraces into the cliffs. Where the wall has been ground smooth, a glassy film is left behind, and the film cracks in the heat and curls, falling in flakes to the floor, where the Sorters collect it.

Then there are the Layers, the least visible and perhaps the most important. They are thin and flat, almost pressed to the stone, and they move very slowly. Their undersides secrete a crust of mineral, a sheet of silica and salt that hardens as it cools. Where a Layer has passed, the ground is sealed, a new skin laid over the fractures. They do not seem to care whether the crust is useful. They lay it because that is what their bodies do, and over centuries their trails have built the floor terrace by terrace, one thin film at a time.

**How they live**

The creatures do not seem to have individual lives in the way we would mean it. Their rhythms are closer to tides. They gather at the vents, work through their cycles, and disperse over seasons. You can see it in the population: some months the floor is crowded to the walls, and some years the hollow thins and the scree rings with only a few scattered clicks. Then the crowds return. Nothing here seems to have a beginning or an end. The cycle is simply continuous.

They molt. When a creature outgrows its shell, it retreats into a warm cavity near a vent, where the rock is soft and the sulfur is thick, and it sheds its armor in a single long pull. The new shell comes out pale and soft, almost white, and it hardens over days as the minerals bind into it. For a time the molted creature is vulnerable, and the others leave it alone. Its old shell remains, a hollow husk, and the Breakers come to it. They break the husk into fragments, and the fragments become part of the scree. The shells of the dead and the shed shells of the living go to the same place.

They eat the minerals released by the vents. Sulfur crystals, salt fans, the thin glass that forms at the edges of cooling flows. These are not foods in any sense we would recognize, but the creatures take them in, break them down through their own chambers, and excrete them as crust or as fine grey dust that sifts down through the seams. The hollow is always giving the creatures something, and the creatures always give something back.

They reproduce in the warm crust. A female, if that is the word, lays eggs in a pocket of soft mineral near a vent. The eggs are small and glossy, and they hatch in the heat over a long season. The young are soft and pale, nearly transparent, and they stay buried until their shells begin to harden. When they emerge, they are immediately part of the crowd, and no one seems to watch over them. They learn the rhythms by being in the rhythms.

**What they are doing**

The creatures do not seem to know they are building anything. But the hollow is changing, and their changes accumulate. The Layers seal the floor. The Grinders cut terraces into the walls. The Breakers break the plates into fragments, and the Sorters arrange the fragments into bands, and the Bellows keep the heat moving, and the heat keeps the rock soft enough for the Breakers to work. Each animal does its part, and the parts fit together into something that none of them can see. A terrace here, a band of glass there, a seam sealed by crust. The place is being made by the making itself.

Their rhythm is the most striking thing about them. Individually they are simple, repetitive, almost mechanical. But across the hollow, the rhythms overlap. The Breakers' percussion, the Sorters' ticking, the Bellows' exhalations, and the Grinders' sawing combine into a single sound that rises and falls like a tide. It is the pulse of the hollow, and it has no conductor. The animals do not listen to one another, yet they keep time, the way the stones of a dry-stone wall keep their shape without mortar.

When you stand among them, the floor shifts under your feet, the walls ring, the air trembles, and the roar of the vents shakes your teeth. You want to say the place is alive, or that it is a machine, or that it is a workshop. But the animals are not separate from the place, and the place is not separate from them. They are its hands, its lungs, and its memory. Their dead become its floor. Their rhythm becomes its pulse.

The scree rings when you step on it. Each fragment is a piece of a shell, a piece of a plate, a piece of something that once moved and breathed in this heat. The sound is faint and everywhere, a thin chime under the roar, and it is the sound of the hollow remembering what it is made of.
