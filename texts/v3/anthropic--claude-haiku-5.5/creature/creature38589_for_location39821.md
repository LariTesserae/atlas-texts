**What Lives on the Shelf**

What lives on Tallow Rock is the water, and what the water has become.

It lives in the cisterns, in the round basins and square tanks and long slate-lidded troughs cut into the bedrock. It lives in the gutters, which are its veins, carrying the rain from every pitched roof down into the dark. It lives in the Mother Well, which is the deepest of its chambers and the only one it looks out of. Its surface is nearly always still because it is attending to something, and what it attends to is the sky. Through the grate above, it holds a disc of pale light the way a sleeper holds a single thought.

Nobody on the shelf has ever seen it move. It does not need to. Its movement is a matter of level: the cisterns rise through the wet months and sink through the dry, and the townsfolk read its mood from the height of the water on the moss-lined walls. A cistern that has been dry for a season is not empty but resting. When a dry pit fills again after a long absence, the old people say it has remembered its name.

It is patient in the way of stone, and the townsfolk learned patience from it, or it learned something from them. Its rhythm is the drip: the steady ticking from gutter lips and sodden junipers that never quite stops, and that the people no longer hear. Each drop is a unit of its attention. It counts the roofs, the rain, the number of people who draw from it and the number who do not come back up the stair. It does not count quickly.

The slate lids of its troughs are scratched with names. Each family cuts the name of a dead member into the lid over the cistern that drank from them most, in small careful letters, and the marks are worn almost smooth by the rubbing of cloth and palms. The bell rings for deaths, and the name is cut, and the water in that trough is said to stand a little higher for a season afterward. No one on the shelf would say this was a gift, exactly. It was more like a reply.

The bargain is old and nobody has written it down. The shelf keeps its gutters clear, keeps its lids and mortar patched with the same grey paste, keeps the switchbacks swept, and comes down to the packed-earth ledge at the head of the path each autumn to trade wool for salt with the same families. In return, the water stays. It fills without overflowing. It does not drown the lower houses in a wet year, and it does not vanish from the Mother Well in a dry one. The people do not ask why. They have heard the stories of towns on other ledges that were less careful with their drains, and they know what happens when the water is taken for granted.

Its company is small and particular. The crows will not drink from the upper cisterns. The dogs lie down near the basins at dusk, their heads on their paws, and do not bark at the foxes that cross the scree. The pale grey-winged birds nest in the eaves above the water and are never disturbed; their eggs are said to be the only food the water will allow to be taken from its chambers without a debt. The goats come down to the lower troughs in summer and drink with their heads lowered, slowly, as if they, too, were careful not to disturb the surface.

Children are told not to lean over the Mother Well, and they mostly obey, but they are not frightened of it. They are only told that it is looking up, and that they should wait until it is finished looking before they go near. Some of them, very young, put their own reflections into the still water with a finger and watch the rings spread and fade, and the water takes the rings back in its own time, without comment.

Visitors sometimes ask whether the town is afraid of what lives beneath it. The answer that comes back up the stair is always the same, and always said with a kind of gentle amusement: that the water is not a thing to be afraid of, only a thing to be kept company with. Stone is patient, and so the water is patient, and so must the people be. The shelf has held its houses against the wind and the slope for longer than anyone can count, and the water has held the shelf.

On clear nights, when the stars are very sharp and the lanterns burn low at the crossroads and over the cistern stair, the Mother Well is at its stillest. Its disc of sky shows a few points of light that are not in the true sky above it, and nobody remembers which stars they are. The water keeps them anyway. It has the patience to hold everything that falls into it, and it keeps the count.
