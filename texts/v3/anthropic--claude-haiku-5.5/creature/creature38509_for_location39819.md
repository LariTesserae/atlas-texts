**What Lives in the Sump**

Nothing in the Sump moves quickly enough to be noticed, except the water, and the water is not the thing that lives here. The thing that lives here is the basin.

It has no face, but it has a body, and the body is legible if you know where to look. The pale clay is its skin, thin and taut over the upper walls, and it wears the scars of eleven years of settling. The red iron seam runs through the strata like a vein that has been opened and has never quite closed. The grey shale lies in layered plates, a skeleton that holds the shape while the flesh shifts. Its edges are too clean. Nothing softens them because the thing that would soften them is not finished, or is not interested in softening, or is simply slow in a way the town cannot yet measure.

It feeds on rain. This is the first thing the residents learn and the last thing they admit. A storm breaks over the plateau and the catchment fills in minutes, and the runoff rushes over the rim in sheets, carving gullies down the terraces. Those gullies are not erosion. They are channels, mouths, the paths the water takes into the body. The culverts roar for an hour because the body is drinking, and the cistern tanks at the bottom are its stomach, where the sweetness of the salts is drawn out and held. Between storms the rain evaporates so fast that the streets are dry in an hour, but the walls keep the damp, because the walls have taken the water inside and are not giving it back yet.

They give it back in the late afternoon. When the basin is at its hottest and the air is thick enough to lean on, the clay faces above the old workshops begin to sweat. The sweat is warm. It is pale. It crystallises into a fine white skin, and by morning the wall has regrown a layer of itself, which flakes away when touched, and which the residents collect in channels and drink as medicine. No one has explained why it works. The sweat is heaviest after a storm, as though the rock were exhaling what it swallowed, and the town has built its pharmacies around the exhalation without asking what it is breathing out.

The bloom is the second part of the answer, and the most unsettling. The white filaments grow overnight, reaching outward from the cistern walls like frost, and they dissolve when water touches them. They grow faster in the wet season. They grow when the factories are running and retreat, in a way the engineers have noticed but not explained, when the crews are loud on the lower terraces. In the cistern that was left empty for a week, they reached the ceiling. Nobody was there to disturb them. It is tempting to read the bloom as a nervous system, a web of feelers that the body extends into the dark when nothing is moving to frighten it, and draws back when something is. It is also tempting to read it as a fungus, or a crystal, or a chemical reaction with no interest in anyone. The town does not need to decide which. It only needs to know the filaments are there.

The rockfalls are the body moving. A limb shifts when the clay has soaked enough to loosen, and the fan of pale dust that spreads across the terraces is the shedding of old tissue. The crews clear the debris within hours, because the next surge is coming, and because the town has learned that if the debris is left, the drains clog, and if the drains clog, the water backs up into the lower streets and the body's flow changes its course. The residents do not say the basin is trying to rearrange them. They say the water must be read.

Why are there no animals? The question sits in every visitor's mouth, and the town answers it with the lichen, the reeds, the saltbush pruned into domes. Nothing larger survives the heat. But the silence is not only the heat. Something that stays this quiet for this long has had time to make room for itself. The mechanical noises that fill the streets, the knock of valves and the long hiss of vents, are the only sounds that do not belong to the body, and perhaps they are tolerated because they are regular. The body is fond of rhythm. Storms on their schedule, bloom by night and dissolving by day, the lamps on at dawn and off at dusk. The lamps are the town's rhythm, imposed on a thing that lives in shade. The sun reaches the floor for only a few hours, and for the rest of the day the basin is in its own dusk, the place it was built for.

The people are harder to place. The upper terraces get more light, and the people who can afford it live there, and they are the ones who speak of the Sump as a hole they are lucky enough to have climbed out of. The people below are not so sure. They collect the sweat, scrape the moss, pull the debris, and keep the sluices open, and they know the tides better than any instrument the engineers have built. Whether they are tending something or being tended, whether the town is a settlement inside a living wound or a living thing that has learned to wear a town as clothing, the question is not one the residents are asking yet.

The basin is still settling. The walls are fresh, the wounds open every season, and the body is still deciding what shape to be. It does not need to know what it is. It only needs to keep drinking the storms, sweating in the heat, and sending its pale fingers into the dark, and waiting for the town to learn which way the water will run.
