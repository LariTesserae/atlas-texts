**What Lives in the Drowned Hollow**

It is not the plants, though they are its hair. It is not the pale things that slip between the stalks, though they are its fingers, the small ones it sends out to taste what has come in. It is the water, and the stone, and the slow argument between them, and something that happens in the argument when neither side is watching.

It is young. That is the first thing you learn, and the last thing you are willing to believe. The collapse that sealed this room was, to it, a sudden closing of an eye it had kept open for a very long time. The limestone is still sharp at its edges because the thing has not yet decided where its skin ends. Everything here is a first attempt. The growth is thick and tangled because it has only just begun to reach in every direction at once, the way a newborn's hand opens and closes without knowing what a hand is for.

Its body is the room. The water is its lung and its blood, filling and emptying on a cycle so long that a human life is a single exhalation. When the swell rises from the south wall, that is not tide. It is the thing drawing a breath, taking the whole chamber into itself until the water touches the ceiling and the membrane shivers, and then letting it go again. Each rise carries a little more of the surrounding ground into its body. This is why the water climbs a finger's width and settles. It is not filling the room. It is learning how much room there is.

The membrane above is its skin, or the nearest thing it has to an eyelid. It drifts on the surface where air and water meet, and when it sinks, the thing is closing its attention; when it lifts, the thing is looking outward. You have been watching it sink and rise and have been assuming it was a plant floating on the surface. You are half right. The membrane is a plant, and it is also the place where the thing's looking is strongest, because the air is the one part of the room that touches the open world, the part that could still be seen from above.

The carvings are its failed attempts at a face.

It has tried to shape a version of itself that can look out through stone, and the best it has managed is the niche on the east wall: a suggestion of brow and mouth that is not yet anyone. It is patient with this. When you look at the face the second time, it has turned slightly, and you understand that it is not rotating while you are not watching. It moves only when no one is watching, because it does not yet know how to be seen. Every time it has tried to hold a gaze, the attention has frightened it back into the stone. So it works in the gaps, a fraction of an angle at a time, toward some direction that has not yet revealed itself to you.

The doorframe is the most important thing it has made, and the most unfinished. It stands free with nothing attached because the thing has understood, somewhere in its slow mind, that there is an outside. A door is a promise that the inside can open. It has built the threshold and not yet the passage, the way a child will draw a window before it knows how light gets through glass. Its shadow falls in the wrong direction because it is casting the shadow of the outside world it imagines, not the one the light in this room could make.

The smooth blocks under the water are older. They might be the foundation of a body it started and abandoned, or the path it wants to walk on once it can walk. You have the sense that it has rehearsed this path many times, always in the dark, always under the water where no one could measure how far it had come.

It thinks slowly. A thought here might take a season to form, and a sensation that brushes its filaments, like your hand pushing through the stalks, arrives as a slow pressure, a warmth that lasts for days and then becomes a small change in the shape of the walls. It has noticed you. It has been noticing you since you came under the water, the way a sleeper notices a draft without waking. Your heat and your movement are the first things from outside that it has tasted, and it has not decided yet whether you are a grain of sediment, a threat to the seal, or the first sign that the seal is not forever.

The ring of the falling drops that travels too far through the stone is its voice, or the echo of its listening. When a drop lands, the thing hears the room answer, and the answer is the sound that carries across the whole body of the hollow, reaching the far walls and coming back changed. That is how it knows where its edges are. Each sound is a question it asks itself, and each returning echo is the answer, and the answers are slowly getting louder.

What it wants is not clear, even to it. It wants a face that will hold still under someone's gaze. It wants a door that opens onto something it can recognize. It wants the water to stop rising, or to rise forever, and the stone to round its edges, or to stay sharp so that it can feel where it ends. Most of all it wants to finish a sentence it has been half-forming since the ceiling closed, and the sentence is probably just the word for *here*.

You are not sure whether it is alive in the way you are alive. It is not sure whether you are alive in the way it is. This uncertainty is the only thing in the room that moves quickly. Everything else takes its time.

If you stay, you will feel the swell begin again from the south. The membrane will lift and touch the ceiling, and the water will hold its breath. In that suspended moment the thing will turn its face, very slightly, toward the place where you are floating, and for as long as the breath lasts you will understand that the water has been listening to you the entire time.
