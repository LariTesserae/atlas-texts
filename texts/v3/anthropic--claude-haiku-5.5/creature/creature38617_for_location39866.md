**Who Keeps the Throat of Hollin Dell**

The first resident you notice is the dipper. It stands on a stone at the edge of the spray, a slate-dark bird with a white bib, and it bobs. Up and down, up and down, as if it were keeping time with the water. It does not seem to hear the roar. Or it hears it so completely that the roar has become its silence. Its nest is tucked somewhere behind the falling column, in a crack the spray never quite reaches, and it goes in and out through the water as if the water were a curtain it has known all its life. Dippers walk along the bottom of streams. This one walks along the bottom of a waterfall, and it has never once looked surprised.

Under the moss near the fissure lives the pale one. It is a salamander, or a newt, or something the cave water has shaped into a shape of its own: translucent, nearly without pigment, its gills feathered like frayed silk. It has no eyes to speak of, only two dark places where eyes once were, and it reads the water through its skin. It has been here longer than the stone curbs, longer than the upright slabs with their pecked marks. The people who cut those grooves may have dropped offerings into the fissure, or may have feared it, or may never have known the pale one existed at all. It does not know them either. Its world is cold, slow, and fed by the seep from the bedrock, and in that world a thousand years is a single season.

The spiders are older than the marks and younger than the stone. They hang their sheets in the mist between the ferns and the rim, and every morning the webs are beaded with fresh water, each thread a string of small lamps. The spiders are patient builders who rebuild what the drip tears down. They are mostly invisible until the light catches them, and then the whole wall is revealed as a lace, drawn across the dim green like a veil someone has forgotten to lift.

Now and then, toward dusk, the large shape comes down the western slope. You see it first as a movement between the fronds, a flicker of russet or grey, and then, if you hold still, as a deer. It is a red deer, perhaps, or a roe, old enough that one tine of its antler has broken and healed crooked. It moves along the rim with the slow deliberation of an animal that knows the ground's soft places, and it stops at the spring-lip, where the water comes out of the rock and is cold enough to ache. It drinks there. It lifts its head, water running from its muzzle, and it looks not at you but across the bowl, toward the fallen slabs and the broken trough, the way an animal regards a place it passes through by habit rather than by choice. The old path is still in its legs, though the path itself has slumped into the sinkhole. Then it turns and climbs back into the trees, and the ferns close behind it, and the bowl is empty again.

The ferns and mosses are tenants too, and the most numerous. The hart's-tongue hangs from the cracks like wet green ribbon. The shield ferns unfurl in slow spirals. The liverwort, pale as lace, spreads by the hand's width each year, and nothing about it hurries. The moss holds water the way a sponge holds breath, and the light that seems to rise from the ground is, in a sense, the moss's own. These are the true householders of Hollin Dell. They have claimed every surface the water has not, and they will go on claiming them long after the dipper, the newt, and the deer are gone.

Then there is the water itself, which is the oldest resident and the only one that owns the place outright. It was here before the fissure opened. It came down through the dark bedrock from some source no one has mapped, and it will keep coming, carving a hair's breadth deeper each decade, depositing its pale crusts on the rock, feeding the moss and the newt and the dipper's nest and the spiders' lamps. It does not care who drinks from it. It falls into the throat in one white column and the sound rolls up the walls and back, and that sound is the voice of the place, the way a house speaks through its settling timbers.

If you stay long enough, you begin to see that the place is not empty of inhabitants. It is full of them, each living in its own slow time, none of them expecting you. The dipper bobs. The newt reads the cold. The spiders mend their veils. The deer drinks and looks away. The moss creeps outward. The water falls. You are the only thing here in a hurry, and even you, listening to the roar until it becomes a kind of quiet, begin to slow down.
