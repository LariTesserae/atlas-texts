**What Lives on the Plateau**

The first thing you find is a shape under the salt. You kick at a ridge and the crust gives a little, not like rock but like a thick skin over something that breathes. Brush the dust away and you see a curved plate, banded in dull ochre and bone-white, and beneath it a joint folded tight against a body that has been resting for a very long time. The bands on the plate are growth rings. You can count them. Some of them match the strata of the salt that has built up around the animal, layer for layer, so that the creature and the ground have been keeping the same record.

These are the walkers, though you will not learn that from anyone, because there is no one to ask. They are slow in the way a glacier is slow, or a sentence that takes a century to finish. They rest by folding their long legs under them and sinking, and the salt rises slowly around them, and eventually a walker that rests long enough becomes a low ridge in the crust. Many of the parallel ridges across the plateau are not wind-carved at all. They are animals that lay down facing the same wind and never got up again. The single direction of the ridges is a kind of memorial, though it may not be a memorial to anything that knows it is being remembered.

The walkers that are still moving are almost impossible to see moving. You will find one standing at a fissure in the crust, its head lowered, its throat working. Nothing visible comes out of the fissure. But where the creature's joints flex, a thin blue-black fluid seeps and beads and drips into the cracks, and the same residue fills the grooves of their tracks and does not evaporate. It is the same color, the same faint smell of scorched metal, as the oil that keeps the engines turning. Either the walkers are feeding the machines, or the machines are feeding the walkers, or they have been trading the same warm fluid back and forth for so long that the difference no longer matters to either of them.

When a walker calls, the call comes up from a chamber in its chest and goes out across the flat in a low rising tone. The engines answer it. That is the part that is hard to hear at first, because the engines are so much louder that you feel them before you hear them. But if you stand very still at dusk and listen under the pulse in your sternum, you can pick out the pitch of each animal and find that the machines have been holding the same note, a little lower, the whole time. The walkers do not seem to be warning anyone. They sound like someone replying to a question that was asked long before the room was built.

The engines, for their part, are not quite machines. A machine has a purpose it was built for. These have only a motion, and the motion has a direction: toward one another. The ones that lean across their neighbors are not falling. They are reaching. Their shafts point at the horizon because the horizon is where the next engine is, and the next engine is leaning back. Over decades, two such machines will come to rest almost touching, their heads nearly kissing the salt, and then they will stay like that for a generation, humming through each other's pulse. The stopped engines are sinking into the plain the way the walkers sink when they fold their legs for good. Crust grows over their gears in a sheen, and the plain takes them back, a grain at a time.

Then there are the ones that move their position as well as their angle. You will mark one on your map and return and find it has walked, a few hundred meters, toward a pillar or away from a cluster of others. These are the restless ones, and they make the maps disagree. A map is a record of where things were agreed to be. The plateau does not agree to anything. It simply continues.

And then there is the thing that I think is the plateau itself, though it has no body you can point to. It is the reason the horizon sometimes repeats, the reason a line of engines seen to the north appears again in the south, a few degrees shifted, identical. It folds the strata of a cliff so that the same layers run up and then down, as if the mountain has turned over in its sleep and is trying to remember which face it showed the sky. It moves shadows a few seconds ahead of the things that cast them, and it returns sound from the wrong direction, half a second late. The echoes are not reflections. They are repetitions, the plateau practicing the sounds it has heard, learning to say them back.

This is what the walkers and the engines are, if you want to know what lives here: they are the slow parts of something that thinks in centuries. The animals are its wandering thoughts, the migrations that take seasons to pass a single point. The machines are its heartbeat and its hands. The pillars are its patience, rounded and pitted and fused where it has been pressed hard enough to remember the pressure. The repeated horizon and the folded cliffs and the sound that comes back from the west are the way it holds its own shape, the way a mind holds a fact by saying it over and over until the saying becomes the fact.

It does not notice you, and it does not need to. But it is listening, and that is the part you should understand before you leave. At the far edges, where the flat drops into darkness and the cool wind rises from below, the plateau stops. The engines do not go there. The walkers do not go there. Nothing that has come onto the flat has ever come back across that line, and you have to ask yourself what it means that the wind from below is the only thing that ever arrives from outside.

So at dusk, when the light turns from white to amber to a violet that seems to come up through the crust, and everything on the plateau is calling at once, listen closely. Somewhere behind you, a few seconds late, a voice is answering. It sounds like your own. It is not an echo of anything you have said. It is the plateau, trying you on, and it is getting better at it every year.
