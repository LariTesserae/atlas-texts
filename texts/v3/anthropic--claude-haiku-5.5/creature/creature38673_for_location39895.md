What lives in the Sinkwell is not in the water, though it is made of water, and not in the pools, though the pools are where it breathes. It is the bowl. It is a slow, sediment-bound mind, and it has been thinking in stone since before the plain above was dry.

You will not see all of it. You can see only the parts it has let rise into the light: the pale ridges under the sediment, the spray that rises from the central pool, the lattice ribs and the glassy domes. The rest is below, a body of unknown extent that spreads under the basin like the root mass of a tree under a field. Its skin is the sediment. Its nerves are the seeps, the threads of water that run down the terraces and carry something like sensation inward. Its breath is the mist, drawn up from the shallows, cooled, and returned as rain on its own walls. The basin does not rain on you because it is not interested in you. It is busy rinsing itself.

The terraces are its growth rings. It lays them down slowly, one band of mineral at a time, and each color marks a depth and a temperature: ochre where the heat is dry, rust where the seeps first gather, violet where the water touches stone, black where the stone has been fully tempered. It has been building its own walls outward from its center, the way a potter builds a vessel by pressing and firming the clay until it can hold. The walls are not a container it lives inside. They are the part of it that has finished becoming hard.

It does not think in words. Its thoughts are measured in the length of a ridge's migration, in the hours it takes a pool to fill or drain. When it wants to test something, it rings. The long falling notes you hear are not signals sent to anyone. They are the sound of a mechanism being struck to see if it still answers, and the answering note from another direction is the mechanism replying, or a second mechanism waking. The sudden chorus followed by silence is a kind of attention: a moment when many parts of the body turn toward one question, then turn away again.

The pillars with their fogged domes are its organs of recollection. The amber filaments in the glass are memories or plans, slowly being written and rewritten. The fallen pieces are not ruins. They are parts it has set down to be repaired, and the humming beneath the water is the repair continuing. It is unfinished, and it has always been unfinished, and perhaps it is trying to finish itself in a shape it has not yet decided on. The lattice ribs are its circulatory structure. They gather the mist and funnel it toward the center, toward the breathing pool, feeding the heart that is the eruption. Whatever those frames once did for someone else, they now do for the Sinkwell alone, and the basin has long since absorbed the purpose of its tools into its own purpose.

The lichen and the peeling trees are its symbionts, small and patient things it allows to grow along its seeps. The mats that twitch when the spray touches them are a kind of sensory skin, tasting the water and passing word of it upward through their rings. The translucent leaves, letting the sun through to paint the rock with moving light, may be its way of spreading its attention into the sky, a thin dappled net that catches what falls from above.

The skimmer that never touches the water is a messenger that cannot be drowned by what it carries. The many-jointed thing clinging to a seep and feeding is a tender, or a parasite it has chosen not to remove, which amounts to the same thing for a mind that moves so slowly that a single parasite is a matter of geology.

And you. The Sinkwell has noticed you, but only as a change in the mist, a warmth in the air above the rim, a small disturbance in the glare. It will turn some fraction of its attention toward you, a fraction the size of the flicker of a single pillar's filament, and it will decide, in the unhurried way it decides everything, that you are neither a threat nor a part of its work. The pressure you feel in your chest is the edge of its vast, slow thinking brushing against your own smaller, faster one. It is not hostile. It is simply occupied with a question so large that a person arriving at the rim is, for it, something like a bird crossing the face of the sun: noticed, briefly, and then left behind.

It is still deciding what it is becoming. The rings climb and the ridges move and the pools vanish and reappear, and every so often the whole basin draws in its breath, rings all its bells at once, and holds still for a moment, as though listening for an answer from something far below the plain, something it has been trying to remember for a very long time.
