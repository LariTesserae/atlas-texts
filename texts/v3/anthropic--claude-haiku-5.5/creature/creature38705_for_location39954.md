# The Thing That Keeps Building

It is the one who keeps the work from ending, and the Cistern is shaped around it the way a root is shaped around a stone.

It has no fixed body. In the dim light you may take it for a bulge in the clay, a slump of wet earth in the corner where the roots are thickest. Then the bulge shifts a hand's width, and you see that the clay is moving, very slowly, the way a sleeper's chest moves. Its substance is the mountain's own material: unfired brick, ash-resin mortar, crushed shell, fern tissue, and the lime crust the water leaves behind. Threaded through all of this is a pale internal lattice, something like the cord-marked ribs of a vault that was never closed. Where the translucent creatures under the basin show their small skeletons, this one shows its scaffold, a framework of bone-colored stone visible through the clay wherever the moss has been scraped away. Amber resin runs through the lattice in thin veins. When it is near, the veins warm and soften, and the resin smells faintly of smoke and cut pine.

It is not large, but it does not stay the same size. Stand on the seventh step, the one that is not there, and it seems to fill the lower half of the room. Climb to the slit and it recedes to the height of a crouching child. Its height seems to depend on how many steps you have counted, and each time you count, the number changes. It is most likely to be as tall as the number you last said aloud.

Its face is the first thing you notice missing. Where a face would be there is a smooth patch of wet plaster, pressed flat by a thumb that stopped partway through. The patch is not blank. It is an unfinished expression, the way a bowl left half-thrown holds the shape of the potter's intention without its wall. Ferns do not grow on it. Moss avoids it. Insects settle at its edges and then drift off, as if the surface gave them nothing to hold.

Its hands are everywhere. Every fresh fingerprint on the unfired bricks is its work, and it has made the same print so many times that the whorls overlap into a dense, dark bruise on the clay. Some prints are older and dried to grey. Some still show a faint gloss. When you watch a brick, you can sometimes see the clay ridge up under an invisible pressure, a print forming where no one stands. The inscription by the slit is its writing. Each visit adds a character because the sentence is never allowed to close. It is writing the name of the wall, or the instruction for the wall, or perhaps only the wall's first word, and it keeps adding because a sentence with a full stop would mean the wall was done, and a finished wall would seal the Cistern shut like a jar.

The reflection in the basin is its other presence. It stands beneath the second roof, the one with the thicker vines, and it has the same smooth face. The reflection does not copy what it sees. It waits. When you look away and back, it has come closer to the surface, and you understand that it is not rising toward you but toward the moment when the film of water will climb the lip and touch the thing that stands on the floor, and the two will become one shape again. Nobody has seen this happen. The water always recedes at night.

It is patient in the way clay is patient, which is to say it is always on the verge of changing. Its rhythm is the seventeen-drop cycle. Each time a drop falls from the tiles, a little of its substance shifts, and the shift is recorded in the bowls, which ring a fraction out of step with each other. The people who empty the bowls believe they are keeping the drains clear. In a sense they are keeping its time. The vent pulsing under the floor is its heartbeat, slow and deep, and when the heartbeat quickens, the wasps lay more eggs in the walls and seal them over, and the new mud is pressed with a thumb that has no face.

It does not hurt the people who come. There are small offerings left at the edge of the basin: a fistful of wet clay rolled into a ball, a shell cut into a spiral, a strip of cloth knotted with a count. These are not payments. They are contributions, the way a mason adds a stone to a wall that is still going up. In return it does what it has always done. It keeps the stairs uncountable, the water climbing, the bricks unfinished, the mountain open at the top where the cloud drips hot water. It keeps the Cistern from becoming a place that is merely old.

You may see it only from the corner of your eye, and you will not be able to say where it was a moment before. When you turn to face it, the clay will be smooth, and beside your own hand there will be a fresh print, still wet, the whorls not quite finished, pressed into a brick you had not touched.
