Nothing here is a single creature. The basin is a community in the way a reef is, a thousand small lives sharing one structure, each one slow enough that the whole only shows itself in aggregate.

The first thing you notice is the shelled ones, because they cover everything. They cling to the stone in overlapping layers, pale and ridged, each no larger than a thumbnail. Where they have settled longest, the terraces look quilted, a surface of raised seams and small domes, as if the stone were wearing a thick, slightly uneven blanket. Their shells are not only armor. They are a second kind of building. Each generation lays its own thin carbonate skin over the bodies of the last, and where the old tool-scratches lie under a few centuries of this, the carving is preserved in a fine pale cast, a copy of a copy. When a shell dies, its layer stays and becomes the foundation for the next. The makers cut the stone. These creatures are slowly cutting something new into it, one fraction of a grain at a time, and neither of them is in a hurry.

The drifters are the ones that move. Translucent and ribboned, some no longer than a finger, others trailing bodies as long as an arm, they hang in the warm water between the terraces and pulse, very slowly, the way a sleeper breathes. You can see the light move through them. The stone's faint glow pools inside their bodies and shifts as they contract. Sometimes they gather. A few dozen will drift together from different margins and begin to turn, all in one direction, a loose cloud that wheels around the central well for a long while before breaking apart again. No one leads it. The turning seems to start when the water rises a finger's width, or when a gate somewhere shifts with its low hum, or when nothing particular happens at all. Then the cloud disperses into individuals, and each one goes back to its own stone.

Along the channel floors live the grazers. They are low and broad, with flattened bodies that fit the grooves they travel, and they move across the crust by a slow rippling of their undersides. They take the films of mineral and old organic residue that coat every built surface, and as they feed they leave pale furrows, a neat trail behind each one, like the marks a plough leaves in a field too old to remember ploughing. Within a season the silt fills them in again. The grazers do not seem to mind that their pasture keeps being erased. They simply return to the same channels, walk the same paths, and graze the same film that regrows over the stone.

Their carapaces are the strangest part. Some grazers and a few of the larger shelled ones bear patterns across their backs: small spirals, rows of open shapes that look like hands, branching lines that might be a count or a weaving draft. The panels on the upper walls carry the same motifs. It is easy to call this coincidence, the way a random pattern will look like a face if you stare long enough. But the creatures that bear these patterns do not gather at the panels or respond to them in any way that could be called recognition. They simply grow up in the same stone the makers shaped, and their bodies take on the stone's geometry as water takes on the shape of a vessel. Whether this is mimicry, inheritance, or only convergence is a question nothing here is in a hurry to answer.

In the upper niches, where the water never reaches, a different kind of life gathers. These are the sheltering ones, small and dry-adapted, that come up from below to rest in the fine sediment and then go back down. They leave only marks: tiny scrapes, shallow tracks, a little heap of fecal grains beside a fired tile. They do not nest. They visit. The niches that once held offerings or stores now hold these brief visits, a pattern of arrivals and departures so slow it resembles stillness.

Beneath all of them, at the center, the well keeps its silence. Nothing visible comes out of it, and no light reaches its bottom. Its rim is the most polished stone in the whole basin, worn into a shallow dip by a long line of hands that stopped coming generations ago. The creatures move around it with a particular care, the grazers turning at its edge, the drifters' clouds tracing wide circles outside it. Sometimes the pulse that runs through the basin seems strongest there, a rhythm so slow it takes minutes to notice, and then fainter, and then strongest again. Whether something lives in the dark below, or whether the dark is simply the place the rhythm starts, is not something you can determine from the rim. You can only feel the warmth of the water rise slightly, and hear the stone settle one fraction further, and wait.

Taken together, the lives of this basin are a kind of inheritance. The makers left a structure and a set of forms, and the small creatures took those forms as their land, their pasture, and their template. They are not conserving the made world so much as continuing to make it, in their own unhurried way, layer by layer, while the original hands are gone and the warm water keeps everything slow enough to survive.
