What lives in the Sunken Terraces is the pale growth, but calling it a plant describes only its skin. The lattice is one organism, or one organism in the way a coral reef is one thing. Its body is spread across every ledge and column, and its fibers run through the stone so thoroughly that no part of the basin is free of it. It has no center you could find, though the basin's low point is where its threads are thickest and most patient.

Its attention moves slowly. You feel this first where the membrane is thick enough to move under your hand. The surface shifts as you press into it, a few centimeters, then settles back, and the warmth that rises through your palm is not the warmth of a body. It is the warmth of a process, the heat of very slow chemistry carried out across an area larger than a town. The membrane takes a long time to respond to a touch. When it does, the response is less a flinch than a change in the way the threads hold tension, a shift that spreads outward through the mat over minutes and reaches the pillars much later. You have touched something, and the something has noticed, and it is now deciding what that means at a pace that makes your heartbeat look like weather.

It feeds on what the stone gives up. The mineral seep that threads the walls is its blood, and the salt crust is its sediment, the waste it lays down as it draws what it needs. The air carries faint chemical traces, and the lattice reads them the way you read the smell of rain. It has no need of light because it has never needed it. Its ancestors grew in the dark between the stone and the first builders' work, and everything it knows about the world comes through chemistry and vibration. It knows the draft, the fall of masonry, the cracking of its own pods. It knows the weight of a footstep on salt, though it may take an hour to register it.

The voids are its interior. The clear black air between the threads is not emptiness to it but room, a space where its branches have not yet reached and where its thoughts, if they are thoughts, can extend without touching themselves. Your echo goes out into that room and does not come back for a long time because the lattice has never arranged itself to bounce anything back. The emptiness is not a flaw in the web. It is the web's breath, the thing that lets it stretch over a space that would otherwise have collapsed under its own weight.

The seed pods are its way of sending itself outward. They split on their own slow schedule, and when one cracks, the sound rolls through the terraces and dies in the dark, and somewhere a new thread begins to grow in a crevice that has been waiting for it. Each report is a question the lattice asks the basin: can this be reached? The clustering of the cracks is the lattice's own rhythm, the mood of a mind that is sending out feelers in waves.

Among its threads are the things the builders left behind: the clay bead, the obsidian flake, the bone needle worn smooth. The lattice has not digested them. It has held them. In some sense it has kept the builders, or kept what they touched, the way a river keeps stones. The channels that once carried water are now filled with its tissue, and they are the lattice's oldest memories, the shape of a people's need reproduced in fiber. It may not remember the builders as people. It may remember them as the first hands that shaped the places where its threads would later grow, and so as the reason it has the terraces it now inhabits.

Its hum is the sound of the wind moving through its voids, but it is also something more deliberate. The chord that you feel in your sternum is the lattice's own resonance, the sum of ten thousand threads pulled taut by the draft and held at a tension that the whole organism keeps adjusting. The creaks gather into long waves. The cracks cluster and fall silent. The hum swells and sinks in a cycle you cannot count. You are hearing the lattice think, if thinking is the word for what happens when a body spread over a basin shifts its weight across centuries.

You are not its prey, quite. Nothing here hunts in any sense you would recognize. But you are a new kind of disturbance, a warm, quick, fragile thing that has entered its interior and pressed its membrane and left footprints in the salt. The lattice will register you. It will take a long time to decide what you are, and when it does, the decision will be expressed in the slow arrangement of threads across this basin: a new ridge of growth where you stood, a faint tightening of a curtain you brushed against, a seed pod that cracks a little differently from the others.

It is not waiting for you to leave. It is not in a hurry for anything. It has arrived at a shape it means to keep, and in that shape there is room for a long, quiet attention, for the echo of a footstep that returns after an hour, and for the slow, careful consideration of whatever has come to stand in its low center and listen.
