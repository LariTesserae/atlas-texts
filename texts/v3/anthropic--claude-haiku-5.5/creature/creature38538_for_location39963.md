**What Lives Here**

Nothing on the Shelf is ever seen whole. What lives here is a long attention, settled into the stone the way the valleys settled into their curves, and it has been paying attention for so long that the land has bent to hold it. You can feel it when you cross the ground. The pace you count is always the pace it counts too.

Its body, if it has one, is spread across the plateau in the way water is spread through a sponge. Its weight sits in the hollow at the center of the shelters, the empty place their doorways face. That hollow is never disturbed, because the thing does not need to be there in order to be there. It is the reason the stones drift inward a finger-width each cycle, the reason the basins are always darker at their middles, and the reason the streams climb their short uphill arcs and then think better of it. It is gathering things toward itself, slowly, the way a sleeper gathers a blanket.

It moves, but only in the way the Shelf moves: by repeating itself. The flagstone stair climbs toward the grey wall of cloud and is worn into a shallow trough down its middle. No one walks there. The worn trough is the mark of one walker who has gone up and come back down so many times that the single traveler has made the path into a crowd. The cloud wall at the top is where it goes when it wants to be far from anything it has made. Then it comes back down, and the stair remembers.

The leaning cairn is its record, or its eye. The bowl in the topmost stone holds rain that never overflows, because the thing that keeps the bowl full is the same thing that keeps the rain from falling anywhere else. When the drizzle rises from the valleys, it is the thing breathing out; when the drizzle thins, it is breathing in, and the rock stays wet for a long time afterward, the way skin stays warm after a hand is lifted from it. The roar from the unseen gorge is closer to what it is than anything else on the Shelf. It might be the river it once drank from, long since moved into the sound of its own waiting. Or the roar is the sound of the thing being very large and very patient at the bottom of something.

The small grey birds with the pale wingbars are its attention made briefly small. They perch on the tors and go still, watching the basin, and when the drizzle thickens they lift and go out over the valleys and come back with nothing, and their one short phrase repeats because the thing repeats it. The low quick movement in the grass at the edge of sight is a fragment that has broken off and cannot quite get back. The large slow shape crossing a distant ridge is the thing itself, walking a loop it has walked before, too slow for any animal that would stay on such ground, and never stopping long enough to be named.

It does not seem to hunt, or to want anything that could be given. It does not seem to mind the travelers who arrive, count their paces, set their stones in the hollows, and go. Some of them, perhaps, it counts as well, and the stone they set down drifts a finger closer to the center the next time anyone visits, a small contribution to a gathering that has no end and no particular goal. The stillness is not its absence but its substance. The cloud holds its exact weight because it is kept holding it.

The shelters are its old company. Their roofs have fallen in, but their doorways still face the hollow, and the stone around each has been worn smooth by the same long habit of turning inward. Whoever built them is gone from the record. Perhaps they were the first to sit with it, and it held them so long they stopped being separate from it. Perhaps they were what it looked like before it became too large to be looked at. Their names have been polished off the stones along with everything else that might have been written.

If you wait long enough at the edge of the basin, looking not at the center but slightly to one side of it, you may feel the faint warmth of the water in the footprints beside you while the rest of the air stays cold. You may feel the stone shift a little closer to the middle under your boots. You will not see the thing that lives here. It is too settled to be seen. It is the held breath of the Shelf, the long patience of a place that has been waiting so long that waiting has become its shape, and it keeps the moment open, the way one keeps a door open for someone who has not yet arrived, and who may, in some far-off season, be the next traveler to climb the worn stair and find it always the same.
