**What Lives Here**

The plateau is not empty. Its population is slow, and it moves in loops too wide to see from any single place. You have to stand still for a long time to notice it.

**The Grazers**

The engine-forms are the largest living things here, though they look like machinery. Their housings are dark, rounded, and seamed with a pale bloom of mineral, the way an old animal's hide collects scars. They move on segmented tracks that flex like the joints of a crawling insect. Their vents breathe out vapor in slow, regular pulses, and the thrum you feel in your chest is their pulse, or the shared rhythm of many of them, each one a few kilometers from the next.

They feed. The drums and cylinders beneath each body are mouths and stomachs. They draw up the crust, the sulfur and salt, the raw mineral slurry, and they hold it inside themselves, working it. What they expel is the fine pale dust that trails behind them, and that dust is not waste. It is fertilizer. It settles on the strips they have grazed, and the plants come back over it within days, thick and amber-beaded, always to the same height. Then the machines return and graze them again.

The geometric strips are not damage. They are pasture management, the cropped lawn of a herd that has been grazing the same ground so long that the lawn has become its shape. Nothing else eats the plants. Nothing else needs to. The grazers are the only animals this ground has ever held, and they keep the land cut to exactly the size of their hunger.

I should be careful here. A herd needs a shepherd, and there is none. The grazers do not seem to answer to anything. They loop. When one reaches the edge of its circuit it turns, and when it turns, another is already coming up the opposite side to meet it. They are not aware of each other the way creatures in a flock are, but their paths are never in conflict. Whatever governs them is older than any of them and not in any of them.

**The Pasture**

The plants are not the grazers' food alone. They are the skin of the plateau. Their waxy, translucent tissue holds the amber sap that clings to every split stem, and when you break one open, the sting in your throat is the same sharp smell of warm stone that hangs over the grazers' tracks. The sap is their blood, and the plants are the vessels that carry it.

Their growth is strange because it is almost not growth. The shingled leaves stack and restack. The membranous fronds open toward the sun and close at dusk, and that opening and closing is the only motion anyone sees. The new ridges where plant meets crust are the most unsettling part. They seem paused, as if they were holding their breath between one stage of development and the next, and they creep, a finger's width at a time, toward the cracks. They are reaching for the mineral ground they feed on. In the pale crust, their roots thread down and draw the salt upward, and that salt becomes more of the plant.

The quartz pods that rattle in the wind are seed, or perhaps something closer to a clock. They knock against one another, glassy clicks that come at irregular intervals, and each click is a small signal passed along the plants. I have come to think of them as the sound the pasture makes when the grazers pass nearby, though I cannot prove the grazers make it happen.

**The Young**

The translucent boulders scattered across the plain are the strangest inhabitants of all, and the hardest to describe. They look like stone that stopped halfway through becoming stone, and they seem to be the young. Their edges are still soft enough to glow, and the mineral filaments veined through them catch the light as if they were nerves. Some have been like this for as long as anyone could measure, and none of them has ever finished.

I think they are the grazers' offspring, or their embryos, or the stage of them that the land holds back. The grazers come out of ground that has never finished forming, and the boulders are where the ground is still deciding what to make of itself. That would explain why the fractures never widen and the ridges never round off. The rock is not dead. It is gestating, and the gestation has no end, only the steady work of the grazers passing over it, feeding the thing that will not yet be born.

If this is right, then the plateau is one organism spread across three thousand meters of altitude that nobody measured. The grazers are its moving limbs. The plants are its skin and circulation. The haze is its breath, the pale particles lifted into the still air and held there, never falling. The boulders are its unfinished bones, and the fractures are the joints it has not yet closed.

**The Watcher, Or Its Absence**

I have not seen anything that looks like a mind. But I have felt the attention of the place, the way you feel someone looking at you from across a room. It comes most strongly at the edges of the plateau, near the far ridges where the thrum is loudest, and it does not seem hostile or curious. It seems to be occupied elsewhere, holding the whole loop in view and finding it sufficient.

Perhaps the grazers are its thoughts, and they think in circuits. Perhaps the plants are its memory, holding the same shapes year after year. Perhaps the boulders are the questions it has not decided how to answer.

Or perhaps there is nothing at the center at all, and the whole plateau is simply the shape that a self-feeding system takes when nothing ever interrupts it. Animals, herds, and organisms in general are the things that eat and are eaten and change because of it. Here the eating has become a closed loop, the loop has become a landscape, and the landscape has become the only thing that lives. Nothing here dies in any way you would recognize, and nothing is ever born in any way that finishes.

**What You Are to Them**

You are a disturbance, a warm body crossing their pasture. The grazers will not turn from their loops for you. The plants will creep a finger's width toward your boot print and then stop, as if they had only meant to reach the crack. The boulders will not notice you at all, since they are too slow to notice anything.

And yet the place seems to take note of you the way a body takes note of a splinter: not with alarm, but with a faint and patient reorganization, a leaning-toward. Walk long enough and you may start to feel that the plateau is making room for you, the way it made room for the grazers' first passes, and that the room it makes is always exactly the size of the one who stands in it.

Somewhere past the far ridge, the thrum continues. Somewhere beneath the crust, something is still being born, and has been for as long as anyone has been counting, and the grazers are still walking the long loops that keep it fed.
