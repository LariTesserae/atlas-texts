**Who Lives in Harrow Reach**

The first thing to know about the people here is that they count in strokes. Ask a sluice-keeper how long a job will take and you'll get an answer like "forty strokes," meaning forty beats of the number-two pump, which is the one with the cracked flywheel and the sound like a man clearing his throat. Children learn to count this way before they learn their letters. They fall asleep counting it, and many of the older residents wake at the moment a pump changes its note, without ever needing a clock.

The sluice families are the oldest people in the Reach, though "old" is relative in a town that has only existed for a few generations. There are perhaps six households who have worked the gates since the first pump house was bolted to its footings. Their hands are the most visible sign of the work: palms ridged with shale-cuts that healed white and then were cut again, knuckles thickened from wrenching rusted valves, fingertips that can feel the difference between a sound seal and a weeping one. They are not sentimental about the land. They speak of it the way a farmer speaks of a difficult animal, with affection that never quite becomes trust. They know the basins by their moods. The grey basin to the north goes sour in July. The lagoon past the reed beds drains too fast when the wind comes from the west. When a keeper walks the causeway at dawn, you will see her stop at a particular culvert, crouch, press her palm flat against the iron, and hold it there for a while, as if listening through the metal.

Then there are the newer residents, the ones who came for the cheap terraces and the promise of a place with room. They work in the canning works and the enamel shop, they keep gardens that the alders are slowly reclaiming, and they complain about the pumps for their first year before they stop hearing them. Within a decade most of them can tell you the pump's pitch when it's struggling, and they begin to speak of the Reach as "ours" in a way that surprises them. A few never make that shift. They leave, or they stay and grow bitter, and the bitterness is a kind of weather too. The town has a word for these people, *drifters*, and it is not entirely unkind.

The surveyors are a category of their own. They arrive every few years in a van with mud-caked tyres, carrying brass benchmarks and a theodolite wrapped in a blanket. They are polite, a little distant, and they carry the particular melancholy of people whose work is to measure something that is always moving. Townspeople feed them, shelter them, and ask them what the numbers say. The surveyors answer carefully. The land is rising. The shelf at Gull Point has climbed another four centimetres. The old quay is a metre higher than it was in their father's time. The townspeople nod and go home and say nothing for a day or two, and then someone moves a garden fence.

**The wet-dwellers**

The eels are the true inhabitants of the culverts, and they were here before anyone thought to dig them. They move through the brick channels in dark, muscular coils, and they gather at the outflow gates in such numbers that the water seems to boil. The keepers say they can tell the health of the system by the eels: when they thin out, something upstream has gone wrong. People fish them from the causeway with hand lines baited on tins, and eel stew is a staple of the Reach's rougher kitchens. Nobody thinks of the eels as sentient, but nobody quite forgets to be respectful of them, either.

The otters are newer, having come up the canals within the last twenty years, and they have become the town's favourite animals. They are seen rarely, mostly at dusk, and the sight of one surfacing with a fish or sliding down a mud bank is cause for a small, quiet delight. Children know the slides by name. The keepers are less sentimental. Otters burrow into the bank supports and weaken the causeway, and every few years someone has to reinforce a section of the wall where a family has tunnelled too deep. The otters are not blamed, though. In the Reach, blame is for things that can be fixed.

The herons stand in the shallows like the keepers' own patient ancestors, motionless for an hour at a time, watching the water with the same attention the sluice families give to their gates. The gulls are opportunists and thieves, and the crows are gossips who follow the rubbish carts and seem to know everyone's business. The frogs are the choir. Their voices rise each evening in waves across the basins, and in summer the sound is so dense that the keepers say you can feel it in your teeth. The town doesn't mind. A frog-filled night is a sign that the water is clean.

**The thing in the lower basins**

Most people in Harrow Reach will tell you there is nothing in the lower basins but mud and reed and the occasional drowned crate. A smaller number, usually the oldest keepers and the children who sneak out past the gates, will tell you otherwise.

The lower basins are where the old sea went last, and the silt there is deep and dark and very old. When the pumps run low, or during the rare dry months, the surface of the mud sometimes shifts in a way that has nothing to do with the wind. A bubble breaks. A slick of grey water spreads in a perfect circle and then folds back in on itself. The keepers call it *turning*, and they have a rule about it: when the basin turns, you do not look directly at the place where it turned. You walk away and count strokes until you can no longer hear the water.

No one has seen the thing whole. A child in the 1960s, according to the story, reported a long pale shape under the surface, like a sheet of skin floating in a drain, with something ridged along its spine that might have been gills or might have been the bones of a shelf. The keepers pumped the basin for three weeks afterward, and the child did not go near the water again. Whatever is in the silt, the old keepers believe, is older than the town and older than the rebound. It is a thing of the sea that was never fully taken away. Its slow breathing is what the pumps are really holding down. Some of them believe the pumps hold it not out of the basins but under them, keeping the weight of the water on its back.

Nobody in town says this with confidence. They say it the way they say the land is rising: as a fact that is true even if they haven't measured it yet.

**The ones who hold the line**

The people of Harrow Reach are, in the end, a small and stubborn population, bound to a place that is trying, slowly and without malice, to take them back. They cut the alders at the causeway edge every spring and watch them return by autumn. They drain the lots and see the water rise again in winter. They rebuild the culverts after the otters tunnel through, shore up the terraces as the shelves shift, and move the drains when the surveyors' numbers say the ground has tilted. They do this with a kind of weary competence that might look like fatalism from outside and feels, from inside, more like rhythm. The pump beats, the keeper walks, the frogs sing, the eels run the culverts, and the town is kept, for now, a little above the water.

On clear days in spring, when the shale shelves glow pale and the lagoons turn a startling blue, a few of the older residents climb onto the causeway with their hands in their pockets and look out over the reed beds toward the places the alders have already claimed. They don't say much. Someone might ask, quietly, how many strokes are left. Someone else might answer with a shrug, and a number, and then the pumps change their note and everyone turns, a little startled, and walks back toward the town to keep the water where it belongs.
