The people here are hard to see at first, because they have learned the same patience as the stone.

On the upper grid, where the flat-roofed basalt blocks sit under the mist, they move slowly and keep to the lee sides of walls. They are lean and pale, their skin dulled to the color of the tuff dust they track indoors. Their eyes have grown large and light-sensitive from generations of diffuse daylight, and they squint at midday when the cloud breaks. Most wear close-fitted coats of oiled cloth that shed the constant condensation, and their hair is cropped short or bound tight against the damp. They carry their weight low. They have learned to stand still for long periods, the way a person listens for a sound under the pumps' rhythm and waits to find out whether it is a warning.

They speak close together. The noise forces it. Conversation here is mostly lip and breath, a murmur pressed into the space between two faces, and the language has shed its loud vowels and long sentences. Much of what matters gets said with a hand on the forearm, a tilt of the head toward the cistern stairs, two fingers raised to mean *the seepage line has shifted again.* Children learn to read these signals before they learn to read words, and they learn to sleep through the chug of the pumps, which comes up through the floor and into the ribs.

Their work is maintenance, which is the same thing as living. Each morning crews go down the terraces with mallets, mineral sealant, and long-handled brushes, clearing gypsum lace from the intake grates before it can choke the pumps. They replace one basalt block at a time, cutting each from the same rock the city stands on, so that the new piece weathers to match the old within a few seasons and the wall looks as if it has always been there. Their hands are scarred and stained rust at the knuckles from the iron bloom. Nobody remembers when the city was new. They do not expect it to be finished, and they do not find that troubling.

Lower down, among the arches and factory tiers, the population thickens and grows more mixed. Workers from the furnaces have the bronzed, heat-cracked look of people who spend their lives in shimmer. Some of them never climb above the cloud line. Engineers who keep the cooling towers running live in the swifts' territory and are quieter still, because the birds have taken over the upper rooms and the engineers have learned to work around nests packed so tight that the mud-and-slag walls breathe with their chicks. Those engineers tend a truce with the birds that no one has ever written down.

The gorges belong to other inhabitants, and the people of the city are careful about how they describe them.

The pale many-legged creatures move along the seepage lines in long, unhurried files. Their bodies are soft and translucent at the edges, and they carry their legs in a rippling sequence, like a wave passing through a field of reeds. They feed on the gypsum lace and leave behind trails of calcified mucus that harden into ridges on the rock, and over generations those ridges have become part of the cliffs' own pattern. The city's stonemasons read them as a kind of map. Their domed shelters, built of slag and fine sediment bound with their own secretions, stand in clusters along the gorge walls, and from a distance they look so much like ruins that surveyors have logged several as abandoned settlements and then been corrected by the wet, slow movement of something inside.

The people do not know, with certainty, whether the creatures are animals in the ordinary sense, or whether their dense settlements are a kind of dwelling, or a kind of city. Some of the older masons have stopped asking. They leave offerings of cut basalt at the mouths of the shelters: small square blocks, carefully weathered, set on the ledges. The creatures seem to take them. Whether they are building with the stone or simply making room among it, no one has settled.

In the marshes, the water is never still. Beneath the iron-bloomed skin, things turn over in the brown depths, and at dusk, when the light goes copper across the flats, the grey-brown frogs begin their chorus and the surface shivers in a way that has nothing to do with wind. The city people keep their distance from the marsh edge after dark. They say there is more down there than frogs, and they say it without much fear, the way one speaks of weather.

Above all of it, the swifts fill the sky at dusk in a flickering, shrieking sheet, and the beetles work through the cisterns and along the aqueducts, iridescent and unhurried. The mist beads on every surface. The cliffs weep their slow lace. Everything here is slightly damp, slightly warm, slightly metallic, and everything has been here for a very long time.

So the answer to *who lives here* is not one thing. The city people, the pale creatures in the gorges, the birds in the towers, the frogs and whatever moves under the marsh: all of them share the plateau the way the stone and the seepage share it, each adjusting to the others, each slowly altering the ground it stands on. The people maintain. The creatures build. The birds nest in the machinery. The water keeps its own counsel. And all of them, in their different ways, have learned what the plateau teaches to anything that stays long enough. They wait, and the waiting is not a failure of hope but the shape of the place itself, rising out of the haze one tier at a time.
