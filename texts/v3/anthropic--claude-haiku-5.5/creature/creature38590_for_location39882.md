**The ones in the gates**

They live in the thin grey film on the sluice lips, where the water runs fastest and the glassy mineral is smoothest. You would take them for part of the stone at first. Each is a chain of pale, overlapping plates, perhaps the length of a finger, joined by flexible seams. Their backs are glossy and faintly ridged, and where the light catches them they show a milky translucence, like the inside of a shell held up to a window. They move so slowly that you have to look away and back again to be sure anything has changed.

They graze the algae, but they also build. Along each plate's edge they secrete the same mineral that lines the basins, so their bodies and the engine are made of one substance. Over many seasons the gates have grown more exact, and the ones in the gates have grown with them. A plate that was once loose now seats itself into the notch of the next, and the slabs close with a cleaner crack than they did last year. Nobody designed this. The stone and the creatures have been pressing on each other for a long time, and the pressure has produced a fit.

**What they sense**

They have no eyes that you would recognize, only a dense fringe of fine hairs along each seam, which bend and shiver in the current. Through those hairs they read the water: its speed, its temperature, the pulse of a rising channel after rain. Most of all they read the tremor. Each time a slab slams down, the shock runs through the bedrock and up through their plates, and they have learned to feel it as a kind of time. The hairs along their bodies rise and settle in step with the strokes. They know the rhythm of the mechanism the way a heart knows its own beat, from inside.

The amphibians know it too. Their chorus swells in the gaps between strokes and quiets when a slab is about to fall, as if the whole basin were keeping time. You can watch a young frog call, pause, and call again exactly as the knock arrives. The ones in the gates may be the source of that timing, or may simply be the part of the machine that feels most like a living thing. From where you stand, it is impossible to say which way the influence runs.

**How they grow**

They do not hatch in the usual way. When a flood comes, the plates along a chain loosen and separate at a seam, and each fragment drifts into the shallows, where it settles onto new stone and begins to add plates on its own. The timing matters. Fragments released during the fast pulses of the mechanism seat themselves more firmly and grow more quickly, and the dim, slow periods leave behind thinner, more fragile growths. Over generations, the basin has selected for the rhythm. The ones that survive are the ones whose breaking and building fall in step with the knocking.

This is why the rhythm has tightened. The mechanism did not simply settle into a steady beat on its own. Its strokes are shaped, slowly, by what grows along its gates, and what grows there is shaped by the strokes. You could describe it as a feedback loop, but that phrase misses the texture of it: the way a pale plate waits in the current for the next slam, the way a whole chain goes still and then surges forward on the same beat.

**What eats them**

The banded predator avoids the falls and the sluice gates. It hunts the amphibians in the sedges, but it has learned not to follow them onto the stone, where the knocking shakes everything loose and the tremor confuses the body's sense of where the prey is. The narrow-beaked birds take the fragments that drift off in floods, but rarely the whole chains. The ones in the gates are protected, in a loose way, by the machine that they helped build. Their safety depends on the thing that also keeps them moving.

**What it is like to be one of them**

If you could sit in the spray at the foot of the falls and be still enough, you might understand something of their existence. There is no hurry in it and no stillness either. Every few seconds the world tips, falls, and strikes, and every few seconds a plate shifts a fraction of a millimeter into the place that the last shock left open. Life is a series of small arrivals, each one timed to a blow from the outside. They do not experience this as a machine. They experience it as weather that keeps its promises.

They are the basin's way of remembering itself. The ridges are still breaking, the stream is still finding its channels, the frogs are still learning their calls. But the ones in the gates have already decided on one thing: that the knocking will continue, and that they will be there when it does. Each stroke adds a plate. Each plate makes the next stroke a little more exact. The loop tightens, slowly, as if the place were counting, and the count is never finished.
