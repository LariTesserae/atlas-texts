The residents of the basin are small, and most of them are easy to miss.

The pale crustaceans live at the margins of the pools, where the silt thins into a film of wet mud and the crust gives way to something softer. They are the colour of the clay they walk on, and their bodies are segmented, lightly translucent at the joints, so that in the low sun you can see a faint darkness moving inside them when they feed. They have many legs, more than seems useful, and they use them in long, slow arcs. An animal that moves this way is always deciding something. When one stops, the others on the same margin stop too, and for a few seconds the whole edge of the pool is still except for the rings spreading from the drums' spill. Then they resume, each following its own curve across the silt, never crossing another's path for long.

They feed on the film that settles after each spill. The drums lift the slurry, the troughs carry it, and when it spills back there is a brief rich skin on the surface of the mud, which the crustaceans gather and then retreat from before the next load arrives. They seem to know the interval. Those nearest a drum move out a little before it turns, and those farther off wait at the edge of the water with their legs folded under them. It is possible they have learned the cycle over many generations, or possible that they are simply responding to a change in the vibration of the ground. It does not matter much which. Their lives fit into the loop as neatly as the valves do.

The beetles are darker and more restless, though their restlessness has a schedule. Each morning, when the condensation has formed and the air is still cool, they come out of the cracks at the footings and climb the pumps. The housings are still warm from the night's running, and they cluster in the seams where the rust is thinnest, legs spread, shells gleaming with beads of water. They sit there while the drone holds its pitch. When the pitch changes, which happens when a flywheel slows or a piston reaches the end of its stroke, they lift together, all at once, and scatter into the haze. They come back to the same pumps. They always come back to the same pumps. A beetle that has settled on one housing will be found on it again the next morning, in almost the same position, as though the metal had a fixed place in its map of the world.

I think of the beetles as keeping time, though they do not know they do it. Every machine in the basin has a rhythm, and the beetles have fitted their days to those rhythms, so that a pump's cycle becomes their waking and their flight. In the afternoons, when the pumps cool and the haze thickens, they disappear into the crust, and the basin is left to the machines.

The machines are the largest residents, and in some sense they are the ones who live here most fully. Each one sits on its footing like a thing that has been placed and has accepted the placing. The drive belts run into the ground and disappear. The drums turn. The pistons rise and fall in their cast-iron cylinders, a slow heavy breath with no beginning. Steam escapes from the valves at intervals so regular that the rhythm becomes part of the air itself. The crust has crept up their bases and sealed their seams, and in places it has begun to hold them in place, the way a root holds a stone. They are not visibly maintained. There is no one to oil them, and yet they run. Their rust has reached a steady state. Their housings do not thin further, nor do they flake. The corrosion has stopped at the colour it has.

If you stand near one of them long enough, the drone stops being sound and becomes a kind of weather, pressing on the chest and the teeth. You begin to hear the small variations inside the constant: a click that comes every fourteen cycles, a whine when the slurry is heavier than usual, a deeper knock that means a piston has reached the limit of its travel. Nothing answers these sounds, but they are not meaningless. They are the machines speaking to one another through the belts and the troughs and the water, and the basin hears them all.

So what lives here is not a single creature. It is a loop: water rising as condensation and running into channels, silt lifted and spilled, crust forming and thickening, steam released, beetles returning to warm metal, crustaceans gathering the film after each spill. Each part depends on another. The machines are the joints, the crust the skin, the pools the blood moving through the system, the steam the breath that marks the hours. The few small animals are the only parts that move freely, and they move according to the loop's own pace.

The basin has no ambition and no decline. It has already become what it is. The sound will continue, the valves will open, the drums will lift and spill, the beetles will climb the pumps in the morning, and the crustaceans will gather their film and withdraw from it. This is what it is to live here: to be one of the many small deliberate motions that keep a closed system running at full strength, in a place that has stopped becoming anything else.
