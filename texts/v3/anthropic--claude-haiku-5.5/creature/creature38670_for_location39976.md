The people of Ysk, if that word fits, are not one kind of creature. They are a population of many kinds, bound together by the buildings and the warm water. Most of them never see one another. They sense each other through pressure, through the rasp of a claw on stone, through the taste of a chemical trail the current carries from a hundred meters off.

**The shoals**

The first thing that finds you is the shoal. Thousands of small silver fish move through the avenues in rolling clouds, and they know the presence of a larger body before it reaches them. Their flank-lines pick up the displacement you make, and the whole mass turns at once, a single flicker of pale flank, then splits around you and closes behind. They click to one another constantly, a dry patter that ripples through the streets like rain on a tin roof. Their lives are short and their numbers are enormous. In the pulse seasons, when the cold water sinks and the shoals climb toward the towers to spawn, the upper stories fill with them until the windows are gray with moving bodies and the air tastes of milt and salt.

**The walkers**

On the walls and in the doorways move the armored crustaceans, dog-sized, slow, and deliberate. They walk on jointed legs that grip the stone with bristled tips, and they carry their weight carefully, as if they understand that the building under them is old. They are the loudest things in the basin. Each shift of a leg knocks against the shell of the one beside it, and the knocks echo down the streets in a broken rhythm, a slow percussion that serves as their language. They drum to claim doorways and drum to answer rivals, and in still hours you can hear them talking across a block, their taps passing from wall to wall until the whole district is quietly conversing.

**The eels**

Coiled inside the drainage channels and the old pipes that once carried water through the city are the eels. They are long and thick-bodied, banded in dull bronze, and they live almost entirely out of sight. You do not see an eel so much as feel its attention: a pressure change in the pipe, a faint wake that swings your hand as you pass a gap in the stone. They ambush the shoals at the mouths of their burrows. When they strike, the sound is a short, heavy slap of water, and the shoal disperses in a blink, leaving one or two fish drifting in the current where the eel has withdrawn.

**The blade-finned hunter**

The largest predator is slow and nearly silent. It lies on the rooftops with its fins folded, and its body is the color of the silt that coats the stone, so it blends into the flat terraces until it moves. Its fins are long and translucent, edged with bone-colored spines, and when it does move it does so in a single heavy stroke that sends a shock wave through the water. The shoals break apart in silent bursts, flashing and scattering, and the hunter drifts through the gap and takes what it needs. Afterward it settles back onto the roof and waits. Its patience is the most unnerving thing about it. It seems to understand that the basin will bring its prey back, and that there is no hurry.

**The color-pulsers**

Octopus-like forms wrap around the pillars and columns of the plazas, and they are the strangest of the mobile dwellers. Their skin changes in waves that run from arm to arm, dark to pale to dark again, and when several of them are close together the pulses pass between them, so that a whole street of pillars seems to flicker like a line of failing lamps. Their color is not a signal for anything you would recognize. It seems to be a conversation with the water itself, a way of reading the light that does not exist here and then answering it. They are intelligent in a way that feels alien, and they watch you with eyes that track your movement through the silt with unsettling precision.

**The builders**

The most important inhabitants do not move at all. On every stone and along every crack grow the sessile colonies: calcareous crusts laid down in fine rings, fan-shaped sponges that open their pale faces to the currents, worm-tubes that sway like grass in the sediment. These are the ones who keep the city together. Their lime fills the gaps where mortar once held the blocks. Their sponges trap silt and turn it into soft ground that then supports more colonies. Every season the reef grows a little thicker over the stone, and every season the buildings take on a new shape, less like something made and more like something that has accreted. They are not building anything. They are simply living, and living here means laying down stone.

**The grazers and the margins**

Around the few patches of kelp and sea-grass, in the thin rim where the city meets open water, gather the grazers: small, dense, bristle-covered things that move in tight groups and crop the films of algae from the stone. They are always hungry and always wary. Their clusters drift toward the green patches like iron filings toward a magnet, and they turn and scatter at the first sign of the eels or the hunter. They are the basin's bottom rung, and everything above them depends on their numbers.

**What lives here, altogether**

No single creature is in charge of Ysk. The basin is held together by a web of small dependencies: the shoals feed the eels and the hunter, the grazers keep the algae in check, the sponges and worms build the ground that the walkers and the grazers cross, and the color-pulsers move among them all, reading the water and answering it. The city's ruins are not a graveyard. They are a set of slots and surfaces, and each creature has found the one it needs. The builders are the deepest layer, because everything that lives here depends on the stone that they keep making. In that sense the whole basin is one slow organism, one that continues the work the old builders began, stacking the bones of a vanished city into the shape of a reef and finding in it a shelter that never quite finishes being built.
