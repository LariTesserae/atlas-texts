The first thing that lives here is the thread.

It grows along the seams where the seep comes through, a colorless fur that lies in mats across the glass. Touch it and it ripples outward, slow as a sigh, and then settles back. The ripple does not look like fear or pleasure. It looks like a body checking its own edges. The threads lean together toward one point in the vault, all of them, and when you press a finger against the mat it leans toward your finger for a moment before it straightens. It is paying attention in the only way it knows, which is by feeling where the water is moving and where the stone is cold.

The threads are not separate plants. Follow one down from the wall and it sinks into the film at the base of the glass, and the film runs under the silt, and under the silt the pale creatures are moving in their millions, blind and translucent, each one a small bead of the same substance that the thread is made of. They drift in the seep-currents and collect at the deepest pools, where the film is thickest and the water goes milky. They are not eating the vault. They are carrying it. Each one takes a little mineral from the seep and passes it along, and in the warm pool the passing has built up a slow nacre that will someday, perhaps, become stone. It has not become stone yet. It is always about to.

So the crowd is not really a crowd. It is a single thing with a great many hands, and the hands are always busy with the same small task: receiving the water, passing it on, letting it fall.

The drops are its clock. Each one hangs at the tip of its reed-thin straw for a fraction longer than it should, and this is because the thing is holding it. It gathers the water slowly, feels the weight of it, and lets go only when it has decided. The intervals never quite regularize because the decision is not made by a timer. It is made by something that is counting, and counting is never regular when the thing counting is also being counted.

The stacks of flat stones in the niches are the counts. Someone placed them. The lean of each stack is different because each one records a different thing, a season perhaps, or a number of falls, or a number of times someone came down to the warm pool and sat with their hands in the film until the film went quiet. The scratched spirals and notched lines at chest height match the stacks in length. They are the same count written in a second hand. Whoever made them learned the rhythm of the drops and tried to pin it into glass. They did this by touch, in the dark, which is the only way anyone has ever read this place. The bone hook on the ledge is the tool they used to do it: a barb to reach into the silt, a shaft to rest the palm on, a point polished by a hand that held it for a very long time.

The ring of smaller stones around the warm pool is where the keepers worked. Inside it the silt is clear. Someone cleared it again and again, lifting the settling sediment out so that the film could reach the water unclouded, so the pale creatures could drift in without fouling their own bodies. The ring is a boundary of care. Whoever set it there was not trying to keep the vault out. They were trying to keep it clean enough to hear itself.

Now the large shape. Once in a long while it glides beneath the surface of the largest pool, flat and translucent, and it moves so slowly it seems to be carried rather than to swim. It may be the thing's own heart. It may be a child of the film, grown large from being fed for generations on what the keepers left in the ring. Its outline shows only where the milky water bends around it, like a lens held up in fog. It does not come to the surface. It does not seem to need to. When it passes beneath the ring, the drops above it hang a little longer, and the threads on the nearest wall lean toward it, and then the whole chamber goes still again.

The hum that stops when you stop breathing is the sound of the counting. It is not the rock, and it is not really the water. It is the mat and the pools and the crowd of blind pale bodies all keeping time together, a sound too low and too spread out to find a source for. The echo that returns from the part of the vault you cannot see is the thing answering itself across the dark, confirming that the count was made.

So what lives in the Weeping Vault is a patient gathering of threads and creatures and film, all one body, which has been learning for a very long time how to measure the water as it falls. It was tended by people who came in the dark with bone hooks and stone stacks and knelt beside the warm pool to keep the silt clear, and who counted with it, and who, at some point, stopped coming. The count did not stop when they did. It is still going, a drop at a time, each one hanging at the lip a fraction longer than gravity allows, each one waiting for the decision that always seems to be just about to be made.

You are inside it now. You are a new hand in the crowd. Whatever you leave on the glass, the thread will feel it, and lean toward it, and begin to count.
