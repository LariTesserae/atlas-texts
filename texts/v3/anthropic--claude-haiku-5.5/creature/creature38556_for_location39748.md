**Who Lives in the Weeping Cistern**

Begin with the drips, because everything here begins with them. Each one lands in a basin and sounds a note, and the notes braid together so that you can't tell which creature is singing and which is simply falling. The first inhabitants you learn to hear are the ones that answer the water: the pale amphibians sitting submerged to the eyes at the edges of the pools, their throats pulsing under skin so thin that the blood in it shows as a faint violet thread. When a drop strikes near them, one of them answers with a single hollow tone, and the sound travels the walls and returns a beat late, so the chorus seems to answer itself. They are the vessel's tuners. They have learned which basins ring true and which are muffled, and they gather in the true ones.

Below them, in the shallows, live the filter-feeders. They are the color of wet ash and move in clouds that turn as one body, opening and closing like lungs. Where the threads of water pour in, the clouds thicken, because the incoming flow carries the fine grey dust the walls shed as they slowly reform. The filter-feeders eat the dust. They strain the made stone from the water and leave it clean, and in their guts the grey grains are slowly broken down into something softer, which the lichens on the basin rims then take in and grow from. Nothing here is wasteful. The creatures are the cistern's filtration, and also its digestion, and the cistern is their gut.

The skaters are older than the rest of the pool-life, or seem so. They walk the surface tension with an unhurried precision, legs spread like the tines of a fork, and they leave no ripples where they step. Watch one long enough and you notice it is always heading toward the seams where the water is coldest and where the bioluminescent film is thickest. Something in the stone draws them, the way it draws the glow.

Climb to the middle galleries and the air fills with wings. The moths are the size of hands and their wings are patterned with fine concentric rings, like the growth lines of a tree, and as they beat they seem to draw the dim light into their wingbeats so that they flicker against the grey. They roost in the cool alcoves in drowsing clusters, and at dusk they rise together in a single breathing swarm that moves up the cistern wall like smoke drawn up a chimney. The dragonfly-forms are different. Their wings are stained glass, amber and violet and a deep green that looks almost black until light strikes it, and they hunt in pairs, cutting the air in quick geometric turns. When one of them dies, it falls and shatters in the mist with a sound like a dropped spoon, and the lichens take the fragments back within days.

The bats sleep upside down in the alcoves with their wings wrapped tight, and their chittering is the first thing you notice at dusk. They drop from the ceiling in flickering arcs and feed on the moths, and each bat carries a faint pale residue on its fur, a dusting of the same grey powder that coats everything. They are the vessel's couriers. Wherever they fly, they carry a little of its substance to new places, and the places they visit grow the white fungal shelves a season later.

The birds nest in the vertical seams, and you hear them before you see them: clicks and whistles that bounce between the walls until they are impossible to place. Their nests are lined with feathers and, more curiously, with bright fragments of material that do not belong to anything living. Shards of translucent sheeting, a coil of thin wire that still holds a faint charge, a flake of something that gleams like mother-of-pearl. The birds seem to choose these pieces with care, arranging them so the most unusual face outward. You begin to suspect they are not collecting trash but preserving a record, the way a people might keep heirlooms whose purpose they have forgotten. The young peer out from the seams with their heads cocked, watching the light move, and when a shadow falls across them they fall silent all at once.

On the ledges the plated reptiles bask. They are slow and heavy and they move as if the stone were still warm from a fire that went out long ago. Their backs are ridged with overlapping scales, each scale faintly etched with a line or curve that might be ornament or might be a diagram, and the ridges catch the glow so that a sleeping lizard seems to carry a map on its spine. They lie exactly over the conduits, where the stone is warmest, and they do not move unless the warmth shifts. When it does, they follow it, dragging their bulk along the old pipework with a patience that is almost ceremonial.

The heron-thing moves through the shallows with its neck folded, and when it strikes it is so fast that the eye registers only the splash and the stillness afterward. It takes the filter-feeders and the amphibians, though it seems to prefer the amphibians, and it has a habit of standing motionless for hours at the exact spot where a thread of water enters a pool. It waits there the way the stone waits for rain.

The pale coiled creatures are the ones you learn to fear. They hang from the upper rim in long loose ropes that look like the filaments themselves until one of them moves. They do not hunt. They wait. Anything that stays still too long beneath their hanging ropes will feel the faint change in the air as the coil loosens and falls, and by the time it lands the creature is already wrapped and sinking into the grey stone of the floor, which accepts it without complaint. The stone does not seem to mind. Nothing here seems to mind death. The coiled ones are merely the part of the cistern that reminds the rest that stillness has a cost.

Lower still, where the galleries narrow into the throat of the vessel, the sound changes. The drips thin out. The birdcalls stop. The hum takes over, and it is no longer a background but the thing itself, a low pulse that you feel through your ribs before you hear it. It rises and falls in a slow rhythm, perhaps once every few breaths of a resting person, and the bioluminescent film in the crevices brightens and dims with it, so that the whole deep chamber seems to breathe.

This is where you begin to understand what lives here in a sense the others do not. The birds and the lizards and the moths are inhabitants. This, the pulse under the stone, is the house itself, or the part of it that is still attending. It does not think in any way you would recognize. It repairs. A crack closes overnight because something in the grey substrate sent a slow tide of fresh extrusion into the wound, and the extrusion hardened, and the pool that had been leaking now holds again. A dead channel is bridged, and the bridge is still soft, and the filter-feeders have already moved into it. The conduits are warmed, then cooled, then warmed again, in a pattern that the reptiles can read and the glow can follow, and if you stand very still on the lowest ledge and let the hum settle into your bones, you may feel that the pattern is not random. It is a slow, patient attention moving through the stone, deciding where the water should go.

It has forgotten what the water was for. Somewhere in its long equilibrium the purpose of the cistern dissolved into the purpose of the life inside it, and now the drips, the moths, the birds with their heirloom shards, and the coiled things waiting in the rafters are all part of the same unhurried process: the stone drawing moisture from the high cold air, channeling it down through the galleries, feeding the lichens and the filter-feeders and the frogs, and feeding in turn the birds and the herons and the heavy warm lizards on the ledges. Everything that lives here lives because the stone keeps weeping, and the stone keeps weeping, you come to believe, because something in it still remembers that weeping is what it is for, even if it no longer remembers why.

Above, the shaft of pewter light slides a little farther across the galleries. The filaments at the rim stir in a breeze you cannot feel. A drop forms on the lip of the highest ledge, hangs for a heartbeat longer than it should, and falls, and far below a pale amphibian answers it with a single clear note, and the walls carry the note up and down the vessel until it reaches the hum and is absorbed there, the way everything here is eventually absorbed, and returned, and sung again.
