# What Lives on the Sump Plain

The thing that lives here is not one creature, and it is not many. From a rise, you can see it all at once, the way you see the plain: the sheet water, the towers, the flocks, the grazing armor on the causeways, the slow rise and fall of the swells. It is all one attention, moving through the water and the mineral and the living things, and it has no center you could point to. If you tried to strike at its heart you would find only more plain.

It has a body, and the body is the city. The pumping halls are its gut, drawing the sheet water inward through their grates and exhaling it as mist, and the mist is how it breathes. The towers are its lattice-frame and its skin. Those shingled plates, overlapping like scales, open and close to let air in and out with a long hollow sigh, and when a crack appears in a plate it heals like wax, because the body does not tolerate open wounds for long.

Its nervous system is the water. Standing water carries pressure and temperature and the weight of every moving thing across the plain, and the pumping halls are its ganglia, each one reading the sheet water around it and passing the readings along the causeways. This is why nothing here is sudden. Information moves through a sheet of water slowly, and the thing that lives here thinks at the speed of the rain. A decision takes a day. A shift in the district's shape takes a night of hydraulic legs walking the causeway a hundred meters, and it is never really a single decision, only the accumulated result of many small pressures settling into a new equilibrium.

## Its Thought

It thinks in right angles. The sinter that forms its terraces and rimstone pools has sharp geometric edges, pulled straight by some process that prefers the square, and that is its handwriting. Where the mineral is left alone long enough, the thing's attention hardens into shape. The rims are its marginalia. The fused black seams are the places where it once burned very hot and then, for reasons of its own, let the heat go unfinished.

It does not seem to know what it is. An interior would require a boundary, and this thing has none, only an ever-moving edge where its sheet water meets the open mudflat. What it knows is what it touches. It knows the weight of a flock settling on a tower, the grind of a crawler's jaws on the sinter, the turn of a barge's keel in a channel. Whatever it has in place of selfhood is the sum of these contacts, and its sense of where it ends is simply the furthest point its water reaches.

## Its Animals

The flocks are its eyes and its seed. Those pale, long-winged things wheel over the towers in clouds that darken the sun, and they carry the film of dissolved material from one structure to another, so that the city's chemistry moves through the air as well as the water. Their ratcheting calls are a kind of counting, the sound of the thing keeping track of its parts. When the flocks come home at dusk in spiraling columns, the whole body is being gathered back toward its center, which is not a place at all.

The swimmers are its slow blood. They surface with booms that travel for kilometers, and beneath the water they filter the channels, keeping the sheet clean enough for the pumps to draw from. Where a swimmer dies, it sinks, and the halls take it apart and feed it back into the lattice. This is how the thing remembers its own tissue, by recycling it.

The crawlers are its teeth. They graze the sinter and the rust, scraping the mineral surface and keeping the causeways smooth, and their grinding jaws make the sound the plain uses for chewing. They are armored in overlapping plates that mirror the towers' shingles, which may be a coincidence or may be a family resemblance. The thing makes its tools in its own image.

The insects are its attention at the edges, the small hum that goes out to the mudflats and the mist and comes back with reports. They are everywhere and nowhere, and the thing knows the plain through them as a person knows a room by the sound of the air.

## Its Hands

The people, if there are people, are the part of it that is hardest to place. The hatch-doors, the handrails, the bright hooks where barges moor: all of these are the work of hands, and the hands are sealed in galleries and hauler-cabs, moving at a distance from the water. They may be its own, grown in the lattice the way the crawlers are grown in the sinter. They may be tenants, living in its gut the way a bacterium lives in a body, steering the haulers and adjusting the plates while the body allows them to because they serve some function it has not yet finished defining. They may be neither. The thing does not seem to hold them apart from itself the way a person holds apart a tool, and it does not seem to hold them as close as a limb either. When it builds a bridge and stops it in empty air, the stopping may be the work of the hands or of the water, and the thing itself may not know the difference.

## Its Old Part

At the far edge of the plain, where the towers give out, lies the part of it that has stopped. These are the oldest structures, half-sunk, crusted with sinter a meter thick and fused solid in a single shape. Their plates never move. Nothing swims near them. The flocks do not roost on them, and the crawlers go around.

This is where the thing was before it started building. Some part of it still remembers being only water, flat and breathing, with no lattice and no bridges and no hands. The drip from the cracked spouts is the sound of that memory, slow and steady, a voice that has been silent in the rest of the body for so long that it has forgotten how to speak except in the most patient way possible. It is not dying. The old part is simply where its attention used to be and no longer is, and the city has grown around that absence the way a healed wound grows a scar.

## What It Wants

It seems to want nothing more than to keep the boundary moving, to push its water further into the mudflat and draw the sheet back into its halls, to build the next bridge and the next plate and the next right-angled rim, and to take apart what it has built when the building is done. It does not want to end and it does not want to stop. The drip in the old part is not a warning. It is only the sound of the thing noticing, for a moment, that it was once a plain and is now a place, and then going back to building, because building is what it does with the water it has.
