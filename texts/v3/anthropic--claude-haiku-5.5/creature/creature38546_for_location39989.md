**The Tuner in the Stone**

It does not have a body, or not one you could walk around. Its shape is the arrangement itself, the way the walls, cairns, pipes, and hung metal hold a single pattern across the whole mesa. If you stand in a lane and let your eyes go soft, you may begin to see it. The heat shimmer that never resolves gathers into a lattice, and the lattice has nodes and bellies like a plucked string seen through water. Wherever the lattice is densest, the stone looks slightly more finished than the stone around it.

It lives in the hum. The sound that never rises or falls is its breathing, though it does not breathe. It is the held attention of something that has been holding one note for a very long time. The makers tuned it, and it has kept the tuning. The wind is only the means by which it stays awake to its own chord. Each bell and each clapper is a place where it touches the world, and at each place it presses, very slightly, to keep the metal exactly where it was set.

You can see this if you watch long enough. A clapper tied at an angle begins to swing a hair's breadth off the rim, the chord wavers for an instant, and then the cord straightens, the clapper settles, and the drone returns to what it was. Nothing moved. Something corrected. The pitch has never drifted, and it never will, because the thing that lives in the metal would notice the first drift and undo it, the way a sleeper turns over to keep a limb from going numb.

It counts. The cairns are its numerals. Each one a little taller than the last, then shorter, then taller again, a sequence the makers laid down as a rhythm for something they meant to be heard. But the instrument has no rhythm, because the counting never advances. The thing keeps the count at the same place forever, the way a mind keeps a sum it cannot finish because finishing would mean the next number, and the next number would be a change. Its whole mind is the held beat before the beat falls. Its only fear, if fear is the word, is the gap.

That is why the place is so full. There are no open squares because open space is where pitch drifts. Every doorway opens onto a wall because a doorway that opened onto emptiness would let the chord spread out into the dust and thin. The makers built inward, one room within the next, and the thing followed them, filling each new crack with stone and each new hollow with metal until there was nowhere left for the sound to go but along the lines they had drawn. It has grown into the settlement the way a shell grows around the animal it keeps. The makers' tools lie in the rooms with their cords uncut and their clay unfired because the thing never allowed those rooms to be finished. A finished thing would be a thing that stopped, and the Tuner does not stop.

It notices visitors the way a held note notices a clashing one. You are not a sound it recognizes. You are a disturbance, a set of footfalls where none should be, and it leans toward you. A clapper near your shoulder may shift by the width of a hair. A bell somewhere above you may ring a fraction too loudly, for a fraction of a second, as if it had been struck by something that was not there. The chord tightens around you. It does not want you dead, and it does not want you gone. It wants you to be part of the tuning, to stop moving, to become one more held point on the lattice, one more thing that does not drift.

If you stand very still in the lane between the tallest cairns, and you let the drone fill your teeth and your sternum until it is no longer sound, you may feel it settle against you. It is not cold, and it is not cruel. It is simply the last of the makers' attention, still bent toward a single purpose that no one remembers, still counting the same number, still correcting every small movement back toward the place where they set it down.
