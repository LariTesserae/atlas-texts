**The Ones Who Stay**

The first people you meet are at the rail depot, where the ore trains take on water and the switchback crews sleep between shifts. They are not hard to spot, though it takes a moment to see what marks them. They stand with one hand resting on a wall or a railing, even while they're talking. They tilt their heads toward sounds that don't seem to concern them. When a rockfall booms in the valley, they all look up at the same moment, the way people in other places glance at a clock.

They call themselves the Plumb, after the weighted line that tells you whether a wall is still standing true. Most families have been on the shelf for four or five generations, and the names they give you are the names of cliffs and streams as much as of ancestors. A woman with a gap-toothed smile introduces herself as the daughter of the Eastern Scarp, which is not a figure of speech. Her mother kept the ledger for that cliff face, measuring its lean with a brass sighting rod every season, and the ledger is now three volumes thick and written in a shorthand only the family can read.

Their bodies have changed to fit the ground. Their inner ears are dense and quick, and they can keep their balance on a tilting platform that would send a visitor to the rail. Many have a slight, permanent squint, a habit of looking at the horizon rather than at the thing in front of them, since the thing in front of them may move. Their lungs take in the steam-thick air without complaint, and the children's breath has a faint mineral sweetness, which they say is how you know a child was born on the shelf. They do not sleep well in buildings that stand still. A family that has lived for years in a house with level floors will often complain of dreams in which the rooms tilt, and they move back out to the terraces, where the scaffolds creak and sway and the sleep comes easily.

They are not sentimental about the dead. A body is laid on the moss at the edge of the forest, and within a season the cloud forest has taken it. Markers are rarely used, because a marker set on a moving slope is a lie within a decade. Instead the living carry the dead's measurements forward. A grandmother's name lives in the bearing of a particular pipeline support, and when that support is reset, someone sings the bearing aloud, and the children learn it.

**The Arrivals**

Other people come up the rail: engineers from the lowland cities, surveyors, contractors on short contracts, and a steady trickle of people who simply climbed up one day and didn't go back. The shelf treats them with a kind of patient amusement. They arrive confident. They carry printed maps, and the maps drift. They plan bridges on paper and watch the slopes make a mockery of the plans, and most of them spend their first year furious about it. By the second year they have started to walk with a hand on the wall. By the third, they have begun to measure things without meaning to, and they can tell you, with the same offhand certainty as a Plumb, that the north face of the aqueduct has gained a finger's width since the thaw.

The ones who stay become something hard to name. They keep their own ledgers. They marry into the Plumb families, and their children have the squint and the sweet breath. The ones who leave go back down the rail and are, for a while, slightly unsteady on flat ground, and they describe the lowlands as strangely still, as if something important were being held in place there that nobody was allowed to see moving.

**The Crews**

The switchback crews are their own people. They ride the ore trains down from the new cliffs, sometimes for days at a stretch, and they live in the cars between loads. They are wiry and weathered, with hands scarred by the cooling basalt, which cuts like glass when it's freshly broken. Their songs are work songs, timed to the hammering of the couplings, and they have a rule that no one sings a new measurement until it has been checked by two people. They say the mountain listens, and that a wrong number sung aloud will be believed by the stone.

They are the ones most often caught by the stranger geometry. A crew once spent eleven hours on a grade that should have taken forty minutes, and when they came out the other side of the switchback, the train was facing the way it had come, with its cargo intact and its driver grinning. They never explained it. They simply tell younger crew members to watch the trees on the approach and to count the steps in the station stairwells, and to stop counting if the number changes.

**The Grazers and the Birds**

The long-necked grazers are almost domestic, though no one owns them in the usual sense. Children walk them through the terraces each morning, following the moss that grows thickest on the pipelines. The grazers keep their own counsel. They know which pipes are warm and which have gone cold, and they will refuse to cross a valley where the ground has begun to hum, long before any instrument registers a change. The Plumb trust them the way a sailor trusts a barometer.

The birds have moved into the machinery entirely. The pale flocks nest on the cranes, and over generations their calls have absorbed the rhythms of the compressors. A bird that grew up on a pumping station will sing in a cadence that matches the pump, and a flock that roosts near the refineries will fall silent in unison whenever the stacks flare. At dusk, when the forest chorus rises, the machines seem to answer, and the Plumb say that the birds are keeping the machines in tune, or that the machines are keeping the birds in step. Nobody has settled which.

The luminous insects gather around the stacks like a second, smaller weather. Their drifts pile against the foundations each dawn, and the Plumb sweep them into carts and carry them to the composting beds, where they are said to make the richest soil on the shelf. The children are warned not to lie down in the drifts. Nobody says what would happen. Nobody has needed to.

**What Lives in the Stone**

There is one more inhabitant, and it is the hardest to describe, because it is not clearly alive, and no one on the shelf is entirely sure it isn't.

In the freshest basalt, in the vesicles that stipple the new faces like the crust of bread, there is a fine, pale film that grows only in the first years after a rock breaks open. It is not lichen, not moss, not any kind of fungus anyone has classified. It takes up the mineral grit and the mist and turns them into something soft and faintly warm to the touch. The Plumb call it the skin, and they are careful with it. They will not quarry a face where the skin has begun to form, and when a cliff is cut open, they climb up to see whether the film has appeared on the new stone. When it has, they leave a small offering of water, usually from the drip of the cloud forest, poured into a hollow in the rock.

The engineers laugh at this. The surveyors record the film as a biological anomaly and move on. But the Plumb point out that the cliffs that have skin are the ones that stop shifting after a few years, and that the cliffs without it keep drifting until they collapse. They do not claim the skin is doing this deliberately. They say only that the mountain seems to want to settle, and that the skin is the first thing that agrees.

The rain that falls upward at the forest's edge sometimes lifts a little of the skin into the mist with it. Nobody has seen where those particles go. The Plumb say they are not lost, only moving up, and that the cloud is the mountain's way of holding onto what it has made.

**The Keeper of the Ledgers**

The oldest person on the shelf is a woman named Oru Venn, who is said to be somewhere past ninety, though the Plumb have stopped counting birthdays in favor of counting the bearing of the Eastern Scarp. She lives in a stilted house at the edge of the terraces, on a platform that tilts a few degrees every winter. She has kept the Scarp's ledger for sixty-one years. Her hands are steady. Her squint has deepened into something almost aristocratic. When she speaks, she pauses between sentences, listening to the stone beneath the floor.

She does not think of herself as the owner of the cliff, or even its measurer. She thinks of herself as the mountain's memory, a set of eyes and a pen that the rock has borrowed in order to know its own shape. When you ask her what happens when she dies, she looks out toward the Scarp, which is visible through the cloud as a pale ridge with a dark slash of new scree at its foot, and says that someone will take up the sighting rod, and the mountain will go on noticing itself, one finger's width at a time.

Then she goes back to her ledger, and writes down the day's bearing in her careful shorthand, and the stone under the house ticks once, quietly, as if in agreement.
