# The Step-Folk

The people of the massif call themselves by the word for a terrace, which is also the word for a household line. Someone who says "I am of the third shelf, the north lip" is naming a family, a parcel of land, and a set of obligations all at once. Outsiders from the plain call them the Stepfolk, a name they tolerate with mild amusement.

They are not many. A few thousand live on the massif, spread across hundreds of shelves, most in clusters of four to thirty households where the terraces are wide enough to hold them. Many have the broad shoulders and strong hands of people who spend their lives climbing, carrying, and hauling water. Their skin is weathered, their hair is usually bound up and kept dry, and they move on slopes with a loose, low-centered gait that looks careless until you try to follow them down a wet ramp. Their eyes are often pale from years of working under mist and glare. The old people sometimes go deaf in one ear from the cataract roar, and nobody thinks much of it.

## Dress and Dwelling

Clothing is layered and practical. Oiled outer cloth sheds rain, and wide-brimmed hats are woven from split bamboo-like stalks, dyed in the dark juice of the orchard fruits so the color stains everything it touches. Sandals have soles cut from old conveyor belt, which grip wet panels better than anything the plain produces. Jewelry is rare, but people wear small pieces of salvage: a bit of copper wire, a bolt with a hole worn through it, a scrap of panel with a maker's mark that no one can read anymore.

Houses are built into the terrace walls or set against them, with the back against the fill and the front open to the sun. Roofs are the underside of the shelf above, which gives every home a ceiling of someone else's floor. The people think this is sensible. The shelf above keeps off the rain, and the shelf below keeps the house from sliding. Many homes have a second room dug into the fill, cool and dry, where food and seed are stored and where the dead are kept for a few days before the fill takes them.

## Keeping

The central work of the people is called keeping. Each household is responsible for a span of terrace: clearing the catchment lip, checking the drainage channel, scouring the settling tank, reseating loose panels, cutting back roots that push into seams, replanting where the slope has slumped. Keeping never finishes. The people say the mountain is always about to fall, and the only reason it has not is that someone is always busy holding it.

The sluices and pumps are kept by a few families who specialize in them. These keepers know the timers by ear. They have learned that a certain click means a gate is about to open, and a certain grind means a bearing is failing, and they have memorized the sequence of the whole system as a long chant that children learn before they learn to count. Ask a keeper why a particular sluice opens at dusk and they will tell you, "Because it always has," and that is usually the truth as far as anyone has traced it. The chant is correct where it matters. Nobody has rewritten it in living memory, and nobody wants to.

## What They Know

The people do not remember building the mountain, and their stories do not claim they did. The oldest tellings say the massif was "carried up," that the stone came from somewhere lower and was set in courses by hands that are now gone. The people are not sure whether those hands were ancestors, strangers, or something else. Their word for the builders is the same word they use for the weather above the peaks, which suggests they have never settled the question.

Old engineering survives on the panels as marks: symbols cut or stamped into the cast faces, arrows and circles and numbers in patterns that repeat. The people treat these as boundary markers, and in a sense they are. A household that owns the shelf with a particular mark on its lip has a claim that the whole valley recognizes. Few people can read the marks as anything more than ownership, but a handful of scholars in the lower villages have worked out that some of the numbers match the timing intervals of the sluices. They keep this to themselves, partly from caution and partly because the knowledge would change nothing about the daily work.

The machines in the core are called the quiet ones. The people do not enter the deep service tunnels, except to clear bat guano or to retrieve something that has fallen. The bats are left alone. The quiet ones hum faintly through the walls, and the people feel it in their feet, the way you feel a large animal breathing in the next room. Some families leave small offerings at the tunnel mouths: a bowl of rice, a length of copper wire, a child's drawing. No one has asked the machines whether they want these things.

## The Course

When a household finishes a major repair, such as rebuilding a collapsed retaining wall or relaying a stretch of drainage channel, they hold a rite called the course. They lay one new stone, or one new panel, in the wall they have rebuilt, and they press a thumb into the wet mortar and leave a print. The print is not a signature. It is an acknowledgment that the wall is now partly theirs and partly the mountain's, and that the mountain will absorb their labor as it absorbs everything.

Marriage is marked by an exchange of stones. A couple joining two households each brings a small stone from their own shelf and sets it into the wall of the other's home. The stones stay there. Over generations, a house can become a kind of mosaic of its family's history, each course a marriage or a repair or a death.

Death is handled by the fill. A body is kept in the cool room for three days, washed, dressed in plain oiled cloth, and then carried to a trench dug in the lower slope, where it is covered with fill and planted over with a tree. The people say the trees grow faster above the dead. Those who have watched closely insist this is true. Trees are the only monuments the people build, and a grove on a lower terrace is often the record of several generations of households. Walking the lower shelves is, in part, walking through a cemetery that no one thinks of as one.

## Time

The people count time differently from the plain. A year is a cycle of rains, but they also reckon in settlings, the span of time it takes a terrace to shift enough that the lip needs resetting. A settling is roughly a decade, though it varies by shelf. Children are often told their age in settlings, and an elder who has seen three settlings on the north lip is spoken of with respect.

Their language is rich in words for slope, water, and gradual change, and poor in words for sudden events. There is no common word for "disaster." A collapse is described as a shelf that has "finished leaning," and a flood is a channel that has "remembered its old course." The phrasing is not a denial of catastrophe. The people have lived through slides and floods, and they grieve them. But they describe such events as moments in a longer motion, the way a sudden gust is a moment in a long wind.

## Relations with the Plain

The plain people trade with the massif through the cable lines and the lower road. The Stepfolk send salvage, cured fruit, dyed cloth, and occasionally a skilled keeper who will be paid to fix a pump in a lowland village. In return they receive metal, tools, medicine, and the occasional book, which they treat with a mixture of fascination and suspicion. A written page is an odd thing to them, a surface that holds a message without any slope or water or cut to tie it to the world. They have a saying that writing is what happens when the mountain forgets to move.

There is tension, as there always is between people who hold a place and people who merely pass through it. The plain wants the turbines and the water. The massif wants to be left to its keeping. Most years the two sides manage an uneasy peace, held together by the cables and by the simple fact that the plain needs the water and the mountain needs the metal.

## What Lives Here

The people of the massif are not separate from the mountain's ecology. They are part of it: keepers, planters, and maintainers, who have shaped the slopes as the slopes have shaped them. The birds nest in their rafters, the frogs sing in their paddies, the bats share their tunnels, and the trees grow over their dead. In the evening, when the frogs begin on every level at once and the lamps come on along the lower walkways, a household on the fourth shelf will be sitting on its step with its feet in the cooling channel, listening to the sluice chant drift up from the keepers' post, and feeling the faint hum of the quiet ones through the stone.

They are, in the end, what the mountain is becoming: a people who live inside a slow process and have learned to hold their own lives within its rhythm. They do not expect to finish anything. They expect to keep.
