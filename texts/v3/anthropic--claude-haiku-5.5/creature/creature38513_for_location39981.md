**Who Lives Here**

The people come first, because they are the only ones who can be counted. There are perhaps forty of them, scattered across the terraces in the sturdiest buildings: a former bank with its vault still intact, a hospital annex with tiled walls, the upper floors of a cement works. They are not a community in any tidy sense. They are a crew, kept together by the pumps the way a watch crew is kept together by a ship.

They look like people who have been out of the sun for a long time. Their skin has a faint cast, not sickly but damp-pale, and their forearms are mottled where the mildew has crept under their sleeves and been scrubbed back, again and again. Their hands are stained, dark from iron and sap, the nails split and dark at the edges. Their boots have deep cleats, because the root-mat is slick and will fold a foot without warning. Their clothes are oilcloth and cut-down canvas, always damp, always smelling faintly of the basin. Most of them cough in the mornings and stop coughing by midday, and nobody remarks on it.

They speak little, and loudly when they do. The pumps take the bottom out of every voice, so conversation happens in shouts at the length of a street, or in the hand signs they have developed for valves, leaks, and clear water. They name the pumps the way sailors name ships, and they talk about them as temperamental. Number Four has a bad seal in wet weather. Number Two groans before it fails. They know the rhythm of the thud in their sleep.

Their work is cutting. Every path they open closes within a season, so the days are a patient liturgy of machetes, shears, and fire-blackened sawblades working back the leaves, while the thing they are cutting returns the favor by growing a little more the next morning. They keep a ledger of the rail cars, which ones are still recoverable and which have been swallowed past saving, and every year the ledger has fewer lines in the recoverable column. They do not go down into the lower streets unless the pumps demand it. When they do go, they go in pairs, roped together, and they come back quieter than they left.

Still, the people are not the main thing that lives here. The green is.

It is hard to say where one plant ends in the basin. The climbers that wrap the warehouses are one organism wearing many bodies, their aerial roots running down every mortar seam and into every gutter until the whole district is threaded with a single fine network, pale as hair where it is young and dark as cable where it is old. The fused trees are not separate trees. They are a body grown into and around itself, and across the street a trunk that holds an iron sign in its heartwood is part of the same body as the moss in the cellar below it. The moss does not so much cover the floor as hold it, binding the loading docks and the rail beds into one soft continuous skin. Nothing that falls here stays fallen. A dropped branch is gone within a season, taken into the mat and returned as growth somewhere else, so the green is not only alive but digests, and the district is its gut.

It has a kind of intention, though not a mind in any sense a person would recognize. It is patient in the way water is patient. It does not hurry, and it does not need to. It has all the time that the stone has, and it spends that time on taking the walls apart, one root in one seam at a time, and turning them into soil.

It is also the thing that keeps the rest of the residents away. The absence of animals is its doing as much as anything. No birds nest in a canopy that is already complete. No insects hum in air this thick with sap and rot. The basin has the eerie fullness of a house with no room left in it. Even the water, which should carry fish or frogs or the small things that live in ponds, has been claimed by the floating mats and the reed-marsh, so the black lake holds only plants and the reflection of the rim.

And there is the pumps. They are the only thing in the basin the green has not taken, and they are not quite part of it either. They are a kind of counterpart, machines that breathe the water out and up through their iron throats, keeping the lake from closing over the lower streets and the district from becoming a marsh. They are tended by people who have learned to love and resent them in equal measure. The green does not love them. It works on them quietly, a seam here, a pipe there, a sign swallowed into a trunk two streets over. The keepers see this and cut it back, and the cutting goes on as long as the diesel does.

So the picture is less a town with some plants in it than a living mass with a small human crew stationed inside its body, keeping a few doors open. The people are guests who came for the industry and stayed to maintain the water. The green is the resident, and the resident has been here the whole time, and it does not need to be told what it is.

Once a day, at midday, a white shaft comes down through the rim and lands on the few square blocks of floor that the sun can reach. For an hour the leaves there blaze, and the keepers stop what they are doing and look up into it, the only outside thing that comes in without asking. Then the light moves on, the green closes over the place it touched, and the basin goes back to being what it has always been: a bowl of water and warmth and slow, patient growth, with the machines still thudding in the middle of it, holding the sea back for now.
