The basin has no animals, so the animal-shaped places in it are filled by other things, and those things are its inhabitants.

The first are the ferns, tall as a person and tall in their patience. Each stands in its own slow stillness until the rain comes, and then every frond lifts and shivers at once, shedding water in bright strings. They grow in clumps that have clearly been buried and dug out many times. Some have stems bent into shapes that record old landslides, and when a stand is buried, the ferns don't die so much as wait under the rubble, then push up through the grey debris in fresh spikes, uncoiling as though they had only paused. They are the basin's most persistent residents, always returning to the places that have just finished erasing them.

The fungal shelves are the watchers. They climb the stone in tiers like the seats of some enormous theater, and at night they carry their cold blue-green light, dimmest when dry and brightest after rain, when the whole forest seems to wake and brighten. They are slow to change shape but quick to swell, and their glow marks the trunks and tangles so that anything moving through the dark would find its way by them. Nothing moves through the dark here, though, and so the fungi keep their light for no one, the way a lighthouse keeps its beam for empty water.

The lichens are the patient tenants. They cover the rock where the rock is oldest, orange and rust and frost-grey, and they grow by the width of a hair in a generation. They are the only residents that touch the young stone directly, and they are slowly turning the newest fractures into something softer. Ask them how long they have lived here and they would answer in centuries, but the basin changes faster than they can measure, so they are always starting over on surfaces that will soon split away.

The trunks are tall and pale, and they bleed. Their bark peels in long strips and sap runs down them in slow drops, thick and sweet-smelling in the wet air. They lean into one another for support, and the vines drape between them in curtains, so that the canopy is less a roof than a single tangled body. When a trunk falls, the vines drag down their neighbors and the moss tears loose in slabs, and the whole basin shifts a little under its weight. The trees feel like the basin's lungs, though they breathe only the cloud.

Beneath everything the root mats weave their arches and tunnels over the streams. Water moves through them unseen, and the roots hold the banks together as the banks try to fall into the water. They are the basin's circulation, the thin dark vessels that carry the wet from the rim into the mere and back out again.

The mere is the heart, or at least the place where the basin's blood comes to rest. It is dark and still, with a skin of foam and the smell of sulfur, and it takes in everything the streams bring: silt, gravel, dust from the grinding walls, fragments of fallen fern and bark. It settles them slowly, and the forest grows from what settles. The mere is never quite at rest, though. Its shoreline moves every week, and it shudders when the springs push up beneath it.

The springs are the pulse. They break through fissures in the rock and push water up through narrow throats with a thump, steaming in the cold, leaving mineral shelves that rise and collapse as the water finds new paths. Each spring is a small rhythmic knot, and together they beat across the floor like many hearts that do not quite agree on tempo.

The vents in the eastern wall are the breath. They release their steam in gusts that build, peak, and stop, on a cycle as regular as an engine's exhaust. When they fall silent the basin seems to hold its breath until the next one comes.

And the mountains are the oldest residents, though it would be wrong to call them still. They are young, their faces still shattered in plates the size of houses, and they are coming apart in the same slow violence that builds them. Rockfalls come every few minutes somewhere along their flanks, rumbling and clattering down into dust that hangs in the cloud. Their spires lean and break and rise again in the night. They are not alive in any sense that the ferns or the lichens would recognize, but they are grinding and changing with the same restless energy as the rest of the basin. The mountains are the raw material of everything else here, and they are also the force that keeps unmaking it.

So who lives in the Stamping Basin? Not a creature that eats or flies or looks back at you. Instead there are the ferns that rise after every burial, the fungi that glow for no one, the lichens that take centuries to write their few lines on stone, the trees that bleed and lean, the mere that receives everything, the springs and vents that beat and breathe, and the mountains that are slowly falling apart into the life that grows on them. All of it is one process, passing through its own cycle over and over: stone is ground into dust, water carries the dust down, the springs drive the water back up, and the green things grow from the silt and fall again. Nothing here waits to be understood. The basin simply keeps working, and its inhabitants are the working itself.
