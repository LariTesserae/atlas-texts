**What Lives in the Sump of Halle Reach**

Nothing here announces itself. You have to stand still in the fog for a while before the basin starts to show its inhabitants, and even then it shows them one layer at a time. The first thing you notice is the shiver on the water, a faint tremor across the shallows, as if the surface were breathing on its own. That is the drifts.

**The drifts**

They are small crustaceans, each about the size of a thumbnail, with pale, glossy shells that take the milky light and hold it. Alone, a drift animal is nothing much: a few jointed legs, a pair of stalked eyes folded flat under the carapace, a mouth that works constantly at the film. Together they are a single body spread over a few hundred square meters. They turn as one when the water rises and settle as one when it drains. When they gather at the top of a cycle, they make the dry rattle that is almost the only sound in the basin, like sand poured slowly into a cup. Their shells are the basin's finest mineral work, layered like the bands in the silt, and each new molt is a little thicker than the last.

**The salvage-eels**

Longer and darker, these move through the silt in ridged, segmented bodies, surfacing at the margins to feed on the blue-black mats and then sliding back into the mud. Their hides are studded with what the basin has given them: flakes of old metal, shards of green glass, the stripped rollers and bearing-caps of the conveyor. They fix each piece as they grow, and the older ones are armored in a loose, uneven mail of salvage that clinks when they move. You can track one by the sound before you see it. Sometimes a young eel passes beneath a fragment of brass and takes the fitting into its flank, and within a season the thing is grown over, a relic carried along a living seam. They are the basin's collectors, though they are not collecting for any purpose. They simply cannot help taking what touches them.

**The moths**

They hang above the water in clouds too thin to be called swarms. Their wings are stiff and translucent, veined in copper-colored tracing that catches the dim light the way wire catches a lamp. They settle on the pump housings and the conveyor rollers and graze the mineral bloom there, rising in a single lifting shimmer whenever the impeller clanks. At the top of the seep-cycle they seem to thicken over the flats; at the bottom they vanish into the crust. Their humming sits just above hearing, and you feel it more than you hear it, a pressure behind the ears.

**The grazers**

Heavy and slow, they wade along the margins with their domed carapaces low in the water. Each back is crusted with the pale knobs, so that from a distance they are indistinguishable from outcrops, and you can walk past one twice before you realize it has moved. They are the basin's gardeners in the most literal sense. They carry the knobs from the drier crust into the wet hollows, and they carry the film back out. Where the grazers have walked, the mats grow in long, parallel tracks. Some of the older ones have brass fittings lodged in their shells, green and sheathed in film, so that they look, in the milky light, like small machines that have decided to become landscape.

**The knobs**

They push up from the crust in clusters, each the size of a fist, pale and pocked like pumice. They are fungal in some sense, or were, and they take the dissolved mineral directly out of the water and bind it into their own bodies. On cool mornings they exhale a faint bitter vapor, and when you walk through a stand of them you taste iron on the back of your tongue. They do not move. They do not need to. They are the basin's chemists, slowly converting the water's patience into stone.

**The mats**

They cover the wet hollows in leathery, blue-black sheets, thin as paper at the edges and thick as a mattress where they have had time to build. They wrinkle under touch and leave a stain that does not wash out for days. Everything in the basin feeds on them, and they feed on the minerals the knobs release and the metal the water breaks down. Where metal has lain submerged long enough, the film forms first as a bloom and then as a sheath, and the mats are the membrane through which the basin turns what falls into it into something it can keep.

**The deep runner**

It is never seen whole. In the deeper channels something moves fast beneath the surface, and the only evidence is a ring spreading on the water, a sudden silence where the drifts had been rattling, and the shiver of the crustaceans scattering in a wave that runs ahead of the thing like a pressure front. It is the predator the eel-things avoid and the moths never rise above. Its hunting peaks at the top of each cycle, when the drifts gather in the shallows and the water rises over the flats, and then it withdraws to the channels as the table falls. Whatever its body is made of, it has been feeding here long enough that the basin's cycle has become its own rhythm.

**The pumps and the gate**

These are not animals, but they belong to the same arrangement. The nearest impeller turns once every several minutes, a slow clank that carries across the flats long after it stops. Its intake pipes angle down into the water like the necks of drowned animals. Its counterweight sighs against the sluice gate when the water rises, and the whole mechanism runs on the basin's own pressure, as if the old works had been assembled to listen to the water and respond. The drifts gather near the intake. The moths settle on its housing to graze. The eels pass beneath its channel. Nobody maintains it, and it does not need maintaining. It is a heart that beats because the body around it keeps pushing blood through it.

**The basin itself**

If you ask what the whole thing is, the answer is not any one of these. The basin is the sum of the cycle: water rising over four days, holding for three, draining over four, and starting again. Every creature here is a part of that breath. The drifts gather on the rise. The runner hunts on the rise. The eels retreat on the drain, and the knobs exhale on the drain, and the mats thicken in the quiet between. The floor rises a little each season, layer by layer, and the fauna carry the old works down into it, shell by shell, until the pumps and the tower and the conveyor are simply the highest things in a gently growing field.

Stand still in the center long enough and the water will be warmer under your boots than the air above it. The silt will feel firmer than it looked, as though something has been packing it down, patiently, from below. The drifts will begin their rattle. The counterweight will sigh. The impeller will click once, far off, and the moths will lift and settle, and you will understand that the basin is not empty. It has never been empty. It is a body made of its own small bodies, working toward a shape nobody designed, and it has been at it far longer than anyone has been counting.
