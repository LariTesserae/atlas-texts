The people of Seep-Hollow walk differently from strangers, and they know it. A stranger's first steps across the flats go through the crust with a wet sound, and the locals watch them with a patience that isn't quite kind. The people who live here roll their weight onto the outside edge of each foot and spread their toes, so the load goes out across the silt instead of down through it. Children learn this before they learn to speak. Their feet are broad and hard-soled, stained grey at the heel, and many wind their ankles in nettle cord as a matter of habit.

Their skin is pale and a little soft, the color of the fog that is always around them, and it has a faint sheen in the mornings from the damp. Their eyes are large-pupilled, adapted to a light that never casts a hard edge. They are poor at seeing sharp distinctions and very good at seeing gradations. A grown woman can tell a seep from a dry mat at thirty paces by the difference in grey, and a man can tell from the color of a pan's surface whether the water beneath is rising or falling. When the sun does break through, they squint and turn their faces away, and within the hour they are indoors again, as if the brightness were a rumor they had heard about.

They touch before they trust. Visitors notice this first: a local will lay a flat palm on a wall, a sill, a roof-post, and hold it there a moment, feeling for weeping or warmth or the faint tremor that means the stone is shifting under the surface. Their hands are scarred with pale knapping cuts that have healed into seams, and their fingertips have hardened into something close to slate. Tar lives in the creases of their knuckles no matter how often they scrape.

Their voices are low and unhurried. A shout in Seep-Hollow is treated as a small violence, partly because walls muffle it into nothing and partly because the ground answers loud sounds with cracks. Conversation happens close, heads nearly touching, in a register that sounds to outsiders like murmuring and to the speakers like ordinary talk. They have many words for water and almost none for wind, which seldom reaches them through the rooftops.

Their bodies keep time with the swell. Most adults wake a little before the rise, a fraction of a breath before the platform under them lifts a finger-width on its driftwood stilts. Sleepers in the lower tiers sometimes wake with the sense that the floor has tilted and righted itself, and they lie still until the feeling passes. Families move up a tier each generation, step by step, the way the sills are raised, and a household's position on the rise is read as a kind of record of how long it has been there.

Sill-raising is their central rite. Every family lifts its threshold a hand higher over the course of a life, and the old sills are not discarded. They are pulled out, carried inside, and laid beneath the floorboards, so that under every house in Seep-Hollow there is a stack of stone thresholds marking each generation's rise. A child who wants to know how long her family has been here can lift a board and count. Outsiders find this macabre. The people find it comforting, the way other peoples keep graves.

Metal is scarce, and a nail is handled like a relic, wrapped in cloth and passed from hand to hand at weddings and deaths. The one kiln at the crest is tended by a few families who guard their clay recipes as jealously as they guard their nails, and the pots that come out of it are given away at births, because a family with a good pot will not go hungry through a wet season.

The boat-builders are honored above almost everyone, and their work is the most ornate thing the town makes. The reed hulls are painted in patterns of ochre and soot that look like decoration to visitors and are, to the people, maps. The curves follow the seams where water runs closest to the surface, the marks along the gunwales show where the drainage cuts are deep enough to float a loaded boat, and a skilled boatman can read a painted hull and know the route without ever having sailed it. Children learn the designs as they learn songs, by repetition, and a boat with a damaged pattern is considered unsafe to launch until a painter has restored it.

They have a story about the lake. Their elders say that once the water stood high enough to drown the ridge, and that it went out through the fault in a single night. Sometimes, digging footings, someone turns up the pale curled shell of a freshwater mussel, far above any seep that now runs through the silt, and the finder carries it up to the crest and sets it in the kiln's shelf, where the heat keeps it for no reason anyone can name. They are a little embarrassed by how much they like these shells.

Among themselves they disagree about the absence of large animals. Some call it a mercy: nothing to hunt them, nothing to hunt for them, no predator to fear in the fog. Others call it an omen, a sign that the land hasn't decided yet whether anything living should be allowed to grow large here. The argument goes on at every gathering, quietly, close-headed, and nobody expects it to end.

What the elders say, when they say anything about the whole arrangement, is that the land is still deciding what shape it will take, and that the town is a wager on the answer. They do not say this with despair. They say it the way a farmer speaks of weather. And they keep building, a hand higher at a time, lifting each threshold as the ground rises under them and laying the old stones carefully beneath the floor, so that whatever the land becomes, the record of their rising stays under their feet.

At dusk, if you walk the crooked passages, you will find people kneeling at their doorsills with a stone in one hand and a mallet in the other, setting the new threshold into the wet silt and tapping it true. They do this slowly. They press a palm flat against the frame and wait, feeling the ground breathe under them, until the fog comes down thick enough to hide the edges of the roofs, and the sound of dripping eaves fills the quiet.
