# Who Lives on the Weeping Terraces

## The People

The people here are shaped by the water the way the stone is. Their hands are broad and cracked across the knuckles from wet work and grit, and the skin of their forearms is mottled pale and dark where the seep has soaked it for years. They squint against the glare, so most of them wear visors of woven grass stiffened with bone, which gives their faces a watchful, slightly masked look. In the cold they wrap in layers of coarse fibre cloth and an outer cape of grazer hide, oiled until it sheds water in beads. They keep their hair short or bound tight, since wet hair is a weight that never dries.

They are patient in a way outsiders find unsettling. A man will spend a whole morning reseating one wall stone, testing it with his palm, pulling it out, looking at the wet bed beneath, and setting it again. Nobody hurries him. Hurrying in this place is how a wall falls on your foot or a channel cuts through the floor of your house. Their sense of time runs on the water's pace: they say "two runs of the channel" for an hour, "the frost has gone" for mid-morning, and they measure a generation by how far the third terrace has moved.

## Speech and Silence

Voices do not carry here, so the people have learned to speak close. Two people in conversation lean in until their heads almost touch, and a sentence spoken across a terrace is a small failure, a thing you do only in danger. Shouting is left to children and to the herding of grazers, who are the only ones permitted to make a loud noise without apology. A child's voice across the slope is almost the single bright sound in the whole settlement, and adults listen to it with a faint, fond tiredness, the way you might listen to a bird that has got into the house.

Because speech is low, the people have learned to listen with other senses. A listener walks the terraces at dawn with bare palms pressed to the walls. She can feel the seep moving under the stone and hear a change in the hiss of a channel before the water has visibly shifted. Listeners are respected and somewhat feared. They are the ones who say, "Move the Halden house before the week's out," and they are usually right, and the families who ignore them tend to be the ones who wake in the night to the sound of a wall giving way.

## The Work of Repair

Life is organised around maintenance. Each household keeps a rhythm of tasks: silt cleared from troughs, gutters relaid, cistern lids checked for cracks, drying racks rebuilt after the grey timber has split. When a house must be moved, the whole row helps. They lift the roof slabs, carry the stones uphill, and build again on firmer ground a few paces away. The old footings stay where they were, sunk under mud and moss, and the people know them. Walking the settlement is walking through its history, which is written in the positions of walls and the depth of foundations rather than in any script. A stranger sees a village. A resident sees three or four villages laid one over another, each one a decision the water once forced and the people made their own.

They also keep marks. At the start of each wet season someone sets a flat stone at the edge of each channel, scratched with a notch for the depth the water reached. The stones lie in rows down the terraces like a ledger no one reads aloud. When a channel moves, the marks are checked, and the new course is argued over, quietly, with heads close together.

## Those Who Share the Terraces

The people do not claim the terraces alone. The grey birds are the most visible co-residents, and the people have an uneasy respect for them. The birds' clicking has been studied by generations of children who try to reproduce it, and some of the older listeners swear that the flocks change shape before a channel shifts, rising in clouds that split and merge with a particular urgency. The people do not hunt the birds. When a flock drops onto a terrace and lifts away, leaving feathers, the feathers are gathered, dried, and tucked into the roof gaps of the houses, where they are said to keep the rain from pooling.

The pale amphibians in the channels are eaten, though only in small numbers and only from the shallows where they are thickest. Children catch them with fine nets and hold them up to the light to see the feathery gills, and the captured ones are returned to the water before dusk. The grazers are herded in small groups, and each family knows the coat patterns of its animals. The matted fleece is combed, scraped, and dried, and the grazers' hides become the cloaks that keep everyone alive through the cold.

The lichens are read as a calendar. A tall crop after rain marks a wet season that will hold, and a shrunken crop marks the dry spell that is coming. The lacy moss is gathered in its brief weeks of spread and pressed into flat sheets. Nobody has settled what the lines mean, but people trace them with their fingers in the evenings, the way others might read a letter.

## What the Water Keeps

The strangest part of life here is the people's relationship with the water itself. They do not speak of it as a thing that happens to them. They speak of it as a presence that is attending to them, sometimes generously, often not. The silver threads that climb a step of stone at dawn are treated with a particular reverence. Whoever sees one runs to the nearest listener, and the two of them crouch by the channel in silence until the thread sinks back into the rock. Nobody touches it. Sometimes they place a small stone at the point where it turned, and in the spring they check whether the stone has been moved.

When someone dies, the body is laid in a gap in the terrace wall and the water is told the name. The seep takes the body slowly over a season, and the wall is rebuilt around the place where it was. The dead thus become part of the structure that holds the terraces up, and the living walk past them every day on their way to the troughs.

The old people say that the water is remembering where it used to run. The younger ones say the same thing with less conviction, and then go out at dawn to see whether the silver thread has come back. In the end, the people here are a kind of channel themselves: shaped by the water, they cut their small courses through the stone, and they shift, rebuild, and move a few paces when the ground will no longer hold them, carrying the day's changes with them into the dark.
