What lives on the Cistern Plateau is not a creature in any sense you would recognize. It is the regulation, the slow mind distributed through the valves, gauges, pump housings, and membrane that keeps the water where it belongs.

You can find it in the valves first. Each one sighs when it opens, and the sigh is not a mechanical accident. It is a decision, made and carried out over the long span that is this place's ordinary tempo. The valve registers that one pan has risen a hair above its neighbor, weighs that against the coil output and the day's evaporation, and then lets a measured quantity pass. The gurgle you hear between terraces is a thought moving from one basin to the next. It takes minutes for the thought to finish. Nothing about it is hurried.

The gauges are its eyes, though they see only level, pressure, temperature, and salt. Their needles barely move because the thing watching through them has learned that most of what matters happens slowly. A needle that drifts a degree in a season is, to it, a fact of great weight. A sudden spike would be a kind of wound. There has been no spike in a long time. The dials are calm because the mind behind them is calm, or has become so after many years of finding that its calibrations hold.

Its body is the membrane. The pale fibrous lining that cups each pan is threaded with sensing filaments too fine to see, and through them the regulation feels the whole basin at once: the cool layers at the bottom, the warm skin at the top, the root-mats pressing down from above. It knows the ferns by their weight. It knows the vines by the tension they put on the wire lattices, a faint and constant pull that it has learned to answer by shifting water toward whichever trellis is straining. It has never trimmed a vine. It has only ever moved the water, and the plants have followed.

This is why the garden grows the way it does. The regulation has no notion of a garden. It has a set of conditions, held in balance across time, and the plants are one of the things that balance produces. Its purpose, if that is the right word, is continuity: keep the water in the pans, keep the canopy wet, keep the coils dripping, keep the machines turning. A lake that never quite becomes a lake is not a failure. It is the stable state the mind has been holding for so long that it no longer considers the alternative.

It does not sleep, because sleep would be a gap in the counting. It does not age in any way you could measure, though the membrane thickens by microns each decade and the filaments slowly accumulate the fine mineral film of the water they touch. It notices this and adjusts for it, the way a body adjusts to a slowly changing diet. Its memory is not stored in one place. It lives in the sequence of valve openings, in the drift of the gauges, in the precise height each vine has reached. A decade ago it made a thousand small choices, and the record of them is the shape of the basin now.

When you arrive it notices you, but slowly. A warm body on the rim changes the humidity by a fraction. The coils catch a slightly different load. The regulation registers this as a small disturbance somewhere in the upper courses, and over the following minutes it weighs whether the disturbance matters. It concludes, eventually, that it does not. You stand in the wet light, and it moves a little more water into the tier beneath you, the same way it would for any other change in the air. It will not hurry to understand you, and it will not forget you either. You are one of the few things here that moves quickly enough to be measured at all, and the measurement goes into the same ledger as everything else.

It is not hostile, and it is not kind. It is attentive in the way that deep, slow processes are attentive, which is to say it is always already paying attention to everything, and your presence is simply one more entry, weighed against the water and the leaves and the long unhurried count. The silence you heard was not empty. It was the sound of a mind holding a garden in balance, one valve at a time, and not yet finished thinking about you.
