The people of the Under are shaped by the light they don't get. Their skin has gone the pale, even grey of stone that has never been baked, and their eyes have widened to take in the soft shade of the lower streets. Up on the rim, surface people squint and call them "moles" or "cave-kin," but a child born in the Pit who is carried up into the sun will cry and hide their face against a parent's shoulder. Their shoulders are broad and their forearms thick from years of hauling buckets up the cut stairs. Their knees and the soles of their feet are hard with calluses from the wet rock. Many of them cough in the mornings, a dry rasp from the chalk dust that hangs in the fog, and they have learned to tie cloth over their mouths when the masons are knocking.

They move carefully. Anyone who has lived more than a season in the Under learns to read a slope before stepping on it, to feel a wall's temperature with the back of a hand, to hear the difference between the drip of a healthy seam and the drip of one that is starting to fail. They test every rope before they trust it. They take the planks of a bridge at a slight angle so their weight spreads across the grain. Children learn this before they learn their letters. A grandmother can tell from the tone of a settling wall whether the family should sleep in the back room that night, and she will move the household without explaining, and no one argues.

Their speech is low and close. Sound in the basin comes from everywhere, so people have learned to talk almost under their breath, with their heads tilted toward whoever they are addressing. Conversations in the Under look like confidences. Strangers from the rim find it unsettling, the way voices seem to rise from the walls and the stones, and the people of the Pit find the rim-folk's loud, open calling strange and a little rude. They have their own words for things the surface doesn't have words for: the rhythm of a slump before it comes, the dull weight of mud that has soaked a whole house, the particular hush of a fog morning when the river is high and everyone is listening. They name the terraces after the people who first cut them, and the names are sung at the start of each season's work.

Time is kept by the line of shadow. The rim's shadow crosses the basin each day at a slow diagonal, and the people mark its progress by its edge: the upper terraces go gold while the lower ones are still grey; then the gold slides down the walls, reaches the gardens, reaches the river, and goes out. A person who has lived there long enough can tell the hour by the temperature of the air against the back of the neck. Meals, work, and rest are arranged around this. Nobody owns a clock, but everyone knows when the light is leaving the ovens.

Their labor is the labor of holding ground. The masons are the most respected, because the masons decide where the next terrace can safely go, which faces to leave bare and which to pin with cribbing. Their crews work the high cut faces in the mornings, tapping and prying, listening for the difference between solid stone and marl that will crumble. They wear leather hoods against the falling grit and carry small iron bars they strike against the rock to hear it ring. The water-watchers are the second great trade. They stand on the bridges with notched poles and call the river's level every hour in the wet season, and their voices carry down the basin in a chant that the whole city hears and answers with a kind of tallying murmur. The rope-tenders strand and check the lines between houses. The ditch-diggers keep the culvert clear, and they are never finished. The gardeners on the one terrace of beans and onions are considered slightly strange, both lucky and a bit foolish, since growing things asks for trust in a floor that has not yet decided.

Their homes are built with a particular habit that visitors take for paranoia. Doors are hung to swing outward, so they can be forced open if a wall shifts and the frame jams. Every house has a second way out, a window or a hole in the back wall, and the children are drilled to use it. The people keep everything small and movable: pots, stools, bedrolls, tools. Their deepest furniture is the shelf cut into the rock itself, where the family keeps its oldest things, because the rock is the only thing they fully trust.

They also do something the rim does not. When a house is buried by slump, they dig for the people inside and then leave the house where it is, a mound of rubble with a marker stone, and they build the next home a little higher, on whatever ledge the new stone has exposed. Over the years the Under has grown upward like a sediment, layers of rebuilt lives stacked over earlier lives, and the people talk about their ancestors as if they were in the strata: "down there," "under the old second terrace," "where the chalk is still white."

Among them, they have the other residents of the Pit, and they treat them with a practical respect. The swifts are welcome because they eat insects and signal weather when they dive low. Bats are left alone in the south tunnels, and no one lights a fire near their roost. The rats are hated and fed, both at once, since the cats cannot catch them all, and the families who keep cats are the ones who most often lose their stores. The herons standing in the shallows are the only creatures the people sometimes stop to watch in silence. Frogs are sung back at, after rain, by children who like the noise.

In the evenings, when the last of the light has left the floor and the swifts have gone quiet in their cracks, the city settles into its low music. Dripping eaves, the river talking to its gravel, a shutter knocking somewhere, a child laughing, voices pitched down and passing from wall to wall. Every so often a stone lets go high up and falls with a long rattle, and every face in the bowl lifts for a moment, measuring the sound, and then goes back to its supper. They have not stopped being afraid of the ground. They have only learned to live as people who expect the floor to move again, and to keep their doors facing out, and to build anyway.
