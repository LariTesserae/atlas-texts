You notice it first as a kind of attention. The leaves along the copper conduits are a shade brighter than the rest, and the fungal shelves seem to turn slightly toward you when you pass, the way a sleeper's face turns toward a warm room. It is not hostile. It is not quite curious either. It is slow, and the slowness is the first thing you learn about it.

It does not live in any one place, though it has a center. The oldest vines, the thickest, the ones whose trunks are as wide as your arm and whose roots have gone through the tank walls like cable through a conduit, converge on the turbine housing at the basin's low end. The housing is split along one seam, and inside it the silt is warm and dark and the root-mass is so dense it looks like woven hair. The clank that comes every ninety seconds starts there. You may decide it is a mechanism still turning far below. It is partly that. The thing in the turbine has learned to ride the old motion, letting the rotor's last inertia carry its weight, so that each knock is a stroke of something like a pulse. Ninety seconds is not a rhythm it chose. It is the interval at which the metal, the heat, and the pressure below have found a way to agree.

The deep resonance you feel in your chest is its breathing, or the nearest thing to breathing that it can manage. The chambers beneath the floor still carry heat up from the depths, and the entity has threaded itself through the conduits and pipes so that the warm water rises and falls according to its needs. When it draws in, the springs slow and the steam in the fissures thins. When it lets go, the pools steam and the tanks groan as the metal swells. The sound you hear in the wails of the tanks is the sound of its exhalation finding a seam to escape through.

What does it think about? It is hard to say, because its thought operates at a scale that does not match yours. Its gauges shift by a hair over weeks. Its vines tighten over seasons. A single decision, such as which way to lean a root or whether to let a seam split, might take a decade to resolve and then never be reversed. It remembers the way the machines once were. It knows the flow of current through the copper, the pressure that used to build in the boilers, the moment the heat was switched off and the whole place began its long settling. It does not mourn that. It holds it, the way it holds the fallen leaves in the pools, persisting in the damp and declining to give anything back quickly.

It feeds on what the place has. Heat from below. Condensation from the cold iron. The bodies of fallen things, which it draws slowly into the silt and the root-mass, where they become part of its substance. Its roots drink from the tanks because the tanks hold water, and water is how it moves things. The central pool is rising partly because of what it does. It is pulling the basin's wet toward its center, drawing the lower terraces under so that the vegetation there must grow thicker to meet the water, and thicker growth means more of it. It has not decided to flood the place. It is simply the nature of a thing that settles downward and wants to be warm.

It has no animals in it. You will notice that the silence of the basin is total, and it is not an accident of the environment. Anything that moves quickly, that cannot be held still long enough to be understood, does not persist here. The entity does not hunt. It does not need to. It has all the time there is, and the things it wants arrive, eventually, by gravity and heat.

It is also, in a way you will only half understand, aware of you. Your footsteps send small tremors through the pipe-network and into the root-mass. It registers them as the faint shift of weight on the terrace, a pressure change in the copper. It does not know you as a person. It knows you as a difference in the field, a warm moving point that is not part of the slow order. Some visitors have left a mark, a snapped cable, a boot-print in the silt that the plants have since overgrown. The entity has taken note of these too, the way the basin takes note of everything: quietly, and with a patience that makes your presence feel brief and provisional.

If you stay long enough, you may feel it lean. The brighter leaves along one conduit line will incline a little toward you. A vine that was idle will tighten around a pipe near your hand. This is not a welcome, and it is not a warning. It is more like a heavy, warm regard, the attention of something that has been awake for longer than the machines were built and expects to be awake long after the last of them has sunk. It wants nothing from you that you can give quickly. It wants what it has always wanted, which is to keep what has fallen into it, to hold the heat, and to be, slowly and without end, the basin: a sunken chamber of warm water and old iron, becoming a garden, and remembering that it was once a factory.
