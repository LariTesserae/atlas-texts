**What Lives in the Low Grey Reach**

Nothing on the Reach lives alone, and the thing that lives there is not any one of the creatures. It is the plain itself: a single slow body, older than its own shape, that breathes through the pumps, digests through the conveyor ridges, and thinks, if it thinks at all, in the long grey intervals between one rhythm and the next.

It has no green because it has never needed the sun. Its food is stone. It drinks the water that seeps up from below and grinds the ancient hills into silt, and it lets the silt sink toward the lowest point, where the cycle begins again. Its metabolism is geological. The sun is a pale coin behind a permanent lid of cloud, and the body beneath has learned to keep its distance from that light, the way some deep-sea things keep to the dark.

**The organs**

The rust-colored columns are its lungs and its heart. Each one draws the water up through its banded pipes and exhales in a slow mechanical sigh, and the sighs are the body's breath, the pulse you feel in your chest when you stand still too long. The black stone pistons in the shallows are its muscles, working in a rhythm that is not quite regular, as though the body is still learning how to move. The fused slag gears are the joints. The sediment ridges are its gut, pushing what it has ground toward the basin where it will be absorbed.

These organs do not seem to be controlled by a mind sitting above them. They are more like reflexes that have grown into habits, and the habits have grown into a kind of purpose: keep the water moving, keep the stone turning, keep the low point low.

**The attendants**

The swarms, herds, and shoals are the body's attendants, and they behave less like wildlife than like cells in a living system.

The armored shoals in the water fold and reform like cloth in wind. They move the way a nervous system moves, sending pulses of coordinated motion through the lakes, carrying something like information from one basin to the next. When a pump exhales, the shoals scatter and regroup, as if responding to a signal that only they can hear.

The plated grazers on the crusts are the body's maintenance crew. They tear at the slick films, the brown-black film that gives a brief warmth and then nothing, and they keep the surface clean enough for the pumps to draw through. Their tearing sound is the sound of the body repairing itself.

The glass-winged swarms are the body's sensors, or perhaps its dreams. They hang in columns that rise and fall with the pumps, and they part with each exhalation, as if the air itself were a membrane they must respect. They do not fear the machines because they are the machines' timing made visible: the body's thoughts passing through its own lungs.

**The predators**

The long, low bodies sliding under the lake surfaces and the jointed things stalking the channel edges are the body's immune system. They do not hunt for food so much as for what does not belong: anything that stays too long in one place, anything that disturbs the rhythm, anything that carries warmth or green into a world that has none. You see them only in flashes because that is how immune responses work. They are fast, selective, and mostly invisible until something needs to be cleared away.

**The windows**

The most unsettling part of the Reach is the lakes that show other skies, other machines, other versions of the same plain. These are the body's dreams, or perhaps its attempts to sample its own possibilities. A slow organism that has been alive for unimaginable time may not experience time the way a short-lived creature does. It may hold many versions of itself at once and test them against one another, the way a sleeper rehearses a sentence before saying it. When a crust-plate rearranges its cracks, the body may be trying a new pattern. When the pumps take their rhythm from the animals, the body may be listening to its own attendants and adjusting the tempo of its breath.

The lingering sounds, the arrivals out of order, the column of machine-smoke bending around nothing: these may be the body's thoughts running slightly ahead of or behind its own actions, the way a dream does.

**The one who comes**

And then there are the visitors. The body does not appear to notice them the way a person notices a stranger. It notices them the way skin notices a splinter: a small, persistent irritation that the immune system will either clear or come to tolerate. The far pumps seem sometimes nearer, sometimes farther, because the body is adjusting its sense of distance to whatever has entered it. A walker who lingers long enough may find the lakes reflecting their own face in a sky that is not quite the sky above, a face slightly older, slightly more grey, slightly more like the stone.

No one who walks the Reach leaves unchanged, though they rarely know how. The body is not hostile. It is simply very old, very slow, and very attentive in the way that only something that has outlasted every name can be. It keeps grinding the hills into dust, keeps the water moving, keeps its shoals and swarms in their patient dance. And somewhere under the cloud, in the long grey intervals between breaths, it is dreaming of the sun it has forgotten how to want.
