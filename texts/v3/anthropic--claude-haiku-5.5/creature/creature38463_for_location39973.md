# Those Who Live in Hwen

Ask who lives in the fen and the people of Corrowe will give different answers depending on how long their families have held ground. A shipwright whose grandmother was born on a barge will say the fen lives in him. A surveyor will say there are several kinds of people here, sorted by how far their deeds sink each spring. Everyone agrees about the eels.

## The Tidemarked

Most residents belong to what the old registers call the tidemarked, families who have held ground long enough to know it moves. The fen shows in their bodies. Their ankles are thick from decades of wet boots and uneven causeways. Silt never fully washes out of their hands, so old men's knuckles look dusted with ash. Their skin takes the damp well and goes soft and pale, and their coughs, from smelter smoke and fog together, have a rattle that every child in the lower wards learns to recognize.

They dress in layers that can be shed or tied on quickly: oilcloth, wool that stays warm when soaked, boots with spiked soles for the new mud. They carry long poles, as surveyors do, and they walk with the slightly rolling gait of people who expect the ground to shift and have stopped being surprised by it.

Their homes are less houses than rafts with rooms. Timber frames sit on steel floats, roofed with corrugated tin and tied to pilings with chain that has been replaced many times. When the water rises, the whole family pays a jacking crew to lift the home and float it to higher ground. Most households keep a second address, a bunk on a cousin's barge or a hut on a neighbor's raised parcel, for the years their own ground goes under. Children learn the tide-marks of their street before they learn the names of saints.

Names here follow channels. A child born the year the Neddle silted shut is called Neddle, or Nell for short. A family that lost its ground to the Scour carries the Scour in its surname. Ask where someone is from and they will name a channel rather than a street, because the street will be gone by next spring.

## The Reckoners

A smaller, stranger group does the arithmetic. These are the surveyors, the sounding crews, and the old engineers who keep deposition ledgers in cramped offices above the pumping stations. They walk with their heads down, reading mud the way others read faces. From the color and grain of a fresh deposit they can estimate how many years it will hold before the next melt takes it. They speak of ground in the language of probability, which the tidemarked find reassuring and faintly insulting. The reckoners are usually right. When they are wrong, a district goes under, and the reckoner's name is spoken bitterly for a generation.

## The Eel-Keepers

The sump-fishers are a tight, wary community. They work at night with long nets and lanterns, pulling writhing masses of eels from the drainage sumps, and they have their own customs: they never whistle near the water, and they knot a strand of reed into every catch they keep. Their eels are pale and bloated from breathing the effluent, and the largest ones carry faint rings of color along their flanks, bands of blue and rust that shift in the lantern light. The fishers do not sell these. They say the rings are a map, that the patterns repeat between eels, and that someone who learned to read them would know where the water was going before the water did. No one has learned to read them. The fishers say this with a certain relief.

## The Reed-Burners

The reed-harvesters work the stands near the foundries, where the stems take up dissolved metals from the old slag and grow a grey-blue sheen. They cut the reeds in winter and burn them in brick kilns. The ash is sold to the smelters, and the work leaves its own marks: blue-stained teeth, lungs that whistle, a patience with heat that makes them useful in the foundries and unwelcome in the offices. They are the only people in Corrowe who will walk into the warm circles without hesitation, and they are also the ones who pull their own children back from the edges.

## The Other Lives

The gulls that ride thermals above the chemical vats have learned the workers' schedules better than the workers have. They wait in tight flocks for the lunch break, and they fight with a violence that the dockhands say is new, as though the birds have started counting on the food. The slag-shelled crustaceans in the smelter outflow live in colonies so dense that their grit shells grind underfoot like sand on a stone floor. They are sometimes harvested for the metal in their shells, and their colonies recover within a season, faster than the fen's own engineers can map them.

The reeds, the ferns, the algae, and the thickets are inhabitants too, in a sense that matters here. The ring-thorn holds the rises together. The fern that opens after each rain seems, to the people who watch it, to breathe the whole fen out. The reed walls are a kind of settlement, moving slowly outward across the new mud, and where they stop, the ground stops being ground and begins to be marsh.

## The Warm Ground

There is one kind of presence in the fen that no one can name with confidence. Where the new silt carries a faint warmth, the reeds grow in perfect rings, and the frogs gather in circles on the wet mud, sitting so still that they seem to be listening. The tidemarked avoid those places. The reckoners' official position is that the bedrock is still cracking beneath the deposits and that the rings are a thermal effect. The reed-burners do not argue with this, but they leave offerings of ash at the edges, and they will not cut reeds from inside a circle. When an engineer's sounding rod is pulled from a warm patch, it sometimes comes up coated with a fine, pale slime that dries into the shape of tiny fern fronds, which no one has ever been able to identify.

Nobody in Corrowe claims to understand what lives in the warm ground, or whether anything does. But everyone who has walked the causeway at night has seen the frogs turn their heads, all together, toward the circle as the lantern passes, and then turn back to the water.

## Dawn on the Causeway

In the early light the people of the fen go about their work: the jacking crews lifting houses, the reckoners sounding the flats, the fishers hauling in their last nets, the reed-burners stacking ash. The gulls rise over the vats. The tidemarked open their doors onto water that was not there last year. Out in the braided channels, a new island is surfacing, dark and wet, and by the time the sun clears the fog, something green has already begun to grow on it.
