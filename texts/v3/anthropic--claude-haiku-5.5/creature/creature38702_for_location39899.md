**What Tends the Water**

The largest inhabitants are the ones you hear first and see last. They move through the canopy like barges through a flooded city, slow and low, each one sealed in a shell of overlapping plates the color of wet slate. The plates are glossy and seamless at the edges, so the cloud beads on them and runs off without soaking in. A body that size should be heavy, but it moves as if the air is holding it up.

Nobody has seen a young one. You find them only grown, and you find them in the drip-chambers, where the vine-ceilings are dark and the standing water is deepest. Some stand in the shallows with their heads just breaking the surface, and from a distance they look like part of the architecture, like columns that have decided to be animals. They do not come out of the water so much as they are gradually revealed by it.

They eat very little that anyone has recorded. They drink. Their long throats fit against the vents where the hollow stalks push water out under pressure, and when they pull, the sound is a slow sucking draw that rises through the roar and falls away again. A creature may stay at one vent for an hour, drinking at the exact rate the vent releases, neither faster nor slower, until the vent's pressure drops and the creature lifts its head and moves to the next.

The knocking is the stranger habit. Each one carries a hard ridge along its forequarters, and it strikes the stalks with this ridge as it passes, a dull hollow report like a mallet on a barrel. The stalks answer with a note. The note changes depending on where the blow falls and how hard, and after a knock the vent beside it often opens or closes. A visitor watching for long enough begins to suspect the knocks are not random. They are too evenly spaced. They fall in places where the pressure has begun to drift and bring it back. They are adjustments, or they are reminders, made with a patience so complete it looks like indifference.

Their eyes are set high on the domes of their shells and are flat, pale, and almost lightless, like the bottoms of cisterns. When one turns toward you, you feel observed without being seen, the way you feel the weight of a door opening in another room. They do not hurry toward you. They do not run from you. If you stand in their path, they stop, wait, and then, when you have not moved, go around you with a precise courtesy that seems to come from long experience of things that stand still.

The other fauna keep their distance from them. The small things in the vine-vaults fall silent when a plated one passes below, and resume their rustling only after it is gone. The empty stretches of mat, the kilometers of nothing that characterize the place, seem to follow the plated ones at a respectful interval, so that wherever one of them walks, the emptiness walks with it, and the emptiness is part of what it is.

Nothing here has a name for them. The word the visitors use, eventually, is not a name but a description of what they do: *the ones who keep the time*. It is a poor phrase, but the place seems to accept it. Nothing about the Held Water is loud enough to argue.

The question a visitor ends up asking is whether the plated ones know the loop. Whether they understand that the drop of water they drink will rise through the stalk, fall through the mat, return to the cloud, and come back to their throats again, the same drop, in the same place, forever. Their faces give no answer. But they knock the stalks at the right moments, and they drink at the right rate, and they never seem to be looking for anything else.

Once, a visitor sat for a full day beside one of the great cisterns and watched a plated one work its way along the far shore. It came down the bank, drank, knocked the stalk at the water's edge, turned, and climbed back into the vines. The visitor, fatigued and half-dreaming in the diffuse green light, lost track of the hours and then recovered them all at once: the creature was passing beneath the same arch it had passed beneath at the start of the day, and the visitor's own shadow, absent as always in that light, seemed to have been standing in exactly the same place the whole time. The knock came again. The vent answered at the same pitch. The cloud did not move.

The visitor did not stay longer. But the sound of the knock followed them out of the plateau, and when they came down into the ordinary weather below, where the wind moved and the light had shadows and a sound could end, they still caught themselves, now and then, waiting for the next blow.
