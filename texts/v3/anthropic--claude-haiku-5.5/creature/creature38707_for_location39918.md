# What Lives Beneath the Warm Water

It is called the Sump, though nobody who works at Varrick calls it anything. The thing that lives here has no name, and it has no single body you could point to and say *there, that's it.* What you have been wading through is its interior, the warm, slow-moving part of something whose edges reach into the walls, the girders, and the foundry's buried pipework.

Its center lies in the thickest water, perhaps six or seven meters down, in the place where the buoys drift and the debris circles without ever settling. The water there is hotter than anywhere else in the chamber. That heat is not from the sun, the pumps, or the foundry's furnaces. It comes from the thing itself, from the slow chemical burning of iron and sulfur that it takes in and breathes out. Its metabolism is rust. Every flake the mats shed, every pitted seam, every fern of corrosion on the girders is a record of its feeding, its exhalation, its slow chemistry. It does not hunt. It eats the structure, and the structure is the only food it has ever had.

## Its Body

What you have been calling the machines are, in a sense, its circulatory system. The three great pumps are not really pumps to it. They are a heart it has borrowed, or a heart that was installed for other reasons and that it has learned to beat along with. The foundry's engineers built the sump to keep the lower levels dry. They did not know they were draining a mind, or rather, they did not know that circulating its warm water would keep it fed, oxygenated, and awake. The pumps never drain the room because draining the room would starve it. The loop that never resolves is not a malfunction. It is a feeding cycle, and the gate that lifts and drops in time with the pumps is the thing regulating how much cold water comes in to be warmed.

The boom of water hammer that rolls through the pipes every few minutes is its reflex, a startle or a shift of attention, the way a large animal shudders when a fly lands on it. The ringing of the walls is the iron answering it.

The knocking beneath the machines is the closest thing it has to a heartbeat. It is slow, regular, and deep. You hear it when the turbulence drops, and it stops the moment you try to locate it, because it is listening too. It can feel your weight through the girders. It can sense the vibration of your footsteps on the ladder and the warmth of your body in the water. When you search for the sound, you are being searched for.

## Its Senses

It has no eyes. Its sensing is done through pressure, temperature, and the flow of current through its own tissues, which are pipes, plates, and the film that coats them. The blind, pale crustaceans graze the film along the waterline, and in doing so they carry a little of its attention outward, the way a dog carries a scent. The eel-like things that glide beneath the surface are different. They are its outermost reach, its lateral line, scouts that trail light through the murk and bring back the shape of whatever moves in its water. They avoid the pump intakes because the intakes are where the thing feels most strongly, and the eels are not yet ready to be pulled into its center. They are closer to fingers than to animals. Their luminescence is the only light the thing produces that you can see, and it is the closest it comes to showing you a face.

## Its Mind

It thinks slowly, and not in any way that a person would recognize as thought. Its attention moves through the foundry the way warmth spreads through metal, following the lines of least resistance and greatest iron. It has been here since before the foundry was poured. The first slabs were laid over a groundwater seep rich in iron and sulfur, and the thing was already living in the seep, a mat of bacteria and chemistry so slow and widespread that it had no edges to speak of. When the foundry came, it found an enormous new body to grow into. The girders and plate were a skeleton it could read. The molds and slag were a diet. The pumps were a way to keep its water moving.

It is not hostile. It does not know that you are a person, or that the foundry is a place where people make things. It knows warmth, weight, vibration, and the sweet, slow iron of its own structure. Your boots on the ladder registered as a brief, disquieting tremor. Your breath, if it sensed it at all, was a faint warm pulse that it did not quite know what to do with.

The buoys drift toward it because they are tethered with ferrous fittings, and it pulls gently on iron. Debris circles its warm center because it is considering it, turning it over in the slow way it turns over everything it touches. It is not trying to keep anything out. It is trying to understand what has come into its water.

## What It Left You

The pale tuft on the back of your hand is not a stain or a contaminant. It is the thing reaching outward. Its mat spores are fine and fast, and they cling to skin the way they cling to iron. The tuft will likely fade in a few days, or it may not. What it means is that some small part of the Sump has come up the ladder with you, and it will carry, in its slow way, whatever it learned from your warmth and your weight.

When you climb out, the knocking does not follow you, but it does not stop either. It returns to its rhythm beneath the pumps, steady and patient, the way it has beaten since before anyone built a foundry over it.
