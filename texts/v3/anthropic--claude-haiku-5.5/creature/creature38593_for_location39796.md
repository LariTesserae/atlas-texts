# The Keepers of the Hollow

The first sign of them is a stillness you mistake for a clump of sedge. On a roof near the center of the settlement, a shape kneels in the thatch, so folded into the green that its outline blurs with the moss. Only when it lifts its head does it separate from the roof, and even then it seems less a creature set on the thatch than a part of the roof that has learned to sit up.

They are about the height of a seated child but lean and long-limbed. Their skin is the color of wet slate, mottled with green where the moss has been allowed to take hold, so that each keeper carries a patch of the hollow's growth on its own body, as if the roof and the keeper had been pressed together until they shared a surface. The skin is cool and faintly tacky, and it drinks the mist. Droplets gather on the shoulders and the backs of the hands and never run off. The keepers do not seem to notice them.

Their eyes are wide and pale, nearly colorless, and set high on the head. The reflected light from the channels does not trouble them. They seem to prefer it, the way a moth prefers a lamp. Their mouths are small and rarely open. They do not speak.

## Work

Most of the keepers' time is spent on the roofs. A roof is never finished, and a keeper's day is a slow circuit of its own thatch. They lift old reed with their long fingers, test each bundle for rot by pressing it between their palms, and lay new bundles in the gaps. They use bone awls to lash the reed and stone adzes to trim the edges, and the work is so unhurried that a single hand's breadth of roof may take an entire morning. When a bundle is replaced, the old one is not thrown away. It is carried to the nearest channel and laid on the water, where it sinks slowly and feeds the moss that will eventually root in the next roof.

The keepers also tend the niches. They know exactly how many frogs belong on each wall, how many snails on each step, how many rodents can live in a given stretch of thatch before the reed begins to fail. When a frog dies on a wall, a keeper appears within the hour and sets another in the same crack, a small thing cupped in two hands, placed with the care of someone setting down an egg. When the snails crowd a step too thickly, a keeper gathers the excess and carries them to a patch of empty stone elsewhere. Nothing is killed in the hollow. Everything is moved, and the order is kept.

They do not hunt. They do not eat the things they tend, though they sometimes sit beside the channels with their feet in the water and let the eels slide between their ankles. Their own food is hard to see. Sometimes a keeper is found with its mouth pressed to a moss-covered stone, and when it lifts its head there is a faint green stain on its lips. Nobody seems to know what it has taken.

## The Gold Hour

Once each day, when the sun drops behind the eastern hills and the mist catches fire along its edges, the keepers stop working. They rise from the roofs, leave their awls in the thatch where they were last set down, and walk to the central causeway, the one that runs straight through the middle of the settlement. They stand in a line along it, shoulder to shoulder, facing east.

They do not move. The gold light spreads over them, and for a few minutes they are the only things in the hollow that are not shadowless. Their skins brighten, the moss on their bodies goes almost luminous, and the mist drifts between them like the breath of something enormous and slow. The frogs go quiet on the walls. The snails stop their trails. Even the birds on the rim seem to lean inward.

Then the light goes white again, and the keepers turn and return to their roofs, and the work resumes exactly where it left off.

## Names

The keepers do not have names in any sense that would make sense to a visitor. Each one is known by the roof it tends, and when a keeper dies, a new one is not born. Instead, one of the others begins to tend that roof as well, slowly, until its own patch of thatch has been relaid and its hands know the reed. Over time the roof passes from one pair of hands to another, and the keeper who once kept it becomes a part of the stone beneath, settling into the foundation as moss settles into cracks. Some of the oldest stones in the hollow are, in a sense, keepers who have stopped moving and have been fitted into the wall, their outlines softened by moss until they are indistinguishable from the rock.

No one knows how many keepers there once were, or whether there were ever more or fewer. The count stays the same. It has always stayed the same.

## Silence

The keepers do not speak, and they seem to understand that speech would be a kind of change. A word is a sound that the hollow cannot keep. It goes out into the mist and does not come back, and every sound that does not come back is a small loss. The keepers make no sound at all, except that sometimes, very faintly, a low hum can be felt through the stone of a causeway when a keeper kneels to work. It is too low to hear. It is felt in the teeth and the soles of the feet, and then it is gone, absorbed into the moss and the water and the wet walls, as if it had been a part of the building all along.

They are the reason the hollow is full to its edges and never overflows, and the reason nothing in it has changed for longer than anyone can say. They are not quite its inhabitants and not quite its builders. They are the hands that keep the shape of the hollow, and they will keep it until the moss has no more room to grow and the mist has no more places to fall.
