**The Ones Beneath the Moss**

The knock comes again, from somewhere to your left, a dull hollow sound like a knuckle against a hollow log. You step back. The moss ahead of you lifts, very slowly, the way a blanket rises over something breathing.

Then the first of them shows itself.

It is broad and low, about the length of a man's body but flattened, so that it seems to pour itself across the ground rather than stand on it. Its back is a shield of overlapping plates, dark and glossy, polished smooth as the basalt around it. Lichen grows on the plates, but not randomly. The orange and violet mats have been encouraged into neat fields, divided by low ridges of shell, and in a few places the lichen has been cut back to bare chitin, which gleams wetly in the mist. The creature is farming its own back.

Its head is almost hidden under a fringe of fine grey silt, which it sweeps aside with a slow motion of two long forelimbs. Those limbs are the strangest part of it. They are jointed and hard-tipped, the chitin at their ends worn to a dull sheen, and they are clearly the ones that made the glass. You can see the work in their posture: when they rest, the tips rest against the ground in a particular way, with the faint tremor of something that has spent a long time striking stone and has not forgotten how.

It stops. The ground under you carries a faint vibration, a rhythm of short taps, and you realise the bowl is full of them. Each tap is answered at a distance, and each answer is a little different, so that the whole basin is a web of knocks passing back and forth beneath the moss. They are not calling to each other, exactly. They are checking the stone. Listening for cracks. Reading the way the vents have shifted the rock since the last time they listened.

**What they do here**

They do not build, not in the way that makes walls or cairns. Their work is maintenance. The silt-choked gullies are their doing: they dig when water backs up, and they leave the channels open for the warm runoff. They scrape the vent-mouths when the fissures begin to seal, so the steam keeps rising and the bowl stays damp. Where a new fracture opens near the heat, one of them will come and press its forelimbs against the edges for days, until the rock is no longer sharp and the crust has closed into something that will hold.

The glass chips in the clusters by the warm pools are the surplus of this labor. They knap the glass into blades and scrapers and use them to open the shells and bones of the things that die in the basin. Then they put the marrow and meat back into the cycle, and the leftovers go into the pools and the silt, where the minerals build up. The terraces you saw in the cliffs were made the same way, over generations, out of shell and bone and mineral skin, cemented by the damp. The bowl is partly made of them.

**The chorus**

The frogs are not the masters of this place, though they sound like it. They are the bowl's messengers. Their throats swell and burst into bubbling calls, but the calls are not only for mates or territory. They are tuned to the pools, and the pools are tuned to the sky. The frogs carry the mineral waters in their skins, absorbing and releasing them as they move between the hollows. When the pools stir an hour before rain, it is the frogs that have been sensing the change in pressure and passing it along, and the knappers, feeling the ground shift through the stone, have already begun to gather at the vents where the clouds will break.

You notice that the frogs go silent all at once and then, the moment the first drops hit, begin again together, as though a single lung had filled. It is not quite a conversation. It is closer to a switch being thrown.

**The gliders**

Above the basin the long-winged shapes circle on the thermals. You had taken them for birds or drifting leaves, but they are longer and more deliberate than birds, with membranes that stretch between narrow limbs and a silhouette that tilts and corrects with almost mechanical precision. They do not call. They hunt the insect clouds that rise from the pools, and they are the only things here that move through the upper air freely, which makes them the bowl's eyes.

They also seem to be the ones who carry things. Now and then one drops a split bone or a shell into the warm water near the vents, and you wonder whether this is accident or instruction. The knappers pick such things up within the hour.

**What they are**

No single thing here is in charge. The knappers keep the stone, the frogs keep the water and the weather, the gliders keep watch from above, and the insects keep the air in constant motion. Each of them does something the others need. Together they form a system that has learned, over more time than any memory the bowl could hold, to repair itself faster than the vents can break it.

The knappers are the oldest of these, you think, though they are not the most numerous. They are the ones who listen to the stone, and so they know the bowl the way a body knows its own heartbeat. Their dead become the terraces. Their tools become the silt. Their knock becomes the tone that rises from the basalt when the wind blows over the rim, the note like a finger drawn around the lip of a glass. When you hear that tone, you are hearing the whole basin ring with a sound it has been making for a very long time.

You put your hand on the warm stone, and the knock comes back through it, not quite a response, but close, the way a long-lived thing acknowledges another that has paused to notice it. Then it moves on, back under the moss, to check a fracture that has not yet closed.
