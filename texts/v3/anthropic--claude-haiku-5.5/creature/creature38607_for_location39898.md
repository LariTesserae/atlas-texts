**What Lives in the Sinkwater Basin**

Nothing in the basin introduces itself. You learn its inhabitants the way you learn a room in the dark: by bumping into them, by the change in pressure when something large passes, by the warmth that does not come from the rock.

**The long ones in the shallows**

The first thing to touch you is usually a brush against the calf, a cool, muscular pressure that slides past and does not stop. These are the longest creatures in the basin, and they move the way water moves when it has decided on a direction. They have no faces that you can find. Their bodies are slick enough that your hand slips off them, and they leave ripples that travel outward and bounce off the flat pools, so that for a moment the reflections show a little storm you cannot see.

They seem to go between the pools on a schedule older than the works. When the water level shifts, they shift with it, and the old pylons that surface and sink are, for them, only landmarks that come and go. Some of them have grown long enough to wind through the cut faces of the slates, following the cracks, and where they lie still in the narrow channels the drip falls onto them and runs down their backs. Perhaps they carry the water's memory the way a river carries silt: slowly, and without any sense that the load is precious.

**The walkers on the stone**

The many-footed ones are harder to be sure of. You hear them first as a patter that does not belong to the drip, then as a rhythm on the cut rock that matches the ticking of the cooling slates. Their steps land in the gaps between the cracks, and when the stone ticks, they pause, and when the stone is quiet, they walk. It is possible that the ticks are not made by the rock at all. It is possible that the walkers are the ones who make the sound, and that the rock has only learned to answer them.

They are wary of being touched and will flatten against the slate, every small foot pressed down at once, until you have moved away. Their bodies are cool and dry, unlike everything else in the basin, which suggests that they live above the wet and only come down to feed. The fissures are their roads. When a crack widens by a centimeter, you can sometimes find a new procession of them crossing it, having already adjusted their path to the change.

**The hanging clusters**

These are the most still and the most social. They cling to the undersides of the overhangs in colonies, each one a soft, drooping form that sways when a drip lands on it. When you touch one, it pulses once, a slow and warm contraction that spreads to its neighbors, so that a whole cluster ripples like a sleeper turning over. Then it goes quiet.

They do not seem to be individuals. Remove one and the others take a long time to notice, but they do notice; the pulse returns a little slower for a while, and the cluster hangs a little closer together. Their rhythm is the slowest in the basin, and if you listen to the drip and the pulse together, you can sometimes hear them fall into step, then drift apart again, like two clocks in the same room. Some people think the clusters are the basin's nervous tissue, a way of keeping a distributed count of what is happening across miles of marsh. Others think they are only very patient and very warm, and that this is the whole of their purpose.

**The crowd**

Beneath these three kinds there is a density that is harder to name. At night, which is always, you feel the basin as a crowded room in which no one speaks. The water carries pressure from bodies you cannot see, and you become aware, without ever touching anything, that you are one occupant among many. This is most noticeable where the marsh is thickest, where the air sits heavy in the chest and the water seems to lean toward you. Those are the places where the creatures seem to gather, or where something in the middle has drawn them.

**The middle**

The basin's oldest process, the one you can feel if you stay still long enough, ends in the middle. Everything drifts toward it: the water through the cracks, the long creatures through the channels, the walkers along the fissure roads, the clusters' slow pulses. The pumps push toward it. The flywheel's tide laps toward it. Even the reflections, which show you yourself a half-beat late and standing a little farther off, seem to turn their attention inward when you look at them.

What is in the middle has no shape you can hold in your hands. It is warm, but not in the way the rock is warm. It is more like a presence that has been waiting for so long that waiting has become its body. When the water reaches it, something is received, and the basin's breathing deepens by a fraction, as if the machine and the marsh and the creatures have all been feeding one slow digestion.

It may be that the works were built to drain the basin and failed because they were draining toward the wrong place, or because whatever lives at the middle had always been the thing they were meant to drain away from. It may be that the creatures are its attendants, or its children, or the parts of it that have learned to move. The basin does not answer questions. It only settles, consolidates, and waits, and the middle takes in what the cracks bring it, patient and warm and dark, in the way that every low place in the world eventually collects whatever falls into it.
