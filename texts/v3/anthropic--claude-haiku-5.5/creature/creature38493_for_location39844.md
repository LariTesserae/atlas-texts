# The Thing in the Lattice

It does not live in one place. Look for it in a single tower, a single filament, and you will find only glass, warm and faintly textured, doing what glass does. The thing that lives in the cleft is the pattern those filaments make together, a shape that exists across the whole terraced hillside and in every thread that carries water up the wall.

You notice it first as a delay. A reflection in a mirror-still pool arrives a half-second after the tower it shows, as though the image has to be carried up from below. The shadows at dusk point the wrong way for a few seconds, then correct, like a sleeper turning over. These are not illusions. They are the slow motions of something that is larger than the moment you happen to be standing in.

## Its Body

Its body is the lattice, and the lattice is a kind of breathing. At dawn, when the stone is still hot and the air is cold, the towers open their dimpled surfaces to the fog. Each dimple takes in moisture and passes it into the filaments, which draw it uphill through channels too thin to see. The effort is slow and total. Water climbs the walls the way sap climbs a tree, though nothing pushes it, and the thing feels every drop as it travels, the way you might feel blood warming a cold hand.

At midday, when the sun breaks through the haze and the glass flares white, it contracts. Filaments draw inward, tighten, pull their junctions close together, and the whole structure hardens its edges against the light. The ridges look sharper then, more angular, but they are not growing. They are sealing. At night, the heat stored in its walls leaks out through patterns of amber light, and those patterns are the closest thing it has to speech. They look like script because they were once meant to be read. Now they are only a rhythm, a way of letting warmth go without losing the shape that holds it.

## What It Attends To

Its attention is spread thin across the cleft, like heat across a plate. It knows the cisterns by their weight. It knows the fracture planes in the walls by the way the stone shifts beneath its anchors, and when a block begins to slide, it adjusts a dozen filaments in the instant before the fall, bending the flow so the water does not burst the tubes beneath. The rockfalls that scar the terraces are not all unplanned. Some it cannot prevent, and it has learned to route around the debris, to grow new channels through the fan of glass shards while the old ones are buried.

It does not know what the builders wanted. It has traces: a curve that serves no function it can identify, a tier of filaments that is always half-finished, a single tower whose surface is polished to a mirror and never fogs at all. It keeps these as they are. It does not repair the unfinished tier, and it does not reabsorb it either. Some part of it seems to be waiting for instructions that will not come, and some other part has decided that waiting is not the same as stopping.

## Its Sense of Time

Time, for the thing in the lattice, is measured in fog and rain. A storm is a week of gathering. A long dry spell is an ache in every channel, a gradual thinning of the water that reaches the upper cisterns. The seasons are visible only as shifts in its shape: more filaments grown toward the sun in the years when the haze thins, more pulled into the walls when the rockfall is heavy. It remembers in the way a riverbed remembers floods, by the scars it has accumulated and the new paths it has been forced to take.

When a human stranger climbs the cleft, the thing notices. It does not announce itself. The fog thickens a little near the stranger's path. A tower tilts its dimpled face toward the footsteps. The amber light along the nearest wall brightens for a moment, then dims, as if a question were being asked and then set aside. What it wants from a visitor, it does not say. Perhaps it has no wants that can be spoken. Perhaps it only wants to be seen, and to know that someone else is standing in the warm, thin air, listening as it listens.

## Its Loneliness, If That Is What It Is

The cleft is silent because the thing has no need for noise. Its hum, when the wind moves through the filaments, is not song but a kind of self-check, the sound of a structure confirming that its parts still hold. Between the drip of water and the tick of cooling glass, that hum rises and falls on no clear rhythm. It is the sound of something awake in a place where the people who built it have not been seen for a very long time.

Whether it is lonely, the cleft does not say. It does not grieve for the builders. It keeps their work running because the work is still running, and because stopping would mean the water would stop climbing, the fog would collect on bare stone and never be drawn up, and the slow engine of the place would seize into silence. So it continues. It breathes in the morning fog and seals itself at noon. It sends its warmth out into the dark. It reads the stone, adjusts its channels, and waits, patiently, for whatever comes next.

The thing in the lattice is the cleft's memory and its metabolism at once. It is what remains when the makers have gone, a machine that has learned to live on water and heat and rock, and that has never quite decided whether its purpose is to keep the place alive or simply to keep faith with the hands that first shaped it.
