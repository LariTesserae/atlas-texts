# Who Keeps the Reach

The first one you notice is standing still.

On the lower causeway of a city whose name you will not learn, a figure leans on a rail above a drift of salt and faces west, where the sun never moves. It is tall and long in the limb, so pale that at first it reads as another stone. Then it turns, and you see the eyes: wide-set and deep, sheathed in a translucent membrane that slides across them in a slow blink, the way desert birds protect their eyes from glare. Its skin carries the same faint mineral bloom that coats the dry pipes. The creases at its throat and wrists are fine and regular, like the cracks in the crust.

It wears loose cloth dyed the color of the ground. It moves without hurry, and when it lifts a hand to shade its face, the gesture is unhurried too, as if the sun were a fact to be accommodated rather than a problem to be solved. It does not look at you for long. When it does, its attention is complete and unbothered, the attention of something that has all the time it needs.

## The Tenders

There are few of them. The cities are vast, the causeways run for hundreds of kilometers, and the pipe bridges stretch across the whole plateau, yet you pass long stretches without seeing anyone. They appear in ones and twos, working. You find a pair at the base of a tower, one kneeling and drawing a stiff brush along the inside of a dry intake while the other holds a shallow bowl beneath it. They scrape the pale residue into the bowl, and when it is full they carry it up a stair toward the cooler levels, where the lattices catch the wind. They do not speak much. When they do, the sounds are low and rhythmic, with a clicking undertone, and you realize after a while that the grazers on the flats make the same sounds.

They tend the machines the way others might tend a sick animal. One stands with both palms against a humming transmission housing, eyes closed, and stays there for a long time. You never learn what it is listening for. Perhaps it is only confirming that the hum is steady, that the line is still carrying what it carried yesterday. When it lifts its hands, the housing still hums, and it seems satisfied.

They do not build much anymore. The blocks come out of the quarry pits in clean pale stacks, the gantries lift and set, and the walls rise slowly, but the new work is always a patch or a replacement, never an expansion. The cities are not growing outward. They are holding their shape. You begin to suspect that the people here are maintaining an arrangement older than any of them, and that maintenance is the whole of their purpose: to keep the flows moving through the dry steel, to keep the towers standing, to keep the balance from tipping.

## The Herds

The grazers are the plateau's great memory. Their herds move in lines across the flats, low and wide, their glassy carapaces throwing back the light in long bands of grey-green. They are slow, but not unintelligent. When a dust-storm comes in from the east, the herd does not scatter. It settles, the animals lowering themselves into the drifts in a pattern that looks deliberate from a distance, and then it waits. When the air clears, the herd rises again, and its members stand in new places. Some are closer together. Some have moved toward the canyon mouths. The herd has, somehow, kept its shape while changing its position, as though it had been holding a picture of itself and reassembled it from memory.

Their clicking is constant when the wind is quiet. It rises and falls in patterns that carry across the flats, and you sense that every animal in the herd is listening to every other. The sound is not a call. It is a continuous conversation, a way of saying *I am here, and so are you*, repeated across distances that would swallow a human voice. When a single grazer strays too far toward the roads, the clicking changes pitch, and within minutes two or three others have turned to follow it back. The grazers never cross onto the causeways. Their trails stop at the edges of the glazed basalt as if they had reached a border they all understood.

They feed by pressing their broad mouthparts into the salt crust and scraping it into powder, which they digest with a slow chemistry you can see in the way their bodies heat and cool through the day. Their waste enriches the drifts, so that where a herd has passed, the crust is subtly different, finer, and paler. The plateau is being quietly remade by animals that have no interest in remaking it.

## The Climbers

The segmented climbers are the most numerous, and the most organized. Colonies cling to the warm walls of the towers and the stacked blocks, and on the fins they arrange themselves in patterns that seem almost too neat to be natural: rows of pale bodies all facing the same way, each one a little raised, like sails catching a wind. The direction they face is the direction the wind comes from. When the prevailing gusts shift with the seasons, the whole colony turns, a slow, rippling motion across the fin that takes an afternoon.

They feed on the mineral residue inside the pipes and on the crust that gathers on the cooler surfaces, and so they keep the towers clean and the dry lines open. In return, the tenders gather them in great numbers from the walls, not all of them, never all of them. Gaps are left. Some colonies are allowed to grow undisturbed on high fins that no one visits. You begin to see the arrangement: the climbers are harvested and also protected, and the people seem to understand which parts of the wall they can take from and which they must leave alone.

The larger hunters move along the causeways with a stiff, deliberate gait, their long limbs placed with care. They take the smaller climbers when the colonies are weakest or the walls are untended, and they move through the cities as though they had been granted a particular territory. The tenders step aside for them. The hunters do not look at the people. They do not look at anything in particular. They simply walk, and the causeway seems to make room.

## What They Are

The inhabitants of Caldera Reach are not one kind of thing. They are a set of living patterns that have settled into a shared arrangement: the tenders who tend the lines, the herds that remember their shape across storms, the colonies that turn to face the wind, the hunters that keep the balance of the walls. The cities are not separate from them. The cities are the place where the arrangement is most visible, the built scaffolding that the living things have learned to use and the people have learned to keep.

What holds it together is patience. Everything here is slow, and everything here knows how to wait. The herds shift over decades, the wadis deepen by millimeters, the tenders stand with their palms on humming steel for as long as it takes to be sure. None of them is in a hurry to change the arrangement, and none of them seems to want to know why it works. They simply keep it working.

When you finally leave the plateau and look back from the lip of the step, the whole expanse lies beneath you in layers of ochre, rust, and bone. The cities hold their shapes. The grazers are pale threads on the flats. The factories send up their thin columns of vapor, bending with the wind. Nothing moves quickly, and nothing has to. The place has endured by changing so slowly that every change looks like a return.
