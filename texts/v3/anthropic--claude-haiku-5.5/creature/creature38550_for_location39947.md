The plateau has no settled inhabitants. Everything living here is still moving in, testing the ground, and most of it will be gone or changed when the land finishes deciding its shape. What lives on Arrin Tahl lives in a kind of provisional tenancy, and you can read that in how each creature holds its place.

**The Ledge-Birds**

The birds are the first things you notice, because they announce themselves before the fog thins enough to show them. They nest on the cliff faces where the shelf breaks off, in crowded tiers of stone shelves and cracks, dozens to a ledge. Their wings are dark as wet slate, and their breasts are pale and faintly reflective, so that when a flock turns in the fog it flashes like a handful of thrown coins, then vanishes. They have heavy, hooked bills and eyes set low, adapted for striking downward into the tarns.

They are the plateau's most confident residents. They fly out into the gray in loose, shouting groups and return with small pale prey, insects hatched from the meltwater pools and the grubs that wriggle under the rotting lichen. The birds go back and forth between tarn and ledge all day, and they are the only creatures here that seem to understand the whole geography at once. Their calls carry down the gullies in long, unlovely cries that the rock throws back in echoes, so that you can rarely tell how many birds are calling or how far away they are.

**The Burrowers**

The burrowers are quieter and harder to see. They are broad, low animals with thick, dense fur the color of wet ash, built to resist cold that never fully lifts. You find them by their works: mounds of gritty soil heaped up around the edges of the tarns, each pushed out of a tunnel mouth that may or may not still be open. In the thaw the tunnels collapse, so the mounds slump and reappear each season in new places.

They eat the roots of the sparse growth that clings to the gullies, and they seem to know exactly where the root-tufts are, because their tunnels converge on them like the spokes of a wheel. Their presence is quiet and patient, and they are constantly reworking the ground, bringing up fresh grit and loosening the very soil they need. Without quite meaning to, they are part of how the plateau gets built.

**The Rock-Grazers**

The grazers are low and broad, with flat heads and short, stubby legs that splay wide on the wet stone. They move in slow, spaced lines across the bedrock, each animal several body-lengths from the next, scraping lichen from the stone with a rasping, toothed tongue. Their coats are patched in gray-green and brown, the same colors as the lichen they eat, so that from a distance they seem to be part of the rock itself, until one lifts its head and the line of them resolves into separate animals.

They keep apart. They do not gather in herds, and each grazer seems to hold a long stretch of rock as its own, returning to the same patches as the lichen regrows. Their feeding is slow and their lives appear to be long. They are the plateau's most visible proof that the lichen is spreading, though the spreading happens at a pace no grazer could ever see.

**The Pale Predators**

The predators are lean and pale, close to the color of the bleached bones that are slowly ground into the grit. They hunt along the gully edges, where the water runs over the stone and the grazers come down to drink. A predator lies flat in the wet grass at the lip of a channel, almost invisible, and waits for a grazer to lower its head to the water. Then it moves, quickly and without noise, and the hunt is over in a few seconds. The bones that remain are picked clean and rarely last long, because the burrowers and the weather take them.

They follow whichever population is rising. When the birds have been feeding well and the insects are thick in the tarns, the predators hunt more often near the ledges, and when the burrowers multiply the predators move toward the tunnels. Their numbers rise and fall in step with their prey, and they are never far from the animal they will eat next.

**The Slow Inhabitants**

The lichen, the moss, and the wiry grass are the oldest living things here, though even they are young. The gray-green lichen spreads over the stone at a pace that is almost imperceptible, as though it has all the time in the world and knows it. The moss gathers in the lee of boulders in hard, low cushions. The grass fills the gullies, pale at the tips and dark at the roots, and it roots only where the stone has already begun to break down. Nothing grows in the open rock, which is still too new and too unstable to hold anything. These plants are the first tenants of ground that is not yet ready for them, and they prepare the way for everything else.

**The Web as a Whole**

Taken together, these creatures form a loop that tightens and loosens as the seasons turn. The birds feed on the insects of the tarns, the burrowers feed on the roots of the grass, the grazers feed on the lichen, and the predators feed on the grazers and the burrowers. Each population rises when its food rises and falls when it falls, and the predators are always in motion, following the wave. The bones that remain feed the grit, and the grit feeds the next generation of moss, which feeds the grazers, and so on.

Nothing here is settled. Every creature is adapting to a land that has only just decided to exist. The rain cuts new gullies, and the animals move to the new water. The ice breaks each night, and the birds return to the ledges that survived. The creatures are not the masters of the plateau but its most recent arrivals, learning its rhythms as they form. The land and its residents are still arguing about what they will become, and for now, the argument is being carried on by the things that eat, hide, grow, and die across the raw stone.
