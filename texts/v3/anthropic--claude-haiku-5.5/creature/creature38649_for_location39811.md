**The Seep-Cleft Inhabitants**

The first thing you notice is the sheeters, because they are the first thing that moves toward you. They lie across the glass in overlapping plates, flat and many-legged, the color of wet slate, with a faint oily iridescence that shifts when the light does. Their legs are too many to count without losing your place, and they seem to move them in waves that start at the back and roll forward, so that a sheeter crossing your hand feels less like a creature walking and more like a patch of water deciding to travel. When your fingers block one, it parts around them and rejoins on the far side, closing the gap with no visible seam. Press harder and it does not flinch. It simply reorganizes its weight, the way a crowd makes room without anyone deciding to.

They are warm, warmer than the glass, and when many gather on one ledge the stone under them keeps a faint memory of their shape for some minutes after they leave.

Below the sheeters, in the seep-water, the lanterns hang at every depth. They are translucent, jellied, shaped roughly like bells that have forgotten what they were for, and inside each one drift motes of silt and sediment, bright specks that rise and fall as the body contracts. They pulse. The pulse is slow and regular and matches the drip from the roof so exactly that for a while you suspect the lanterns are the drip's echo, or its source. They do not seem to notice you. If you move your hand through the water they bob aside, then drift back into their places as if returning to a post. Sometimes one dims, and its motes settle to the bottom, and a few heartbeats later the body is gone and only a faint ring of disturbed water remains, which the others pulse through and smooth away.

The waiters live in the silt. You would not see them if you did not see their eyes. Each is a low mound of grit, broad as a palm, with two or three stalks rising from it, and at the tip of each stalk is a lens, clear and faceted, catching the white light from the slot above and throwing it in small hard points across the floor. When you walk, the points swing toward you. When you stop, they hold still, and you realize you have been standing in a crosshatch of tiny bright dots, all centered on your feet. The waiters do not move much. They are patient in the way of things that have never had to be anywhere else. The glass beneath them is pitted in small circles, each a shallow bowl, and you suspect that over a long time the lenses have been burning patterns into the floor that no one has yet read.

The shapers are the ones that unsettle you most, because they are the ones that seem to have a purpose. They are dark and low and roughly the size of a fist, with blunt heads and a ring of small mouths around the rim, and they drag themselves along the sharp edges of the basalt with the slow deliberation of a file moving across wood. When they reach a shard they close their mouths on it, lift it, and carry it along their backs or in the crooks of their mouths. Some of them have collected whole stores of glass in the hollows of their bodies, bright as sorted coins. Others simply score the edges and let the flakes drop, and the flakes lie scattered across the silt, each one sharp enough to draw blood, each one perfectly placed to catch the next foot that passes. Nothing here builds. But the shapers have been at the cleft for a long time, and the walls have the look of work that someone has been trying to finish.

The swarm is pale and fine, a cloud of tiny bodies that hangs above the seep until something large and slow drifts beneath it. Then it comes down all at once, and the surface shivers, and the creature beneath, which has a dome of translucent shell and a long trailing body like a ribbon of glass, begins to dim. The swarm covers it. For a while the water is only a seethe of pale motion, and then the seethe thins, and the swarm lifts back into the fog, and where the dome was there is only clear water and a faint lingering tint. You watch and do not look away, and you realize that you have seen this exact sequence three times now, each time the swarm forming in the same shape, each time coming down on the same drip. The creature beneath, you think, may be the swarm's own grazing, or the swarm may be the creature's way of letting go. The pattern repeats the way a hymn does, not word for word, but in the same breath.

The filaments cover the walls in a glassy bloom that you first take for frost. They grow toward the drip, each one a fine thread with a bulb at its tip, and when the drops strike the bulbs the threads bend to catch them, then slowly pull back, drawing the water into the bloom. When you touch one it contracts under your finger and leaves a sticky residue that smells of rain on hot stone. The residue dries in thin bright crusts along the cracks. You can see that the crusts are thicker in some places than others, and that the thicker ones follow the lines the tilting walls are opening up. The filaments do not seem to be doing anything so deliberate as building. They are simply drinking and then laying down what they cannot use, and the wall is growing out of their leftovers.

And then there is the deep tone.

It does not come from any creature you can see. It rises from beneath the silt, a pressure more than a sound, and when it passes, the whole cleft seems to shift a few degrees on its axis. The walls creep. A ledge that was level a moment ago is now tilted by the width of a fingernail, and the sheeters on it roll gently toward the low side, and the waiters' lenses swing and settle on a new point. You have never seen whatever makes the tone. You suspect it is very large, and very old, and mostly asleep, and that the cleft is built on its back the way a village is built on a riverbank. It turns over now and then. The sheeters know it, the lanterns know it, the waiters know it, and they all go still for the length of the tone, the way a room of people goes quiet when someone in the next room begins to speak.

The reflections are the last thing to learn. In the seep-pools, the creatures' reflections move a few seconds ahead of their bodies, and you begin to think that some of them are watching the water for signs of what they are about to do. Once, a shaper drops a flake into a pool, and the ripple it makes spreads outward in the reflection before it spreads on the real surface. The shaper, whose blunt head is turned toward its own reflection, does not look surprised. It keeps dragging its load along the edge and carries on.

You count the lanterns, the sheeters, the shapers, the waiters, the swarm, the filaments, and the slow dimming creature in the pool, and you lose count somewhere past forty, and you start again. Every time you finish, the number has shifted by a few. The drip continues its count from above, steady and unhurried, and the fog hangs thick and warm in your lungs, and the white slot of sky at the top has begun, very slightly, to look less like a window and more like the underside of something very large, very far away, and not yet finished becoming itself.
