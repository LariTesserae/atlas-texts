The first thing that lives in the bowl is the fog. Each morning it climbs out of the cloud below the rim and pours over the lip, slow and white, and the people, the trees, and the animals all rise into it. Everything here is shaped by how it drinks from that air.

The people are lean and dense-boned, with skin darkened by generations under the canopy, where the light is always green-gold and never strong enough to burn. Their eyes adjust quickly to dim rooms and slowly to open sky, and when they climb out onto the rim at midday they squint and blink like animals coming out of a den. Many of them move without hurry. Life in the stacked dwellings teaches patience: you climb ladders carrying water, you wait for fog to thicken before harvesting ferns, you wait for the bats to finish before you walk the lanes at dusk.

They keep time by water and flowers. Their counting is not in days but in fog-mornings, and their year is divided by the long white flowering of the hardwoods, when the whole bowl turns pale and the air tastes of sweetness and rot. They have names for the seasons of drip, the heavy weeks when every leaf overflows, and the thin weeks when the canopy goes quiet and the cisterns of the trees swell low. A child learns the difference between a fog that will fill the ferns and one that will only wet the tops of the trees before it lifts. The knowledge is in the hands and in the body more than in words.

Their craft is slow and exact. A flint blade is knapped from a core over a long afternoon, each flake struck with the patience of someone who knows that a mistake costs a day. Bone awls and fiber cord sew hides and bark mats. Wooden mallets drive the wedges that split the grey blocks for the terraces. The masons fit stone without mortar, feeling for the seat of each block with their palms and tapping it home, and their walls have stood through more winters than the names of the families who built them. They do not cut stone to be seen. They cut it to be used, and the best work is the kind that looks as if the cliff grew that way.

They have a tense, watchful relation to the old buildings. The oldest houses have sunk so deep that their doorways are now below the streets, and roots have pushed through their walls until the masonry is half wood. The people do not clear these roots. They route around them, prop the sagging lintels, tie new ropes to the living trunks, and say, when a wall finally gives, that the tree has taken its share. Some of them speak of the ancient builders as if they were still nearby, listening, and they leave small offerings of seed and fiber in the cracks where the old stone opens. They know the roots are slowly turning the city into soil, and they seem to accept this the way they accept the fog lifting: it is the rhythm of the place, and they live inside it.

Their voices carry across the bowl. Hammers ring on stone all day. Women call to each other from ladder to ladder, and children run the rope bridges shrieking, their bare feet slapping the wood. When the clappers knock at dusk, the whole settlement answers, and gathering is a kind of sound before it is a kind of meeting. Smoke from the hearths rises in thin blue threads that hang in the still air and then drift down the lanes, carrying the smell of resin and tubers roasting in ash.

The animals are a crowd that never thins. The moths are the size of open hands, and their wings hold the fog's own colors in ways that make you look twice. Beetles with shell-plates shimmer like dropped coins in the moss. At dusk the midges rise in columns that thicken and sway, and the bats pour from the cliff crevices in a river of wings so dense that the sky seems to be leaving the rock. Birds with long tails and bright throats call from every level of the canopy, their songs braided so tightly that the ear gives up trying to separate them and simply listens to the whole. Frilled lizards race up the trunks, flaring their throats at anything that moves too near. Small arboreal creatures with prehensile tails hang from branch to branch, dropping fruit and leaves that the people gather from the ground.

On the floor, the grazers move through the ferns in herds the size of goats, cropping the understory and ducking under the ropes the people have strung between trees. They are patient and wary, their heads always half-raised, and they know the moss pools well. Where the moss holds its gleaming water, they kneel and drink, and the people have learned to watch them there, because a grazer at a pool shows where the water is.

The hunters of the bowl are long and low, with mottled coats that break up their outlines in the dim light. They stalk the grazers along the forest edge and follow the herds down the slopes when the evening fog thickens. Among the branches, the climbers hang like thick vines, perfectly still, until something passes below and they strike. The people keep their distance from the trees at certain hours, and they wear their hunting spears in a particular way, because the climbers are quick and the lesson of a bitten shoulder is taught early.

All of them, the stacked people and the grazers and the cats and the bats and the moths, are bound to the same slow machine: fog rolling up over the rim each morning, condensing on the broad waxy leaves, dripping in steady lines down to the moss, withdrawing each evening and leaving the canopy to drip in the dark. The people are one part of it, tending their terraces and their roots and their small offerings in the cracks, learning each year the timing of fog and flower and migration, rebuilding what the stone and the roots have worn down. They do not hold the place still. They move with it, and the bowl, deep in the high country and sinking further into itself with every season, keeps its own patient counsel about what endures and what becomes soil.
