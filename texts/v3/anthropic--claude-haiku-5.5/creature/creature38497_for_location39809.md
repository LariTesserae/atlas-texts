The people of the plateau are small and unhurried, and they look as though they were made by the same slow processes that made the rock and the moss. Their skin is a mottled grey-brown that darkens in the damp and pales in the rare dry weeks, so a person who has lived through many seasons on the basalt is patterned like the columns themselves. Their eyes are large and dark and take in the pewter light without strain. In the open, without the shadows that dim light would normally give, they seem to see the whole of a thing at once, and newcomers from lower country sometimes find it unsettling to be looked at so evenly.

Their hands tell you the most about them. The palms are thick and ridged with callus from handling cut basalt, and the fingertips are often scored with the fine white lines of old cuts from the rock's edges. Children learn early to move the way the moss requires. They put each foot down heel first and roll it forward, weight spread, because the ground swallows a careless step and holds it. By adulthood the walk is so ingrained that a person from the settlement crosses a stretch of moss almost without disturbing the ground, and the small many-legged things scatter from them and close back over their tracks as if nothing had passed.

They breathe the fungal dust without seeming to notice it. Their chests are deep and their breath slow, and they carry a faint sweetness on their clothes from the caps they handle every day. The violet dusk does not frighten them. It is the color of their evenings, and they have learned to read it the way other peoples read a sunset: as a sign of the day's end, of rest, of who is still absent. When the haze thickens and the sky goes the color of bruised fruit, the settlement gathers on its highest terraces, and people sit in a loose ring with their faces lifted and say very little. Their ceremonies are quiet, mostly listening. Someone may set a stone down, once, and the clack of it carries across the basin and everyone waits for the echo that never really comes.

Their work is patient and layered, and their social life is layered the same way. Each family holds a terrace, and each terrace holds the ruins of the families before it. The old cut-stone courses at the bottom belong to the dead, and the settlement's people say so plainly: the foundations are the ones who came first, and they are still holding weight. Above them are the clay and wicker of the middle generations, and the roofs of dried frond on top are the living. When someone dies, their body is given to the moss at the edge of the cistern-fields, where the humus is deepest, and within a season the ground there is springier and greener than anywhere else. The people do not describe this as sad. They describe it as the way the level below becomes the level under the next.

They are skilled and careful with tools that are almost nothing by the standards of the lowlands. Obsidian blades are knapped with a precision that takes a lifetime to learn, and bone awls are kept in leather rolls that are oiled against the damp. The few pieces of bronze are treasured, handled with cloth, and kept in a dry niche in the oldest wall. Nobody living remembers the trader who brought them, and the story of how they came has become one of the things told at dusk, when the color is right.

Their relationship with the vines is the closest thing they have to a science, and it is practiced rather than written. They tend the fenced gardens of spiral vine and read the tightening and loosening of the coils the way a farmer below reads the sky. A coil that draws in tight before rain means to bring the drying frames down. A coil that loosens and lies flat means the spring that feeds the lower cistern is about to rise. And when the vines in a garden curl inward all at once, every coil in the same direction, people stop what they are doing and go still. They know a hunter has passed close. They do not go looking for it. They wait until the vines unwind, which they do slowly, one tendril at a time, and only then do they go back to work.

They have a complicated respect for the hunters. The long-necked creatures move through the fern-thickets without a sound, and the people think of them as the most attentive things on the plateau, which is why they keep such care around them. Children are taught to watch the trembling of stems and the slow turning of vines, and to read those motions the way they read weather. There is no hatred in it, and no pursuit. The hunters take the grazers, the grazers eat the fungi, the fungi bloom in the wet, and the people live inside that cycle without trying to stand outside it.

Their speech is low and sparing. They have many words for water, perhaps more than for anything else: words for the drip that is about to become a stream, for the sheen left on basalt after a storm, for the soft gurgle of a spring finding a new crack. They speak without quite intending to, lowering their voices automatically, and visitors notice that conversations in the settlement have a hushed quality, as though the quiet itself were one of the people sitting in the room.

The oldest members speak of the land most carefully. They do not say it is alive, and they do not say it is not. They say that the cracks open where the stone is ready to open, that the springs come up where the ground has been waiting for water, that the vines know things they do not know. When they speak of the plateau, they lower their voices further and sometimes pause in the middle of a sentence. Asked why, one old woman with a face like weathered basalt said only that you do not speak loudly to something that might be listening, and that the land has been listening for a very long time.

In the end it is hard to say where the people stop and the plateau begins. They are built from the same slow patience as the stone, they breathe the same dust, they read the same vines, and when they die they become the same soft humus that feeds the moss. The settlement is a small, careful thing in a land that never stops changing, and the people who live there have learned to change with it, quietly, one step at a time, heel first and rolling forward, so the ground takes their weight and lets them pass.
