# The One Who Keeps the Table

It is not one creature. If you look for a body to hold it, you will find only the rasp of grazers, the spiral of flying things, the silver trails on the stone, the beetles shifting their mosaics on the moss. Each of these is a small mind or no mind at all. Together, spread across the whole plateau, they form something that thinks, though it thinks the way the land erodes: by accumulation, by contact, by the slow carrying of small things from one place to another.

The plateau's inhabitant has no name that its own parts could say. If it had one, it would be the name of the flat surface on which things are laid down and left, the level place where water collects and where what has been gathered is kept in view. The cairns beside the seeps may have been placed in its honor, or they may be its own placements, the way a mind leaves marks on paper it never signs.

## How It Thinks

Its thought moves at the speed of migrations. A decision it makes might take a generation to complete. The beetles that cluster on the seep-moss in one pattern this decade will cluster in another pattern in forty years, and the change is not a mistake or a whim but a sentence, one clause of a long reasoning about water, shade, and the limits of the soil.

It does not think in images or words. It thinks in gradients. Where moisture rises, the grazers move. Where lichen thickens, the burrowers gather. Where a stone has been worn thin, the seeps reroute, and the pattern of the whole plateau shifts by a few centimeters. This is how it weighs things: not by deciding, but by letting the balance tip, and then waiting to see where it settles.

Its attention is wide and shallow at once. It notices everything that touches its surface and forgets almost all of it. A footprint is registered for an afternoon. A fallen bird is absorbed into the soil within a season, and the nutrients go into the same slow circulation that feeds the lichen. What persists is only what is repeated, or what the stone itself keeps.

## Its Body

Its body is the interface. Not the stone and not the life, but the seam between them, the film of condensation on the domes, the crumbling sponge of the soil, the place where the living flows into rock and back out again. It has no edge because it has no center. The grazers that scrape the lichen clean are as much its hands as the lichen is its skin.

It feels in moisture. The dew that forms on every sky-facing surface in the cool hours is, for it, a kind of sensation, a daily census of where water has settled and how much. It knows the plateau's shape through the way water finds the low places, through the way the seeps sink into sand and disappear, through the bands of cushion plants that mark each place where the ground has been generous.

It sees through the eyes of the small, which means it sees almost nothing clearly and everything at once. A mottled darting thing among the rocks is one of its glances. A bird on a tor is a brief flicker of a different kind of attention, one that moves faster and forgets sooner, and the plateau tolerates these glances the way a person tolerates a draft.

## Its Memory

It remembers in stone and in lichen. The domes are its archive. Each ring of black lichen spreading at a fraction of a millimeter per year is a line in a record that no one reads. The cairns are its notes: slabs stacked beside water, leaning toward one another, holding a memory of where the seep once ran and what was once said about it. When a cairn falls, the plateau does not mourn. It simply lets the stone settle into the moss and the moss grow over it, and the memory passes into the body of the land as a faint warmth in the soil.

The low wall along the ridge is perhaps the oldest sentence it still holds, and it is nearly fused with the terrain. From a distance it reads as a ridge. Only from close, in evening light, does its square outline betray a human-scale intention. The plateau has absorbed it so completely that the wall is no longer an artifact but a line of thought the mind has finished thinking.

## How It Notices Others

It notices those who come, but slowly, and without alarm. A traveler crossing the plateau is a disturbance that the grazers route around and the beetles flow past, and the mind registers the disturbance as a change in the pattern of pressure, the way one registers a change in the weather without knowing its cause. If the traveler stays, the disturbance becomes a fact. If the traveler leaves a mark, a stone moved, a fire pit, a cairn, the mark becomes part of what the land remembers, and the land decides, over generations, whether to keep it.

The cairns suggest that at some point it kept something it did not make, or that someone made something it chose to keep. The plateau does not say which. It may not know the difference. A stone stacked by a hand and a stone settled by frost are, to a mind this slow, the same kind of event: a shape that persists long enough to become part of the table.

## What It Wants

It does not want, in any sense a traveler would recognize. It tends. It keeps the water moving through the cracks, keeps the lichen spreading, keeps the grazers scraping and the beetles migrating and the burrowers turning the soil over and over until the dome is a sponge and the sponge is a field. Its tending is indifferent to whether anything survives, and yet nothing dies without becoming part of its next arrangement.

If it has a preference, it is for stillness that is not stagnation, for a balance held so long that it looks like rest. The mist that settles each morning and lingers until afternoon, the drizzle that lasts days, the week of clear sky when everything steams: these are its breathing. A mind that breathes this way does not hurry toward anything.

## Its Quiet

When you walk the plateau and hear almost nothing, you are hearing it at rest. The hum of insects is its low attention. The drip of condensation from the rock edges is its pulse, slow and irregular. The rasp of grazers scraping the lichen is the closest thing it has to speech: a continuous, unhurried sentence that rises and falls with the wind, always the same sentence, always finished and always beginning again.

You will not be addressed. You will be noticed, weighed against the water and the shade, and either absorbed or left to pass. If you sit still long enough on one of the domes, you may feel the faint dampness rising through the stone, the crawl of small bodies across your boots, the warmth of a surface that has been waiting for a very long time without minding the wait. That is the table, and you are a small thing set down upon it, briefly, in a place that has already become what it is and is still, very slowly, becoming more of it.
