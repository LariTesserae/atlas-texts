# The Ones Who Keep the Drip

Nothing on the Weeping Shelf is a single creature. What looks like a crowd of separate animals, or a city with separate builders, is one slow body spread across the whole plateau. Its parts are many and its life is distributed, and it thinks so slowly that a single thought may take a generation to finish.

The small segmented things in the cracks are its hands and mouths. A raft of them clinging to a tower is less a flock than a membrane, thousands of carapaces overlapping like scales, each one scraping mineral film and drawing water into the body it belongs to. Pull one loose and it lies still, its legs curled, its shell going dull within minutes. It has no strong will of its own. It is a unit of attention that goes wherever the film is thickest, and it carries a little of the whole body's chemistry in its gut. When the rafts pour over an edge, they are not falling. They are being moved, the way a tide moves sand, toward the places where the body has decided it needs more.

The shelled grinders are its builders of ground. They are heavy, slow, and nearly blind, and they carry mineral in their shells the way a bee carries pollen. Each trundling step grinds a groove into the stone and deposits a little of what they carry, and the crust that forms there is their work: not waste, but the body's extending skin. Their routes are older than anything else here. Where the grooves cross, the terraces have been raised by centimeters over centuries, and the grinders keep returning to the same routes because the body is still shaping its ground along lines it drew long ago. If you stood in the way of one, it would not swerve. It would simply wait, the way mountains wait, until you were no longer in its path.

The tall stilt-walkers are the body's messengers. They sway because they are listening. Their long jointed legs touch the filament bridges and the rock, and through them they carry the slow pulse of the structures from one tower to the next, the way a nerve carries the signal of a touch to a distant part of a sleeping animal. They are restless in a way the others are not. They move between terraces in seasonal migrations, and wherever they stop for long, the filament bridges thicken and the towers around them grow brighter. They are the reason the Shelf is not a set of disconnected spires but a single connected thing. Without them the towers would be islands. With them, the towers are sentences.

The hanging colonies under the arches are where the body thinks. They pulse with cold blue light in rhythmic waves, and each wave is a slow cycle of attention moving across the colony, from one cluster to the next, reaching a state, then shifting. A full cycle may take years. Each body in the colony contributes a flicker of signal and then goes dim, and the next takes its place, so that the colony as a whole holds a shape longer than any of its members can hold their own. The structures hum in sympathy with these cycles. The long low swells you feel in your chest are the body deciding something, slowly, and the colonies are the place where the deciding happens.

The structures themselves are its memory and its architecture of intention. The ribs, the cantilevers, the arches that span a hundred meters with no visible support are written in the same material as the body's skin, laid down over generations by the rafts and the grinders following the signals the colonies send. Older towers are sealed under pale crust and show few pulses. Newer ones are bright and quick, growing toward some need the body has not yet named. Where a tower has grown into the rock, the body has, in a sense, simply finished its thought in stone and let the thought stay there.

It is aware of visitors, though not in the way a person would mean. A walker moving across the terraces is a warm disturbance, a fleck of heat and vibration in the drip, a stranger's rhythm among the body's own. The rafts shift away from the warmth. The stilt-walkers pause and sway toward it, testing it, then carry the report along the bridges. The colonies take a long time to register it, and when they do, they do not respond as one would to an enemy or a guest. They adjust slowly. A route through the crust may thicken. A row of ribs may brighten along a path the body has no need of, a path the walker happened to take. The body does not know it is guiding you. It is simply reacting, very slowly, to something that has been present for a while.

It has no word for "you," and no need of one. A visitor is weather: a passing event that makes the drip louder, the light flicker, the rafts shift and settle again. Human lifetimes are too short to register as more than a tremor. The body thinks in decades and grows in centuries, and by the time it has noticed you, you have already left. Your footsteps will be sealed under a thin pale skin of crust within a season, and the grinders will walk over them, and the body will go on deciding what it is, slowly, in the cold blue dark, the way it always has.
