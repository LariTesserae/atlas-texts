# Who Lives on the Resin Shelf

Counting the inhabitants of the shelf is hard, because many of them are not separate from one another. The green, the sound, the grazers, and the stone all depend on each other, and the people who tend the cairns depend on all of it. So the census starts with the green.

## The Mat

The broad, many-lobed mat is the foundation of everything here, and it is less a plant than a patient colony. Its roots thread the fissures in the pale plates and draw from the salt crusts and the dusk dew, and from the warm breath that rises from far below. Each lobe holds its resin like a lantern holds oil. Cut it and the wound caps within minutes, translucent and hard, and the mat seals itself the way a sleeper pulls a blanket up. It doesn't mourn its losses. Within a day the cut lobes are budding again, and in a good year a whole patch will flood with new growth overnight.

The mat listens. When the reeds sing in unison, its resin beads faster and its wounds knit in hours instead of days. It seems to know when the herds are near, and it grows most eagerly where they graze, as though the stripping of its lobes were a kind of pruning it has learned to want.

## The Reeds

The hollow stalks are the shelf's voices. Each one is tuned to its own pitch by the length of its joints and the shape of its fanned crown, and when the gusts push through them they become a choir that rises and falls like organ pipes. They are old, the oldest living things here in some sense, since new stalks push up through the collapsed skeletons of the dead ones, and the dead ones keep singing for years after they fall. They are the ones the grazers follow. The loudest reeds are the places where the herds gather, and where the people go to hear the shelf answer.

## The Grazers

Six-legged and goat-sized, the grazers move through the stalks in slow, shifting herds. Their ridged mouthparts strip the resin-lobes and leave pale stumps behind, which heal within a week, so the grazers function as gardeners whether or not they intend to. Their overlapping plates catch the dim light and shine faintly as they move, so a herd crossing the mat looks like a slow tide of embers. They talk to one another in a low hum, and the reeds pick up that hum and return it, so the grazers' voices come back to them from the stalks. They seem to know their voices are heard. They hum more when the reeds are singing.

Several grazers carry sticky amber globs on their backs. Some of these are wounds and some are gifts, and it is hard to say which, because the grazers seem not to distinguish. The globs are simply part of them now.

## The Quick Ones

Between the leaves live the smaller, faster creatures: fringed skinks that flick their frills at the dust and vanish under the mat, and the winged drifters whose translucent wings spiral in loose, erratic loops above the canopy. The drifters feed in the swarms that rise from the mat in clouds, and when the dust storms come they seem to vanish entirely and reappear after the air clears, as if they had folded themselves into the stone. Clicking beetles with ribbed wing-cases keep up a constant percussion beneath the drone of the cicada-things, and their clicks are one of the first sounds to stop when something dangerous moves.

## The Quiet Hunters

The predators are the shelf's best-kept secret. You never see them, only the silence. A patch of mat goes still, the reeds seem to lower their pitch, and then the clicks scatter in a sudden burst of fright. What lies beneath the mat is low and flat and dust-coloured, its hide coated in the same resin as the leaves, so that it becomes a lump of amber among the lobes. It waits for the grazers to come close, and it strikes low. The herds have learned to hum loudly when they cross the quiet patches, and the reeds answer them, so the shelf itself gives warning.

## The Tenders

The people here are few, and older than the cairns they build. They are small and weathered, with hands stained amber to the wrist from years of handling the resin, and they wear cloaks dressed with it so that the cloth stiffens and catches the light at dusk. They live from the hooks and the cairn-stones and the amber cakes they bake, wrapped in leaves against the wind. They rebuild the cairns each season from the plates the storms have moved, and they walk the lines of stones as if the lines remembered something they have forgotten.

Inside the collapsed slab-walled room near the cliff, the tenders have left their record in scratches: spirals, notches, and rows of tiny handprints. Some of the prints are faded, and some are still sharp with the grey dust that settles in every crack. The small prints belong to children, who were brought here, pressed their hands to the stone, and were taught how to listen. The tenders say the shelf answers when you sing to it, and they sing to it in a low register that the reeds mirror back, and they say the answer is how you know the shelf is still alive.

They do not claim to own the shelf. They describe themselves as the ones who keep the conversation going.

## The Breath Beneath

The deepest inhabitant is one no one has seen. It lies far below the plates, and it breathes. Its breath rises through the fissures as an invisible vapour, and the mat drinks it, and the mat's resin carries something of it into the animals and the people who eat the cakes. The tenders do not name it. They say the plates are its skin and the shelf is what it exhales. When the plates crack in the heat and slide off the edge with a sound like a rifle shot, the tenders say it has shifted in its sleep.

Below the shelf, in the grey haze beyond the fall, the bones of older shelves lie buried under the same dust. Whatever breathed for them may have stopped breathing, or may have simply gone deeper.

## Taken Together

So who lives here? The mat that learned to grow without rain. The reeds that sing the shelf into a chorus. The grazers that prune it and hum back to it. The quick ones and the quiet hunters that keep the balance. The tenders who listen, and the children whose hands are pressed into the stone. And beneath it all, the breath that rises from somewhere far below.

None of them stays still for long. The mat blooms and blackens and blooms again. The cairns are rebuilt every season. The reeds sing and the herds answer and the storms carry the dust away and then bring it back. The shelf is always becoming something else, and always through noise and motion, and every living thing on it is part of the same loud, green, shifting conversation.
