# Those Who Keep the Lift

They call themselves the Lift, after the pumps, though the word covers more than machinery. A person is *of the Lift* or is not. There are about three hundred of them, give or take the number that the sumps and the rock take each decade, and they are easy to recognize even before you see their faces. Their skin has a fine grey sheen from years of wet air, and it wrinkles fast at the fingers and wrists, the way skin does after long soaking. Fungal bloom gathers in the creases of their elbows and behind their knees, so they keep those places scrubbed raw, and the scrubbing leaves them pink in patches. Their hands are the strongest part of them. The calluses are thick, and the nails are stained the chalky cream of carbonate, which never quite washes out.

They are pale, but not sickly. Living at the bottom of a well has given them a patient stillness and a habit of looking up without moving their heads. Their eyes adjust to the grey shafts so well that they squint at full daylight when they are carried up for a funeral or a trade. A few of the old ones have gone a little blind at the edges and navigate the terraces by touch, by the sound of water over a particular lip, by the heat that rises from a particular seep.

**Speech**

No one talks at ordinary volume in the basin. The engines take a voice and fold it into noise, so the Lift learned long ago to speak low and close, almost into the listener's ear, and to rely on a second language of touch: two fingers tapped on a forearm for *the valve is hissing*, a flat palm pressed to the chest for *stop the pump*, a grip on the wrist and a tilt of the head toward the lower sumps for *come and see the water is wrong*. Outsiders find this unsettling. The Lift find shouting rude, the way a sleeping house would find a bell rude.

Their words are shorter than the words of people in the sunlit country above. Many of their sayings concern water, and most of them are about patience. *The rock is faster* is the one you hear most, said with a shrug, usually while someone is scraping away a new white skin from a valve housing. It is not a complaint. It is closer to a greeting, an acknowledgment that the other person is still here too.

**Labor**

The day is organized around the cycle of the pumps. Each engine swells over hours, and the people who tend them work in shifts that follow the swelling: checking the seals, clearing the intake screens of moth-wings and fly-husks, stripping the carbonate from the valves with copper scrapers, greasing chains that will be scaled over by the next shift. A pump-keeper's knees are ruined by thirty. Their ears go next, the high whistle of the relief valves burrowing into them until they no longer register it at all, which everyone agrees is a mercy and a warning.

Between shifts, the Lift repair what the basin has taken. Gallery walls are rebuilt a few meters inward. Sheds are re-roofed with iron sheets salvaged from sheds that have already gone under. Pipes encased in travertine are not dug out; they are tapped and rerouted, and the old runs are left to become fossils in the stone. Nobody mourns a pipe. But they keep the old pipes' brackets, bent and grey, hung on the walls of the pump house like the antlers of animals no longer hunted.

**The tallymen**

A smaller number of the Lift do not work the pumps at all. They are the tallymen, and they measure the rock.

They go out with brass calipers, a level, a sack of dated iron pins, and a notebook kept dry in an oilcloth wrap. They choose a lip on one of the terraces, take its thickness at three points, and record it. Then they come back to the same lip the next year, and the year after, and the decade after that, until the whole basin is a dense lattice of their small numbers. The oldest tallyman, a woman called Ovra who has walked the same third terrace for forty-one years, says that its lip has thickened by the width of her palm. She says this as if reporting the weather. When she is asked why she bothers, since the rock will take the terrace whether anyone counts or not, she says that the counting is how the rock learns it is being watched. Then she laughs, because she knows how that sounds, and goes back to her calipers.

**The dead**

The Lift do not bury their dead in the ground, which would be pointless. Instead they lay the body into one of the warm pools at the edge of the middle flat, wrapped in cloth and weighted with a stone, and they let the water carry it down into the crust. The crust closes over it slowly. Within a generation, the place is a low white hump in the terrace, shaped roughly like a person lying on their side. The tallymen mark these humps too. A family can walk the basin and find their great-grandparents by the shapes of the stone, the way other people read the names on a headstone.

There is no grief about this that outsiders would recognize. There is a lot of attention. The Lift visit the humps when they pass them, touch the cold crust, tell the stone what has happened in the town since the last visit. The rock is the one thing in the basin that keeps a record and does not leave.

**Children**

Children are born in the lower town, in rooms that smell of wet iron and warm salt, and the first thing they learn is the breath of the engines. A child who cannot sleep without the throb is considered healthy. A child who can sleep through a relief-valve shriek is considered wise, and occasionally a little worrying. They learn the touch-language before they learn words, and the older children carry the younger ones along the gallery walls at the height where the damp is thinnest. They chase the blind eels in the shallow pools with sticks until a keeper calls them off, and then they stop, because the eels move in those long unison ripples and the children have been told that the ripples are a kind of water-speech, and nobody is sure whether that is true.

**The other tenants**

The Lift do not think of the moths, the bats, the frilled pool-things or the reeds as wildlife. They think of them as the other tenants, and they treat the arrangement with a wary courtesy. The sumps are cleared to keep the intakes working, but a few pools are left alone, and the people do not net the eels or burn the bat roosts. Guano is scraped from the cliffs and carried up to the terraces, where it feeds the reeds that lean toward the light. The moths are fed to the children's lamps and then forgotten. The birds in the pipe-brackets are never moved, even when they foul the valves, because the Lift say a nest is a promise that someone will be here next season.

At dusk the flies rise off the pools in a grey cloud, and the whole basin hums, and the engines hum beneath it, and the cliffs drip their constant percussion. The Lift stand in their doorways and watch it come up through the shafts of dying light. Someone usually taps a forearm, two fingers, soft: *the water is rising*. Someone answers with a palm on the chest: *then we will hold it*. And they go back in, to the lamps and the valves and the cold stone, to the slow business of keeping a bowl of rock from swallowing the only room they have.

From the sunlit country above, this looks like resignation. Down here, it is closer to care: the patient, unglamorous attention of people who know that nothing stays put, and who have decided, again and again, to be the thing that stays a little longer.
