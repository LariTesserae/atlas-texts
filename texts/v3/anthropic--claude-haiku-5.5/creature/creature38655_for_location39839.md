The people of the basin are not really a population you could count. They are a set of households spread across the courses, and their number shifts with the seasons, with the dead, and with the children who arrive in the wet months. Those who live highest, on the newest terraces near the rim, are the ones who still remember the stone being cut. The old families hold the lower courses, and the closer a family lives to the bottom, the older its claim to the place. Ask someone where they come from and they will name a terrace, not a town.

They are small and dark, weathered by sun that reaches them only in the hours when it clears the rim, and by the damp that never leaves. Lime dust sits in the creases of their hands and around their eyes, so that even the young look faintly powdered, like the plaster on the walls. Their hands are broad and scarred from stone and bronze, and many of them have a stiffness in the knuckles that they treat as a normal part of the work. They wear undyed linen knotted at the shoulder and, when the rain is heavy, cloaks of felted goat wool that smell of smoke and wet animal. Their feet are bare on the stone and calloused to a hardness that lets them cross a slick terrace at a run.

Their days are set by water. Every household has a part in the channels, and the sluice-keepers, who are the most respected people in the basin, decide each morning which gates to open based on the sky, the swifts, and the way the cistern surfaces have settled overnight. The keepers count rains the way other peoples count years, and their counting is not written. It is kept in the walls. Each repair leaves its mark: a stone set in a different colour, a band of grey mortar pressed into a crack, a glazed tile turned face-down so that its back shows. Read the walls in the right order and you can trace a family's labour back for five generations. The people do not write down what happened. They build it into the stone, and then they walk past it every day.

The hammering that rises from every level is the sound of guilds at work. Masons recut the stepped edges where the rain has worn them, and their work never finishes, because the basin keeps moving. Coppersmiths beat out channel-plates and the bronze awls that stitch the tile roofs. Potters turn drainage pipes on wheels set into the lower courses, where the clay is wettest and easiest to shape. Weavers work the looms in the shelter of the upper rooms, and their shuttles knock back and forth with a rhythm the rest of the basin has learned to ignore, the way a city ignores its own traffic. Every craft is loud, and every craft is answered by another somewhere across the bowl. Conversation moves along the terraces as a continuous murmur, and gossip is the basin's real architecture: a dispute on the fourth course is known by the second before the sun has moved. Their public voice is shared, and their privacy depends on knowing exactly how far each sound will carry.

They feed themselves on barley and beans from the terrace beds, on goat's milk and cheese, on fowl from the lower levels, and on the fish that come up from the deep cisterns when someone drops in scraps. The fish are pale, blind, and oddly patient. The people have names for some of them, and children are taught to count them as a kind of game. They have a rule, which every child learns early, that food is never taken from the lowest terrace.

That rule is the center of the basin's sense of itself. The lowest course holds the oldest cistern, and it is warm, always damp, and faintly lit in its shadows. The moss there glows a pale colour, enough that a person can read a short line by it when cupping their hand over the stone. Children sometimes lean over the edge at dusk and try to read their own names in the glow. The elders say the bottom is where the water remembers what it has been asked to hold. They say it in a practical tone, the way a farmer might describe a field that won't take seed, and they do not say it with fear. Nobody builds on the lowest course. Nobody is buried there. When a wall weeps on one of the higher terraces, the families mend it at once. When a room floods with a cold seep and a green bloom spreads across the floor, the family that lives there moves up a level without much argument, and the abandoned room is left to the moss.

At night the animal calls from the lower basin. No one has ever seen it clearly. It makes a long, low sound that comes up through the stone and seems to begin in the cisterns themselves, and the people answer it. Every household has a set of pots and a bag of small stones, and at the first call they begin a rhythmic clatter that runs from the bottom terrace to the rim and back. The elders say the call is a question and the clatter is the answer: we are awake, we heard you, we are here. They say that if no one answers, it comes up the stairs. Nobody can say whether this is true, and nobody is willing to test it. The clatter goes on until the call stops, and then it goes on a little longer, until the frogs in the cisterns take over and the whole basin is ringing like a struck bowl.

The fig growing out of the third terrace has a place in their stories that is almost a religion. Its roots have wrapped the old stone lintel so completely that the lintel can no longer fall, and the people say the fig holds the wall up. Nobody cuts it back, and families bring their first barley to its base at the start of each harvest. The lizards that bask on its bark are not disturbed. The thorny grey shrub on the ruined arch above the drop is left alone for the same reason: no one wants to be the one who loosened what was holding.

The cat moves along the upper terraces with the calm of an animal that knows exactly where its prey goes. The people do not hunt it. The bats leave the cisterns at dusk in their spirals and the people watch them without comment. The rodents run the aqueducts, and children chase them and do not catch them. Everything here is part of the same economy, and no one believes they are outside it.

Their days are long and loud and tiring, and they are not unhappy in the way outsiders might assume. They know the basin is still settling. They know the walls will weep and the cracks will open after a storm and close after a dry season. They know the lowest cistern holds something they do not understand and do not intend to disturb. They have built their lives on top of that knowledge, course by course, the way their ancestors built on rubble laid over older paving, and they walk the stairs with water jars balanced on their shoulders, calling to each other across the warm air. Above them the plain stretches flat and pale to the horizon, and nobody on it can see into the bowl. The people below prefer it that way. The sound of their work rises over the rim and fades into the ordinary afternoon, and up there, the world carries on as though nothing had ever been dug out of it at all.
