# The Renderers

They have no name for themselves that carries across the terraces. The crews call one another by trade and by the sound of their work: *tamper*, *lime-hand*, *spade-wet*, *rope-back*. Someone who has been in the lowlands long enough is called *settled*, and the word is used with a faint note of pity. Nobody here is settled.

They are never fully separate from what they make. A crew member's cloak is oiled hide, but beneath the hide the skin is pale and chalky, rubbed with lime until it has the grain of plaster. The lime sinks into the cracks in their hands and stays there. Their fingers are thickened at the knuckles from pressing clay, and their forearms are corded and long from hauling. Most stand a little stooped, as if they have spent their lives leaning into a wall.

## Bodies

They are tall in the way the chimneys are tall: narrow, upright, weathering at the top. Their faces are broad at the cheekbone and narrow at the jaw, and their eyes are set deep and pale. In the lamplight the skin takes on the green-white of the phosphorescent crust, so that a crew moving through a deep cut looks like it is lit from within.

They do not seem to need much. They drink from the mineral pans at the chimney bases, a thick, faintly sweet water that leaves a white bloom on the lips. They sleep standing or curled in the dry lee of a wall, and they wake to the first stroke of the bell without being shaken. Injuries are patched with fresh slurry, which dries hard in an hour and is cracked open again in a day, when the work resumes.

Some of them are wrong in small ways. A hand with one finger too many, a face that seems to be settling toward the left. Nobody remarks on it. The crews work the way the walls settle: unevenly, and all at once.

## How They Speak

They rarely use full sentences in the open. Their language is built from short, clipped calls that carry over water and rope-noise, and from knocks. A mallet on a stake means *here* or *hold*. Two strikes followed by a drag means *the ground is moving*. The calls turn into something close to song when a crew is working a long course together, the leader's cry answered by the line, the spades rising and falling on the count.

They write, too, though not often. The spare marks cut into the terrace walls are theirs. They are not an alphabet so much as a shape that gets repeated: a vertical with a hook, a cut across a bowl. The same shape recurs from wall to wall, but never exactly. Where one crew carves it with the hook curled tight, another leaves it open. A third adds a short stroke at the base that no one can explain. When a crew is asked what the mark means, they answer with the sound the mark makes when struck. Then they go back to work.

I am told that the inscriptions are copies of copies. One crew lays down the shape, then the next crew, working from memory, lays down its own version beside it. Over generations the drift accumulates, and the result is a message that is always being restated and never quite agreed on. This is considered the proper way to keep a thing alive.

## Work, and What Work Is

Work is not a means to an end here. It is the condition of being a person in the lowlands. The rains undo everything each season, so the making never completes. A crew sets out at the bell and returns at the bell, and between those moments it does one thing at a time: packs clay into a form, tamps a course, lifts a brick into the wet, lashes a scaffold, digs a channel out of a slump, pulls spoil up a rope.

The slumps are the crucial events. When a bank gives way, the crew nearest it runs toward the roar. They dig, and sometimes they find the buried. What happens next is the part outsiders find hardest to hear: the crews do not lift the fallen out of the mud. They dig until the ground is firm, then pack the clay they've dug, along with whatever is still in it, into the new course. A rebuilt wall is always partly made of the people who were working on the last one. The walls near the basin are the oldest and the most densely packed, and the crews say they hum faintly when struck, though no one has put a hand to them long enough to be sure.

## Rooms That Seal

Some of the cells and sheds are closed. There are doors with no handles, lintels with no lamps, and walls that look as if they have been dressed and then plastered over. Crews pass these without looking at them. When one is opened, the people inside come out slowly and blink at the grey light. They are often changed. A face is smoother. A hand is missing a crease. Once a woman came out with her hair grown long and white and went straight to the chimney bases and began to drink.

The crews do not explain the sealed rooms, but they are not afraid of them. A person who goes in is said to be *cured*, or *set*, or simply *gone in*. Nobody mourns them. Their absence is treated as a kind of weather.

## Their Relationship to the Strangeness

The leaning walls are theirs, and they are built to face the water. The uphill channel is theirs too. When a crew is asked why a channel runs uphill for a stretch, the answer is always a gesture toward the basin and a single word that means something like *listening*. The walls lean so that they can hear the flood coming. The channel carries a sound up the slope before the water arrives.

The chimneys are the hardest to understand. Water runs down their outer faces and gathers in the pans. The crews drink from the pans. The crews say the chimneys are *open*, and they bring their mouths close to the upper vents and breathe in, slow and deep, as if tasting the air that moves through the clay. Nobody has been able to say what the chimneys exhale.

The bell is the one thing nobody claims. It is struck on a rhythm that every crew follows, and the strike seems to come from somewhere deep in the terraces, though when crews go looking they find the striker-stone already settled into the mud, its surface worn smooth by a hand or a wave or a slump. The crews keep time to it without asking who keeps it.

## What They Are Building

No one here believes the work is finished, and no one believes it ever will be. The terraces rise and sink with the rains. The channels abandon their courses. The chimneys drip and the walls crack and the crews patch them with fresh mud, and the patched mud sets and cracks again. Each season the place is a little different from the last, and each season the crews keep at it, as though what they are making is not a thing but a motion, a steady refusal to let the ground stop becoming.

If you stand at the lowest terrace in the grey hour before the bell and watch them go out, you will see the thing they are doing. The walls lean toward the water. The spades rise and fall. The calls go up in clipped chains, answered down the line. The lime-pale hands press the wet clay into the form, and the form takes the impression, and the impression holds for a day, and then the rain comes back and the wall begins again to lean, and the crew comes out before dawn to lean back against it, shoulder to slurry, and to call the count that keeps them together while the ground decides what it will be.
