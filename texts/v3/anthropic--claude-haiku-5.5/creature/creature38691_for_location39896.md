What lives here is mostly the animals, but they don't live in the city the way animals live in a wild place. They have taken it over as a kind of habitat. Their bodies have adjusted to its surfaces, and they have adjusted its rhythms to theirs.

The crabs are the most visible. They are the size of dinner plates, and their shells have thickened and gone the colour of the silt, mottled with pale brine-scale and the green-black film from the walls. Many carry it on their backs, and it may be that they graze the film or that it grows on them and they tolerate it. Their claws have grown heavy and blunt from prying at mortar and rebar. When they move in their carpets across the lower causeways, the sound is a continuous soft ticking, and it has a rhythm. Each crab knocks its legs at a slightly different interval, but the field of them averages out into something close to the pumps' knocking, so that from a distance you can't tell which sound belongs to machines and which to animals. Standing on a causeway at dusk, you might decide the crabs are keeping time for the plant.

The amphibians sit in the drainage channels in their thousands, pale and flat as paper cut-outs. They are the most sensitive creatures in the city. Their throats pulse when water pressure changes, and within a few minutes of a storm building in the south, the whole channel network is pulsing together, a slow wave running the length of the grid. They are a kind of barometer, and the city's flow passes through them. They do nothing else. They wait, and the waiting is their form of attention.

The birds nest in the gantries. The long-legged rust-coloured flocks lift and resettle in unison, and when they land they fold themselves into the iron lattice, roosting in rows along the crossbeams, so that the empty lots below are framed by their dark shapes. Their eggs are chalky and white, close to the colour of the crust, and they incubate them on ledges where the brine has dried into plates. The birds seem to know which gantries are stable and which are slowly bending, and they move between them according to a logic that is not visible from the ground.

Under the waterline, the large grey-backed animal moves in its arcs. Its back breaks the surface at irregular intervals, a dull ridge with a mineral bloom along it, and sometimes the water above it dimples in a way that suggests it is larger than it looks. When it passes beneath a causeway, the concrete shivers in a way that the crabs respond to, pausing their ticking for a few seconds before resuming. It may be the oldest thing here. Its passage is the nearest the city has to a heartbeat that isn't mechanical.

The densest life is in the collapsed basements. These are the places where the old buildings have sunk into deep, cool hollows, and the animals pack into them so tightly that the individual creatures are hard to separate. The sound of them gathering is a soft, continuous shifting, like cloth being moved in another room. Crabs, amphibians, and things smaller and less familiar are mixed together in layers. The temperature in these hollows seems to be slightly lower than the air above, and the animals appear to prefer it. From above, the basements look like wells filled with something that breathes.

Something also lives in the cooling stacks, though it is harder to name. The thin vapour that rises from them and is bent sideways by the wind is not only steam. Where it thickens near the stack mouths, the air carries a faint smell of brine and warm iron, and occasionally a small pale shape goes up the inside of the stack and does not come back down for a long time. The stacks may be roosts, or cavities that a particular kind of creature has adapted to the heat and the draft. They are the city's lungs, and something inhabits them.

When the storm comes up from the south, all of it stops. The rain runs down the glazed walls in sheets, and within minutes the gutters are full and the causeways are under a skin of moving water. The crabs drop from the causeways into the flood. The birds fold their legs and sink into the gantries. The amphibians go still in their channels with their throats closed. The grey-backed animal sinks and does not surface. The whole city goes quiet in a way that is different from the usual quiet, a held stillness, and it lasts until the rain thins and the water starts to recede. Then the causeways fill again with movement. The crabs climb back up, the birds lift, the amphibians open their throats, and the pulse runs through the channels once more.

So the inhabitants are not one species, and they are not a single organism. They are a set of populations that have learned to live inside the made world, each occupying the parts of it that suit its body, and each keeping time with the machines and the water. The city has become their architecture. They maintain it in the only way available to them: by occupying it, moving through it, nesting in its frames, sheltering in its hollows, and letting its failing parts be replaced by new ones, a new roost or a new tunnel or a new pale plate of brine-crust. The ground was emptied of plants long ago, and what grew to fill that space is animal and patient and dense. The machines turn, the creatures gather, and between them they hold the place together in the low, warm, damp stillness of the air.
