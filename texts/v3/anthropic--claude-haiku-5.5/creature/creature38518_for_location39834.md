What lives here is the velvet. It has no single body. It is the dark, thick moss in the seams of the concrete, the black fur along every drip line, and the thin green-black skin that crusts the joints where the pipes weep. Where you can see it, it looks like a stain or a bruise. Where you can't, it is still there, running under the surface of the wet grooves in threads finer than hair.

It is slow, but it is not still. Its growth follows the water, and the water here follows the machine. When the gate opens and the surge rolls through the gutters, the velvet along the channel edges clenches. Its outer fronds lie flat and shine, as if they were trying to shed the flood, and the fine threads underneath draw in against the stone. The surge goes over it and leaves it hammered and gleaming. Then the pools drain, the gutters go quiet, and the velvet opens again. It spreads in the hour after a flood, when the stone is still sodden and the light is poor, and it pushes into the grooves the saw left behind, following the chevrons down the granite like writing it is slowly learning to read.

It has learned the pumps. Under the duet of grinding machinery there is a rhythm the velvet keeps time to, and you can see the evidence in the way it thickens in a pulse, a band of new growth across each seam every few cycles. It is not hungry for the pumps. It is simply shaped by them, the way a riverbank is shaped by a river. Each time the valve shrieks and slams, the velvet shudders in its moisture. It seems to have no fear of the noise, because nothing here has ever stopped the water, and the velvet has never known any other rhythm.

It feeds on what the place gives. The condensation is its drink. The rust that bleeds from the steel is a mineral it draws in, and where the orange tracks run through its colonies, the fronds go a darker, more purple-black, as if the iron had been folded into their tissue. It has taken the chalk tags too. Each chalked mark the crews leave washes away in the next shower, and where the chalk meets the moss, a faint pale stain remains for a day or two before the velvet absorbs it. Something of every visitor ends up in the growth, though nothing has ever been named. The dropped glove on the ledge has been partly swallowed by a mat of green-black filaments, its fingers softened and furred, and it is no longer clear which parts are glove and which are moss.

The birches in their rusted cage are its neighbours and, in some sense, its accomplices. Their roots have worked through the gaps in the wire and into the wet ground beneath the grate, and where the roots meet the velvet, the two mingle without a border. The birches creak in the wind from the drop, and the velvet hums faintly in response, though only at a frequency you feel in your teeth. The leaves are yellow and sparse, but the roots are alive and reaching, and the velvet has grown to meet them in a thin, tangled skin across the cage floor. Between them they make the only soft texture in the pocket, the only place the eye rests.

It does not bite, nest, or wander. It does not need the animals to be absent, though their absence suits it. Nothing that might eat it has ever found its way down here, and nothing that might carry it away. It exists by staying exactly where the water is, and the water here is never going away. It is the pocket's own patience, the thing that accumulates in the places where the machine's work doesn't quite reach: the lowest corner, the joint that is always wet, the gutter a spall has dammed.

If you stood here long enough, you might feel it under your boots. The concrete would be soft where the velvet was thickest, faintly spongy, giving back a warmth that the rest of the pocket doesn't have. You might see that the growth had come up over the edge of a gutter since you arrived, that the dark band along the granite had widened by a finger's breadth between one surge and the next. It would not hurry. It has never needed to.

When the crews finally come to close the cut, to set the last stones and pour the last slab, the velvet will be the first to know. It will be the last thing in the pocket to dry, and the first to return to any seam that is opened again. It will remember the shape of the wet ground, and it will wait, as it always has, for the water.
