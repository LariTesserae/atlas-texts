# What Lives in the Sunken Terraces

Nothing in Halvane moves on its own. Nothing hunts, nests, grazes, or flees. If you wait in the lowest court long enough, you will see wind, dust, falling seeds, and the slow drip of brine. What lives here is the arrangement itself: plants that pull water out of the air, walls that hold it, and a salt crust that binds the two together. Everything in the basin is a part of one patient organism, and the city's builders were among the first things it absorbed.

## The Columns

The ribbed columns are the oldest-looking inhabitants, and they behave like patient sentinels. Their trunks are stacked drums of bone-white segments, each ring pitted with a fine lattice of pores. Walk between two of them at midday and you may think they are inert, but their growth is continuous. Each segment thickens a few millimeters a month, pressing against the mortar of the nearest wall until the mortar gives way and the column settles a little closer to the building it has chosen.

Inside, they are hollow and porous, and they breathe. Air moves up through them, and the wind's passage across their open tops makes the hum that fills the basin in the afternoon. Some people would hear this as a voice. It is more like a tuning fork struck by weather. The columns do not sing to anything. The sound is a byproduct of their shape, and the shape is the result of millennia of pressure from the slope, the winds, and the old stone. They are the basin's lungs and its clock, though the clock is only a side effect of how the air moves.

## The Leaf-Sponges

If there is a heart in Halvane, it is the leaf-sponges. They are huge, overlapping sheets, each the size of a tent, crusted in salt crystals as fine as frost. Their surfaces are a mosaic of tiny cells that open and close with the humidity. In the dry heat of midday they shrink and go pale and crackly, nearly indistinguishable from the crust. As the air cools in the evening, they drink.

The drinking is slow and chemical. Salt crystals on the surface pull moisture from the dew-haze, the leaves swell, and their edges lift like the pages of a book opening in damp air. Then they begin to weep. The brine that runs from them is thick and sweet-smelling, a dense mineral liquid that slides along the grooves in the paving and collects in the troughs.

The sponges do not seem to want the water. They make it, and then they hold on to it, and then, when the salt has done its work, they give it back to the troughs, where it crusts over again by morning. The cycle has no purpose beyond itself, and that is what makes it feel alive: a rhythm that repeats because it has always repeated.

## The Pod-Vines

The pod-vines are the most restless part of the system. They spill down the walls in tangled ropes, gripping the brick with thin, wiry tendrils that feel for cracks and seams. Their pods are papery and dry, packed with seeds that rattle like dice. Through the morning the pods swell in the warming air, and by midday they split, one after another, sending cracks across the terraces that sound like distant gunfire. Seeds rain down onto the stone, bounce off the plaster, and skitter into the crevices between flagstones.

The seeds that land in the troughs sprout within a few days, and the salt pulls them up into the leaf-sponge cycle. The seeds that land on bare salt wait. Some wait for years. The vines are how the basin reaches into its own gaps, how it spreads the mechanism of its moisture into every corner that has not yet been claimed.

## The Walls

The walls are the most peculiar part of the living system, because they were never meant to be alive. The builders cut them carefully from the salt slope and faced them with fired brick and gypsum plaster, and their drains and niches and stairs were designed for water that never came. Now the walls channel the brine, shelter the sponges from the wind, and hold the columns in place. The plants have grown into the architecture until it has become their skeleton.

You can see the process in the mortar. Roots push through the joints, and the joints widen. The plaster flakes, the bricks shift, the stairs slump into ramps of rubble and salt. Every few seasons a wall leans a fraction further toward the slope. The city is not decaying in any ordinary sense. It is being reshaped into a better machine for collecting water from the air, one repair at a time, by something that has no concept of repair.

## The Salt

Beneath everything is the crust, the hexagonal plates that cover the basin floor and climb the lower terraces. The salt is the foundation and the medium. It holds the moisture, it reflects the copper light so that no shadows fall, and it cracks and reforms with the temperature, slowly breathing as the cold and heat pass through it. The salt is the basin's body. The plants are its hands, and the walls are its bones.

## What the Whole Thing Is

If you ask what lives in Halvane, the answer is a closed loop, a single organism distributed across salt, stone, plant, and air. It has no center, no mind, no animal to speak for it. It knows only the cycle of dew and dawn, heat and splitting, wind and humming, and the long slow drip of brine back into the troughs. The builders are gone, but the loop they built around is still breathing, and it has taken them in as part of its structure.

Standing in the lowest court at dusk, with the brine dripping at your feet and the columns humming in the last gusts, you are not alone with the place, exactly. You are inside it. The walls around you are doing what walls do, the sponges are doing what sponges do, and the whole basin is doing what it has always done: gathering the thin moisture of the air, one terrace at a time, and holding it until morning.
