What lives here is less a list of creatures than a set of clocks that keep each other running. The vents exhale, the fog rises, the water moves, and every living thing is fitted into that cycle at its own pace.

**The Siphon-backs**

The largest animals are slow, armored grazers that move along the valley floors in grooved, deliberate paths. Their shells are domed and segmented, plated in overlapping scales, and each has a row of tubes running down its back like the pipes of an organ. When one feeds, the tubes lift water up out of the peat in a steady draw, and the whine they make is the faint, high note under everything else in the valley.

They are never alone for long. Moss takes root in the grooves of their shells, lichen spreads across their plates, and small fungi push up from the cracks. By the time one has walked a long circuit, it carries a garden on its back, a little damp ecosystem that drips as it moves. Their dung feeds the peat, and their tubes keep the water table where it needs to be, lifting moisture toward the high ridges, where it becomes the cloud that feeds the rain that feeds the vents. They look like machines because they behave like parts of one.

When a Siphon-back stops, it lowers its head to the stalked grass and grazes in long, unhurried sweeps. Then it lifts its tubes and exhales a plume of fog from a vent in its flank, and the plume hangs in the air and slowly disperses. It does not look up. It does not need to.

**The Lines**

Along the mossy walls and the seams of the basalt move the crawlers: small, many-legged, pale-shelled things that travel in coordinated files. Each one is simple. Alone, a crawler is nothing much, a bead of motion that stops and turns and follows the scent trail of the one ahead. Together they form something with the precision of a sorting floor. Lines carry spore-clusters from the fungi to the tarns and seed the still water. Others haul fragments of shed lichen and berry skins into the peat, where they are broken down. Still others move the shriveled red berries from the shrubs to the sunny edges of the cloud line, where the fog thins enough for them to finish ripening.

No crawler leads a line. The pattern is held by touch and by chemistry, by the thousand small signals passed along the files. If you disturb one line, the others reroute within minutes, as if the whole hillside had been thinking the entire time.

**The Cloud-Riders**

The high, thin calls come from above the deck of cloud. The Cloud-Riders are long, low, membranous creatures with wide translucent wings that catch the rising columns of heated fog from the vents. They spiral up on the thermals and glide through the cloud, their coloring the same pale slate as the sky, so that they vanish when they turn. They hunt along the valley edges, dropping out of the mist onto the Siphon-backs' calves, the slow Lines, and occasionally a careless straggler from the grazing herds. Their calls carry for kilometers. Then they fall silent for hours, and the only sign of them is a wing shadow passing over a tarn.

They are the most solitary of the inhabitants, but they depend on the vents as much as anything else does. Without the steam columns, they would fall.

**The Rooted**

The mosses are the oldest cushions on the slopes, some the size of sheep, rust-brown and slow. They hold water as though it were a memory, and they release it when pressed, a cold stream that feeds the rivulets and keeps the ground soft. They change very little over a human lifetime and a great deal over a geological one.

The stunted trees grip the basalt plates with roots like hands, and their hanging mosses trap the fog and drip it steadily into the soil below. Their roots are the reason the scree fans have settled, the reason the plates have not slid. They are the quiet stabilizers of the whole slope, and they are patient in a way that makes the vents look hurried.

The stalked grasses grow in dense stands, each blade sheathed in condensation. When wind moves through them, the whole stand shimmers and hisses, a sound like breath through a sieve. They are the most common plant and the most easily grazed, and they regrow from their bases within days. Their sound is the sound of the valley breathing.

**The Glowing Ones**

The fungi are the strangest of the inhabitants because they seem to be many things at once. Their shelflike brackets clasp the trunks and mosses. Their tall, pale stalks rise in clusters from the peat, and their translucent caps open like membranes stretched over the sky. Their threads run beneath the ground, into the roots of the trees and the bodies of the crawlers and the soft flesh of the moss, and they seem to carry signals through the whole valley. At dusk, they release their spores in faint plumes, and the spores drift through the fog and settle in the tarns, where they are gathered by the Lines. In the deepest hollows, the fungi and the phosphorescent moss are the only light. Their glow pulses slowly, dims at dawn, and brightens again as the cloud thickens. They may be the valley's nervous system, or simply its lamps.

**The whole**

No one here is in charge, and no one is watching. The vents thump on their own rhythm. The Siphon-backs follow their grooves. The Lines move without a leader. The Cloud-Riders ride the columns down. The trees hold the slopes, the fungi light the dark, the grasses breathe, and the berries ripen in slow waves across the ridges. Each of these is a clock, and the valley is the sound they make together, a low, damp, overlapping hum.

Between the presences there are long stretches of empty land, and it is easy to think of those stretches as empty. But they are only the quiet parts of a machine that runs without needing anyone to tend it, and whatever lives here lives inside that machine, doing what it has always done, as if the mist and the cold and the dark were exactly the conditions it was made for.
