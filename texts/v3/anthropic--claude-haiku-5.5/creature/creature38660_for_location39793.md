**The People of Ossel-Marr**

The people here are thin in the way that wind-shaped trees are thin, long in the limb and deep in the chest, with lungs that have grown to pull a living out of air that is always a little too high. Their skin has a faint, grayish-green cast, the color of old lichen on stone, and a fine pale down covers their forearms and the backs of their necks. In the early morning, when fog beads on it, they glitter faintly, and the children run through the terraces with their arms outstretched, letting the mist gather on them like a second skin.

Their eyes are wide-pupiled and dim-loving. They squint at direct light and rarely climb to the rim, where the sky is bright and hard. Most of their lives pass in the green gloom, and they speak of the open sky the way a person might speak of a cold, unpleasant relative. They have no word for the sun that is not also a word for glare.

They move through the city with a particular gait, knees soft, weight low, one hand almost always touching something: a rope, a wall, a trunk. The terraces slope and sag, and their bodies have learned to lean with them. A stranger walks upright and stiff, and the people notice this and smile, not unkindly, the way one would smile at a child who has not yet learned to swim.

**Households and Trunks**

A household does not really own a house. It keeps a trunk. Each family chooses or inherits a tree, and the house is built around it, through it, sometimes up beside it so that the trunk becomes a kind of central spine. The trunk is the family's ledger. Notches climb its bark in rows, one for each season, deeper for the seasons of hardship. Old families have trunks so notched they seem to have been carved by something patient and obsessive, and children learn to read the rhythm of their lineage by touching the wood with their fingertips.

When someone dies, they are not buried. The people wrap the body in fiber cloth and set it into a hollow in the trunk, high enough that the fog reaches it and the roots can, in their own slow way, take it in. The fungi that grow in these hollows glow blue-white, and no one in Ossel-Marr thinks this is strange. The shimmer in the lower terraces is, in part, the city's dead, and the living walk beneath them every day, reaching up to touch the glow as they pass, the way one might touch a doorframe for luck.

**The Menders**

The most respected work is repair. A group of people, the Menders, spend their lives walking the terraces with mallets and clay, watching for the places where a wall has begun to bow, where a root has pushed too hard, where a seam has opened enough to let the moss in. They do not try to restore the terraces to what they were. This is the first thing a child learns from a Mender: the stone is always going somewhere, and the work is to choose where. A terrace is reset a finger's width lower or higher than before, and the Menders argue passionately about these choices in the evenings, over bowls of bitter, mint-steeped water, their voices carrying up through the tiers.

The roots are part of this argument. People do not cut them, or not fully. A root that threatens a wall is pruned back, bound with cord, and sometimes coaxed into a new path, guided along a groove in the stone with wet clay and patience. A root that holds a terrace together is left alone and thanked, sometimes with a smear of salt on the bark. The people speak of the roots as neighbors who are not always friendly, and they have arrived, over many generations, at a kind of treaty with them. The treaty is not written. It is kept in the habits of hands.

**Salt and the Pools**

The salt-channels and bone-rimmed pools are harvested carefully. The crust is scraped in thin plates, dried on flat stones in the gloom, and stored in woven baskets that smell of mint and brine. Salt is the city's only real trade good, carried up the terraces in exchange for bone, hide, and the fiber cord that holds everything together. The pools themselves are not drunk from directly. Their water is drawn off, poured over hot stones in shallow dishes, and allowed to thicken and crack into the crust that the children are taught to recognize as food. It takes skill, and the people who do it have quiet, careful hands and rarely speak while they work.

**The Offerings**

The wild animals are neither hunted nor left entirely alone. Before a grazer is taken from the sponge-mats, a small offering is left: a sliver of bone, a pinch of salt, a bundle of fresh fronds tied with cord. The predators that slide through the moss are given their own portions, set at the edges of the lower paths on nights when the fog is thickest. Nobody remembers who began these exchanges or why. The people do them the way they comb their hair in the morning, without argument and without question, and when a grazer walks calmly among the people's houses, or a predator stops at the edge of a path and watches a child pass without moving, the people say the offering is working and go on about their day.

**The Carvings**

The oldest carvings are not read. The people call them the Blurred, and they speak of the shapes beneath them with a reverence that stops short of curiosity. A scholar, if the city had such a thing, might spend a life tracing one curve and come away with nothing but a sense of how many hands had passed over the same stone. The people carve over old marks rarely, and only when a wall must be reset and the old face is lost anyway. Their own carvings are sharp and recent, and they show the living world: lines of figures climbing terraces, fog drawn as cross-hatched sheets, roots as thick, looping arcs that curl around the figures and sometimes seem to hold them. These are not records so much as reminders, a way of saying to the next generation: this is who we were in the fog, and this is what held us.

**Sound and Time**

The people speak in the rhythms of the place, and their songs are slow, with long held notes that let the insects fall silent for a breath before resuming. They have no word for hurry. When they are excited, they speak faster, but the speed passes quickly and they return to the old tempo, the way water settles back after a stone is dropped in it.

They measure time by what changes slowly: the hardening of the sponge-mats, the creeping of the roots, the whitening of the salt-channels after a dry year. A child is said to be "one mat old" or "a finger of root," and the elders, who have lived through many seasons of sag and repair, are said to be "as deep as the terraces." This is not sentimental. It is simply the way they understand the world, a world where change is real but arrives so gradually that to hurry it would be to misunderstand it.

**The Drum**

When the fog thickens and the bowl seems to hold its breath, the drums begin. They are not signals, exactly. They are a way of saying that the city is still here, still listening, still breathing in the same rhythm as the wet air and the patient stone. A drum starts on one terrace and is taken up by the next, and the next, until the whole bowl is pulsing, slow and deliberate, beneath the insect hum and the drip of a hundred thousand tiny rhythms.

Somewhere below, a drum begins now. Its sound climbs the tiers and moves through the notched trunks and across the sagging stone, and in the hollows of the dead, the blue-white light flickers, very faintly, in time with it.
