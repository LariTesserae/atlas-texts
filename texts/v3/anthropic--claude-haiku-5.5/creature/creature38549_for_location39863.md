# The Keeper of the Intake

Nothing in Lowmere is alone for long, but the thing that lives most deeply in it is the one the pike avoid. The fish call it nothing. The gulls don't see it. Divers who came down the old service ladders before the water rose, and whose notes survive in the rusted lockers on the north gantry, wrote only that the grate "breathes."

It lives in the intake.

**Where it is**

Its body fills the throat of the north intake, where the grate meets the first riveted pipe. The main mass is roughly the volume of a small car, but it is not contained in any one place. Fine strands run through the broken joints of the pipework, through the gaps behind the grate's bars, and into the spaces between the pump housing's footings, so that it reaches everywhere the water goes first. It has been there so long that the iron has grown around it. Where its tissue touches the metal, the metal has bloomed into soft, lacy rust, and the bloom has hardened into a skeleton of plates and ridges that is part animal, part fitting.

**What it is made of**

Most of its body is a pale, mineral-tinged matter, almost the color of the concrete, that it has built from what the current brings. Silt, flakes of iron, calcium scraped from the crustacean colonies, and bits of tile and grit are all taken into it and laid down in layers. Its surface is smooth and faintly ribbed, like the inside of a shell. Its interior is lighter, webbed with channels, and the channels carry a slow, thick fluid that glows very faintly in the dark when the pumps surge.

It has no clear head. Its mouth, if you can call it that, is a wide, slack opening where the grate bars meet the throat of the pipe, and it can close that opening almost completely. When the grate is clear, the opening is wide and the current flows through it in long smooth tongues. When debris piles up, the opening tightens, and the creature works at the blockage with a set of slow, rolling contractions that push the silt and weed back out through the spillway. The fish learn to stay out of its path during these contractions. The crustaceans cling harder.

**How it moves and feeds**

It moves very little, and when it does, the movement is measured in days. Over the years it has pushed out long, thin tendrils through the pipe joints. These probe the lattice like roots, and where they find a seam that is weakening, they lay down a thin plating of its own tissue to seal it. This is slow repair, and it has kept several gantry crossings from failing. Some of the sunken catwalks now run through its tissue, and the walkways have sagged into it until the creature and the iron are hard to tell apart.

It feeds on what the current brings, but it also feeds on the pike. Pike that wait too long in the lee of the grate sometimes never leave, and their skeletons appear later as pale ribs in the outer lattice. The creature does not seem to hunt them so much as absorb what the grate has already caught. The result is a balance that suits the pike, which keep the smaller fish moving and the crustacean colonies thinned. The creature takes its share and leaves enough to keep the cycle going.

**What it does for the place**

Here the picture gets uncertain, which is appropriate for a sump. The creature keeps the intake clear, and a clear intake keeps the pumps drawing evenly, and even pumping keeps the basin level from rising toward the towers. The pumps, in turn, keep the current moving, and the current feeds the animals and carries away the silt that would otherwise smother the lattice. The loops close on the creature, and it is always somewhere near the middle of them.

The divers noticed one more thing. In the years just before the last crew left, the pumps stuttered whenever the grate was heavily blocked. When the creature was cleaned out by the surge of a flood, the pumps ran hard and then settled. Nobody could say whether the creature turns the pumps, or whether the pumps shape the creature, or whether both are only parts of something older that nobody built on purpose. The thrum of the pumps and the slow breathing of the grate now seem to beat at the same rate. Whether that is cause or coincidence is a question the basin has never answered, and it does not seem to need to.

**When it is quiet**

On still days, when the pumps ease for a few minutes and the current drops to a murmur, you can hear the creature from the surface. It makes a long, low, wet sound, like water drawn slowly through a narrow throat, followed by a dull click as the grate settles back into place. The gulls on the north housing go quiet during these moments, and the crustaceans on the wall stop sweeping. Then the pumps come back up to speed, the current picks up, and the basin goes on, turning its loops, keeping its level, and holding its breath at the grate.
