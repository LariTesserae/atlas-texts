**Who Lies in the Channels**

They look like logs. That is the first thing and mostly the last thing anyone notices about them. A long ridged back breaks the surface of a slow channel, furred with moss and pale lichen, crusted with the same rust-red silt that stains the roots. Epiphytes have rooted in the grooves along its spine. Small beetles walk its flanks. A heron may stand on it for an hour before the heron notices that the log has, very slightly, risen and fallen.

Each one is a single long body, thick as a fallen trunk and many times longer, though no one has ever seen where one ends. The skin is not scale and not bark but a layered armour, plate over plate, each plate banded in fine seams. Most are the red of the sediment. Some carry grey oily bands, dull and faintly iridescent, the same seams that run through the banks where the old industry leached into the peat. When the light comes at a low angle through the canopy, the banding catches and the back of the creature shows a sheen like the film on the channel water. It is not camouflage, though it works as camouflage. It is closer to a record. The armour has taken in what the ground carried, layer by layer, and laid it down.

**Breath and water**

They breathe slowly, perhaps once in several minutes. The surface of the water above them lifts a little with each breath and settles, and the rhythm is so long that the insects on the water have learned to ignore it. Their lungs are not in the chest but run along the length of the body, and they draw air through openings in the ridges, so that a whole animal can be seen to exhale in a rolling wave from head to tail, like a current passing under a skin.

They are heavy, and they do not hurry. Their bodies are built to lie in the water with the head low, and the head, when it is visible at all, is a blunt mossed shelf with two pale eyes set near the top, the lids half closed, always half closed. They see little of the dim green world. What they feel is the water. Every movement in the channel reaches them as a pressure, a tilt, a slight change in the way the silt settles. The drip of condensation, the slap of a frog's leap, the sway of a root: all of these are read through the body and taken in. Their hearing is almost entirely through the water, and so they are attuned most of all to the thrum from the far haze. It arrives through the channels as a long, low pulse, and they have learned to match it. Over generations this has become a kind of slow rhythm in their bodies. Their breathing, when the thrum is strong, lengthens to meet it.

They do not go toward it. Nothing here goes toward the haze. They lie where the water is good and wait for it to come to them.

**Feeding**

They eat what the channels carry. The iron-rich silt is not poison to them. Their armour draws it in and uses it, and the grey seams in their plates are where the old salts have been bound and stored. The sweet-chemical channels are the ones they prefer. They move slowly into the sheen-thick water, lower the head, and simply open the mouth and let the current pass through, filtering the fine suspended matter, the drowned insects, the sweetened sediment, and the tiny bodies of the amphibians that gather in the shallows. Sometimes a larger thing comes too: a snake, a sleeping lizard, once in a long while a bird. They take these slowly, in one long unhurried motion, and then lie still for days while the meal is processed in the heavy warm body.

Feeding is not a contest. The basin is full of food, and the creatures that eat it are full of the basin. The amphibians multiply in the shallows and sing with their throats, the insects rise in clouds, and the logbacks lie in the channels taking what comes past. Nothing needs to be hunted in a hurry. The whole system is a long circulation, and they are one of the slow valves in it.

**Time**

A logback can live for longer than the water takes to rise and fall through several full seasons. It does not count time by days. It counts by the height of the water on its back, the line where the silt is laid down, the bands of grey and red that build on its plates like the banding of the bank itself. Each year's water leaves a new seam in the armour. An old one has many. The oldest are so banded that the grey dominates, and the sheen across the back is thick and slow-moving, like oil on a pond.

They mate once a generation, or perhaps less often, in a slow tangle that lasts through a whole night of rain. The young hatch from eggs laid in the mud at the channel edge, and they grow in the shallows for years, lying low under roots, feeding on the smallest particles until their plates thicken enough to take the first grey band. When they are large enough to be mistaken for a fallen trunk, they have become part of the landscape.

When one dies, it does not rot quickly. The armour holds. The body sinks into the silt over decades and is worked by the same slow chemistry that worked the peat and the old industry. Its banding is laid down in the sediment at last, a seam among seams, and new roots grow into the place it lay.

**Among the packed life**

They are not alone in the channels, but they are not crowded either. Between them is a space of about the width of a body, a space that everything else in the basin has learned to respect. Frogs sit on their backs. Insects walk their flanks. Fish gather in the eddies off their heads. Nothing is afraid of them, and they are not interested in anything except the water.

In the long dark green of the lowland, where every cubic metre is taken and nothing is bare, the logbacks are the slowest things that move. They carry the old chemistry on their backs and breathe it out again as sheen. They take in the sweetness and bind the poisons into their plates, and when the water rises they lie low and let it pass over, and when it falls they lift their heads an inch above the silt and go on breathing, in time with a thrum that comes from very far away and that they have never once gone to see.
