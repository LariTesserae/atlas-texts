**Those Who Keep the Water**

The first thing that lives here is the fog. It is not weather passing through. It arrives before dawn, pours over the stubs of the peaks like milk into a bowl, and settles in the hollow until the basin is a single grey room with no walls. Anything that breathes in it breathes water. The fog is patient and enormous, and it takes its time leaving.

Inside it, the lattice-tenders are already working.

You hear them before you see them: a soft, rhythmic brushing, like a broom on wet slate, then a small wet click where teeth close on a bead of water. Then a long pause, and the brushing starts again further along the line. The sound moves from post to post across the basin, a slow tide working its way uphill.

When one finally comes into view, it is longer than you expected. The body is a chain of segments, each one the width of a man's forearm, joined by membranes that flex as the creature walks. It moves along a thread with four pairs of limbs, the first pair braced near the front, the rest spread wide to balance on the filament. The threads do not sag under its weight. They tighten, a fine shiver running out ahead of it and returning, so that the whole strand seems to lift to meet each footfall.

Its fur is not fur. It is closer to a fibrous moss, pale and absorbent, combed into long strands that catch the fog and carry it to the body's surface. Where the fur is wet, it shines. Where it has dried, it has a dusty bloom. Along the flanks run small pits, rows of them, each lined with a fine membrane that trembles when the threads hum. The creature's head is almost lost in the fur, but there are two dark, lidless eyes set low, and a mouth like a seam, which opens only to take a bead of water from the crosspoint and close again.

It does not look at you. It looks at the thread.

Watching them for a while, you begin to suspect they are not simply tending the lattice. They are reading it. When a strand grows slack, the tender stops, lowers its head to the filament, and holds still. The hum changes pitch. Somewhere up the line another tender answers with a click, and then a second, and the sag is taken up by a rhythm of pulls and releases that runs from post to post until the thread is tight again. There is no conversation you can parse. But there is an agreement, older than any single body, about what the water should do.

Near the posts, where the hum is strongest, the tenders are fewer and slower. Some sleep wrapped in tree-fern fronds, folded like spent umbrellas, their flanks rising and falling in time with the threads. Their pits glow faintly in the dark, a dim blue. When one wakes, it rolls its whole length along the strand before settling back to work, as if stretching a body that has been holding a long shape.

---

Under the lattice, the frogs are the basin's voice.

There are so many of them that the sound stops being a sound and becomes a condition. Every cistern holds a choir. Every pool has a chorus whose calls overlap and cross, clicks layered on trills layered on the wet, knocking bass of the larger throats. At dusk the chorus rises like a tide, and at some point you realise it has a structure: call and response, a long syncopated count passed from pool to pool, each group waiting exactly long enough for the last to finish. It is counting something. You can't tell what.

The frogs are small and mottled, with pale throats that swell like bubbles of glass. Their skin is slick with the same moss-bloom as the tenders, and they sit on the stone rims of the cisterns in such density that the rock is hidden beneath them. When you lift a hand near the water, the whole chorus stops at once, a sudden silence like a door shutting. Then, one by one, the calls return, starting with the nearest pool and spreading outward.

The silence is the warning. You learn to watch for it.

---

The insects are a second weather, a cloud of iridescent bodies above the fern canopy, their translucent wings tilting and flashing as they turn. They come down to the flower-bells at night and rise again before dawn. Their hum sits high and bright against the lower, wetter roar of the frogs, like a second tone in a chord.

The flowers themselves open only in the fog. They are pale and bell-shaped, hanging from thin stems among the fern crowns, and their interiors are lined with a soft, ridged tissue that seems to be listening. When the hum of the threads swells, the bells tilt toward the nearest post. When the fog lifts, they close with a faint sigh you can feel more than hear, and they release a sweet resin that sticks to your fingers and smells like rain on warm stone.

---

The predators you rarely see. What you see is their absence.

A hush starts at one edge of the chorus and travels outward through the pools, a ripple of silence moving faster than any animal could run. In the gap it leaves, the insects go still and the threads hum louder, as though the basin is holding its breath. Then, slowly, the sound comes back.

Once, in the grey light before the fog lifted, you glimpsed one. It was standing on a post, long limbs folded beneath it, perfectly still, its body the same colour as the stone. Its head turned, too slowly, and you felt the attention pass across you the way the threads feel a bead of water: weighed, and not yet claimed. Then the fog moved, and it was gone.

---

But the thing that lives here most fully is none of these.

It is the hum.

It sits in the threads like a held note. It is strongest at dawn, when the beads begin their slow crawl up the strands toward the crosspoints, against gravity, gathering and climbing as if something at the top of the lattice were calling them. The tenders answer it. The frogs go quiet for it. The flowers turn toward it. Even the mosses on the stone seem to lean.

It is not a voice. But it has a direction, and a purpose, and a kind of patience that makes the stone seem hasty. Over decades it has drawn the basin's water upward and then down, into the cisterns, into the roots, into the bodies of the things that drink. It has taught the tenders to mend what breaks. It has grown the fern trunks and bent the posts and cut the grooves in the rock by working, very slowly, toward some end that the basin has never finished describing.

When the fog is thickest, you can feel it most clearly: a faint pressure at the back of your attention, like someone standing just outside a door, waiting for you to notice them. The threads are humming. The beads are climbing. The frogs have gone silent, all of them at once, and in the hush that follows, the lattice-tenders stop their brushing and lift their heads, every one of them turning toward the same point in the grey.

Then, far above the fern crowns, where the fog is thinning and the light has begun to show through as a pale gold smear, something is climbing the lattice toward the sun.
