**The Standing Ones**

The tall stalks are the oldest residents, and they are not quite like anything else here. Each one is a single organism, and each one knows where the others stand. They grow so far apart that a traveler may walk a kilometer between them and feel alone, but the distance is deliberate. Their roots run out through the skin of the ground and meet the roots of their neighbors far below the gravel, and the spacing keeps any one stalk from shading or starving another. A stalk that is struck or dies leaves a wide scar in the moss, and in a season the scar fills with new growth from the edges, as if the nearest stalks had leaned in to close the gap.

They think slowly, if they think at all, and they speak through sound and water. Wind across the hollow stalks sets up the constant low chord, and each stalk has its own pitch, shaped by its height, its porosity, and the number of breathing holes along its ringed joints. When the wind shifts, the chord shifts, and the stalks answer one another across the kilometers in long swells of harmony and dissonance. The fronds drip in rhythms that follow the wind's pattern rather than gravity's, which means the drip is a second voice, slower and wetter, carried downhill through the moss and into the streams. Where a drop hangs in the air before falling, the stalk seems to be holding a thought, or perhaps only the shape of one.

**The Skin**

Beneath the standing ones, the ground is one continuous body. The lichen rosettes, the velvet moss, and the pale fungal mat that threads through the gravel are not separate colonies but a single living web that stretches across the whole highland. Where the mat is thick, the scree holds. Where it thins, the slopes slide. The warmth you feel under thin lichen is the web's metabolism, a low burn that keeps the soil from freezing solid and carries nutrients from the stalks' roots to the fissures where new growth is needed.

The spores are its breath. When the web exhales, the spores rise into the damp air and drift on the storm fronts, and some of them land in fresh cracks and sudden scree. A spore cloud that holds a spiral shape longer than it should is not defying physics so much as riding a current the web has learned to create, a kind of slow convection the mat draws out of the ground's warmth. The web is patient. It can wait a generation for a crack to open and then cover it in a single season.

**The Voices in the Air**

The winged flocks are the highland's messengers. Their membranous wings catch the mist-thick air and carry them low over the moss in tight, shifting formations, and their sharp overlapping calls bounce off the basalt walls until the calls return doubled and altered. This is how they map the land: they know where the echoes are sharp, where the ridges throw sound back cleanly, where the stalk hums are loud enough to mask a predator. They follow the weather along the ridgelines, and where they settle to feed, they leave behind seeds and spore-laden droppings that the web takes in and turns into new growth.

Their calls are not only navigation. They answer one another in patterns that repeat across the valleys, and the patterns travel. A flock calling in the east will set off a chain of replies that reaches the western slopes within the hour. When a storm comes over the ridge, the flocks go quiet and drop low, and the chain of silence moves ahead of the wind like a warning.

**The Burrowers and the Hunt**

The thumping under the ground comes from the burrowers, heavy, blunt-bodied creatures with forelimbs like shovels and sensory pits along their flanks that read vibration through the gravel. They are the turners of the highland. Their tunnels aerate the scree and bring the lower fungal threads up toward the surface, and their bodies, when they die, sink into the soil and feed the web. They rarely come out in daylight, which here means nothing more than the hours when the cloud is thinnest. At night they move in short, sudden bursts, and the whole surface shudders.

Between the stalks, in the dark, the hunters move. They track by sound, and their bodies reflect it: flat, broad heads for catching echoes, long jaws that open silently, legs that spread wide to keep them balanced on the shifting gravel. The wet cough and the long descending whistle are their calls, used to locate prey and to warn rivals. The prey answer. A burrower thumping in its tunnel will answer a hunter's whistle with a thump of its own, and the hunter will follow the sound toward the pit. Clicking, rustling, the faint ticking of stones: the whole landscape is a conversation in which every participant is also a target. Nothing here is quiet for long, but nothing crowds, so the hunt is spread across the highland like the drip from the fronds, each strike a single drop in a vast and steady rain.

**The People Who Came Quietly**

They were never many, and they did not stay. The highland has a way of thinning out the things that try to take too much from it, and the people seem to have understood this. Their marks are small and scattered across the ridges and in the lee of the stalks, and they show a patience that matches the land's own pace.

They moved in small bands, following the migrations of the flocks and the timing of the storms. The cairns on the ridges, with their weathered spirals, were probably waypoints, or markers of where the weather turned, or places where a band agreed to meet again. The shelters faced downhill, into a wind they expected from above, which means they knew the squalls came over the ridges first and rolled down into the valleys. They had learned to read the sky the way sailors read water, and they built accordingly.

The bone hooks, each carved to a different angle, tell the most about how they lived. Hooks at those angles could catch and hold the falling drops from the fronds, gathering water in fiber cord hung in the wind. They could snag the spore threads that drifted on the storm fronts and bind them into cloth or cord. They could fish the streams that ran dark with peat and pale with stone. Each angle had a use, and the makers knew all of them.

The hollow stalk segments set upright in the moss are the most intriguing of their remains. They were cut from the standing ones, perhaps from fallen crowns, and set in rows that catch the wind at different angles. They hum faintly, and they hum in a key that is close to the chord of the stalks but not quite the same. Whoever tuned them was listening carefully, and may have been trying to join the conversation rather than merely hear it. Whether they succeeded, or whether the stalks heard them at all, is a question the highland has not answered.

They left nothing in a hurry. Their tools were stone, bone, sinew, and hardened wood, and they never brought metal here. Their work was patient, their shelters were modest, and when they went, they left their things to wait. The highland has kept them, the way it keeps everything, in the slow soil of the web and the hum of the stalks.

**What Holds the Silence Together**

The highland is not a collection of things that happen to share a place. It is a single body held together by sound, water, and spore, with the stalks as its deepest voices, the web as its skin, the flocks and burrowers and hunters as its nerves and muscles, and the people as the one part that tried to listen from outside. The spacing of the stalks, the distance between the hunters, the flocks' chain of silence: all of it serves the same end, which is to keep the body from crowding itself into exhaustion. The emptiness is part of the system, a cold, wide breathing room where weather can build, cross, and pass without tearing anything apart.

And on the rare nights when the cloud breaks, the green light unfolds over the ridges and the whole body goes still. The flocks stop calling. The hunters freeze in place. The stalks cease their chord, and the drip from the fronds hangs in the air, and the spores stop their slow spiral, and for an hour the highland seems to listen to itself. Whatever it is doing in that hour, it is not hunting or feeding or singing. It is simply attending, the way the people's hollow pipes attended, though far more completely. Then the cloud closes, the chord resumes, and the squall begins to build again over the eastern ridge, rolling toward the valleys where everything is waiting, patient and cold and humming, for the next wind to come down from above.
