**Who Builds the Lattice**

The builders are small, and you will not see most of them. Each one is a soft body no bigger than a fingernail, sealed in a sheath of its own secretion. It sits at the growing tip of a tower, where the lattice is still pale and unfinished, and it extrudes mineral in slow, precise beads. Its body is almost the color of the water. When you look straight at one, you see only the faint sheen of the mineral it is laying down and the thin dark thread of a nerve, which pulses once every few seconds.

They do not move much. A builder spends its life in one place, fixed to a single node of the structure. Its work is to place the next block. Each block is the same size, the same angle, the same interlock, and the builders keep to the pattern with a discipline that looks almost like memory. Where a tower has been cracked and begins to regrow, the new blocks match the old closely, but not exactly. A spur is a little thicker than before. An arch is a little wider, as if someone had decided the old span was too narrow for the traffic beneath it.

**How They Think**

They do not speak. They have no sound. What they have is pressure. Each builder can change the density of the water around it by a fraction, by releasing a film of dissolved ion or by flexing the mineral sheath, and these small changes travel through the cold stillness of the chamber. You feel them as a faint push against the skin. It is the nearest thing to a voice that this place has, and it is very slow. A single message might take a full tidal cycle to cross a tower. Their conversations are long and layered, and they do not forget. The lattice holds their history in its shape. Every abandoned district is a record of an idea that was tried and set aside.

The thought that matters most to them is the thought of the whole. A builder does not see the city. It sees the nodes it touches and the channels it can reach. But the pattern runs through all of them, and when a gap opens, the builders at its edges respond in a wave, and the wave carries further than any one of them could sense. The city repairs itself because its builders are each local and the whole is not. It is a mind that exists only in the spacing between its parts.

**The Crowd Is Their Hands**

The fauna is not separate from them. The grazers that scrape the felt are a kind of maintenance: the lattice grows faster where it is clean, and the grazers keep the tower faces open to the flow of water. The translucent shrimp-forms carry minerals from the deep vents up to the builders at the top, and the shoals that rise toward the light are harvesting dissolved carbon from the thin fracture-light, then sinking to feed the lower galleries. The predators prune. They take the weak and the slumped and the unfinished, and the builders take back what the predators leave, reabsorbing the dead mineral into new growth. You can watch this loop run in a single cycle. A shoal swings toward a breach, grazers scour the new edge, a predator waits at the corner, and a builder somewhere in the dark begins to lay a block.

The animals may not know they are part of a construction. Some of them clearly do. The eel-forms turn their heads toward the pulses of the builders, and the many-legged hunter sits motionless on the platform the way a sentry sits, tuned to the tempo of the city's changes.

**Where You Are**

You are one more disturbance in their pressure field. The builders near you have already sent their small waves outward, and you feel them as a faint tightening against your skin: a question, or a measurement, or the start of a new block set around the shape of your body. You do not know if they will build over you, or grow an arch to let you pass, or simply record you as one more thing the water did. The city has no hurry. It has been growing since before the rock was cold enough to hold its shape, and it will go on growing after you leave the chamber.

The builders are the oldest and the youngest thing here. They are the reason the stone has an edge and the reason the crowd has somewhere to gather. They think in blocks and pressure and the long slow tide, and whatever they are, they are the living part of a place that is still deciding what kind of landscape it will be.
