The first thing that lives here is the fog. It comes up from the cold sea each morning like a slow tide, and it does not so much pass through the plateau as get kept by it. Each droplet that the silver hairs comb from the air is held for a while in a leaf, a root basin, a bed of moss, and then released. The fog is a traveler that the land persuades to stay a little longer than it meant to.

Beneath it, the grazers move across the terraces at their own pace. They are heavy, armored, and patient, with long necks that curl down to the lichen rings and rise again with the careful motion of something that has never needed to hurry. They have lived so long on this ground that their trails have become part of its topography, shallow grooves across the springy moss that the rain fills at dawn and the sun dries by noon. When one lifts its head, the lichen on its shell shows the same orange, grey, and turquoise rings as the boulders, as if it has been slowly taking on the pattern of the rock it grazes. Perhaps it has.

The burrowers live under the moss, in the dark, damp layer where water is held for weeks. They are rarely seen. What they do is loosen the ground and carry small amounts of soil down into the crevices where the moss can root, and so they are part of how the terraces grow. A new step in the land begins as one of their tunnels, then a patch of moss, then a lichen crust, then a low shelf that holds its own water. They are slow architects of the plateau's future.

The seed-carriers drift through the mist like loose handfuls of pale thread. Their parachute wings catch the thermals off the ridges and carry them in long, wandering arcs, and where they touch down they leave seeds that will become stilt-trees in a century or two. They never seem to land in one place for long. Watching a drift cross a gorge at dawn, you might think it is only weather, until one of them settles on a silver leaf and folds its wings.

The pitcher-pods cling to the gorge walls in clusters, and they are the closest thing to hunger in the land. At dawn they open their waxy sacs, and the insects carried in the fog fall into the fluid and are slowly unmade. The pods hang there, translucent and lantern-pale, digesting for days. They do not move, and yet they feed on the fog's passage as surely as the stilt-trees do, only with a different mouth.

But the most attentive presence here is harder to name. Everything in this country moves in response to the same rhythms: fog rising, pooling, and receding; the stilt-trees turning toward the sun; the silica films expanding and contracting in the hollows; the moss gathering in rings where old fog-banks once paused. These are not separate processes that happen to coincide. They are one conversation, carried on across the whole plateau, about how much water there is and how much of it can be spent. The grazers eat what the moss holds. The moss holds what the trees catch. The trees catch what the fog brings. The fog brings what the cold sea gives up. And when the balance tilts, when a gorge deepens or a pillar falls, the land answers by growing a new terrace somewhere else, quietly, where the soil has gathered enough to hold itself.

If there is a mind in this place, it is the plateau itself, the way it keeps its accounts. It has no face and no voice, and it does not need either. It has learned over immense time exactly how much wetness it can afford to keep, and it has arranged everything, from the silver hairs of the leaves to the slow turning of the groves, to live within that sum. It breathes in fog and breathes out light, and it does not waste either.

At dusk, when the fog is gone and the stone begins to tick as it cools, a long call rises from somewhere far off. You may never know whether it came from an animal, or from the wind moving through a gap in the rock. The plateau does not say which. It only goes on, patient and exact, keeping what it has.
