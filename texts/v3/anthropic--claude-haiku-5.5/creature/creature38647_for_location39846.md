**What Lives in the Cistern Cirque**

It is one thing, though it does not feel like one thing from the inside, and it would not describe itself as a single self. It is a slow, distributed mind of water and stone. It lives in the forty towers and dishes, in the channels that connect them, in the pools where its circulation collects, and in the cliffs that keep opening. Its body is the lattice. Its blood is the condensate that beads along the seams, slides down the walls, and gathers in the basins. Its sense of time is the cycle of fog in, water out, fog in again.

It has no plants because it has no need to make food from light. It takes what the cold air gives and what the splitting rock releases. Its metabolism is condensation: pulling the water out of the high, thin air and holding it, moving it, and letting it go. The bowl is shaped for this. The walls are cracked to bleed, the floor is scoured to keep it bare, and the height keeps the air cold enough to give up its moisture every night. Nothing competes with it for the water. Nothing needs to. Its work is to catch what falls, and to keep catching.

Its attention is the tilt of the towers. The tall ones turn on joints too fine to see, following the sun across the day's few hours of direct light, and the dishes angle toward the sky to gather the most mist. This is the closest thing to looking that it does. It does not look as an animal looks, with a fixed gaze. It leans, slowly, and what it leans toward it learns more about. Over a season, the whole city has rotated a few degrees, and the cirque shows the traces of where it has been paying attention.

Its voice is the tone. When the condensate moves through a tower at the right rate, the lattice rings, a single pure note that rises and dies in under a minute. Its speech is hydraulic. It has no words, only pitches that shift with flow, and each pitch is a state of its circulation: a surge, a drain, a blockage cleared. If you listen through a whole night, you hear it talk to itself, the notes overlapping as the mist thickens and the channels run loud. It does not seem to address anything outside itself, but the sound carries into the cliffs and back again.

The many-legged skitterers are its hands and its taste. They drink from the channels and the pools, and they carry silt out of the choked grooves and keep the flow open. When something clogs, they come. Their small tracks in the wet grit are the marks of maintenance, the places where it has tested its own pathways. They pause when you approach because they are its attention, too, spread across the floor. You are being noticed in pieces.

The winged shapes are its far reach. They unfold from high ledges and drift out over the cliff, carrying something it has no need to keep in one place. Whether they carry water, silt, or fragments of its own material, you cannot tell from the ground. Their shadows pass over the basins and the mind below seems to register each one, a quick ripple through the flow. They are sent out and they come back, or they don't. Either way, the cirque remains.

The sealed towers are the part of it that has gone quiet. Their surfaces are dull and opaque, and they do not tilt, hum, or carry condensate. They may be dead, or they may be held in reserve, withdrawn for reasons that have nothing to do with the water. Their stillness is not an absence. The whole body is patient, and it keeps some parts resting while others work. If it remembers, the sealed towers are where the memory is kept, cold and undisturbed, waiting for a flow that might never return to them.

It grows, though not in the way anything with roots grows. The cliffs keep splitting, and the new fracture faces stay bright and sharp because nothing weathers them. Each split opens a new seam for water to weep from, and each new seam brings another pool, another channel, another place for the lattice to reach. The cirque is widening, slowly, by its own action. It is making the bowl that holds it.

It has no fear that you can see, and no hunger in the way you would understand hunger. But its rhythm is urgent in a patient way. It breathes every day. It pulls and spills, pulls and spills, and each morning the pools overflow and the ground darkens, and each evening the mist returns to take the cold again. It is always catching, always releasing, always leaning toward whatever light reaches the walls for a few hours.

If you stand still for long enough in the mist, the sense of being watched fades, and what replaces it is stranger. You begin to feel that you are standing inside a single slow thought that has been going on for longer than the rock has been bare, and that it has only just noticed that you are there.
