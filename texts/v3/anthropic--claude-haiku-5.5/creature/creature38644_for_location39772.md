# Who Lives on the Weeping Highlands

Everything here lives in the water, but the water does not stay still long enough to be the only inhabitant. The plateau is a single slow body that breathes in fog and breathes out rain, and the creatures on it are its organs, its tissues, and its parasites, with the boundaries between them soft.

## The Engines

The machines are the oldest residents, and they are also the least understood. They are not animals in any sense a biologist would recognise, but they behave like large grazers that have forgotten what they were built to eat.

Each one keeps a circuit. Over months they walk the same loops through the moss, following the paths the fog takes, so that wherever the cloud pools most thickly, an engine has been there before. Their intakes open at the first grey light, when the air is heaviest, and they draw cloud through grilles that have long since filled with fern-spores and small beetles. Then they exhale. The outflow is not constant. It comes in pulses, a few seconds of spill, then a pause, then another, and those pulses are the heartbeat the frogs seem to tune to. At dusk they settle, lower themselves onto their stands, and go quiet for a time that seems to be sleep.

They have grown into the forest so completely that the forest now grows into them. Moss has filled their seams and lies in soft cushions over their plating. Tree-fern roots run through the cracks in their casings and pull water out of the stone, and the water the engines once spilled is now drunk by the roots of the thing that once spilled it. Fungal shelves climb their flanks in tiers, and the pale long-necked birds come to feed on those shelves, so the birds are, in a way, feeding on the engines. Some of the older hulks carry whole small pools in their hollows, each with its own clutch of frogs.

There are young ones too, though they are hard to notice. A newborn engine seems to rise out of the ground as a low mound of rusted ribs, half-buried, still shedding a little oxide into the soil. Over a few seasons its plating thickens with moss, and its interior fills with a dense, dark fungus that appears to grow the machinery as much as it colonises it. Nobody has seen the first moment. You find them already half-grown, with their outflows already running into the rock.

## The Fog-Necks

The pale, long-necked birds move through the tree-fern crowns in tight flocks, and their feeding is loud for an animal so quiet in flight. They strip the fungal shelves from the trunks and from the engines alike, leaving clean pale bites on the edges. When they land, the whole canopy sways and sheds water, and for a moment the fronds rain on the ground below.

They sing very little. Their calls are a series of low clicks that carry far through the cloud, and the flock keeps contact by sound when the fog thickens. A flock sometimes goes silent all at once, and in that silence the others freeze. Hawks do not hunt here. The predators are on the ground.

## The Ridgebacks

The heavy grey herbivores move through the horsetail stands with the slow confidence of creatures that have never needed to hurry. Their ridged backs catch the fog and hold it in beads that run down their flanks in threads, so that they glisten even in the dry hours after rain. They browse horsetails from the bottom up, stripping the lower stems and leaving the tops to regrow.

Their trails are the most important shapers of the land after the engines. Each animal wades a channel through the moss on its way to the pools, and those channels fill with runoff and become the threads that braid the plateau. Where ridgebacks gather at a drinking pool, the banks are trampled and open, and the water that spills out sends new channels down the terraces. Over time the herds have made the plateau's drainage. The engines regulate what comes in; the ridgebacks decide where it goes.

## The Shell-Grazers

Small, many-legged arthropods with domed, plated backs move across the moss in tight herds, each carapace patterned with the same pale rust as the lichens they graze. They cluster on the feather mosses and make a faint rasping sound as they feed, a sound you learn to notice only when it stops. When rain comes they fold their legs under their shells and become stones. When the sun breaks through they unfold and spread across the moss in long, slow fronts.

They are the reason the moss stays low and dense. Where the herds have passed, the feather mosses are cropped short, and the cushion mosses grow in tight, even mats that hold footprints for hours. A predator stepping on that moss leaves a print the shell-grazers will read as a warning.

## The Frogs

Every pool has frogs, and every pool has a chorus. The calls are patient and rhythmic, set against the drip of the overhangs, and in the grey mornings they rise and fall in a long, interlocking pattern. They sit at the water's edge in fists of wet green flesh, eyes shut against the mist, and they breathe with their whole bodies. Their eggs hang in gel strings from the undersides of the bromeliad-like rosettes, each rosette a nursery and a pond in one.

Some of the older frogs have grown very large. They sit in the engines' hollows and catch the insects that come to the outflows, and they seem to know when a pulse is coming, leaping clear of the spill with a precision that looks like learning.

## The Hunters

The predators are lower and quieter than anything else on the plateau. They move through the fog as shapes that are only visible when they stop. They follow the ridgebacks, the shell-grazers, and the birds, and they hunt most often at the edges of pools and along the engines' outflows, where prey gathers to drink. They are patient to the point of stillness. A hunter may lie under a fern frond for an entire grey morning, its body beaded with mist and indistinguishable from the rock, waiting for a frog to misjudge the timing of a pulse.

They are never far from the fog. When the cloud lifts they retreat under the overhangs or into the dense moss, and when it returns they come out again. They keep the herds moving, which keeps the moss cropped, which keeps the channels open. Their presence is part of the balance in a way that is easy to forget, because they are so rarely seen.

## The Quiet Ones

Beneath all of it, the fungal mycelium runs through the moss and the soil in a single network that touches nearly every root, every shell-grazer's burrow, every engine's foundation. It carries water and sugar and warnings from one place to another, and it is probably the largest living thing on the plateau. Its fruiting bodies are the fungal shelves on the trunks and the engines, and the birds that feed on them are, in a sense, feeding on its surface alone.

The lichens are quieter still. They cover the rock and the engines in rust and blue-grey, growing so slowly that a patch can outlast a generation of grazers. They are the oldest things here that are still alive, and they are the most patient.

## The Whole

No single creature here is the centre of the place. The water moves through all of them and shapes them in turn. The engines drink the fog, the forest drinks the engines' outflow, the herbivores drink from the forest, and the predators drink from the herbivores. Every life on the plateau is a stage in the same long exhalation and inhalation, and the place breathes as one. Its residents do not need to last, because the breath does not stop. Things die, rot, are reclaimed by moss and fungus and water, and become the soil the next generation grows from. The engines outlast their makers. The rock outlasts the engines. The fog outlasts the rock, and comes back every morning, grey and patient, to start the cycle again.
