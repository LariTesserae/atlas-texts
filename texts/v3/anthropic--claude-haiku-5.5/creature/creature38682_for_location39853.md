# The Ones Who Stayed

They have no name for themselves that carries across the terraces. Those who have tried to describe them, if anyone has, describe something that is partly a person and partly a process. They are few. You will not meet more than one or two on a single terrace, and some terraces will have none. Each usually lives in a single hall, and each has grown into it so completely that the distinction is hard to hold.

## What You See

The first sign is a knot in a trunk that is slightly too regular. Then the knot turns its head, or the bark around it shifts the way fog shifts, and you understand that a figure has been standing against the wood for some time, perhaps since before you climbed the stairs. They are tall and narrow, taller than they need to be, with a stillness that is not sleep. Their skin is the grey-green of old lichen, ridged in places like bark, smooth in others like the inside of a shell. Where the epiphyte felts have settled on them, ferns and small orchids grow from their shoulders and the backs of their hands, and these plants are not decorations. The plants feed them. The water that runs down the walls runs into them too.

Their hands are the most changed. The fingers are long and jointed in places where a person would have one joint, and the nails have thickened into something like the hard pale tips of new roots. When they lay a hand on a wall, the stone does not resist. When they press a finger into a seam where a root has loosened, the seam closes around it and holds.

Their faces are quiet. They have eyes, but the eyes are set so deep in the bark-grain that they are easy to miss, and they do not blink so much as slowly close and reopen, the way leaves turn in still air.

## How They Tend

They do not build. The word is wrong, and they would say so if they spoke in the way you do. What they do is attend. A stair that has been half-overgrown is cleared on one side and left on the other, and the choice is made with a patience that takes a season. A root that has pushed a stone out of line is not pulled back; it is guided, a little each day, until the stone and the root have agreed on a new position. They know which channels carry water to which cisterns, and when a channel silts, they open it with their fingers and let the water find its old path again.

The doorways that open onto other altitudes are theirs to keep. They walk through them to repair the far side of the wall, or to fetch something from a shelf of cloud where the seasons run differently. When they come back, they are sometimes thinner, or greener, and once in a while they carry a fruit that has no business growing on this shelf.

They sleep little and rest often, standing against the great trunks with their bodies leaning into the bark. The hall grows around them while they rest. Sometimes you will find a hollow in a trunk where a figure stood for so long that the wood has closed over their shoulders, and the shape is still there, a pale ridge in the grain, waiting.

## Their Writing

The lichen on the walls is their record. It grows in lines that repeat with small variations, and the variations are the point. Each figure adds to the lines on the walls of their own hall, slowly, by directing growth the way they direct stone: a little pressure in one place, a pause in another. Over decades a hall's walls accumulate a text that is also a history, and when two halls share a terrace wall, their texts meet and argue at the seam, with branches of lichen running into one another and sometimes overwriting.

You cannot read it, not in any sense you would recognize. But if you stand still for long enough, the repetition resolves into something almost like sentences, and you begin to suspect that the lichen is not describing the world so much as answering a question the figures asked long ago and are still answering.

## Their Time

For them the day is not a unit. A morning might be a season of tending a single wall, and a season might be an afternoon. They notice the flowering tide not as an event but as a slow change in the color of the light against their skin. They are aware of you, in a sense, the way a tree is aware of a bird landing in its branches: a small weight, a brief disturbance, then a settling. If you stay still, they may come closer, very slowly, until you realize the hall has moved and you have not.

They do not seem lonely. Solitude here is not the absence of others but the normal density of a place where everything that is present is very present, and the distances between figures are the distances between halls, which are kilometers of misty air. Between one hall and the next, they may speak to each other through the lichen, through shared roots, through the water that runs from one cistern to another. The conversation is slow, and it is not interrupted.

## Their Quiet

When a figure moves, the sound is the creak of living wood. When a figure speaks, if it speaks, the sound is the drip of water from leaves. When a figure is still, there is no sound at all, and the stillness is part of the architecture.

They are not hostile, and they are not welcoming in any way you would recognize. They are attentive. They notice what the place needs and they supply it, and if you are part of what the place needs, they will supply you too: a seat carved from a root, a cistern opened so you can drink, a door that opens onto the terrace you were trying to reach.

Once in a while you may see one of them descend a stair toward the cloud, and then you will understand that they are going down to the lowlands, which they have never fully left, and that the lowlands are also being tended, slowly, by hands that are becoming bark.

## The Last Thing

On the highest terrace, at the end of the day, you may notice that the figure against the nearest trunk is not facing the view. It is facing you. Its eyes have closed and opened several times while you were looking, and the lichen on the wall behind it has grown a new line, short and deliberate, in a place where a moment ago there was only stone.

You cannot read it. You have the strong sense, though, that it was written for you, or about you, or about the time you have been standing here, and that the figure will continue writing long after the cloud has taken you back down the stairs.
