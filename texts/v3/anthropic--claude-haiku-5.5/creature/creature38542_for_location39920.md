Nothing here walks, flies, or burrows, so whoever lives here has to be found among the things that change too slowly to look like change. The clearest candidate is the green-black film.

It is not a scatter of separate plants. Press a fingertip into it and the give runs away under the surface in a way that a mat of distinct threads would not. It is one body, laid across the basin like a dust sheet over furniture. It sealed the cracks in the clay and filled them with silt, and it did this the way a wound closes, from the edges inward and without any visible order. Where it meets the channels it thickens into the sage mats on their smooth floors. Where it climbs the north faces of the rock it becomes the underlayer of the lichen, and the rings and rosettes are only the places where it has chosen to show itself above the stone. It has no centre. It is spread so thin that any one part of it is much like the next.

It knows the world through damp. The beads that gather on the overhangs and fall one at a time are its breath. The warm rain that comes over the ridge every few days is a meal and a long drink together, and the silt it carries goes into the healed cracks and is slowly made into something the film can keep. Heat reaches it as the pale disc behind cloud. Its surface turns toward that disc, though the turning is too slow to see and can only be inferred from the way the rings have grown, a fraction of a millimetre a year, always outward and always on the same side.

Its sense of time is the stone's. It does not count days. It counts how far the lichen has got.

The shrubs are the exception to its spread, and it seems to have made its peace with them. Each stands in a bare circle of clay where the film does not enter, and the circles are wide, evenly spaced, and kept. They look less like gaps than like agreements, places where the body has decided that a particular kind of thing will be left alone. The waxy leaves and peeled-leather bark grow there unbothered, and the film keeps its edge a careful distance from their roots.

The built marks it has taken in without fuss. The ring of fitted stones has its gaps filled with clay and its interior sunk under green, and the film has gone so far into the joints that the stones can no longer be told apart from the ground they stand on. The lintel carries its band of grooves, and the film has grown into those lines, so that the carving now holds moisture and green while the rest of the stone stays pale. It may no longer be able to separate its own growth from the old cutting, and it has not tried. The cairn at the western ridge is down to a fan of rounded stones, but a few still hold their original stacking. There is nothing holding them now except the film, which has bound the fallen stones to the clay beneath them, and that binding is the only reason some of the old order survives. The clean-edged slab with its square hole is being sealed from its margins inward. The sediment in the hole is thickening, and the film is in no hurry to close it. Closing it would be an ending, and the film does not seem to recognise endings as something to rush toward.

It does notice weight. A foot pressed into the film leaves a dent that holds its shape for an hour and then slowly fills, and in the days after, the ground under that spot is a little warmer and a little more swollen with damp, as if the body had taken a note. It does not object to visitors and does not welcome them. It only registers them, the way it registers a falling bead, and goes on.

This is what lives in the basin: a continuous, patient skin that has kept the clay closed, the channels smooth, the lichen growing outward at the speed of the stone, and the old marks slowly absorbed into the ground they were set on. It is in no sense a guardian. It is simply the process by which the place stays the way it is. The basin agreed with itself long ago, and the film is that agreement, spread thin and kept in place by damp and warmth and the slow pull of the rock. When the drip falls into the small pool and the single round note goes out and takes its long time to fade, the film is there in the sound, the way it is in every surface, unhurried and already complete.
