# Who Lives on the Shelf

Almost everything that lives here has learned to hold on. The wind is constant, the water is thin, and the stone gives back heat and light without keeping any of it. The residents are the ones that have worked out how to lean into that, to shelter in a crack, bunch close to the ground, or wait out the glare in the shade of a pillar.

## The Lichen

The oldest residents are the lichens. They cover the pillars in rings and rosettes of orange, black, and yellow, and they grow so slowly that a ring a finger's width across may be older than any herd that now grazes beneath it. Each lichen is two organisms living as one: a fungus that builds a dense, weathered shell, and a partner inside it that makes food from light and air. Together they can survive on bare rock where almost nothing else can. They do not need soil or rain. They need only the dew that forms before dawn and the mist that drifts in each afternoon.

On the north faces, where the dew lingers longest, the lichen grows thickest. A patient observer could read the shelf's history in those patches, the way a tree is read in its rings, though the lichen records only its own slow expansion and the seasons it survived.

## The Cushion Plants

In the cracks between stones, the cushion plants hold their ground in tight grey-green domes. Each one is a mass of short, packed stems and leaves, so compact that its inside stays warmer and wetter than the wind outside. Other plants could not survive in such a place, but these hold water and heat in their own bodies. A cushion plant can be decades old and no wider than a cup, and its shape is the same as the shape of the gusts that have blown across it for those decades. It is less a plant growing than a plant made of the weather's refusals.

## The Beetles

Before dawn, when the dew is heavy, small dark beetles climb the north faces of the pillars and stop at the top of a slope. They raise their backs into the cold air and let the moisture bead on their shells, then tilt forward and let the water run down into their mouths. By the time the sun has dried the stone, they are gone, down into the cracks and the lichen where they graze on the thin algae that the dew has loosened. Their whole day is measured by the same hour of wet and dry that governs the rest of the shelf.

## The Lizards and Rodents

Lizards the color of lichen lie flat against the rock at the edges of the sun, where the shadows begin. They are hard to find. Their scales break up their outline, and they hold still until something passes close, then vanish into a honeycomb hole faster than the eye can follow. In the morning they warm themselves on the pale stone and turn to match the mottled orange and black around them.

The burrowing rodents live below the white dust drifts. Their tunnels run through the piled grit and hold it still, so the drifts that look as if they should slide in the wind stay where they are. Each drift is a small city of runs, with entrances hidden in the lee of the pillars. The rodents come out at dusk, when the amber light makes the pillars glow, and they forage the grass roots along the tussock edges.

## The Grazers

The horned grazers are the largest animals on the plateau, and they move in long lines across the stone. From a distance a herd is a slow smudge of dust. Up close, it is a crowd of heavy, shaggy bodies with ridged horns curling back over their heads. They follow the same paths year after year, and the paths have worn into grooves between the pillars, shallow channels a body wide. The grooves are the herd's memory, and each new generation walks into them.

They gather in the lee of the tallest pillars, where the wind is weaker and the grass grows thicker. When a long, low shape crosses the grassland at the edge of the herd, the grazers lift their heads and watch. They watch until the shape goes out of sight, and then they return to grazing. Their bellowing carries for kilometers, and it is part of the chord the pillars play, one more voice in a sound that has no single source.

## The Birds of the Gorge

The birds are the most numerous creatures on the shelf, and they are the ones that have changed it most. Broad-winged birds come up from the river in clouds and settle on the cliff ledges, where colonies crowd shoulder to shoulder along the rim. The stout-billed birds among them carry shellfish and cracked eggs up from the gorge. They fly above the anvil heaps at the base of the cliffs, drop their prey onto the smooth, flat stones, and circle back to see whether the shell has broken. If it has not, they drop it again, and again, until the shell gives.

Over generations the drops have hollowed the stones and rubbed their edges bright. The heaps grow a little higher each year, pale with droppings and carved with the marks of thousands of impacts. No bird planned them. Each one dropped a shell where the last bird had dropped one, and the repetition built a structure that no one designed and no one will finish.

## The Chorus

The pillars themselves are the last residents, though they are not alive in any sense the birds would recognize. When the wind enters their hollow cores, each one sounds its own note, and together they make the chord that fills the plateau. The grazers and birds add their voices to it. The waterfall underlies it all. The result is a sound that belongs to no one and that everything on the shelf has, in a sense, helped to tune.

## What Lives Here

Taken together, the shelf's inhabitants are a network of patience. The lichen grows by millimeters, the cushion plants hold their shape against the wind, the beetles harvest dew, the rodents hold the dust in place, the grazers walk the same grooves, and the birds build their anvils one drop at a time. None of them is building a world. Each is doing what it has always done, and the sum is a place that persists by repetition.

At night the animals sleep in the lee of the pillars, the wind keeps moving through the hollow stone, and the chord goes on. Whoever lives here, they live in the same rhythm as the shelf, and the rhythm does not need them to notice it.
