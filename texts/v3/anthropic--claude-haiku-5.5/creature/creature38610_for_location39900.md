The district has no census. Most of its residents are rooted, and the rest are too small, too fast, or too far down to be counted, so what lives in the Lowest Ward is less a population than a set of arrangements that have outlasted the people who made them.

Start in the silt. The black skin over the streets is alive in a way you can smell before you can see. Where the water stands still, it turns faintly sweet and then faintly sour, and the film on its surface shifts with colonies of things that break down steel and rubber and old paint into something the plants can use. Nobody has named most of them. They work through the night, in the dark under the mud, and their labor is the reason the vines can get a grip on ground that should not hold anything. The fig's roots feed on them. So do the reeds. So, in a sense, does the whole bowl.

The vines are the most numerous. They have colonized every vertical surface and most horizontal ones, and they behave like a crowd that has learned to be patient. They are not one plant but a generation of plants that have grown over each other for decades, the older stems thickened into ropes the color of bottle glass, the younger ones still green and quick, feeling along the cornices for anything to hold. They have bored into mortar, lifted curbs, and pulled the gutters off the four-story offices. They do not move, but they are always reaching, and where they have stopped reaching they have simply settled in.

Above them all is the fig. It has taken the substation at the district's center the way a landlord takes a building that has been abandoned for taxes. Its roots went through the footing and split the transformer housing, and its aerial roots hang in curtains over the dead copper, drinking the condensate that forms every night on the cold metal. Its canopy shades the reed beds to the east and throws the cooling towers into shadow in the late afternoon. The fig is the tallest resident and in some ways the most settled. It does not compete with the vines so much as absorb them, and when you stand beneath it, you feel the weight of a long decision that was made without you.

The reeds stand in the sinkhole basins and along the canal, chest-high and rattling. They are the district's loudest plants, though their noise is only the wind moving through them. Herons live among them. These are the one set of residents who seem to have chosen the place rather than merely inherited it. A heron stands motionless in the same stretch of shallows for hours, its neck folded, its eye fixed on the water, and when it moves it does so slowly enough that the movement seems to be part of the stillness. There are only a few. They appear to have territories and to respect each other's edges. In the morning they stand facing the sun with their wings half open, and the steam comes up off the reeds around them like a second, thinner breath.

The kingfisher is a visitor. It cuts across the canal in a flash of blue and disappears behind the weir, and you cannot tell whether it lives in the district or only passes through on its way to better water. It doesn't matter. Its presence is a reminder that the canal, for all its concrete and roar, still holds enough fish to be worth a bird's time.

The insects are everywhere and nowhere. Their whine sits at the edge of hearing, and the pumps cover it entirely. They are the most numerous creatures in the bowl after the silt bacteria, and they move through the vines and the ferns and the curtains of aerial roots, pollinating, decomposing, feeding one another. You would never see them unless you stood still long enough to notice the faint motion in the air around the blossoms.

Then there are the machines, which are residents in their own way. The two pumping stations hold the water back, and they keep a schedule that is not kept by anything in the bowl. Their diesel engines thud through alternating cycles, and their rhythm is the rhythm of the whole district: a drumming that comes up through the mud and into your chest before the sound reaches your ears. They have no mind of their own, but they have a caretaker population that lives beyond the levee and never walks down into the bowl. The pumps belong to a relationship between people who will not come and a system that will not stop, and that relationship is part of who lives here too.

The gantry crane is the strangest of them. It still has power. Once every few minutes it creaks forward one meter along its rail, pauses, and stops, carrying nothing, and the yellow light at its top blinks through the whole cycle. It does not seem to be going anywhere. It seems instead to be keeping watch, or keeping time, or simply doing what it was built to do in a place that has no more use for it. If there is a figure in the Lowest Ward that understands the place as a place, it is probably the crane.

And what is absent matters as much as what is present. There are no large animals. There are no nests in the old trees, or none that are visible from the road. No dogs, no cats, no rats in the gutters, no people in the windows. The bowl belongs to its plants and its machines, and the two have made a working arrangement. The pumps keep the water from rising. The plants keep the ground from sliding. Each depends on the other and both are slowly reassembling the district into something that might one day be a forest, though it would not look like one from the levee.

So who lives in the Lowest Ward? The fig, which holds the center. The vines, which hold the walls. The reeds and the herons who share the shallows. The silt, working in the dark. The insects in their thin whine. The kingfisher, passing through. The crane, blinking and carrying nothing. The pumps, thudding on their schedule, kept alive by people who never come down. And the arrangement itself, which has its own slow intelligence and is in no hurry to change.

Nothing here is changing quickly, and nothing here asks to. The place keeps its equilibrium and waits. Eventually someone will have to decide whether the pumps should be turned off, and when they are, the residents who have grown used to the drumming will find out what they were really holding back.
