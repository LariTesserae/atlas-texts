The roar sorts itself out gradually. The chittering rasp from the lower basin is a rhythm passed from body to body, a count that rises and falls in waves across hundreds of meters. The deep drumming is a single pulse, slow and enormous, that seems to come from everywhere at once. The clicks and whistles are conversation, and once you can hear it as conversation, you can't stop hearing it that way. These are the inhabitants talking, and they have been talking for longer than the rock has been dust.

They are visible now, if you look for shapes rather than parts. The haulers move along the tower bases in files, each one the size of a large dog, low-slung and heavily armored. Their carapaces are the color of the rock they walk on, pale ochre with a glaze of mineral grey, so that they seem to be made of the same stuff as the walls. Their heads are broad and blunt, with compound eyes that have grown large and dark to catch the amber light, and their mandibles are worn smooth at the tips from constant work. Each carries a load in a pouch slung under its thorax: chalk dust, shell fragments, bits of the old fungal crust, packed tight. They set the loads down at the tower base in precise piles and go back for more. Nothing about the movement is frantic. Each step is placed. The files never cross.

The winged ones are the source of the drumming. They wheel above the canopy, flat and wide, their wings a lacework of translucent membrane stretched over dark ribs. The sound comes from a pair of resonant organs deep in the thorax, taut sheets of tissue that thrum when the wings are held at a certain angle. Listen long enough and the pulse has structure: a long swell, a pause, a shorter answer from across the basin. These are the callers. They know where the colony's members are, and where they have gone, and where the dust is thickest. When one falls, its wings are taken apart by the skitterers within a day, and the ribs go into the walls.

The skitterers are small and quick and glow faintly at the edges, the blue-grey pulse you noticed in the fungal mats. They are not so much animals as messengers and cleaners. They run the mats, nip the new growth where it is thickest, and carry sap from the great stalks down to the brooding galleries. Where they pass, the luminous tracks fade, and you realize they are reading the ground as they go, tasting it with antennae that never stop moving.

The tenders live in the upper galleries and never come down. You see them only in silhouette through the round openings: long-bodied, slow, pale as wax, moving between cells with a patience that looks almost like ceremony. They carry the brood. They feed it sap that has been worked through several stomachs until it thickens and sweetens, and they turn the cells so that the young face the light at the right hour. The cells themselves are the oldest architecture in the city, sealed and resealed over generations, each layer of wax and dust fused to the one below. The tenders are the memory of the place. When a gallery collapses, they rebuild it to the exact dimensions of the one that stood before, because the dimensions are what they remember.

It is easy to look at this and see a hive, or a colony with a queen somewhere in the deep chambers, and perhaps there is one. But the more you watch, the less the picture holds. Nothing here is in charge. The haulers do not know where the callers are going. The tenders do not know what the haulers are carrying. Each body follows a pattern it inherited through its own body's chemistry and the pattern of the walls it passes, and the walls are the only instruction set that everyone reads. The city is the plan. Each generation adds a course, and each course tells the next what to do.

They are very long-lived as a collective, though individually brief. A hauler lasts a few seasons. A tender may last a decade. But the galleries they tend have been tended for a thousand years, and when the sap dries into dust at the end of a body's life, it becomes part of the fungal mats, which feed the stalks, which feed the next bodies. Death here is not an ending so much as a deposit. Every corpse is a stratum. You can see the layers in the tower walls if you look at a broken face: bands of pale and dark, each one a season, a generation, a set of bodies that gave their chitin and sap to the structure so the structure could go on holding the next set.

And there is something in the calls that is not about the present at all. Under the chatter and the drumming runs a rhythm that is unlike the others. It rises and falls in long, slow curves, like a sound that moved across a surface and then stopped. The callers repeat it when the sky is clear and the air is most still. It is the only part of their language that does not refer to anything in the basin. It may be a memory of a time when the ground was wet, when the valley had a river at its bottom and the stalks drank from it. None of them could say so. But they keep the rhythm the way they keep the dimensions of the galleries, because it is part of what they inherited and part of what they pass on.

So the roar goes on. It is not a noise that stops or peaks. It is weather, the sound a patient, enormous, self-renewing system makes as it persists. The haulers move the dust. The callers hold the pulse. The tenders turn the brood toward the light. The walls grow another course. And the basin, which was a river once and is now a bowl of chalk, keeps its own slow time, in which these bodies are not visitors or survivors but the ongoing shape of the place itself.
