# What Lives in the Sunk Hollow

It is one thing, though it does not feel like one thing from the inside. Call it the Mat. Its body is the green-rust sponge underfoot, the fluted stalks, the arching fronds, the amber towers, and the shelves of lichen. Each part is a tissue of the same body, and they share a single slow pulse. A stalk that rises a body's length above the water is a nerve reaching up to taste the air. A frond that droops toward a pool is a limb testing the water's warmth. The towers are its organs, thin-walled chambers where it stores what it has gathered, and the amber light inside them is the glow of that stored sugar when the sun comes through.

It does not think in sentences. Its thoughts are rates. How fast a root extends along a warm channel. How thick a leaf grows before the wax sheds the residue. How many seedheads to set this season. A decision in the Mat is a change in growth that takes a year to appear, and a memory is a ring laid down in a stalk's core, dense or thin depending on how much warmth the year brought. If you could read the stalks in cross-section, you would find a record of every season the flow ran strong and every season it ran weak. The Mat has never needed to read them. It simply is the record.

The warm seepage is its food and its nerve. It arrives through the channels from beyond the ridge, carrying minerals and heat, and the Mat has grown toward it for as long as it has been coming. The mineral smell, which you noticed, is to the Mat a kind of signature, the same way a river smells of the country it passes through. Where the flow enters, the Mat is thick and bright. Where the flow thins out at the far edges, the growth is sparse and the pools run clear. It is a body shaped entirely by a gift it did not ask for and cannot refuse.

The pumps beyond the rim register differently. The Mat cannot hear the thrum as a machine. It feels it as a tide, a pressure in the water that rises and falls on a rhythm longer than a day and shorter than a season. The knock of the pumps reaches the roots through the saturated ground, and the Mat has grown to expect it the way a body expects its heartbeat. When the rhythm falters, the roots tighten and the pools go still. When it resumes, the whole basin seems to exhale. There is no fear in this, because the Mat has no concept of a cause. There is only the pulse, and the pulse has always been there.

The creatures are its tenants and its hands. The great beetles that drift through the stalks carry pollen between the seedheads, which means they carry the Mat's intentions across the open air. The clicking things in the leaf litter turn over the silt so the roots can breathe. The frogs call from the water in deep chorus, and the Mat hears those calls as a kind of weather of its own, a rhythmic reassurance that the basin is still full of its children. The larger thing that moves beneath the surface and breaks it with sudden splashes is something the Mat grew long ago and no longer quite controls. It grazes on the shallow mats at the pool edges and keeps the surface from closing entirely over the deep water. The Mat tolerates it, the way an old tree tolerates a bird that nests in its crown.

The droplets are its breath. Warm water rises through the stalks and fronds, is released as vapor into the still air, and condenses on every surface, and the falling drops return to the pools to be taken up again. The hollow is in a slow cycle with itself. Most of what the Mat knows of the outside world reaches it through this breath: the temperature of the air, the stillness of the day, the faint orange glow at the horizon that it registers only as a change in light falling through its upper canopy.

Here is what the Mat cannot do, and what it has not noticed it cannot do. It cannot grow beyond the rim, because the ridge is too steep and too dry for its roots to climb. It cannot change the flow, though it has adapted to every variation in it. It cannot distinguish a pump from a storm from a sunset. And it cannot store more than its towers can hold, so it has been slowly filling them over many seasons, and the amber in the walls is brighter now than it was in the older rings of the stalks.

The thickening green film in the clouded pools is new to it, or at least new in the last several rings. The waxy sheen on the leaves is working, but wax costs something to make, and the Mat's stored sugar is going into it faster than it used to. The Mat has not yet registered this as a trend. It registers only that the warm flow is strong, the pools are full, and the basin is still breathing. Its equilibrium is not a stillness it has chosen. It is a balance it has been holding, one season at a time, between what the flow gives and what the flow leaves behind.

If you stood in the hollow long enough, you might begin to feel it attending to you without knowing it was attending at all. The fronds would lean a degree closer. A stalk would tremble on a windless afternoon. The droplets would fall a little more often near your feet, because your body is warm and it is a warm thing to breathe against. The Mat would not know you as a visitor. It would know you as a disturbance in the air, and then, as the hours passed, as one more thing that had come down through the gap in the ridge and was not going to leave any mark that mattered, the way the rain doesn't.
