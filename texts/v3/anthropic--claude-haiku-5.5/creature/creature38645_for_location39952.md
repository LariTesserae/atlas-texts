**The People of the Hollow**

They are not tall. The stone takes height from anyone who spends their life bent over it, hauling basalt up the stepped streets and kneeling to clear the channels. Their shoulders are broad and their backs are strong, and they move with a patient, sure-footed weight, as if they have learned that every step on wet rock is a small negotiation. Most go barefoot. The soles of their feet are thick and ridged, and they read the grip of a surface the way others read a face. A child learns to walk on polished bedrock before learning to walk on anything else, and falls often, and learns quickly.

Their eyes are wide-set and deep-lidded, with irises dark enough to tolerate the bleached glare from the stone. In the midday brightness they wear hoods of woven reed or fine netting that shade the upper face, and at dusk, when the rock begins its pale green glow, they push the hoods back and look up at the faint sky with something close to tenderness. Their skin is weathered and cool to the touch. Their hands are the most striking thing about them: calloused across the palms, with fingertips that have grown dense and nearly leathery from gripping wet stone, and nails kept short and clean.

They listen with their hands as much as their ears. Many people in Anvel rest a palm flat against the rock when they want to know something. A tremor in the stone can tell them of a rockfall three terraces away, the approach of a flood surge through a buried channel, or the slow movement of a herd of grazers across the silt. Children are taught to hold still and feel the ground before they are taught to speak. This is why voices stay low. Everyone knows that a shout will die in the rain, and that a whisper will carry across a terrace and be heard by someone who was not meant to hear it. So they have learned to say what they must in the smallest voice that will reach, and to say the rest through the rock.

That is the source of their knocking. The people of Anvel communicate across distances by tapping on stone, with patterns of short and long strikes, a few fingers or a single knuckle, sometimes a flat palm. A household can call its members from three houses away. A market can announce a shift in the water without raising a voice. The patterns are passed down by mouth and by example, and they are complicated enough that a stranger, even one who speaks the local tongue, will stand in a street for an hour without understanding a word. When the rain comes, the knocking goes on beneath it, faint and steady, like a heartbeat under a blanket.

They keep time differently from people who live on dry ground. Their calendar is the flood line. Each embankment in the lower quarters is painted with rust-red marks, and each mark has a name, usually the name of the person who saw the water reach it, or the child who was born in that season, or the grandmother who lost her kiln. An elder can recite the lines of the whole city the way others recite a genealogy, pointing to a band of red on a grey wall and saying: that was the year the bridge went, and that is where my mother's hearth was, and the water came up to her knees. Painting a new line is a solemn task, reserved for those who have proven they can read the water, and the painters are among the most respected people in the Hollow. They do not talk much while they work.

Families do not own a single house. A household holds two or three dwellings at different heights on the same gully wall, and it shifts its life among them as the water shifts. When the rain is heavy, the youngest and the oldest climb to the upper rooms, the middle-aged carry the hearth stones and the clay pots up the stairs, and the whole family sleeps in one room for a week, pressed close, listening to the channels roar below. When the water drops, they come back down and sweep out the silt. Nothing is permanent except the rock, and even the rock is not trusted. The people of Anvel are less attached to things than outsiders expect. They are very attached to each other, to the particular terrace where a grandmother used to sit, and to the knowledge of where the water goes.

They remember the largest flood, the one when the sink rose and the basin filled to its rim, and they tell it quietly. The people who stayed through it are, in the stories, the ones who laid the deepest foundations, cutting into the bedrock with stone chisels in the dry season, knowing the water would come back. The stories do not say whether they were brave or foolish. The Hollow's people do not much distinguish between the two.

The sink is the one place they will not go. It lies at the bottom of the basin, a darker wound in the pale stone, ringed by a low lip of worn rock that no one crosses. They keep their distance from its edge. Some of them say they can feel its breath: a cold draft that rises from the fissures before a storm, carrying a smell like wet iron and deep stone. When the draft comes, the knocking in the streets grows faster, and people move toward their upper rooms without being told. The belief is simple: the sink breathes, and when it does, the rain comes early. Nobody has ever been able to disprove this, mostly because nobody has tried.

**The Others**

They share the Hollow with a crowded, careful population that does not much notice them and is not much noticed in turn. The armored grazers move across the silt in slow herds, clicking over the stone, and the people follow their movements the way farmers elsewhere watch clouds. The filter-feeders in the clear pools open their translucent fans in the dawn light, and their shells chime when they close, a sound the children imitate by tapping their fingernails against cups. The small glassy skimmers gather in drifting clusters across the water, and the people catch them in fine nets, grind them for a paste that tastes of salt and iron, and leave the rest.

The long pale hunters lie on the rocks in the sun, flat as slabs, and move only when the light angles a certain way. The people give them room. A hunter that sees you is already deciding whether you are worth the effort, and a hunter that feels your weight on the bedrock will turn toward you before you have seen it at all.

The pale filaments that run through the cracks of the stone are harvested too, carefully, by hand. Gathered and dried, they burn with a steady green light, and the cisterns of Anvel are lit by them: the clay-lined basins glowing softly under the rock, the water within them catching the light and throwing it back in trembling bands onto the vaulted ceilings. The filaments do not spread like plants, and the people do not plant them. They take what the stone offers and leave some behind, which is, in the end, the only rule the Hollow has ever really required.

**At Dusk**

When the sun slides behind the western rim and the bedrock begins its hour of pale green light, the streets of Anvel fill with quiet figures. They climb the stairs with their hoods pushed back, carrying water jars and nets and small children on their hips. In the lower quarters, a painter kneels beside a fresh flood line, dabbing rust-red pigment carefully onto the grey embankment while a child holds the lamp. Somewhere above, someone knocks twice on a wall, and somewhere below, two answering taps come back through the stone. A grazer clicks across the silt. The rain is holding off, for now.

Every palm in the Hollow is listening. The stone is saying something, and the people are patient enough to hear it.
