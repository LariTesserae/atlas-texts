**The Tender**

You don't see it first. You feel it, a low pressure in the chest that comes and goes with the pump. The frogs in the cisterns go quiet, then start again, and the whole basin seems to lean toward one of the lower galleries, where the iron stairs have sunk into root-mat and the light goes brown and then black.

When it moves, you hear it before you see it. The lowing rises up the shafts, long and sliding, and it lands on the eleven-second beat as if the two were one sound. The pump thuds. The call answers. Then the chorus of insects swells into the gap, and the thing comes forward into the grey light.

It is large, perhaps twelve meters from the blunt front of its body to the tail that drags through the gallery drains, and it is built low, the way a thing is built when it has spent a long time under weight. Its hide is not quite hide. It is a crust of fern-hybrid growth, dense and fibrous, pierced in places by fruiting bodies the color of old brass. Beneath the crust, you can see the ribs. They run along its flanks in rows, pale and hard, and they are the same stem fiber that braces the basin walls. Wherever the plants climbed into it, they stayed. The creature has grown into the structure as the structure grew into it.

The plates are the strangest part. Rivets stud its shoulders and back, dozens of them, their heads swollen over with bark until they look like scales that have been polished by rain. Some are still bolted to fragments of iron plating, half-buried in its flesh, and the fungus threads through them and feeds on the rust. When it shifts its weight, the plates grind softly against one another, a sound like a conveyor that has finally found its rhythm.

Its head is low and broad, and it carries no obvious eyes. Instead there is a cluster of pale, swollen nodes along the brow, each one the size of a fist, and they are wet and faintly pulsing. You realize after a moment that they are tuned to the gauges. The needles in the deepest gallery tremble, and the nodes tremble with them. The creature does not look at the machines. It listens to them through the roots, and it answers in its low voice.

It eats the way the basin eats. It lowers its head to the old steam vents and breathes in the warm mist, and the fungus on its flanks brightens. It drinks from the cisterns, pressing its mouth to the surface and drawing up the water in long pulls. In the pools where the flower-sap has fermented, it wades in up to the shoulder and stays there, slowly swaying, while the sweet liquor drips from its plates. Afterward it staggers along the gallery edges with an odd, careful gait, like something that knows exactly how far it can lean before it falls, and somehow never leans too far.

It is not alone. Smaller versions of itself move in the lower halls, young ones no bigger than a cart, their backs still soft and their plates only half-grown. They follow it along the conveyor tracks and time their crossings to the belt. When the older one lows, they answer, and when the pump thuds, they all fall silent together, and the basin holds its breath until the next beat.

The predators keep their distance from it. The long-legged hunters that wait beside the steam vents will strike at anything that flees the hiss, but they do not strike at the Tender, and they do not strike at its young. Something in the way it moves seems to sit outside their logic, the way the pump sits outside the forest's, always there and always keeping time.

The mesh is its concern. The copper lattice over the largest cistern is where it sleeps, curled with its nodes turned toward the gauges, its plates sliding softly against the bars. The plants have grown through the mesh so thickly that the creature is half inside it now. Its flowers appear in the lattice patterns, repeating, and when it wakes, it lifts its head and the blossoms along its brow open all at once, as if answering a signal you cannot hear. Whoever fitted that copper long ago meant it to hold something in. It may have held the creature in, or it may have held the machine's voice inside the forest, where the two could speak to each other through the roots without anyone having to choose.

When the lowing stops and the creature settles again, the basin goes on around it. The pump keeps its beat and the insects keep their chorus. The steam valves sigh, and a warm drop falls from the canopy onto the iron. The Tender lies with its nodes turned toward the gauges, listening, and it seems to be the place where the argument happens. Its body is part animal, part root, part boiler, and neither the machine nor the forest has been able to take it entirely. It keeps the eleven-second beat, and the basin keeps time with it, and as long as it breathes, the two will go on holding each other in place.
