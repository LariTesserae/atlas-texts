# The Tenders

The people who live here are few, and most of them live high. They call themselves nothing in particular. Outsiders, when there are any, call them the Tenders, because that is what they seem to do: they tend the slopes, the culverts, the root-cords, the machines that still knock. The word is inexact, but nobody here has a better one.

They look almost like anyone. Their faces are lean and weathered, their hair is often long and braided with fern fibre, and they walk the terraces with the same half-closed eyes the place seems to require. The difference is in the skin. In the creases of the elbows, behind the knees, along the seam of the jaw, a fine grey-green felt grows, the same material that covers the towers. It is soft and cool to the touch, and in the damp it beads with water the way a leaf does. Some Tenders have it across their shoulders and down their spines, and the oldest have it on their faces, so that when they close their eyes it looks like moss settling over stone. They do not seem to mind. Asked whether it hurts, they say no, that it is more like being very slowly remembered.

Their breathing is slow. They take the damp air deep into their chests and let it out in long, unhurried sighs, and in the mist, when the whole city goes quiet, you can sometimes hear a dozen of them breathing together in the upper tiers, a sound that is nearly the same as the pumps. They sleep in the hollow trunks of the towers, curled among the fern-shelves, and wake when the sap rises at dawn. They do not eat much. The moths and the blue leaves seem to be enough, along with the water that collects on their skin and drips into their mouths when they tip their heads back in the rain.

## What They Do

Their work is slow and mostly invisible. A Tender walks the lower terraces after a storm and kneels in the mud, pressing her palms against a root-cord until she can feel where it wants to grow. Where it is pulling too hard on a retaining wall, she lets it bend. Where a culvert is about to choke, she opens a second channel with a flat stone and a few words, and the water finds it. They do not so much command the growth as negotiate with it, the way a farmer negotiates with weather. Their hands are green to the wrist, and the green is warm.

They also keep the memory. This is the part outsiders find hardest to accept. When a machine reappears in a clearing, rebuilt and running, a Tender is usually nearby, sitting very still with her eyes closed, and there is a common belief that the remembering happens through her, that the city reaches for something it lost and she is the hand that holds it out. When a street vanishes under new growth overnight, the Tenders say the city has forgotten it, and they go and ask the walls what they remember instead. Nobody here is sure how accurate these recollections are. They say that is the nature of memory, and that they have stopped expecting it to be correct.

The Tenders keep track of the cycles. They hum the pump rhythms to each other, a flat low drone that moves up and down with the knocking of the machines. The flowers that open on pump time are opened, in a sense, by their humming. They know which turbine is due to whistle, which steam valve will hiss before dawn, which pipe will groan in the second hour of the night. A child learning to be a Tender learns the city as a song before she learns it as a map.

## The Bell

The keeper of the bell lives in the cathedral on the high terrace, in a room at the top of the stair where the old organ pipes rise through the ceiling like a stand of bare birch. Her name is not important, and she has not used it in years. Her hands are green to the elbow now, and the felt has spread up her neck and into the grey of her hair, so that from a distance she looks like part of the stonework.

She says the bell rings when she wakes, and that she wakes when the bell rings. This is either a joke or a description of something she does not fully understand. When the fog begins to thicken, she climbs the last ladder into the belfry and lays her palm against the metal, and the metal, which is warm and wet and covered in fine green bloom, begins to hum. She does not strike it. She listens for when it is ready, and then the sound comes out through the mist in long, low tolls that the whole city can hear.

Those who have visited the cathedral say the organ pipes move when the bell rings, very slightly, the way grass moves before wind. Those who have not visited say it is a water tank. The keeper smiles at both and does not argue.

## The Ones Below

There is one more kind of inhabitant, and the Tenders speak of it carefully. The culverts are deep and cold, and the water in them moves in ways that seem to follow an intention. Once, long ago, a Tender went down into the lowest channel to see where the flow began, and did not come back up the usual way. The others found her in the great basin under the foundry, sitting in the water to her shoulders, her felt grown out over her whole body in long pale strands that moved in the current. Her eyes were open. She was breathing slowly, in time with the pumps. When they called her name she turned her head, very slowly, and smiled, and then sank beneath the surface.

The footprints in the mud along the lower terraces, three-toed and wide, are hers, or her kin's, or her descendants'. The Tenders leave bowls of rainwater at the edge of the basin and do not go down to look.

## What They Are For

If you ask a Tender what she is for, she will probably tell you about the root-cords, or the culverts, or the bell. If you press her, she may look out over the terraces, at the long grey shoulders of the mountains worn smooth, at the foundry sagging like a sail, at the green rising over everything, and say something about the plain.

The city is being folded back into the land, and the land is slowly flattening, and somebody has to hold the memory of what the city was while it happens. The Tenders believe this is their work. They do not think it is a sad work. When one of them dies, her body is taken down to the lower terraces and laid in the mud at the foot of a tower, and the felt that has grown on her closes over her slowly, and in a season there is a new trunk there, with blue leaves hanging from its cross-members, and a faint hum in the wood when the pumps turn over at dawn.

Then the keeper in the cathedral lays her palm against the bell, and the bell rings, and somewhere in the fog the next Tender wakes up and begins to hum along.
