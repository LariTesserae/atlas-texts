**The Sunk Works: Who Keeps It**

Nothing here is a single creature, though many things are individuals. The bowl is a system, and its inhabitants are parts that have learned to hold their shapes against the heat, the damp, and the slow fall of the floor.

**The Hummers**

You feel them before you find them. In the concrete generator bays, where the low frequency sits in your teeth, the hum has a source. The Hummers are large and slow, each one longer than a bus and coiled like a sleeping cable around the housings of the engines. Their skin is grey-green and ribbed in parallel fins, like the cooling vanes of the machines they wrap, and where the fins meet the metal they have grown in, so that some are half-fused to the old casings. When you put your palm on one, the skin is warm and faintly vibrating, the way a sleeping animal's chest moves. Their bodies are full of something that conducts. They drink the stray current the engines still leak and they vent heat through their fins, and in doing so they keep the machines running at a standby pulse. The bays are warm because the Hummers are warm. Nobody built them to do this. They found the engines and stayed.

They move very little. A Hummer may take a full day to shift its coil by the width of a hand. When the floor tilts, they shift with it, slowly, and the grinding of their fins against the casings is the only sound that does not come from water. Their pace is the sinking's pace. They are, in a sense, the most patient things in the bowl.

**The Lean-Walkers**

On the high pipes, where the moss stops and the fern-things begin, there are pale four-legged creatures that watch you. They are narrow through the body and broad in the shoulder, with long splayed feet whose pads are ridged like the teeth of a comb, and they grip wet metal without slipping. Their skin is nearly white, the color of tuff after rain, and it is thin enough that you can see the dark cords of their circulation beneath it. They have no eyes you would recognize. Instead there is a row of small pale lenses along the brow, and when they turn their heads, the lenses catch the light from the openings and throw it back in slow flickers.

They walk the pipes in long patrols. Their work, as far as anyone can tell, is maintenance of the lines. They strip moss where it grows outside the pipe's shadow, scraping it off with their combed feet and dropping it to the street, and they leave the bands inside the shadow alone. This is why the moss keeps to the width of the pipes. They also press their flanks against the marks on the rock walls, the old survey notches and the tide lines of the sinking, and leave a waxy film. Some of the marks have been rubbed so often they shine. A Lean-Walker that reaches a mark and finds it altered will stand for an hour, flickering its lenses, and then move on. What it is recording, or who it is reporting to, is unclear.

They do not come down to the streets often. When one does, it moves through the vine corridors on the fallen-wire paths, placing its feet exactly where the old curbs were.

**The Skitterers**

The moss is alive with small things. The Skitterers are a few centimeters long, flat-bodied, with a dozen or more jointed legs and a shell the color of wet slate. They move in streams. You will see a line of them cross a street in the shadow of a vine roof, a few hundred bodies in a band no wider than a hand, and then the band splits and goes up a wall and into the moss at the foot of a tower. Their undersides carry the same blue-white glow as the fungal growths. At night, the streams are visible as moving lines of light along the old grid, which is how the streets are still visible after the lamps have died.

The Skitterers carry spores. They take the fungus from the walls and the wiring and drop it into the moss, which is why the moss is as dense as it is. They also carry something else. When two streams meet, they stop, touch antennae for a moment, and then go on, and the new stream often takes a slightly different route. The grid is kept this way. Each generation of Skitterers follows the trails of the last, and the trails are re-laid by their wandering. The vines are not following the city's lines by themselves. They are following the Skitterers, who are following the glow, which is following the wire.

**The Single-Note Birds**

Birds are rare, and they do not live in the bowl. They nest on the highest ledges of the rim, where the wind reaches, and they come down only to feed on the fleshy fern-things on the upper pipes. Their call is a single clear note, held for a long time and then dropped. It carries across the whole bowl. When one calls from the rim, you hear it at the bottom almost at once, and then it is silent for minutes. People who have lived near the rim say the birds call more often in the wet season, and less in the dry. Nobody knows what the note means, or whether it is a warning, a location, or a count. It is the only sound in the Sunk Works that comes from above.

**The Green Mind**

Beneath the moss and the vines and the canopy there is a network that connects them all, and it is the closest thing here to a single organism. It is fungal, but it is also root, and it is also wire. When you cut a branch and find wire inside the bark, you are seeing the network's nervous system. The vines grew around railings because the railings carried current, and the network grew into the current the way a root grows into water. Over time it stopped distinguishing between the city's metal and its own tissue.

It keeps the plan. The grid of the old streets is not a memory stored in stone, because the stone has been sinking and buckling for years. It is stored in the network's pattern of growth, in which strands run along the old power lines and thicken where the streets crossed. When the Skitterers lay a trail, the network reinforces it. When a Lean-Walker strips moss, the network responds by moving its growth back to the shadow. When a Hummer drinks current, the network feels the loss and grows a little toward the engine. It is not thinking in the way you would recognize. It is responding, constantly, to the state of a city that no longer exists in full, and the city's shape is what it keeps.

The network is also what makes the sinking bearable for everything else. As the floor drops, the network sends its roots down into the silt and anchors the buried towers, holding them upright in their graves. The Lean-Walkers' marks on the walls show the descent in centimeters, but the network is the thing that decides where the descent will break and where it will hold.

**The Sleeper**

If there is a single center to the Sunk Works, it is not any one creature. It is the arrangement: the Hummers keeping the engines warm, the Lean-Walkers keeping the lines clear, the Skitterers laying trails, the birds calling from the rim, and the green network running through all of them, following the old plan. None of them knows the whole city. Each knows its part. The city goes on running in them the way a sleeper keeps the rhythm of a day, not because anyone is awake to direct it, but because the rhythm is in the body and the body has not yet stopped.

The floor will keep dropping. The towers will go under, and the streets will fill, and the vines will climb the buried walls. The Hummers will go quiet when the engines finally fail, or else they will keep humming in the silt for another generation. The Lean-Walkers will have fewer pipes to walk. The Skitterers will lay their trails in the mud. The network will keep growing toward whatever is still warm.

Somewhere on the rim, a bird calls once and falls silent, and the note crosses the bowl and reaches the bottom, and everything that lives there stops, for a moment, and listens.
