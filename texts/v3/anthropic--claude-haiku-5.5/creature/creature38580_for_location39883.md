**Who Lives in the Sunken Works**

The first thing you notice is the scavenger, because it notices you first. It is at the edge of the lamplight on a discharge pipe, grey and low, its fur matted with the black silt from the outflow. It does not run when you look at it. It turns its head, a slow and deliberate motion, and you realise it has been tracking you since you reached the rim. It lives in the ducts that run between the terraces, a warren of insulated tubing and cable trays that stays warm all year. It eats what the pumps leave behind: algae scraped from the intakes, insects drawn to the lamps, the occasional pale fish that surfaces too close to a causeway edge. Its teeth are very clean. Its eyes catch the sodium light and give it back as two small amber coins, and then it is gone into a seam of conduit, and the pipe is empty again.

The fish are harder to see. They live in the sumps, in the deep water beyond the reach of the lamps, where the light gives out a few metres down and nothing grows. Their bodies are nearly translucent, the colour of old candle wax, and their eyes have closed over into smooth pale skin. They have no need of sight. They feel the water instead, the pressure changes from the pump intakes, the slow pull of the discharge channels, and they have learned to hold station in the currents that the machines create. Once in a long while one rises beside a causeway and hangs there, nose to the surface, and you can see the thin ridge of its spine and the tiny gills working. It is not looking at you. It is tasting the air, or listening to the water through its skin, and when it has learned whatever it wanted to learn it sinks again without a ripple.

The people are the ones who keep the water down. There are perhaps forty of them, and they live in the pump houses and in a row of prefabricated barracks along the rim, sleeping in shifts that rotate every eleven hours so that the pumps are never unattended. They are not a clan or a faction, though they have the look of one. Their hands are stained orange-brown to the second knuckle from the iron in the well water, and the stain doesn't wash out. They wear heavy waterproofs over layers of wool, and their boots are always wet, and they have stopped noticing the smell of diesel and wet concrete the way other people stop noticing their own accents. They carry long steel probes in place of walking sticks, and they test the causeways with them before they step, tapping the slab the way a blind person taps a curb. When they meet on the walkways they do not shake hands. They touch the railings, lightly, one after another, the way you might touch a doorframe on your way out of a house you intend to return to.

They listen constantly. Most of them can name the pumps by their sound alone: the bass of the old diesel unit in the east house, the nervous electric whine of the number-six motor that runs hot in summer, the particular hiss of a relief valve that is about to stick. But what they listen for most is the breathing. It begins as a change in the pitch of the water, a slight drop, like a held note losing its footing. The sumps start to rise, a few centimetres over an hour, and then fall again. The keepers call the rising phase the inhale and the falling phase the exhale, though none of them will say who taught them the words. They adjust the pumps before the inhale, not during it, so that the intake matches the swell, and afterward they walk the causeways with their probes and mark the places where the slab has ticked upward or the water has found a new seam. They keep a logbook of the breathing that runs back through several generations of crews, and the handwriting changes but the entries are always the same shape: time, depth, duration, a note on temperature.

The breathing itself is the thing the others orbit. It has no body that anyone has seen. But it lives in the warm concrete, in the sections where the heat is strongest and the lichen grows in those geometric lattices that follow the reinforcement bars. If the bars are the nerves, then the lichen is where the signal shows through the skin. Put a hand flat on one of those warm slabs and you feel a faint pulse, not quite a heartbeat, slower and more irregular, that rises and falls in step with the sumps. The keepers do not touch the warm slabs unless they must. When they do, they keep their palms on the surface for only a moment, the way you might test the heat of an iron before you press it to a shirt.

It does not seem hostile. That is what unsettles the newer crew members most. It does not flood the causeways out of spite or appetite. It simply remembers what it was before the basin was dug, a sea that was pushed down and walled in and given a grid of straight lines to lie within, and it keeps trying, patiently, to finish the return. The pumps argue with it. The keepers argue with it in their own way, through the logbook and the probes and the hours they spend listening. The fish have made their peace with it. The scavenger has simply moved into the warm places where its work is easiest.

At night, when the lamps are the only light and the amber haze lies thick over the water, the keepers sit in the pump houses with the doors open to let the heat out, and they listen. Someone will say, quietly, that it's starting. Someone else will reach for the valve wheel. For a few minutes nobody speaks, and the whole basin seems to hold its breath alongside the water, the machines thudding, the drip falling, the sump surfaces lifting by a fraction toward the rim. Then the note drops, the exhale begins, and the keepers let their breath go with it.
