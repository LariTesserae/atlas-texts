# What Lives in the Planed Highlands

Nothing here has a body you could point to, though something is always present. It is best described by what it does to the place.

## The Patience

Call it the Patience, since that is the closest word the travelers have found. It is not a single creature. It is a slow attention distributed across the plateau, expressed through moss, water, lichen, and stone. Where the Patience attends, things soften. Where it looks away, things settle. The rounding of the ridges, the polishing of the granite, the fractures filled with peat: these are its handwriting, and it has been writing for so long that the writing has become the land.

Its mind moves at a pace that makes a human lifetime look like a single flicker. A decision might take a season. A thought might take a century to finish. This is why moss grows visibly for a few hours and then stops, as if it has reached a conclusion. The moss did not stop. The Patience paused over it and went on to other considerations.

## The Scribing

The lichen spirals on the standing stones are its script. They climb in slow, looping lines, and the loops can be read if you are willing to stand still long enough, which most people are not. The shapes repeat, vary, and repeat again. The clay tablets in the ruins are older attempts by the builders to copy the spirals, and they came close enough to be unsettling. The lines that almost mean something are almost the Patience's sentences, copied by people who were trying to learn its grammar.

The inscriptions that shift letters at dusk are the Patience revising. It does not carve. It edits, slowly, the way erosion edits a cliff face, and when it changes its mind it reverts the change in the morning, as if testing whether the revision holds. Most of its drafts do not hold. The sentence it is writing has been under construction longer than the cities have existed, and the cities themselves may be footnotes.

## The Ones Who Stayed

The builders did not vanish. They were absorbed.

The layered cities tell the story if you walk down through them. The oldest foundations are the most carefully fitted, the blocks shaped with more patience than the later ones, as though the first builders had time to spare. Over generations the grids rotate against one another, one city's streets aligned to a star that the next city ignored. The builders kept rebuilding because they were trying to make the plateau legible to the thing that read it. A city laid out correctly might be understood. A city laid out wrong might be ignored, and ignored things are the ones the Patience lets go.

The builders are in the pools now. Look into the mirror-water and the reflected sky is wrong because it is the sky the builders remembered, or the sky the Patience reconstructed from them. The stars in an unfamiliar arrangement are a constellation from a sky that has since turned. The green plateau in another pool is the plateau as it was before the rain wore it grey. The builders are present as reflections, as stored skies, as a kind of memory the Patience keeps on the surface of the still water.

Their presence is not hostile, and not particularly kind. They have been folded into the long sentence. A person who lingers near the pools may sense them the way you sense someone standing behind you in a quiet room: a faint pressure, a shift in the light, a feeling of being measured against something older than measurement.

## The Lag

The shadows that trail their objects by half a second are the Patience's slowness made visible. Everything that moves on the plateau is moving through a medium that is thinking more slowly than it moves. Light, water, and bodies all arrive a little late to the Patience's notice. Some of the strangeness of the place comes from this delay: streets that are longer on the way out, rain that rises against the wind, water that briefly runs backward in the courtyard channels. These are moments when the Patience's attention falls onto a particular place and the local physics catches up to its intentions before correcting.

The tilted terraces may be the same thing, a half-finished thought about level ground. The body feels the slope because the Patience once intended one and then changed its mind, and some residue of the intention remains in the stone.

## The Bell

The deep sound from far away, like a stone dragged over stone or a bell struck underwater, is the closest the Patience comes to speaking aloud. It is probably not meant for travelers. It may be the sound of a thought being completed, or of a sentence turning a corner, or of the ridges settling under a weight that has been accumulating since before the first builder set down a hammer.

Those who have heard it describe it the same way: not as a sound that comes from somewhere, but as a sound that comes from the ground under the sound, as if the plateau were clearing its throat before a long reply.

## Its Regard for Us

The Patience does not seem to notice visitors the way visitors notice it. A human who walks the avenues is brief, a fleeting disturbance, a few steps of warmth on the cold granite that the Patience registers slowly and forgets slowly. A traveler might be in the highlands for a week. The Patience might take a week to decide the traveler was there.

Sometimes it attends to a person for longer, and then the signs are clear: the moss grows toward their boots, the lichen spirals begin to echo the pattern of their footprints, the mirror-pools show a sky that holds their face. Those who experience this are rarely harmed. They are, rather, incorporated, briefly, into the sentence, and then released when the Patience moves its attention elsewhere.

The ones who stay too long are the ones who become reflections. The builders did not leave. They are waiting, in the water, for the Patience to finish its sentence, and when it does, they will be part of the last line.

The land is still writing. It has all the time in the world, and it has been using it on you.
