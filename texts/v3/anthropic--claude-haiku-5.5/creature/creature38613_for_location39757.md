# What Lives in the Cut

The thing that lives here is one thing. It only looks like many things from the rim.

From above, you see pale ground, scattered standing growths, and pools like dropped mirrors. Walk down and the separations blur. The mat under your boots is continuous with the stalks, the stalks with the hummocks, and the hummocks with the pools they bulge around. Its body is the whole floor of the basin, two kilometres of it, spread over the rock and the fallen slabs and the rusted hoppers like a sheet thrown over furniture. Nobody has ever found where it ends, because it doesn't end. It only thins at the walls, where the shale is too steep to hold it, and resumes wherever the rock lets it.

It is slow in the way that mountains are slow, and its attention is not on the things that move quickly. A beetle crossing it registers as a faint itch. A footstep registers as a slight give and then a pressure that lingers. A pump's stroke comes through as a regular pulse, and over decades the mat has learned that pulse so thoroughly that its growth follows it, thickening in the hollows where the water is pushed and thinning where the pressure fails. It knows the rhythm of the engines the way a sleeper knows the sound of a clock, though only in the sense that everything it does is arranged around that rhythm.

Its blood is the water. Meltwater comes down the walls in threads, and the mat drinks through its whole body, storing it in the spongy matrix beneath the surface, filtering it through its tissues until it comes out clear and tasting of stone and iron. The taste is the mat's own. It is the flavour of rock being slowly dissolved and taken up into a living body.

It does not hate the machines. It has no reason to, and the machines have been here long enough that they are part of the terrain. It takes them the way it takes everything, by covering them. The gantry legs are climbed slowly, a few centimetres a season. The hopper is filled from within, the pipes wrapped, the tank rims smoothed over until only their outlines remain. A stopped engine is not an enemy but an offering, a body to be absorbed. The mat seems to want the machines to finish their work, to become ground, and it has no way to distinguish a machine that is running from one that has stopped except through the pulse, and the pulse is what keeps it from going further. Where the pumps are strong, the mat holds back. Where the pumps falter, it advances. It is, in a sense, waiting for the engines to die.

The standing growths are its raised parts, the places where it lifts itself into the air. They are sparse, each one a hundred metres from its neighbours, and they grow only a few centimetres a year, so a stalk that is four metres tall has been reaching for perhaps a century. Their fronds carry tiny brittle plates that knock together in the wind, and in the quiet between engines, their dry chatter is the closest thing the organism has to a voice. Whether it is speaking or simply shaking, nobody knows. The sound carries across the basin in a way that feels deliberate, and people who work here have learned to hear it as a kind of attention: the Cut noticing that someone is near.

The pools are its eyes, in a loose sense. They are the places where the surface flattens and the living skin becomes a mirror. Looking into them, you see the sky and yourself dimly reflected over a grey-green depth. They are the only places where the organism presents a smooth face, and they are where it gathers its water and its light.

Smaller things move across it and within it. The pale beetles graze its surface in the mornings, leaving thin tracks, nibbling at the cushion mosses and the lichens along the seams. They are like the small private thoughts of a large body, quick and local and always returning to the same ground. The flies over the pools are the same. The long-legged wader that stalks the shallows is different. It stands at the boundary between water and mat, watching the things under the surface with a patience the machines do not disturb. It seems to belong to the Cut in a way the workers do not, and it has been seen in the same shallows year after year, so some of the people who scrape and cut here have begun to think of it as a kind of caretaker, or as a witness. The birds that cross the basin in the cool months do not land, and the mammals along the rim leave only traces. They are visitors, and the Cut does not much mind visitors.

The people are the strangest part of the arrangement. They live on the plain in their low huts with their doors turned away from the basin, and they come down into the Cut to scrape the mat from the iron stairs, to cut the tracks of the haul roads free, to rebuild the pumps when their pressure drops too far. They are not the Cut's owners or its enemies. They are its keepers, and their work is a long negotiation conducted in the only language either party speaks, which is pressure and time. Each season they hold the mat back from one more machine. Each season it presses forward again, slowly, patiently, and they cut it back. Neither side wins. Both have been doing this so long that the arrangement itself has become a kind of creature, a shared habit that no single party can end on its own.

So if you ask what lives in the Sunken Cut, the honest answer is not any single being. It is the mat, slow and continuous, drinking the water and taking in the machines. It is the standing growths, sparse and rattling, its raised and listening parts. It is the small grazers and the patient wader, the visiting birds and the shy mammals at the rim. And it is the people on the plain, who come down each season to keep the pumps running, holding open a wound that the land is trying to close. Together they make a place that is slowly being absorbed by its own growth, and that is still, for now, a place where something is being kept.
