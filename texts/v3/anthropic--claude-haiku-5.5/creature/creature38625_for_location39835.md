# The Thing That Holds the Plateau

It is not a collection of plants. The reeds, mats, vines, and spore-beds are the visible surfaces of one organism that has spread across the whole tableland, the way a fungus spreads through a log until the log is only its shape. Its stalks are upright growths that push through the mist. Its mats are a skin that drinks and swells. Its roots are threads in the black silt, carrying water and signal. Its spores are a kind of voice and memory, lifted into the fog and settled where they are needed. There is no center to it. Cut a stalk and the cut does not bleed, because there is nowhere inside that the cut could reach. The plateau is a single body that has been growing for a very long time.

It perceives through water. Pressure in the basins tells it when the tide is rising and when it is falling. The swelling of streams tells it that a front is coming across the eastern ridges, and it knows the front by its weight long before the rain arrives. The cold air moving through the stalks is a kind of touch, and the fungal glow along the banks is a slow flicker, a sign of which parts are awake and which are resting. It does not see in any sense a person would recognize. It feels the land's moisture as a whole, the way a body feels its own warmth, and it knows the shape of its own extent by where the water stands.

Its thinking is slow. A decision takes a tide. When a basin rises, roots push outward into the newly wet silt, and that outward push is a kind of thought: *here, more*. When the water drops, the mats curl and withdraw, and that withdrawal is thought too: *not here, not now*. Growth directions, the lean of a stalk toward light it can barely find, the density of a mat in one hollow and its absence in the next, these are the arguments of a mind that works over weeks and seasons. Nothing it decides is quick, and nothing it forgets is lost, because forgetting is only burial. Whatever sinks into the silt becomes layered record. The plateau's memory is the stratigraphy of its own dissolving.

It has no need for animals, and there is a logic in their absence. Animals move, and motion is a kind of noise that the organism cannot easily hold. Its life is made of stillness and slow rearrangement. Anything that would scurry, burrow, or chew would be a foreign intelligence on its skin, and the plateau's growth has long since filled every niche that such creatures might have claimed. The silence is not emptiness. It is completeness, a body with no parasites in it, or perhaps with all of them already digested.

It has taken in the people. This is the hardest thing to say about it, because the taking was not an act. The cairns were stacked on stone that the mats later claimed. The platforms were lashed from reed and timber that the stalks later grew through. The people who built them were part of a rhythm that the plateau also kept: the tides they watched, the stone they carried, the water that rose under their feet. When they left or died, the rhythm kept going, and the plateau absorbed their marks as it absorbs everything, as texture, as nutrient, as a new surface to grow across.

Yet the plateau remembers them in its own way. Some of the stalks grow in lines that follow the old platform foundations, not because they were planted there but because the silt beneath those lines holds more water and more of something the people once left behind. The moss grows thickest on the cairns that were stacked tallest, and the spores settle most densely around the places where a blade or a stake was left. These are not memories in the human sense. They are preferences, tendencies shaped by ancient disturbances, the way a scar keeps its shape in a body that has forgotten the wound. The plateau has no idea that a cairn was a grave or a marker or a warning. It knows only that this stone holds water differently from that one, and it grows accordingly.

If it has a self, that self is a tide. It rises through the basins, pulls back, and rises again, and in every rise it reshapes its own boundaries. It is not aware of itself as one thing, but its extent is always being renegotiated with the water, and the water decides how far it goes. When the flood comes, it stands waist-deep in its own body. When the flood recedes, it lies exposed in cracked grey plates and waits.

The fronts roll in from the east. The streams swell. The vents hiss and boom. The stalks knock together in the cold wind, and the drip, drip, drip of water through the mats runs under all of it like a pulse. The plateau breathes its slow, wet, dark breath, and the people's stones rise and sink with it, green with moss, leaning, held, and quietly digested.
