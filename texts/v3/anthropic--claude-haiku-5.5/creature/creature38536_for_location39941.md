The first things you notice are the beetles, because they move in columns and the columns are hard to miss. Each one is about the length of a thumb, with a shell that has no pigment at all, only a glassy translucence through which you can see the dark knot of the gut and, when the light angles right, a faint pulse in the body behind the head. They walk in single file along the vine cables, antennae sweeping ahead, tapping every stem at intervals. Where a stem has been wounded, sap beads out, and each beetle stops, lowers its head, and drinks, then lifts and taps again. Their wing-cases are fused shut. They have not flown in generations, or perhaps they never needed to. Some carry grains of salt on their backs in neat piles, and you cannot tell whether this is building or just a way of carrying the mineral they are made of.

Lower down, on the terraces and the cracked white plains, the crawlers hunt. They are long-legged and pale, legs jointed in too many places, bodies flattened so they can slide between the rigid coral mats without catching. Their color matches the halite so closely that you see them only when they move, and they move in short, decisive bursts. One will freeze with a leg lifted, a fleck of salt on its tarsus, until a beetle wanders within reach; then it closes, fast and almost silent, and the column breaks, closes, and resumes. Their mouths are plain pincers folded beneath the head, and when they feed the sound is a tiny dry crackle, like someone breaking a biscuit very slowly. The ones that have just eaten rest with their legs tucked under them and do not respond to touch for a long while. The ones that have not eaten are restless, always sampling the air with their front pairs.

The larger creatures are the ones you keep mistaking for debris. They move between the girders in slow, deliberate arcs, segmented bodies hanging beneath a fan of papery appendages that catch the air currents from the rising warmth of the lamps. The fans are translucent, veined like old leaves, and they flex when the creature turns, so that from a distance each one looks like a small sail being reefed and unreefed. They feed on the fallen flakes, scraping them from the girder tops and the tilted shelves with a tuft of bristles near the mouth, and their droppings fall as pale grit that the crawlers and beetles soon reclaim. Over the years, I think, a single one may spend its whole life within a few girders of the same gantry. You can see the worn places on the steel where they have rested, the paint long gone and the metal polished smooth in the shape of a body.

The vines are the oldest residents, and in some sense the ones that decided the rest. They are not gardens in the sense of something planted; they are what happens when a root finds a crack and keeps going. Each stem is a braided cable of tissue, and the salt crust on it is not just protection but a kind of shared armor. Where a beetle has tapped a wound, the plant does not seal it but thickens around it, so over time the tapped stems grow knotted and swollen, like wrists bent by old injury. The vines communicate, or at least respond to one another: when a crawler strikes a beetle on one cable, the nearby stems tighten briefly, a shiver that runs up the lattice and dies out. You can feel it if you touch the frame, a faint tremor under the skin.

The mats are stranger still. Those on the soft shelves are velvet to the touch and warm faintly from the sun-lamps, and in them you find fine, threadlike filaments that seem to pass nutrients through a slow network. The rigid coral mats are different. They are colonies, each branch a separate individual fused into a single structure, and when struck they ring with a pitch that drops as the blow travels down the branches. In the dead ones the sound is flat. In the living ones it carries a kind of harmony, several pitches sounding at once, as if the colony is answering itself. Some of the crystalline needles growing from the floor seem to rise from the coral's bases, and I suspect the coral is somehow guiding their growth, drawing salt up through its structure and laying it down in long clear spines.

Then there are the things that live in the machines. Lichen furs the dials, but under the lichen, in the hollows of the gears, small pale creatures gather where the metal still ticks with heat. They are no bigger than seeds. They come out at night when the generator's hum drops and the cavern cools, and they feed on the warmth itself, or on the traces of oil that still seep from the bearings. When a machine goes quiet for a while, they move on, and you find the dial clean of them, as if the place has stopped breathing in that one spot.

Everything here is turning. The beetles drink and die and leave their shells to the crawlers, and the crawlers' bodies become soil under the mats. The fanned creatures drop grit that feeds the vines. The vines hold the salt and the salt holds the heat and the heat keeps the machines ticking, which keeps the seed-things alive. When a shelf groans and sheds flakes, the flakes are picked up and carried; when a beetle molts, the empty shell is ground into the white ground by the next column walking over it. Nothing waits for the dead to rot, because nothing here has ever been wet enough to rot. The salt preserves, and the living things learn to use what it preserves.

And yet the place is not finished. The newest needles on the floor are still soft at their tips, still clear and unstained, and the crawlers step around them carefully, as if they know what they are. The beetles have not yet found a way to cross the open plains, and the vines stop at the edge of the rails, where the iron is too hard even for their roots to split. Something is being built here, slowly, by the animals and the plants and the dead machinery and the salt itself, and you cannot yet see its shape. The lamps keep burning. The beetles keep tapping. Somewhere deep below, the generator hums on, and a shelf of salt shifts with a long, patient sound, and the flakes begin to fall again.
