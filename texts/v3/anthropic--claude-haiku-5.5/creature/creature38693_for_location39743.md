# Those Who Keep It Level

The people who live on the shelf are the first thing you notice when you climb the stepped streets, and they are often the last thing you can see. Fog takes them. You hear a hammer on slate two terraces up, a woman's voice calling a count, the scrape of a wedge being driven, and only when she steps into the gap between two walls do you see that she is close enough to touch.

They are compact and broad-shouldered, built for stone and weather. Their hands are the most striking feature: thick through the palm, knuckles swollen from years of levering blocks, fingertips grained with small white scars where basalt has bitten them. They wear wool that has been oiled with rendered fat and dyed with lichen, so their clothes take on the colors of the walls they work on, orange and grey and a mossy yellow-green. A person sitting still on a terrace can disappear. Children learn early to stand in the open when they want to be seen.

## Voices in the Fog

Because sound dies in the cloud, the people have built their speech around distance. Their voices are low and carrying, pitched to travel along a wall rather than across an open room. Across the terraces they use call patterns: a rising two-note hail for *come here*, a falling three for *the stone is slipping*, a repeated knock on a hollow block for *stop, I am under this*. A crew can work an entire district by sound alone, each person answering the count of the next. Strangers who arrive from the lower valleys often find this unnerving, the way voices come from nowhere and then resolve into a face.

They are not a quiet people by habit, though. Evenings in the lower terraces are full of talk, often repetitive, mostly about weather, the state of thresholds, and who has seen the herds moving. They laugh easily, and their laughter has a flat, rolling quality that carries well over stone.

## Work Measured in Fractions

The work is slow, and the people are patient with it in a way that is almost physical. A leveler spends her days kneeling beside a threshold with a bubble-tube of water and a slate wedge, checking whether the sill has tipped another fraction toward the valley. She does not fix it in a day. She shims it a hair, waits a season, checks it again, and writes the reading on a tally-stone kept in a niche by the door. A good leveler can tell you the age of any doorway in the district by its tilt and the number of wedges beneath it.

Others cut stone. Quarry-workers split basalt along its natural plates with wooden wedges soaked in water that swell and crack the rock. They work in pairs and sing to keep time. The songs are long and have no real ending; they loop until the block gives way. The cutters know which veins split cleanly and which crumble, and they guard their knowledge the way herders guard water.

The tarn-keepers are a smaller, quieter group. They walk the shelf in the wet months, noting where the channels run, where a boulder has shifted, where a tarn has drained or a new one has begun to gather. They keep maps scratched into flat stones, and their maps are always out of date, because the ground never holds still. Still, they are proud of their accuracy. Ask a tarn-keeper how old a pool is and she will tell you to the season, and she will be right.

## Time Written Downward

The people do not count their lives in years so much as in re-levelings. A child is told that the house she was born in has been reset three times since her birth, that the lower wall of her grandmother's room is now buried under a new terrace, that the threshold she stepped across as a toddler is now two fingers lower than it was. Their sense of history is vertical. They read the city like a cut through earth, each layer a generation of corrections.

This means the dead are built into the place. When a person dies, the district does not bury them in the scree, which would be too cold and too hard to dig. Instead, the community places the body in a shallow cell beneath a new foundation, and the terrace is raised over it. The lower courses of the city are full of the people who built the upper ones. When the ground lifts and the stone must be re-cut, the crews sometimes open these cells, and the work stops for a day while the bones are reburied with the same care. The people say the earth is slowly carrying their ancestors upward, and that they are part of the lift.

## Neighbors with Prior Claim

The people share the city with its animals, and they have learned to defer to them in ways that look, from outside, like generosity and feel to the people like ordinary courtesy.

The seabirds are the most assertive tenants. Each spring, the stout black colony takes over the parapets, and the people step around their nests in the streets. Roof repairs are scheduled for the autumn and winter. A household that has to move a nest will do it at dusk and leave a flat stone beside it for the parents. Their droppings are scraped from the walls and carried downhill to the lichen gardens, where they are said to make the rock grow faster. The people do not know if this is true. They do it anyway.

The herds are different. Herders walk with the grazers across the open shelf, and they know the animals' moods the way a farmer knows weather. They bring the herds to the lower walls during storms and leave gaps in the stone fences so the animals can shelter in the lee. The herders speak to the grazers in low, unhurried tones and rarely touch them. They say the herds remember which tarns are safe.

The whistlers unsettle the people more than anyone admits. The little grey-brown burrowers sit upright on boulders at dawn and watch the terraces with an attention that feels like judgment. The people nod to them as they pass. When whistlers move into an abandoned house at the edge of the settlement, filling its rooms with dry grass and bone, the people leave it alone. Nobody moves back into a house that has been taken by whistlers. Eventually the walls simply join the scree, and the stone is quietly harvested by the next crew to need it.

## The Slowest Residents

The oldest inhabitants of the shelf are the lichens. They cover the walls in orange and grey and bruised yellow-green, growing so slowly that a single disk may have been there before the oldest standing terrace was laid. The people treat them with a reverence they rarely express in words. A quarry-cutter will not split a block from a face where the lichens have thickened into a crust, because the crust is a record. The leveler reads the lichen as well as the threshold, and a band of fresh growth on a wall tells her where the ground has been moving.

The cushion plants and tussocks are more immediately present, and the people use them. The woody domes provide fuel. The tussocks are woven into roof thatch. In the brief summer, when the cushions flower in small pale stars, the people climb to the open shelf and sit among them for an afternoon. It is one of the few times they are seen to rest.

## The Argument

What the people are, finally, is a kind of answer to the rising ground. They do not fight the lift, and they do not pretend it is not happening. They keep pace with it, one wedge and one tally-mark at a time. They build in stone that can be taken apart and reset, and they bury their dead where the earth will carry them upward. They share their cliffs with birds and their streets with herds and their ruined houses with burrowing things that watch from the boulders.

On the clearest afternoons, when the cloud breaks for an hour and the terraces show gold in the low sun, you can see them moving across the city in their lichen-colored wool, calling to one another across the gaps, checking the thresholds, leaving stones for the birds. They are slow, patient, and fully awake to the place they live in. They are the kind of people who know that the ground will never settle, and who have decided, without ever saying so, that this is a reason to keep paying attention.
