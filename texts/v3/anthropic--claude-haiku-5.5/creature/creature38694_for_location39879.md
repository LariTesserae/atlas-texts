The basin is not empty, but the people who live in it are hard to see. You learn them first by what they have arranged.

Most of what moves here is the herd. The fleece-bodied grazers spread across the moss in small loose bands, each animal facing outward so that a boulder is always between its flank and the slope. Their paths are worn into the mat in the exact shape of their bodies, and the bands seem to know one another by a language of stillness and sudden bursts. They are not wild in the way the cliffs are wild. The wear on their trails runs in lines that bend around the springhead and the cairns, as if they were herded there long ago and have kept the route since, and when a band crests a rise and sees a figure on the far skyline, it does not scatter. It turns, lowers its head, and waits.

The people are a seasonal band, or perhaps a few bands that take turns. They come up with the thaw, when the cloud is thin and the sun clears the eastern rim before noon, and they leave before the first hard frost splits the faces. They are light on their feet on the scree, which tells you something about their bones and their habits, and their clothing is grey-green fleece dyed from the moss itself, so that a person standing still against a boulder is indistinguishable from the stone. They move quietly because the moss takes their footsteps. They speak rarely and softly. When they do speak, the sound goes flat and short, as if the air were too thin to hold it.

Their work is the cairns. They rebuild what the weather and the grazers knock down, setting fresh flat slabs among the old ones so that the new stone shows pale and the old stone shows dark, a record of every repair. They keep the roofless house on the terrace clean, sweeping the floor each thaw, though they do not sleep in it. They sleep in the rings. A person who settles for the night in the lee of a large boulder is wrapped in the same pale radial shrubs that lean toward it, and the shrubs, in turn, seem to lean toward the sleeper. It is hard to tell whether the people planted the rings or whether the rings drew the people, and the question seems to bother no one who lives there. Warmth is warmth, and both the shrubs and the people face it.

The hunter is a separate matter. It is long-limbed and lean, low to the scree at dawn, and the people do not hunt it, nor it them. The arrangement is older than memory. The herd keeps its faces outward, the hunter keeps its patience, and the people keep the grazing paths open so that neither side is forced into the open too often. The bones in the scree are the price of that balance, and the people gather them and carry them to the foot of the cairns, where they are stacked in small heaps and left to the weather. Whether this is burial or offering or simple tidiness, I could not say.

The birds ride the updrafts off the cliffs and call their falling note across the water. They circle the cairns more often than the cliffs, and the people look up when the calls come, not with alarm, but the way a person looks up when a familiar name is spoken across a room. The insects hatch in the warm hours and die by dusk, and the people brush them from their hair without breaking their stride. The fat slow salamander moves only after rain, and the people leave the wet places alone after rain for days.

The flowers are the people's clock as much as the sun's. Each bloom turns to the arc of the light and closes with the cloud, and the people plant their cairns and their rings and their sleeping places along the same arc. Walk the basin at midday and everything faces one way: flowers, shrubs, the carved stone by the springhead with its worn face, the cairns climbing toward the western pass, the swept floor of the house, the doorway that faces downhill toward the spring. Everything points at the same moving point of light.

The carved stone is the oldest thing here that anyone can name, and nobody names it. Its face has worn almost to nothing. Its orientation is correct. The people touch its top when they fill their skins at the springhead, a brief hand laid flat and then lifted, the way one might touch a doorframe on the way out of a house.

The tools are few and used with care. The notched stave leans against the nearest cairn where its owner will find it again. The knapped blade in the boulder crack is left where it can be retrieved, sharp, because whoever set it there expects to come back for it. Nothing is discarded. Everything is returned to the place it came from, which is how the basin keeps itself.

They are not here now, or they are here in the way that a held breath is here. The house is swept. The cairns are fresh. The rings lean in, empty, toward the warmth of the rocks where their sleepers lay. And the land goes on with its cycle of fog and sun and thaw and hunt, the herd facing outward, the hunter waiting, the birds calling the falling note over the water, while the people who arranged all of it keep to the cloud and the moss and come back, each thaw, to take up again the shape they left.
