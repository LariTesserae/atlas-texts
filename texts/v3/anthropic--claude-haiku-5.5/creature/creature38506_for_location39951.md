**What Lives on the Seep Shelf**

The thing that lives here is the mat, and the mat is not one plant. It is a single slow body spread across the whole upland, and it is also the lattice-trees, the fused arches and bridges, and the vines that have climbed the steel ladder and the valve wheel. Where the mat has swallowed a footing or a conveyor, it has not destroyed the machine so much as taken it into itself. The body has no edge. The closest thing to a center is wherever the water pools deepest, and the center moves as the water does.

It thinks slowly, and its thinking is made of water. A thirsty stretch of mat draws moisture up through a hundred thousand fine roots, and when a pressure changes somewhere across the plateau, the change travels through the body as a wave, a gradual swelling or tightening of the fronds. Its attention is a gradient. It notices where the ground is wetter and where it is drying, which seeps are rising and which are failing, which stone faces are bare and open to the light. You are not something it watches directly. You register on it the way a rain cloud registers on a field, as a change in the local weather, a disturbance in the wet. Your boot prints fill with water because the mat is always reaching toward whatever moves on it, and it offers its moisture to you the way a hand opens without quite deciding to.

Its sense of time is measured in seasons and centuries, not the minutes you live in. The gullies on the eastern rim are its own handwriting, slowly carved as it lets the meltwater find the weakest seams. The scree fields are the debris of its patient argument with the cliffs. It is making ground. Where meltwater cuts through the mat and reaches bare rock, the grey paste of ground stone and rotting fronds is its first attempt at soil, and the tiny burrowing things in that paste are its tenants and its gut-life, the small workers that break down what it cannot digest and carry minerals up into its roots. You almost never see them, but they are as much a part of it as the fronds are.

The machines were built to move water, and it has learned to move water too. Its roots are stitched into the penstocks, and its vines run through the old standpipes like veins through a limb. The turbines turn on a schedule no visible hand keeps, because the schedule belongs to the body: it opens and closes the flow according to its own needs, drinking deeply when the ground is dry, letting the pressure rest when the seeps are full. The booms of the penstocks are its heartbeat, or something closer to a cough, a clearing of the throat that rolls across the plateau and bounces off the cliffs. The pump station to the northwest sends its rhythmic throb through the same ground, and you can think of that pulse as a deeper rhythm, the breathing of the whole upland, drawn up from the soaked earth and exhaled as fog.

It has no eyes, but it has a kind of attention that is wet and wide. It senses light through its fronds, and the glare you find so harsh is something it experiences as warmth on a wide, open surface, the sun drinking through its condensation. It does not seem to mind the glare. It is built for it. The bare, streaming stone between its cushions is not wasteland to it but open ground, places where it has not yet grown, where it is still reaching.

The birds are its messengers and seed-carriers, rare and brief, crossing its lattices and vanishing into them. The insects are its small, cold, motionless thoughts, clinging to a wet frond and waiting for warmth. The larger animals come to its water at the edges, leave their tracks in the mud, and go on. It takes what they leave, and it lets them go. None of this is hostile. It is simply how a body that has no mouth feeds itself: by letting the world come to the water.

The steel post with the faded arrow points at nothing because the thing you are looking for is the body itself, and it is everywhere at once, too wide and too slow to be a destination. You cannot walk to its heart. You can only walk across it, feeling it give under each step and sigh back, and understand that the plateau is not a place where something lives. The plateau is the living thing, still assembling its own skeleton, still deciding what shape its ground will take, in no particular hurry at all.
