**The Ones Who Stand Where the Walls Were**

At first you take them for another heap of silt. Something stands at the edge of a terrace, half a head taller than a man, and its outline is so soft in the haze that it could be a bank that has risen on its own. Then it shifts its weight, and the silt on one side slumps and falls away, and you see that the shape has been holding itself upright on purpose.

They are built of the plain. Their bodies are the same grey-brown clay as the ground and the walls, pressed and layered in courses that are never quite level, as if someone set them by eye. Up close the layers show their seams: a band of fine pale mineral like the bloom on old brick, a darker stripe of ground silt, a thin streak where water once ran through and dried. The surface of each one beads constantly, so they always look freshly wet, and where they touch a wall the two surfaces seem to lean into each other and blur.

They have a hollow at the center of the torso, a cistern-shaped cavity that holds water. You can hear it when they move, a slow slosh and tick, the same rhythm as the drips in the pans. Some of them carry more water than others. The ones with full cores move carefully, bent a little forward, the way a person carries a pot too full to spill.

Their hands are the most finished part of them. Each finger bears ridges like the thumbprints pressed into the plaster, and the palms are scored with the fine parallel lines of an adze. When they touch a wall they seem to be reading it, sliding their fingers along the joints, pressing and lifting, the way you might test a loaf. When they find a brick that has been carried off into a silt bank, they dig it out with patient, wet strokes, turn it over, and set it back into a course. They do not measure. They tilt their heads, close one eye of their faceless surface, and lay the brick where it looks right. Then they go on to the next gap. By dusk the wall stands again, and nothing in the wall shows that anything was done to it.

They also pull walls down. This is the part that is hard to watch. A standing one of them will lean a shoulder against a course that is still good, feel for the seam where the mortar has gone soft, and push. It does not seem angry or sad. It seems to be doing what the hands want to do, the way a river goes downhill. The bricks slide, the bank groans, and the thing steps back and watches the collapse with the same attention it gave the building. Then it turns away and, after a while, begins to gather the fallen bricks into a pile.

You stop trying to decide which of these it is doing. It does not seem to decide either. Making and unmaking appear to come from the same place in them, a single habit of the hands that the plain has never trained into anything else.

They do not seek company. Two of them may pass within a few meters across a pan, and they will not look at each other, but they will each stop, lift a hand to the nearest wall, and tap it three times, slowly. Sound carries strangely in the haze, and the taps seem to arrive from underfoot, so it's hard to know whether they are signals or only the sound of hands meeting stone. Later you find that the wall between them has a new course in it, set by one and finished by the other, and that neither ever stood close enough to touch it while it was being laid.

They come from the silt. Where a wall has sheared along a line nobody planned, a figure may rise out of the disturbed ground, first as a seep of water, then as a mound, then as something that stands up and shakes the clay from its shoulders. You never see the moment of their beginning. They are always already standing, already reaching for the nearest brick. They end the same way. A figure may lean too long against a failing course and stay there while the rain beads on its back, and then it sinks, slowly, into the soft ground, and a shallow pan opens where it stood. The pan holds still water for a week. Sometimes, when the haze lifts, the water shows a sky that is not the sky overhead, and in that reflection a figure can be seen standing, very patient, with its hands still pressed flat against a wall that is not there.

The glass knobs hum when they pass. The violet mats spread a little wider in the damp they leave behind. Nothing else in the plain seems to notice them, and they notice nothing else but walls. Their faces, if they can be said to have faces, are smooth and unmarked until the water on them shifts in the light and makes a brief pattern, something almost like a set of signs, and then the pattern runs off and the surface is blank again.

Watching them, you begin to suspect they are not the makers. The makers left, and their marks repeat at regular intervals across the plain, and nobody reads them. These figures do not read them either. But they carry the marks in their hands, and they press those marks into every wall they touch, the same ridges, the same adze strokes, the same thumbprints. They are what is left of the work once the workers are gone: a habit of the hands, continuing in the wet, building and tearing down and building again, with no end in view.

The drip keeps time. A wall gives a long low groan somewhere out in the mist. One of the figures turns toward the sound, lifts its head, and then goes on across the silt, carrying a brick it found in a bank, toward a gap that will be a wall by dusk and a heap of bricks again by morning.
