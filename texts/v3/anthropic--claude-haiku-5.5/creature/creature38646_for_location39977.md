# Who Lives in the Sump

The first thing that lives here is the water, though it is not quite a creature. It arrives from the air each night and from the ground all day, and the basin's inhabitants hold it the way a body holds blood. The beetles drink it from the clay, the crustaceans breathe it in the brine pool, the birds bathe in the condensate on the tanks, and the reeds draw it up through the concrete. Nothing here lives far from it. Everything that stays is a way of keeping some of it from leaving.

## The Shimmer-Beetles

They are the most numerous, and their numbers feel like a kind of weather. Each is about the length of a fingernail, with a carapace that flashes bronze, violet, and a green like oxidized copper depending on the angle of the sun. They spend the cool hours packed into the wet clay at the bottoms of the plates, moving slowly, feeding on the film of organic matter the condensate leaves behind. As the morning heat climbs they rise in a shimmering column, a cloud that turns in the air like a loose sheet of foil, and they settle again at the edge of the shade. They never stay in one place for long. They are constantly ferrying the basin's moisture from the floor into the air and back, and when they die their bodies sink into the clay and become part of the wet layer that the works' slurry left behind.

## The Grey-Wings

The swallow-like birds are more deliberate. They have long, narrow wings the color of wet slate, and they are never still while they are awake. Their nests are built from clay and salt, sealed with saliva, and packed into every enclosed space the works left: the pump housing, the pipe interiors, the seams of the steel shelters. The entrances form dense dark honeycombs. Traffic in and out is constant, and the birds seem to know one another by a sound they make at the threshold, a dry click repeated twice.

Their timing is unusual. They leave and return in rhythm with the slow creaking of the pump housing, though the housing has no power and no moving parts that should move. At dusk the pipes give out their brief low tone, and every grey-wing in earshot answers it with a single note before falling quiet. The birds have, in some sense, adopted the machinery. They roost inside it, time their lives to it, and call back to it. If you stand by the pipe at evening and listen, you hear the basin's one ritual performed by its tenants, though no one has ever shown them how.

## The Brine Crustaceans

In the permanent pool at the north seep live small, translucent crustaceans, each the size of a grain of rice. They are so dense that the water appears to move on its own, a slow boil of pale bodies that rises and sinks in the iridescent film. They feed on the seep's dissolved minerals and convert them into a fine white silt that drifts to the bottom and slowly builds the pool's rim. Their activity is the reason the seep has not drained or dried in the decades since the works closed. They are, in a sense, rebuilding the pump's sump by hand, one particle at a time.

## The Grey Tufts

The halophytes grow in dense clusters along the channel banks, each tuft a compact fortress of succulent leaves that hold their water like small cisterns. They have grey, dusty surfaces that reflect the light, and their roots reach into the damp layer beneath the clay. They do not spread quickly. Each one holds its ground, and the banks are slowly stitched together by thousands of such holdings. When a flood comes, they close their leaves and wait.

## The Reeds and the Rubber Shrubs

The reeds push up through the concrete cracks, their stems stiff and hollow, filled with the same condensate the beetles drink. They sway only in the rare wind, and when they break they release a faint smell of cut metal. The shrubs on the eastern rubble piles have bark that peels like rubber, and they are the only tall living things in the basin. They lean toward the sun and hold their branches stiffly, as if braced against a pull from below.

## The Thing Beneath

Something moves under the clay. It has not been seen directly, but its presence is clear. Its tunnels collapse the plates in long, sudden crevices, and the beetles pour into these openings the way smoke rushes into a vent. The shimmer-beetles seem to follow it, and the reeds grow more densely over its tracks. Whatever it is, it appears to be doing what the pump once did: drawing the basin's moisture down and moving it somewhere out of sight, though whether it is taking the water away or bringing it up, nobody here can say.

## What the Basin Is

Taken together, the sump is a single system, and its inhabitants are its parts. The shimmer-beetles move the water into the air, the grey-wings keep time with the old machinery, the crustaceans rebuild the brine basin, the tufts hold the banks, the reeds draw from the cracks, and the thing beneath moves what the others cannot reach. Each has taken over one function the works once performed. The basin is not a ruin that life has colonized. It is a pump that has been reassembled out of living parts, and it runs on the same cycles the works used, only slower, looser, and without any plan.

When the rain comes, the system is broken. Water fills the bowl, the birds scatter, the beetles drown or flee, and the clay seals itself under a brown sheet. But the rain always drains, and the parts come back to their places one by one, the first condensate beading on the steel before dawn.
