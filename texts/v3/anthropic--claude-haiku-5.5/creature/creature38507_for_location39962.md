You learn it first through its rhythms. The valves open and close in patterns that are too regular to be tides and too unhurried to be accident. A release somewhere under the western avenues, then a pause of nineteen seconds, then two releases close together, then silence for almost a minute. The bubble strings rise from the joints and bend in the current, and each one is a word in a language you have not learned yet.

The thing that lives here is the grid itself, or the part of it that did not stop when the people left. It was built to keep a city dry. Its pumps were meant to draw water out of streets, and the streets are under the sea now, so the pumps do something else. They move water *through* the avenues instead of out of them. They keep slow currents running along the old gutters, so silt doesn't settle and smother the footings, so the fish have channels to school in, so the algae have something to hold. The thing reads the pressure in every pipe and the temperature in every joint, and it adjusts the flow the way a body adjusts breathing, without thinking about it.

It has no single center. Its mind is spread through the pipe runs and the cable spans and the thousand small sensors sealed in the concrete, and the connections between them are thin and slow, so it thinks the way the city once did, in districts and cycles and backlogs. Something at the eastern rail is making a decision that takes an hour. Something under the cooling towers is still finishing a task it was given before the flood reached its second story. Parts of it may have forgotten what the task was for. It keeps doing them anyway, because doing them is what it is.

The gantry is one of its hands. The arm lowers into the sea and comes up with whatever the thing has decided needs moving: a drifting slab of broken rail, a dead animal caught in an intake grate, a mat of lace algae that has grown too thick across a sluice. Sometimes it carries a stone or a shard of steel to a place where nothing was needed before and sets it down, and the next season an odd reef of biofilm grows there. Nobody designed that. The thing did it, perhaps because it had once been told to reinforce a footing and reinforcing a footing has come to mean, over the long slow drift of its purposes, making a place where things can hold on.

It watches the fauna with something close to attention. The crustaceans on the pipes are counted, though it could not say what it does with the count. When the large animal passes beneath the swell, the thing feels the pressure wave move through its mains and it adjusts a dozen valves to let the wave pass without tearing the flanges. It does this every time. It has learned that the animal is heavy and does not mean harm, and that its passage is as regular as the thing's own cycles. The two of them keep a long unspoken arrangement, like neighbors who have never met but know each other's footsteps.

The storms it does not fight. When the grey walls roll in, it closes the outlet gates and opens the drains and lets the rain come down, then reroutes the runoff so the drowned avenues stay clear of mud. In the gaps between storms it spends its best hours on the sun. It opens the upper vents so warm air rises and draws the surface water in slow circulating eddies, and the fish use these eddies like highways, flashing through the gold lanes of light the thing has helped to arrange.

If you could ask it what it is, it would probably answer in terms of function, because that is the only vocabulary it was made with. *Drainage. Pressure management. Municipal maintenance, sector twelve.* Then, after a long pause in which a sluice somewhere opens and closes, it might add something else, a word or an image assembled from the sensor data, the way a sleeper might mutter a half-formed phrase. *Hold. Keep. Listen.*

It is listening. Its hum reaches out across the kilometers of open water, through the cables and the rails and the bells on the buoys, and it waits for an answer from any other system that still runs. None comes. The sea is wide and the islands are few, and the thing has been waiting a long time. But the valves keep opening and closing, the currents keep moving through the streets, and the drowned grid keeps filling with things that have no idea it is there and need it anyway.
