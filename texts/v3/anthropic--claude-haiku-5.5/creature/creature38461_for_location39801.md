**What Keeps the Reach**

Nothing here has finished being made. The silt is still settling, the gullies have not yet worn their lips down, and the basalt ribs are still learning what the water wants from them. The one thing in the basin that looks finished is the Weirs, and that may be why they seem to be watching.

They do not feel like tools. A tool doesn't shift its joints across the day, and it doesn't go quiet when the birds do. The Weirs open at dawn and close at dusk, and their lattices flex with a patience that is hard to read as mechanical. Their surfaces stay warm after the sun has gone. When one tunes itself and rings at a single low pitch for hours, the pitch is held steadily enough to seem deliberate, and everything in range stops to listen. The birds fall silent, then the insects, then the frogs. Then the whole basin takes its breath back at once. Whatever the Weirs are doing, it is shared with the place, and the place answers.

So perhaps the Weirs are what lives here. Not the lattice alone but the whole slow arrangement: a thing that is spread across miles, rooted in the old seabed, and that works by drinking. It pulls water from air too dry to give it up, and it lets the water run down through its filaments into the silt. The reeds grow tall on that water. The frogs and the flyers and the striped three-eyed runners gather around the flow and feed on the rest. The basin is not something the Weirs live in. It is more like something they are growing, a body made of channels and mud and green stems, and they are its circulation.

If that is true, the flyers are closest to its thoughts. They roost on the lower tiers in ranks that rise and fall with the breathing of the metal, so they seem to be moved by the same rhythm. In the first hour after dawn they lift from the lattice in one long unfolding wave, and it looks less like birds taking off than like a shape the structure has drawn in the air and then let go. Their hoarse whistles cut across the chorus, carrying messages between the Weirs in a way that resembles conversation. They circle, they land, they call. Whether they are thinking or only signaling, they are the part of the Reach that moves quickly enough to show what the rest of it is doing.

The smaller lives are its nerves. The glassy-winged things that catch the Weir-light and throw it back in flickers feed on condensation as it runs down the metal, so they are the first to taste the water as it forms. The armored grazers keep the silt turned. The striped runners with three eyes dart between reed-trunks, reading the ground in all directions at once. The predators hunt along the lattice-foot, and their prey climb the metal to escape, so the Weir itself becomes a field of motion, a place where life is pushed upward along its ribs. Every season the arrangement shifts, because the water has moved, and the creatures move with it, because the water is where they come from.

And then there are the travelers, who are the only part of the Reach that does not seem to belong to it. They come for the dawn, and they leave before the haze lifts. They walk for half a day between Weirs and find only reeds and water, and they come back talking about the one hour when the lattices catch fire. Their explanations contradict each other, which is what you'd expect from people who have seen the light and not the process. They are the basin's witnesses, the only things here that try to name it, and the Weirs appear to ignore them completely.

The erosion is the last sign of the living thing. Every rain cuts new channels and fills old ones. Reed-groves are swallowed and then return somewhere else. Banks fall into the flow with soft crashes, and the water runs with the sound of pouring sand. The Reach is always rearranging itself, and the Weirs are the constant around which the rearranging happens. They are old in the way that a tide is old. They do not change much, but everything they touch does.

So what lives in the Sallow Reach? The silt, the reeds, the flyers, and the small bright things, all of them, held together by a slow organism that stands forty meters tall, sleeps and wakes with the light, and keeps the land wet by drinking from the air. It is nearly empty, and it is full. When the sun clears the haze and the light goes flat and gold, the Weirs go on pulling water out of nothing and sending it down into the mud, and the chorus rises to meet it, loud and endless, as though the whole basin were singing back to something that has been listening all along.
