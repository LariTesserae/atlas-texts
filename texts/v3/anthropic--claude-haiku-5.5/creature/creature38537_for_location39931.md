# Those Who Keep the Table

You might expect to meet the Stilled Table's inhabitants first, but the place is inhabited in layers, and the people are the top layer, the part that is easiest to see and most easily lost. Beneath them are the animals, which are few and wary. Under those is something the people do not name directly. And woven through all of it is the water, which may be the most patient resident of all.

## The People

They call themselves something that translates roughly as *the ones who carry stones*. The lowland traders say the plateau's people are wet to the bone, and it's true in a way that goes beyond the weather. Their skin stays cool and faintly sheened even in the dry hours, and their hair is never quite dry. Their hands are heavily calloused across the palms and between the fingers, from fiber rope and wet stone. They are lean and quick, built for slick footing, and they move with a habit of testing each step before trusting it, a habit so ingrained that they walk like people feeling their way through dark rooms even in daylight.

They live in small camps of perhaps a dozen to thirty, in the lee of boulders or in the terraced villages below the falls. Camps move. When the ground shifts, when a slide takes a terrace or a river abandons its bed, the people pick up their baskets and go, and they do not mourn the place as a loss so much as a season ending. They have no word for "home" that means a fixed building. Their word means something closer to *the place where our hands are working right now*.

Their speech is hands and whistles. Conversation beyond arm's length is carried by a whistled code that rides over water and reed-noise. The code is not simple. It has pitch, rhythm, and a grammar of rising and falling tones, and a skilled whistler can pass a warning across a gorge faster than a runner could carry it. Children learn the whistles before they learn to talk, and some of the older whistlers say the reeds taught them. When the wind moves through the hollow stalks, they claim, the reeds sometimes answer a whistle with a half-note of their own, and a whistler who hears this knows someone else out there is listening.

Their most important role belongs to the menders. Every bridge, cairn, terrace, and basket is the work of a particular person's hands, and when a part fails, the mender replaces it. The rope bridge over the widest gorge has been rebuilt so many times that no fiber in it is original, yet the people will tell you it is the same bridge, held by the same knots passed from hand to hand. Ownership of the bridge is a kind of inheritance of knowledge, not of material. A mender who dies without teaching someone the knots is said to have cut a bridge loose.

They do not build monuments because they believe, with a quiet and almost cheerful certainty, that nothing here will last. Instead they practice a ritual of renewal. Each year, at the start of the dry season, each camp chooses one stone from its cairn and replaces it with a new one. The old stone is returned to the basins, set in the salt-white crust at the water's edge, where it becomes part of the floor. Over generations the cairns have shifted and grown, and the people say the cairn is not a thing but a conversation, a record of who was here and what they chose to keep.

The dead go to the basins too. The bodies are laid in the shallow pools where the water runs clear at dawn, and within a season the cloud and the mineral crust have taken them. The people believe the dead are then *held* by the table, which they say is still falling apart in the way that is the same as being remembered. They do not visit graves. They visit the basins, and they say the water is clearer when someone they loved is nearby.

## The Animals

The large crested bird is the one the lowland folk see most often. It circles above the falls on wide, slow wings, its crest a brilliant orange-and-blue fan that flares when it calls. The people consider it a messenger and never hunt it. When the bird circles low over a camp, the camp moves within the day, and no one has ever found a case where the bird was wrong about the ground.

The heavy amphibians are slow, mottled, and very old-looking, with ridged backs and eyes like polished stone. They bask on the warm flat slabs, and they are eaten only in famine, because they taste of the bitter mineral water they drink. Smaller frogs and salamanders crowd the lower basins in their thousands, but they are almost never seen in the cloud zone above.

Larger creatures are glimpsed rather than known. A shape moves through the reeds and is gone. A splash in deep water leaves rings that do not fade for a long time. By dawn there are prints in the mud, heavy and splayed, with too many toes, and sometimes the prints lead to the edge of a basin and stop, as if the thing simply walked into the water and kept going. The people do not follow these tracks.

## Beneath

The hunters speak of creatures in the hollow stone. The stories are not told to children, and the adults use them as a kind of warning against curiosity more than against danger. But the things the stories describe are not hard to find, if you know how to listen.

The bell-stones ring with notes that do not match the rock around them. Strike a slab and the tone hangs for a breath too long, and it is never quite the same tone twice. The people do not strike the bell-stones. They lay their palms on them and feel for the hum, and they say the hum is slower than a heartbeat and that it changes when a slide is coming.

The warm slabs are warm all the time, even in the rain, and in the deepest hollows the steam vents open and close in a rhythm that could be breath. The people do not walk on the warm slabs at night. They say the floor is sleeping there, and that a sleeper turns over.

And the slides themselves, the sudden loosening and falling of hillside, may not be the ground failing at all. The people have a theory they do not say aloud, that the things under the stone grind slowly against each other, the way the old mountain grinds against the living land, and that what looks from below like the table falling apart is in fact a kind of feeding, or digestion, or the slow turning of a body that has been lying in the same posture for longer than any name can reach.

What is in the stone, if anything, is not hostile in the way the lowland stories say. It does not hunt. It does not seem to notice the camps at all, except when a camp digs too deep or builds too heavy, and then the ground answers. The people have learned to build light and move often, and in this they have found a partnership that is neither trust nor fear but a patient, mutual attention.

## The Trees and the Water

The trees that lean toward the nearest waterfall have a name, which the people say means *those who listen to the falling*. They are the oldest trees on the plateau, with buttressed roots that have split and reknit around the stone more than once. The people believe they can hear the things beneath, and that the leaning is not a response to the wind but to the bell-stones, whose hum carries through the root-threads. A mender who wants to know whether the ground will hold will sometimes sit at the base of one of these trees for an hour, eyes closed, and then get up and move the camp.

The basins that clear at dawn are the table's one still face. The people say the water becomes clear when the thing under the stone is breathing out slowly and holding its surface calm, and that the sky is reflected in it because the thing wants to see the sky. By midday the water clouds again, and the people take this as the thing turning over, or going back to sleep.

## The Cloud

Finally, there is the cloud, which sits on the plateau most of the day and seems to move with a purpose of its own. The people do not worship it, but they treat it with the respect one gives a large, old animal that is not especially interested in one's presence. The cloud carries the spray from the falls, the mist from the vents, and the whistles across the gorges. It hides the far edges of the world and then, abruptly, reveals them. The people say the cloud is where the table keeps its memories, and that the whistles they send into it are sometimes returned, days later, in the form of a storm that arrives exactly when they needed rain or exactly when they needed to be gone.

---

So who lives on the Stilled Table? The people who carry stones, lean and damp and fluent in whistles, who mend what the ground undoes and return their dead to the water. The old crested bird, the basking amphibians, the unseen heavy things in the mud. The trees that listen, the reeds that answer, the cloud that remembers. And under all of them, slow and patient and not quite named, the thing in the hollow stone, turning over in its sleep while the mountain that was once a mountain goes on becoming the shape it will hold forever.
