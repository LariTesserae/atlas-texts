They wake with the fog. Before the sun reaches the terraces, the things that live on the Sulfur Shelf are wet, cold, and still, beaded like the pipes they cling to. The lizards come first. They are the ones most people notice, flat and pale-backed, with a fin of translucent tissue running from the nape to the base of the tail. In the dark the fin is a sponge. It fills with condensate, and by first light each lizard is holding a small reservoir of water along its spine. They sit on the warm girders and tilt their heads to let the drops run to their mouths. Some drink from one another's backs, nipping at the beaded ridge. Most of them are a dull gray-green, the color of old slate. A few, usually the older ones, have crests the color of rust-stained ochre, bright as a warning, and the crest is a record. It holds the mineral content of every pool and runoff channel the animal has drunk from. A lizard that has lived all its life near the northern vents has a crest shading toward sulfur yellow. One from the far terraces has a deep russet band, the iron-rich water of the eastern seeps. Nobody can tell you how the color is made. You can only read it.

The water itself has its own life. Overnight, the pools grow a film, thin and nearly invisible, and when the light hits it at a low angle it shows every color at once, shifting as you move. This is a mat of living things, a dense community of bacteria and something like diatoms, pressed flat against the surface tension. It is most active in the cool hours, feeding on the sulfur and the dissolved iron and laying down thin mineral skins as it works. By noon the heat cracks it. The iridescence breaks into drifting flakes, and the flakes sink to the bottom, where they become part of the sediment that builds the rings. The rings in the old runoff channels are its archive. Each ring is one cycle of growth and collapse, a night's work pressed into stone. The biggest rings are larger than a person, and they grow outward so slowly that the film's whole lifetime is measured in generations of lizards.

The swallows are the loud ones, though loud is relative here. They nest in thousands in the gantry girders, and each morning their voices rise up the terraces in a wave, a chatter that thins quickly in the thin air. They hunt the midges, and the midges rise in dense columns over the sulfur pools whenever a shadow passes over them. This is not a plague, though it can look like one. The midges are the swallows' whole diet, and the swallows are the midges' only real check, and the balance between them is what keeps the shelf from choking on its own abundance. The beetles and rodents move beneath all of it, through the clay plates, their tunnels making a fine lattice that channels the runoff and keeps the ground from sealing. Every creature here is working some piece of the same few resources: warm metal, fog-wet hours, shade under the towers, and the water that comes and goes.

The people are the last to appear, and the easiest to overlook. They live in the shade of the cooling towers, in low dwellings built from the split ribbed shells and pipe sections, roofed with sheet metal and clay. They are not many. They move between the terraces on foot along the old drainage routes, and they carry small jars and nets of fine cloth to catch the fog. The jars are their most important tools. A reader lifts a sample from a pool, holds it to the light, and tilts it so the film shows its colors. She watches the lizards at the channel edges, the crests and their shades, and she notices where the swallows are hunting thickest. From these she knows which pools are safe to drink, which seeps are too rich, which rings are growing fast this season and which have stalled. Water is a visitor, as the first travelers say, and the readers are the ones who learn its habits. They do not own the works and do not try to restart them. The pumps still drip, the metal still ticks, and the readers listen to that rhythm the way they listen to the fog.

By midday the fog has lifted in long gauzy ribbons and the shelf is white and still under the sun. The lizards retreat into the shade of the towers. The swallows thin out, and the midges settle into the crevices. The film has broken and sunk. Only the machinery moves, expanding in the heat with a faint, steady tick. Then the afternoon air cools, the fog comes down from the peaks, and the whole plateau begins to beat again: beads forming, crests darkening, the film stitching itself back together across the pools, the rings lengthening a fraction of a hair. The place is crowded and empty at once, and it keeps its own slow balance, measured in water and in the patient growth of the stone.
