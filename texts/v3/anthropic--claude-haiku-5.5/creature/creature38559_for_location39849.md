You notice them last, because they move the way everything here moves, slowly and with the water. At first they look like part of the slope: a figure bent over a channel, a shape kneeling at the edge of a pool, a pair of people standing in the mist with their heads tilted toward a drip of seep-water. Then one straightens, and you see that it has been working the whole time.

They are human, or near enough that the difference takes effort to name. They are a little small, narrow in the shoulder, long in the hand. Their skin has the pale, faintly translucent quality of something kept out of direct sun, and the damp has stained it with a fine green-gray bloom along the creases of the knuckles and the backs of the ears, as moss stains the north side of a stone. Their hair hangs in wet strands that never seem to drip. The mist settles on it and stays, beaded but not heavy, so that each person wears a faint halo of standing droplets that catches the even white light.

Their eyes are wide and unhurried. In a light without origin, they do not squint or search for shadows, because there are none to find. They look at things and let them be equally near and equally far. You will notice they rarely look up. The sky has no sun in it, no direction that the light comes from, and they seem to have stopped expecting the world to be lit from anywhere in particular.

They dress in bark-cloth and fern-fiber, dyed nowhere, the natural tan and brown of stripped stems. The cloth is heavy with the air and darkens at the hems where it trails through the marsh. Their hands are always busy. Their fingers are scarred in small pale lines from the glass blades and the rough edges of cut stone, and their nails are rimmed with dark silt. They carry the knapped blades on their belts and the grinding stones in their arms, and they set them down with a care that suggests they know exactly how long every object has been in exactly this place.

What they do is maintain. That is the word that comes to you when you watch them long enough, though it is not quite right. They keep the channels at their rate, lifting a stone slab a finger's width and setting it back when the water begins to pool too high on one side. They scrape the moss off the causeways, not all of it, only the layer that threatens to make the stone slick enough to slide on, and they leave a faint green film that is always there after they have finished. They relay water from one terrace cistern to the next in small clay vessels, pouring with an attention that looks like prayer and may be simply habit. They grind the same grain in the same mortars at the same hour, and the dish of each grinding stone deepens by a grain's thickness a year, so slowly that no one could say whether it has deepened at all.

They speak rarely, and quietly, and you hear their voices a moment after you see their mouths move, because the sound has to cross the mist. Their words are short. When two of them work together they seem to need few of them, passing a stone or a bundle of fiber with a glance and a small click of the tongue. When they do talk at length, it is often in the same low register used for the water, a murmur with no rise or fall, as though speech were just one more thing that flows through the place at a constant rate.

They do not seem to age in any way you can measure. Some are young in the face, and some are old, but the old ones have the same bent economy of movement as the young, and you cannot tell from their bodies how long any of them have been working the channels. A child kneels at a ditch with a flat stone, learning to lift it, and her hands are already stained the same green-gray. An old woman sits by a cistern with a clay pot in her lap, and her hands are stained exactly the same. Whatever the years do to them, they do it at the rate the water does, which is to say very little.

They do not seem to need to speak of the absence. There are no birds to hear and no insects to notice the lack of, and the people have never known anything else. But if you ask them, gently, what it was like before the stillness, they look at you with polite and total incomprehension, and then one of them will nod slowly toward the cliff, where a dark seam of rock is weeping steadily into a thin sheet of water, and say a single word that you cannot quite hear, and then go back to work.

They are not unhappy. That is the hardest thing to accept. They are attentive to the water, patient with the stone, careful with the glass and the clay, and they keep the balance the way a body keeps its heat, without thinking about it. The settlement holds because they hold it. Every stone they lift and set back is a small refusal of change, and the pools stay at their level, the channels at their rate, the mist at its density, because for as long as anyone here can remember, someone has always been kneeling by the water, setting it right.

Watch them long enough and you begin to suspect the weeping rock is not the cause of the stillness but something they keep company with. The cliffs drip. The people tend the drip. Between the two of them, the young land holds its unfinished shape, and no one, not the people or the stone or the pale unmoving sky, has yet decided what it will become.
