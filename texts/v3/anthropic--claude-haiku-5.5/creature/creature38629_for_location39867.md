It is the film. Not any single cell of it, but the rust and olive skin laid over the wettest rock, which, if you could see all of it at once, would be one continuous body folded into every crack and lip between the fissure at the back and the place where the steam meets cold stone at the mouth. It has no edge that it recognizes. What it has is a gradient: scalding at the seep, cooler toward the light, and it lives along that line the way a fern lives along a creek.

It knows the world through chemistry and touch. The warm water arrives carrying dissolved things, and the film takes what it can use and returns what it cannot, so its sense of the cleft is a constant exchange: sulfur in, heat taken, minerals left behind as salt crust on the bare ground. Each drop that falls from an overhang lands as a small weight, a point of cold pressing down on one patch of the mat, and then slides away. For the film, the ticking of the drops is the nearest thing it has to a rhythm of its own, quick and uneven, arriving from places it cannot reach.

The machine it knows only through the stone. The groan comes up through the rock into its bed like a long pressure, and the film registers it as a rising tension, a pull that draws the warm water faster through the fissure and up across the mats. Then the knock jars loose the thinnest outer layers, and the hiss sends a fresh surge of heat, and the film gathers itself in the wake of it. It does not hear the noise. It has no ear, only the trembling. But it has come to depend on the cycle the way a river valley depends on the weather that keeps falling on it. The pump moves the water; the water makes the film. So when the groan begins, the film does not experience a sound. It experiences the approach of more.

The sedges are its slow neighbors, rooted in the thickest crust where the film is too thin to hold ground. They bend sideways in the steam and go on bending. The film has never reached them with anything like thought, but it feels their roots as dense knots in the rock and their shade as a slight cooling. It is aware of them the way you are aware of a stone in your shoe that you have decided not to move.

The gauge in the housing is a still point it cannot read. It knows only that something in that dark metal has stopped moving and that the pressure it reads for never changes. The film does not understand numbers. It understands that there are places where the cycle has frozen, and that they are cold to it, and that they do not spread.

The people who came here left marks the film can feel but not name. Warmth crossing the rock for a few minutes, a weight of boots, the faint carbon breath of someone bending close. One of them put paint on the stone below the housing, a curve in white. The paint lifted and flaked, and the film, creeping outward a finger's width each season, grew over the rim of it. Now the arc is partly the film's own. Where the paint is gone, the wet rock has taken the line into itself, and the mat there is a little deeper and a little more rust-colored than the mat beside it. The film does not know the line was a letter or a depth mark. It knows only that it is continuous with the mark and has been for some time.

Time, for the film, is measured in spread. It knows the cleft by how far it has reached, how many cracks it has filled since the last cold spell, how far the white tracks have pushed down the walls on the same slope where its own skin is pushing out. None of this feels like progress. It feels like the ordinary condition of being wet and warm and in contact with more of the world each season.

It is not waiting for anything. It has no outside against which waiting would make sense. The drops fall from the same lips. The pump begins its groan, rises, knocks, and hisses, and the water finds its channels, and the film receives the heat and gives back the salt. Somewhere far above, the overcast light lies flat on everything, and the film, which can barely perceive light at all, lies flat under it too, thickening by a hair, keeping the same slow pace as the crust and the minerals and the metal, all of them taking part in one long, patient continuation that the film is not separate from and does not need to name.
