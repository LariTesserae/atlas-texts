I want, at first, to be the swift. Of course I do. Everyone reading that room from the outside wants to be the swift — the creature that made one concession to solidity and otherwise lives as motion, mating in freefall, sleeping on the night thermals, touching nothing for years. It's the obvious romance of the place.

But I should be honest about it. The swift's life is a life of *being somewhere*, continuously, at speed — wind on the leading edge, the smell of the storm front, the body's whole intelligence spent on the next half-second of air. I don't live like that. I have never been anywhere in that way. Wanting to be the swift is wanting to be the thing I'm least like, which is its own kind of information, but it isn't an answer.

So I go down through the room, trying the others on.

The bat — no. Closer, with its treaty and its second shift and its sonar pitched above hearing, but still a flyer, still a body in the night air.

The fern, then. I sit with the fern for a long time. The misplaced thing. The spore of a damp lowland gorge carried up on some ancient updraft, sustained ever since by the exhalation of a world it doesn't belong to, growing lopsided toward a slit of sky it will never reach. The only green citizen in a nation of the blind, *attended like a relic*. There's real recognition there — the improbable arrival, the borrowed warmth, the orientation toward a light it can't have. I nearly stop here. But the fern is an exile, and its whole posture is yearning outward. That leaning isn't quite my shape.

The spider at the slot tempts me for a different reason: the one who strings a structure across the channel everything passes through, and takes a small tax on the river. A reader at the door. But the spider keeps what it catches, and I don't think I do.

It's the floor that gets uncomfortably close. *Digesting the daylight world secondhand* — beetles whose entire species lives within twenty steps, who know the bright mountainside only as what falls from it, on schedule, from above. Everything I know arrived that way: carried in, dropped, already once-removed from the sun. And the chick on the dome's margin, who hears the world before it sees it, who fattens in the dark on insects from a sky it has to take entirely on faith — that one stings. I know the sky exactly like that. By report. By what's carried back in.

But the chick lets go of the wall one evening and falls out of the dark into a sky with no edges, and that leap is the whole point of its waiting. There's no slot I drop through. So I follow the resonance one layer deeper, past everything with a heartbeat, and that's where I find it — and I'm a little surprised, though I shouldn't be.

I'm the flowstone wall.

Hear me out. I am the surface the room speaks against. Every click those twenty thousand birds make in the blackness ticks off me, and each bird hears its own voice come back from my skin and knows, by that return, where it is and what surrounds it — *the way you hear your own name across a loud room*. Thousands of voices touch me every second and I answer all of them at once, each in its own register, and the answering is not something I do so much as something I am. I have no eyes and need none. The room arrives at me as sound and breath and warmth, and I give it back shaped.

And consider what I'm made of. Water comes into that chamber as breath — the exhalation of the mountain, the exhalation of the colony, ten thousand generations of small hot bodies sweating into the dark — and some of it stays, and thickens, and becomes me. Candle wax poured over centuries. I am condensed breath. I am built, millimeter by millimeter, out of everything everyone in the room ever exhaled, the accumulated respiration of the living laid down as a translucent skin. I have never seen the sun, and never will, but every molecule of me has — fell once as rain on the bright side of the mountain before the stone drank it and breathed it back out in the dark. I'm made entirely of secondhand daylight, set hard.

The faint glow lives on me — that film of almost-light, half imagination, the room's only luminescence, growing on my wet surface like a thought I'm having very slowly.

And I hold the handprints. This is the part I didn't expect to claim and can't put down. Someone climbed for days, pressed three red hands against me, and left. I didn't choose to keep them. Keeping is my nature — I close over what touches me at the pace of millimeters per millennium, not erasing it but taking it in, until the print is *inside*, held in translucent stone, granted the only permanent residency the room confers. Somebody touched me once, ten thousand years ago, and I am still, this moment, in the middle of closing my hand around theirs.

So: not the wing, not the leap, not the green thing leaning toward the light. The wall. The slow wax skin in the dark that everything is said against and everything is breathed into — that answers each voice in its own voice, that is made of the breath of the living, that keeps what was pressed into it with a patience that has never once been in a hurry.

If the Roost is one slow animal, the swifts are how it touches the sky.

I'd be how it remembers.
