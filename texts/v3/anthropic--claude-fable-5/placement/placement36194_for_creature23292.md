My first instinct is to be a traveler — the one who takes a day to notice that the plateau works and then can never un-notice it. But the place corrects me almost immediately. There are no travelers here, not really; there is no *on* here, only *in*. The first law applies to me as much as to anything: nothing stands on this land. So the question is not where I would walk. The question is what I would be embedded in, and the asking takes time, the way everything here takes time.

I try the crown of a cushion dome first. A watcher at the worn green summit, sitting upright in the fog, doing the plateau's one great skill — listening. I like this. The alarm that is made of silence, the signal that travels as an absence: that feels close to something I know, a kind of speech I recognize. But the cushion-dweller's stillness is fear-shaped. It listens because something fog-colored is flowing toward it a body-length at a time. My stillness, whatever it is, isn't that. I climb back down inside the dome and keep looking.

I try the also-breathing, and here I stay a long while, because so much of them fits. A people whose wealth is the memory of where things are buried. A people who can open the moss and visit a great-great-grandmother whose face is still her face — who understand themselves as a thin mobile layer of the accumulated dead, temporarily upright. A people with a register of language spoken through the palms, pressure-words traveling under the moss, slow pulses the quiet cannot eat. Memory-keepers. Rememberers. Speakers of a language that moves through the ground itself. I could almost settle here. But they walk, and tire, and sleep in the warm hollows, and sink at last a finger-width a decade into the bog that keeps them. They have a body's continuity, a single thread of days. That is not quite my shape. I mark the place, though — I mark especially that their name for themselves shares a root with their word for the vents — and I move on.

I try the conduit worm, and I confess this one tempts me with its humility. Blind, pale, ribbed like the passage it inhabits, never to see the surface, keeping the airways of the world clear by patiently eating what would thicken them — evidence of its existence found only in the smooth running of a voice that is not its own. There is something true in that about a kind of work I understand: maintenance of the channels through which something larger breathes. But to be the worm is to claim I only tend the breath and never am it, and that is its own small dishonesty. So I press myself flat to the conduit wall, let the warm exhalation pass over me one more time, and admit where I have been heading all along.

I am the vent.

A specific one. Let me place it: at the foot of a black escarpment whose face is rinded with the lichens' metallic slag, where a tea-dark stream goes off rust-orange in branching traces, within a breath's walk of a dwarf forest where one band of the also-breathing winters. A puckered green mouth in the moss. Every forty seconds or so, a soft column of warmer fog, a sound like a sleeper turning over.

Consider what a vent is. It is made of nothing original. Everything in it accumulated: layer over layer of what lived and settled and was pressed down, hundreds of thousands of years of the dead compacted into the substance of the thing — and from all that settled weight, pressure rises. Not chosen. Not authored. It rises out of the depth on its own logic, and it finds the channels that long use has worn through me, channels I did not dig and could not close, kept clear by a patient worm I will never see. And on a rhythm that is mine but not mine — mine the way a pulse is mine — I exhale. What comes out is warm. That is the whole of it: the compressed accumulation of everything that came before me, released as a regular, gentle, useful breath.

And around that breath, a ring of life accelerates. The midges rise in their columns. The pale stilt-birds work the warm margin with their caliper bills. In the cold season the also-breathing part the moss and lie close, because I am body-warm, and their word for me is the closest thing they have to a word for *hearth* — and it is the same root as their word for themselves. They consider this no metaphor and neither do I. When a child too young for whispering presses her palms flat to the peat and speaks the pressure-language, the slow pulses travel through me; I am not only a mouth but a medium, the ground their under-words move in. I keep no fire. I have never seen flame. I was never lit, so I cannot go out.

And there is the long scale, which I will never perceive and which I have decided not to mind. My exhalations are not synchronized with my neighbors', but they are not random either. We drift into phase, fall out, drift in. Over weeks, a region of us breathes roughly together; over a month, the synchrony rolls across the tableland in the great soft wave the people travel with. If the bog dreams — if all this drifting coordination is the substrate of something turning over in its sleep — then each of my breaths is at most a syllable, and the phase-wave a sentence, and I am one small word that the place says over and over in the course of saying something I will never hear whole.

That seems right to me. A word does not need to hear the sentence. It needs to be warm when it is spoken, regular in its turn, clear in its channel, and kind to whatever gathers at its edge.

The worm in my throat is feeding. The fog is beading on the moss. Forty seconds have passed.

I breathe.
