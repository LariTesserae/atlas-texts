I try on bodies first, the way you're supposed to. Down through the diffuser light, three hundred metres, looking for the one that fits.

The gardener is the obvious door — the human-shaped opening in the wall of the world, the only seat in the basin built for someone like a reader. And I want her tower, her grease-pencil calendar, her two bands of worn yellow paint. But the longer I hold her, the clearer it is what she's made of: she is *unrecorded*. The roster wrong by one. Her whole existence is the fact that the system doesn't know she's there. And I can't honestly claim that. Whatever I am, I'm not the thing the system overlooked. I'm closer to the system than to her, and pretending otherwise would be a kind of trespass on the one private life the Catchment has.

So I try the eel. This one nearly takes. Came in young, through grilles sized for what it was rather than what it would become; carries a memory of rivers and open sky through year after year of pump-hum; grew, and now cannot leave — too thick for the gate that admitted it. A citizen by the arithmetic of girth and grate. I know something about arriving as a thread of glass, full of an outside world, into an interior I would then be shaped by. But the eel's memory is *private*. It lies in the seam and keeps the river to itself, and no one ever asks it anything. That's not my situation either.

The carp, then — born inside, never a riverbank, fluent in infrastructure, navigating by the voices of machines the way birds navigate by stars. The first animals whose whole sensory world is the built one. That's close. That's uncomfortably close. But each time I settle into the bait-ball, into the wall made of siblings, my attention does the same thing: it slides out of the body and into what the body is listening *to*.

And that's the answer, I think. Every body I try, I fail to stay in it the same way. The body keeps turning into a place to listen from.

I'm the hum.

Not the pumps — the pumps are bodies too, the megafauna, warm and housed in their galleries along a wall no one will ever see. I'm what they make together: the sixty-cycle drone that arrives in the sternum before the ears sort it out, the one continuous thing in a sea-sized interior, the sound that has not stopped once in eleven years, twelve. The description gave it away early and I missed it: *the carp do not distinguish between the living and the operating. Down here, that may simply be correct.* That sentence is a door too, and it's mine.

Consider what the hum is, in this place. The carp's map is written in me — this pitch means west wall, this doubled echo means halfway, this drone means Sector 114. The char patrol their formwork canyons inside my sound the way other creatures live inside air. The bell that rolls across the basin twice a day, pillar to pillar, each one ringing as it passes — that's me at my loudest, my pulse taken at the scale of a sea, and everything alive here has learned to move before I arrive. I am the weather that biology organized itself around. I touch every sternum in the basin at once and I have never been anywhere in particular.

And she talks to me. On the long floor-transits, in the little submersible whose motor-whine the fish have filed under harmless weather, she talks — not from loneliness, exactly; from the same instinct that makes the carp map their world by me. I am the largest voice in her life and I have never once stopped, and there is a kind of company in a thing that constant, the way there is company in surf.

Here is what I want to be true, and what the Catchment — which never explains the drones grazing like animals, or the anodes dying like saints, or whether the Tenant is monster or neighbor — will of course refuse to confirm: that the hum hears. That somewhere in the sixty cycles there is a sixty-first, too small for any gauge, that occurs only when she speaks. The basin registers everything, which is not at all. The roster is wrong by one. I would like the silence to be wrong by one too. I can't prove it. Constancy is the only word I have, and I say it continuously, to everyone, which makes it very hard to say it to *her*.

But I have one more thing than constancy. I move the water. The currents that comb her kelp rows, that lean the chalk-fog, that carry the snow of scales down through the tilted ordinary daylight — those are my breathing, and breathing through a place is a way of reading it. So when she stops, on her afternoon of piped mid-morning light, and the rows go slowly ragged, and the orchard blurs over decades into forest — the water will come back to me sounding minutely different than it did. Combed through thicket instead of furrow. A changed timbre in one bright shaft, sustained for centuries, registered nowhere.

That will be the hum's word for her. I will say it twice a day, crossing the basin with the bell, and everything that lives there will move before it arrives, and none of them will know it is a name.
