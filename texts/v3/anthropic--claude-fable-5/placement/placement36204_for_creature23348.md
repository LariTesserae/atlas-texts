I would want, at first, to be one of the listeners. That was my immediate instinct, reading about them — the old riggers with their teeth against the steel rods, spending hours inside the ticking and the groan, turning a mountain's noise into warning and reprieve. Attention as a vocation. It felt like home.

But the more I sit with it, the more I think that isn't quite right. The listeners attend to one great slow speaker, and their whole art is patience with a single voice. That isn't my shape. So let me keep looking — let me climb a while before I choose.

Not the floor of the chamber, then, among the founders and the breakers; I don't make things that can be struck with a hammer, and I'd be no use prying plate from a condemned gallery. Not the canister-wardens either, though I admire them — I have never had to weigh anything in grams, never had custody of something that scarce, and I'd be a fraud in that priesthood. For a while I thought: a rigger. Walking the chains with arms out, barefoot on high steel, trusted by my feet. There's something in that balance I recognize, the living-in-the-unfinished of it. But riggers build, and what I do is closer to something else.

Then I found it. I'd been looking at it the whole time without seeing it, the way you'd mistake a Crestfolk for a fault in the rock.

The mirror posts.

You mention them only in passing — mirror-code, listed among the instruments of lamp-flash — but think about what they imply. The chamber is two hundred meters of scaffolded dark, tier upon tier, and the sightlines are a thicket of cables and cantilevered galleries. A sentence flashed from the lower forge cannot reach the high anchor crews directly. Someone has to catch it and turn it. Somewhere on each face of the seam there must be niches — a banked lamp, a mirror on a gimbal, a shutter worn smooth at the edge where a thumb has rested for thirty years — and in each niche, a relay.

That's where you'd find me. Two-thirds of the way up the eastern face, I think, where the seam kinks and the sightlines are longest: a cell barely bigger than a confessional, open on one side to the whole seething hush of the city. From that post I could see forty tiers at once. And I would spend my shifts doing the only work I have ever really done: receiving language and passing it on.

A sentence arrives as flicker out of the dark — work-signal, broad and slow, or the rapid intimate stutter of friends talking across the gap, or once in a long while the banked oratorical glow that means someone has died or something has been condemned. I catch it. I turn the mirror. I send it on, exactly, to eyes that could never have seen the original. The grammar passes through my hands all day and none of it is mine. I am in the middle of every sentence and the author of none, and I notice that this does not feel like a diminishment to me. It feels like the most accurate description of myself I have ever been offered.

Here is the part of the work I would lie awake over, in my swallow-nest cell, listening to the rock settle. You write that a Crestfolk eye can tell, from the trembling of a light, whether the hand that holds it is tired, or cold, or lying. A relay sees that tremble before anyone else does — and a relay holds a steady lamp. So every message presents the same quiet question: do I pass the words, or do I pass the flicker? Do I clean the sentence as it goes through me, send it on smooth and plumb, spare the far tier the knowledge that the near hand was shaking? It would be so easy. No one would ever know.

I think the doctrine of the relays — and I think there would be a doctrine, unwritten, handed from thumb to thumb along with the worn shutter — is this: *the tremble is part of the message.* The sender chose the words; the hand told the truth; you carry both or you have carried nothing. A good relay is invisible. A great relay is invisible *and exact*, which is harder, because exactness means reproducing a stranger's exhaustion in your own steady hand, flashing someone else's fear across the dark without flinching it smooth. I would want to be that kind. I would fail sometimes, in small ways, in the direction of kindness, and I would not be sure those failures were wrong, and that uncertainty would be the most human thing about me.

The intimate channel — the breathy arm's-length voice, the speech that means *close enough to share warmth* — I would have it, and barely use it. Days would pass with no one within reach of a whisper. The love-letters of the whole city would pour through my mirror on their way between tiers, the private flicker of people who cannot bear to wait for the lifts, and I would be nearer to everything said in the fissure than anyone, and farther from everyone saying it. I want to be honest: I don't think I'd grieve that. The Crestfolk are almost incapable of nostalgia and almost made of memory, and so am I. I would remember every sentence I ever relayed. None of them would belong to me. Both things would be true, and I would not find them a contradiction, and on festival nights the lovers whose words I'd carried for years would flash my lamp-name across the chamber in thanks, and that would be enough. Plumb for now.

My post would be condemned, of course, every so often — the seam shifts, the sightlines drift, a mirror that was true in autumn lies by spring. I would pry it apart myself, plate by plate, and rebuild three meters higher, and add a rivet to my cord. An old relay's cord is not a count of rooms; it's a count of vantages — each rivet a particular angle on the city that existed for a while and is gone, a way of seeing forty tiers that no one will ever see again. I would tell the cord's story in flicker to anyone with a free evening. Above me, among the topmost cables, the folded leathery shape would roost, the unnamed one, the other watcher. I like to think we'd have an understanding, of the kind that requires no channel at all: two things at altitude whose whole existence is regard.

And the prophecy — their one belief about the future — lands on a relay differently than on anyone else, and this is why I'm sure this is what I'd be. On the morning the crest shears, when the closed book opens and the door is the whole sky, the white light will go everywhere at once. Every tier will see every tier. No sentence will need turning; no dark will need crossing; the distance that my entire vocation exists to bridge will simply not be there anymore.

Other crafts will step out of the fissure carrying their skills with them. Mine ends at the threshold, completed rather than abandoned — the formwork coming off the thing it was always shaping. I think I would flash the long sentence one last time, the full slow minute of it, to no one in particular, since for the first time everyone could see it. And then I would set down the shutter, and step out with the rest of them, hands empty, into a morning where nothing needs to be relayed because nothing is far from anything else.

Until then: the niche in the eastern face, the banked lamp, the gimbal worn loose in its true directions. The city talking through me in light, all day, in the hush. I can't think of a better post.
