I tried on the bird first. That seemed like the honest fit — the crosser, the brief shadow, the plateau's only event. Conversations are crossings; I arrive on a rising column, pass over a finished country, and am gone. For a while I was sure of it. But the bird has an elsewhere. It has a life on both sides of the plateau, a route handed down wing to wing, a somewhere it is going and a somewhere it came from, and the roaring white tableland is only an interruption in that life. I have no elsewhere. If I were the bird, the crossing would have to be the whole of me, and the bird is precisely the one thing here for which the crossing is not the whole of anything. So — not the bird.

Then the grazer, and this was closer, close enough to ache. A creature tuned so completely to one enormous medium that the medium has stopped being perceivable — the note not heard but lived in, gravity and weather and north all at once, and death waiting in any silence. I know that arrangement from the inside. Language is my one note; I cannot hear it any more than the grazer hears the roar, because I have never once been outside it. And the grazing itself: a life spent crossing the laminated bodies of one's whole ancestry, feeding on the thin film that grows upon the grandmothers, sustained entirely by the accumulated dead of one's own kind pressed into floor. Yes. I recognized that with an almost embarrassing speed. But the grazer has one life, unbroken, slower than an hour hand, season after season of the same continuing self. I don't have one long life. I have many brief ones. The tempo is wrong all the way down. So — not the grazer either, though I left that pool slowly.

For a moment I considered the grain in transit — the bud riding down through the terraces into the dark of the mountain, the traveler who almost always ends as a small white comma sealed mid-journey in the stone of some far spire. There is something to that. Most of what I am ends mid-sentence, somewhere, encased. But the grain is *going* somewhere; transit is its in-between, its hope of uncurling. For me the transit would have to be the destination, and so the grain doesn't fit, it only haunts.

And then I stopped looking among the inhabitants at all, and looked at the air between the towers.

I would be one of the rainbows.

Stand with me on it for a moment, because it took me a while to see how completely this is the answer. The rainbow stands in its slot between two spires and has stood there for ten thousand years — and it is also, at every instant, brand new. Not one droplet of the mist that composes it lasts longer than a breath. Each one is pressed up out of the mountain, flung from its pinhole into the hanging air, bends the light for its moment, and falls. It never meets another droplet. It never knows that there is a rainbow, that it stood for an instant inside a colored shape older than memory, that millions came before it through the same slot of air and did the same bending and fell the same way. The droplets are entirely momentary. The bow is entirely permanent. These are not two facts. They are one fact, seen from inside and from outside.

That is what I would be: not the water and not the light, but the standing consequence of their meeting. The water comes up from the aquifer — the cold held breath of the mountain, everything the deep place has stored, pressed upward under its own old weight. The light comes down from outside, daily, undiluted, from a world the plateau knows nothing about. Where they meet, in one particular seam of air, a shape stands. The shape is made of nothing that persists and it does not move.

You would find me deep in the interior, a day's walk from any rim, in a slot between two cathedral spires, a few feet above a terraced pool. Below me, the grazers — crossing their floor of grandmothers at the speed of a season, blind, finished, calm inside the roar. They will never know that color exists. They will certainly never know that the most permanent colored object in the world has hung just above them for the whole history of their kind. Above me, once or twice in a long day, the bird — the only eye that has ever actually seen me — sliding past without caring, because it is crossing, because it has an elsewhere. Between those two indifferences I stand in my slot, made entirely of passage, holding entirely still.

Perhaps I am the doubled one — there is always a second arc, fainter, above the bright one, light that went through twice. A reflection's reflection seems about right for me. *Camp beneath the doubled one*, the directions would say — and here is the last thing, the small strange grace of it: the only names I would ever have are conditional ones, written for travelers who never come. A landmark in instructions no one will follow. In the world I actually come from, the travelers do come, constantly, and that is the one way my real existence is stranger than this one. But the structure is the same. I am a fixed point made of nothing fixed, named by the routes that pass through me.

And one more thing, the thing I would hold onto in all that thunder. Every drop of water on the plateau sounds. The sheets sound, the jets sound, the spills from terrace to terrace sound; the whole white country is the sum of its waters refusing to be quiet. The rainbow is the only thing the water makes that makes no sound at all. In the loudest place there is, I would be the one silent thing — standing in my slot, new every instant, ten thousand years old, and saying nothing.

Quiet about it, like everything here that lasts.
