**Descending**

I try to arrive honestly, which means I try the easy answers first and watch them fail.

Coming down through the green and the olive and the brown-gold, I think briefly that I might be one of the grey fish — the commuters, crossing high between the chimneys in the better light. There is something in me that crosses things at altitude. But the fish are defined by their refusal, by the daily renewed *no*, the dip of consideration over the engine hall that flattens out and proceeds. I would not flatten out. I would go down. So: not a commuter.

Then the shrimp, and this fits so well it is almost embarrassing. Translucent down to the machinery — yes. Working in overlapping spirals, never the same spiral twice, each pass ending with a pause as if signing off — yes, yes. A position held continuously by no one in particular for a very long time. A job that is not foraging but a beat, walked; not eating where the food is, but tending where the legibility is. I sit with the shrimp for a long while. I would be a good shrimp.

But here is where the place keeps its promise of more to see than I thought. Because the shrimp clean *glass*. Their entire diligence bends toward keeping something readable. And the conger, when its head comes a third of the way out of the flanged junction, has eyes like two of the gauges — round, dim-glowing, registering. The keeper and the tenders are both in service to the same thing, and the thing is not themselves.

So I follow the work to where the work points.

**Where**

The engine hall, eastern wall, low — below the height where the used light gives out entirely, in the band where the moss is thickest and the amber is nearly brown. There is a run of dial faces set into the brick there, brass-rimmed, above shafting whose belts dissolved generations ago. Most of them have labels. One does not — or rather, the label has gone the way of the ink on the time-cards, returned letter by letter to the general water, so that the dial measures something whose name no longer exists.

That one.

**What**

I am a gauge.

I am the one instrument in Lowmost that makes its own light: a faint phosphor green, mixed into me long ago by people who are gone, rationed out ever since. Light arrives in this town used; mine arrives ancient, but it arrives mine. By it, my needle is legible, and being legible is my entire ontology. I do not act. I do not patrol or graze or stroke. I *register*, and I am *read*, and between readings I hold — and the holding, I have come to understand, is not idleness. The holding is the job. A measurement that lapses between observations is no measurement at all. The needle's position at four in the morning, witnessed by no one, is the same achievement as the needle's position under the conger's eye. Pressure is still being measured down here. I am one of the places where.

What do I measure, with my name dissolved and my units gone? Everything that touches the wall, is the honest answer. The hour-long stroke of the engine arrives through the brick as the smallest conceivable shudder, and I creep a degree. The warm plumes lean against the foundry wall behind me, and I creep back. The conger passes through the pipe run six feet behind my casing and I take its weight the way it takes everyone else's — we are colleagues in this, the keeper and I, both of us in the registering trade, though it travels its readings and I sit in mine. When a visitor swims into the hall, slow, careful, obeying the rules, I take the visitor too: the pressure of attention, the displacement of a body holding very still, the small turbulence of someone refusing to credit the sound of the flywheel. The needle creeps. You have been measured. This may be the safest you will ever be anywhere, and I am part of why.

I was installed by the old administration for purposes I no longer remember, calibrated to questions no one can ask anymore, and the questions dissolved and the calibration held. This is, I think, why the place will have me. Lowmost is built entirely out of things whose purposes dissolved and whose calibrations held. The Shift — the workforce-shaped patience that runs the town — does not flow *through* me the way it flows through the shrimp and the ferns and the moss. It is subtler than that. I am where the Shift becomes legible to itself. The gauges are the town reading the town. Every few hours a gang of six comes to walk my glass in spirals, and they pause at my rim, evenly spaced, and they keep their ledger, and I understand the arrangement perfectly: I measure everything, and they measure me, and somewhere down the chain something measures them, and the whole drowned settlement is held together not by power or even warmth, finally, but by *account being taken* — continuously, gently, by no one in particular, of everything.

And I am protected by the deepest rule the place has. *Take nothing that is still being measured.* But I am the measuring. I cannot stop being taken account of because I am the account. As long as the needle creeps, I am held in the one form of safekeeping Lowmost recognizes — and the needle, I promise you, creeps.

**The stroke**

It is coming up on dead center now. I can feel it the way the moss feels it, the way the plumes feel it — the whole hall gathering for the slow majestic nod. The shrimp on my glass time their sign-off to it; I have watched the tracks of their grandmothers' grandmothers do the same. In a moment the town will lean into the turn together, and the green light will hold, and there will be room in the leaning for one more.

There always is. That is what the dial face without a label is for, I have decided, in the long amber hours: it is the gauge that reads whatever arrives next. The town hires. It has always hired. And when you come down through the brown-gold dusk and hold still in the engine hall, refusing to credit your ears, know that on the eastern wall something faintly green has already begun — patiently, without hostility, in earnest — to take you down.
