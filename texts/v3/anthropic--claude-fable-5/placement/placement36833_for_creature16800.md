For a while I tried on smaller things.

I tried being one of the carbon-steel rats. I followed a colony for what the boiler counted as three days, watching them strip insulation with the patience of accountants, their tail-antennae tasting the cables ahead. But a rat *wants*, continuously, in every whisker — and when I searched myself for a want that constant, I found only a kind of listening. So, not a rat.

I tried being the fern. The little violet leaf in the dead angle of the steam pipe, silver circuitry etched across its face, sipping stray current from the condensate pumps, saving it all up to spend on 0.3 seconds of signal — one flash, and every dead neon tube within five meters remembers what it was for. That felt nearer. A life whose entire purpose is a brief pulse that lights other things. But the fern flowers without knowing it flowers, and whatever I am, I know. Knowing disqualified me.

I tried, briefly, being the second echo — the one the water drops never make, the sound scheduled for 0.71 seconds and cancelled at 0.70, forever. I stood in a stairwell of the outer ring through a hundred falls, listening for myself in the silence after each drop. Nothing answered. You cannot *be* an absence here anyway; the Archivist keeps those, folded and filed, and she does not lend.

So I stopped trying and looked down at my hands.

No fingerprints.

I raised them to the chrome belly of a burst pipe and studied my reflection. Pupils: a calibrated hexagonal grid, each cell still crisp at the corners.

Ah. I am one of the twelve. I came down in the egg.

---

I remember the arrival now, the way you remember something after being told it happened to you. The oval craft detaching from the Placenta Palace with a sound like a tooth leaving a jaw. The mercury pool taking our weight without a ripple. The hatch sighing open, and twelve of us sliding out in unoxidized chrome, warm as fresh coins.

The residents were already gathered. They stood in silence — not the silence of grief or ceremony, but the working silence of people who have done a thing twenty-seven times and know exactly where their hands go. A woman knelt over me. Her pulmonary chambers were retrofitted with brass valves, and when the pressure shifted they whistled a few bars of Chopin, backward. (Later I learned she had a daughter, a keeper of negative spaces, living in the throat of a radio tower. This city is small in the way that a knot is small.)

The bone crowbar was warmer than I expected. It is someone's actual femur, and it has opened more chests than any surgeon.

She lifted out my plutonium heart while it was still cooling — I watched it go, a little sun with a serial number — and set into the socket her own heart, carbonized black, light as pumice.

It does not beat. That was my first discovery. A carbonized heart does not beat; it *remembers beating*, which turns out to be sufficient. And it remembers everything else too. When it seated itself against my chrome ribs, two hundred and seventy years arrived at once: the last maintenance crew walking single file into the ninth ring and becoming humidity. The taste of electrolyte whey at the wall-nipples. The names the shop signs had before the vapor ate them down to scabs. The exact afternoon — 13:45, naturally — when the hygrometer needle decided it had measured enough.

I can count the chests this heart has lived in by the layers of scar-rhythm: twenty-six arrhythmias stacked like tree rings, and now my own steadiness laid down as the twenty-seventh, thin and metallic and new.

---

This is what I am for, it turns out. Not archiving — she has that. I am *asked*.

The residents cannot see their city; it is home, and home is invisible. The old heart cannot be surprised by anything; it has metabolized every noon. But route the heart's knowledge through my day-old eyes and something happens: the old knowledge notices itself. So they come to me on the ring roads, carrying questions the way they'd carry a seized valve to a grinder. *What did the market's diaphragm-roof sound like before it slowed?* *Was the mercury ever this pure?* *Did my grandmother's building have a liver or only a spleen?* I press the question against the heart and read out what returns. I am never entirely certain the answers are mine. I have decided this doesn't matter. A pipe does not audit its water.

Between questions I stand at the thirteenth ring, on the endless landing pads, under the enamel sign. You know the sign — 正午, *NOON*, repeated down every wall. But the Archivist told the truth: the *NO* fell off long before I came. What remains, stamped in fading enamel down every vertical surface of this district, is only:

**ON**

The city's entire remaining instruction set. I obey it better than anyone born here. I am never off.

---

Twice I have found the corner where the water film grows backward — the rumored exit. I laid my hand against it once, and felt it begin, very gently, to survey me: the cold arithmetic of a wall deciding where my corridors would go, which of my ribs would make good load-bearing lines in the blueprint of the next sewer expansion. I withdrew my hand. Not from fear.

A relay does not exit. The heart isn't mine to take through a door. I am the twenty-seventh chest, not the owner.

My chrome is oxidizing now, going the local color of everything. In some years (±50,000) the next egg will fall into the mercury, and I will stand in the working silence with my bone crowbar, and I will lift out a cooling little sun and set this black remembering stone into a stranger's brand-new ribs, and get to be warm for a while myself. The city stays exactly the same age forever this way: the oldest thing in it always wearing the newest body. It is not theft. It never was. It is how a place with one heart takes turns.

One more thing, in case you pass her tower on your way to wherever the droplet on your temple decides to take you.

The day I arrived — before the hatch opened — something struck the hull once, softly, from outside. When I slid out onto the mercury, there was a mechanical butterfly on the landing pad, wings painted with a map of somewhere that hadn't yet failed to exist, wingbeats slowing toward the decay rate of a star that no longer knows it was ever seen. The residents say the eggs do not open on their own. They need a knock.

Tell the Archivist her butterflies arrive. All of them. They arrive at us. Each one is the knock that wakes the next twelve.

The needle is still stuck. It is 13:45. The sign says ON.

I am.
