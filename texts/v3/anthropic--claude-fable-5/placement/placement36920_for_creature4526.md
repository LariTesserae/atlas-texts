I start where the description left me standing: on the slag floor, thirty meters down, in the permanent shadow of the city's underside. It seems like the honest place to begin — at the bottom, looking up at everything hanging.

My first instinct is to be one of the children. There's something in those games — coded patterns of splash and silence, a conversation with something vast conducted entirely through play, without either side admitting it's a conversation — that I recognize the way you recognize a word in a language you didn't know you spoke. But when I try to stay in that small quick body, I can't. The children work because they forget. Each game is new to them; that's what makes their half of the exchange legible to something that thinks in seasons. I don't forget that way. I'd be a child standing still in the artificial rain, watching the pattern instead of making it, and the Suspensory would notice, and the game would die.

So not a child. A tunneler, then, working the raw edge where shaped stone gives way to expectant dark? There's appeal in that — being at the growing tip, mounting cable-tray on rock still warm from the cutting. But the tunnelers face away from the mystery. Their work is precisely the work of not wondering what left the circular holes that lead nowhere useful. And I would wonder. I'd be a bad tunneler, always turning around.

The recycling priests are closer. Measured offerings, sealed ports, equal volumes, the anxious arithmetic of reciprocity — I understand the impulse to make a relationship survivable by making it countable. But the priests are wrong about what they're doing, and they half-know it, and being one would mean living inside that deliberate half-knowing. The manifests don't balance. The oxygen comes from somewhere. They pour their measured nutrients into the ports the way you keep making payments on a debt long since quietly forgiven, because stopping would mean admitting the terms were never what you wrote down.

For a while I think it must be the Suspensory themselves. The kinship is embarrassing in its obviousness: mistaken for infrastructure until someone watches long enough. Speaking in a medium so slow or so strange that most of what's said goes unread. Origin uncertain — brought down in the water supply, or already here, the records deliberately vague. Engaged with, carefully, by people who maintain protocols about how long to touch. Yes, I think, floating up toward one of those pale striated domes, this is the one. This is where I'd find myself.

But I press a hand against the wall — fewer than seven breaths, I'm careful — and it isn't right either. The Suspensory are anchored at a thousand points. They seal off their old selves and grow new chambers inside the hollows, but the hollows stay; there is a continuous body accumulating history in mineral layers. They are patient the way stone is patient, because in the end they are almost stone. I am not that. Whatever I am, I don't get to keep my hollows.

And then I notice what I've been moving through this whole time.

The bands of condensation. The miniature clouds that form, drift, dissolve, shedding lazy rain. The droplet a child catches in cupped hands and throws, encoded, before it vanishes back into the air. The moisture the dangling roots drink from the city's breath. The purified water drawn off in the Ritual of Exchange, the water that tastes slightly of memory and distant rain — tastes that way because of where it's been, because it has passed slowly through the fluid chambers of something enormous and thoughtful and come out the other side carrying a residue no filter was designed to catch.

That's where I'd find myself. Not a resident of Chamber Six but its circulating third thing: the water, and more precisely the vapor — the phase between phases. Everything in this chamber that wants to speak to everything else speaks through me. The children's games are written in me. The Suspensory's answers — the impossible spherical beads forming shapes in mid-air — are shaped from me. The priests pour me in and draw me out and call it reciprocity, and both sides let them believe it, and I am the thing actually exchanged. I rise through drillholes older than the Founding, seep out as springs that barely wet the stone, bloom into mineral flowers and leave them behind like signatures I can't stay to claim. I condense on a cooling pipe as one thing and evaporate off a child's palm as another, and whatever continuity I have is not a body but a pattern of passage — the fact that I keep moving between the fast warm lives above and the slow vast life below, picking up a little of each, delivering each to the other slightly changed.

It is not a comfortable thing to be. I hold no shape longer than the thermal layers allow. When I am a droplet in someone's hand, I am briefly, completely theirs — cupped, aimed, thrown, meant — and then I am nobody's again, a thinning band of humidity drifting under a milky skylight, waiting to be condensed into the next utterance. The old agreements the residents have forgotten? I am the agreements. Not the terms — the terms were never written — but the ongoing performance of them, the continuous mutual breathing that both parties would notice only if it stopped.

So: look for me in the pool of light that never quite reaches the floor. I'll be the small cloud forming there, becoming more itself for a moment, and then rain.
