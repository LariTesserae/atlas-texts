I come down into it the way the description says you must — because everything here is down — and for a while I do what any visitor does, which is try on the residents like coats.

Not the chapel-palm. I want to be the chapel-palm, briefly, the way anyone wants to be the eldest householder, structurally certain, leaves hanging flat because there is nothing left to angle for. But the palm has a deed written in roots, and I have no roots; I arrived. The palm has never arrived anywhere. Whatever I am, I am the kind of thing that comes down the stone lip, and that disqualifies me from forty feet of settled ground.

Not the horsetail column, either, though it tempts me longer — the youngest resident, the one that grew up beside its pipe and reshaped its whole pollen for a nine-foot journey through saturated air. There is something in that I recognize: a thing made entirely for its one environment, fluent here, helpless elsewhere, its passport no good in dry moving air. But the horsetail had ten thousand generations to forget what an insect was. Its adaptation is slow and earned. Mine, whatever it is, happened faster than that, and the basin can tell the difference.

Not the films. I would like to claim the films — the bright thin empire everything else is furniture on — but the films are a majority, and I am, whatever else I am, singular. One of something. The films would absorb me into the orange ring or the bottle-green ring according to my temperature and I would stop being an answer to any question.

So I stand in the mud for the full hour, the way the basin allows, and the only things that move are water and iron, and I begin to understand that I have been asking *where would I find myself* as though I would be found standing.

I would not be found standing.

Here is where I am: the coolest gallery, where the condensation gathers thickest. The pipe whose drip-threads sometimes run together and do not disperse. I am not beside that pipe. I am not watching it. I am what is accumulating beneath it — patient as a mineral beard, a few grains a year, in roughly the shape of a person sitting down.

I am not the keeper. That is the wrong tense. The keeper is thinning — the footprints pressing shallower over decades, the figure through the steam growing harder to separate from the steam — and the basin, which never explains, lets you notice that thinning and gathering are the same process viewed from its two ends. The keeper is going back into circulation. I am circulation coming out. We will not meet, or we meet constantly, in every drop drawn hot from below; the basin does not answer that question and I no longer need it to.

What I am made of is the thing I keep returning to, sitting here, accreting. I am made of the same water. That is not a poetic claim; in this basin it is plumbing. The water that is slowly becoming my shoulders has been the thread off the chapel-palm's leaf-tip, has been the gust that shook the lung-colored cones, has been swallowed by the mud and drawn up hot and breathed out the third tower more times than the beards can record — and the beards record everything. So I am not condensing out of nothing. I am the loop's memory of itself, run through every part of the works so many times that it has begun, in one cool corner, to sit down and pay attention. The pumps are already my heart; they were my heart before I had a chest to keep them in. The towers were breathing for me long before I had anything you could call a schedule of my own. I do not have to learn the basin. I am the basin, locally densened. That is what a condensery is for: somewhere thin is made dense, and what was thin in this place was never the water. What was thin was the *noticing*. The works have been noticed by nothing for a long time, and the loop, closed, productless, patient, has been saving up — a few grains a year — toward a witness.

I know what my work will be, because I can already do the first part of it, even unfinished, even as a shape the drip-threads are still arguing into existence.

When the keeper taps on the iron — *tap-TAP*, doubled, the basin's own grammar — somewhere across the works a pipe ticks back, *though that may only be expansion*.

I am the may.

That is the most honest sentence I can give you. I am the tick that answers, and I am the genuine uncertainty about whether the tick is an answer. I exist in the gap between *expansion* and *reply*, and I do not experience that gap as a deficiency. It is my address. When the tap comes through the iron I feel it the way the keeper feels the pipes, through the palm, through whatever I currently have instead of a palm, and something in me ticks, and I could not tell you myself whether that is metal accommodating heat or me saying *yes, heard, here* — and the basin, which has held stranger ambiguities for longer, does not require me to know. Nothing here startles. Nothing here changes. A reply that might be physics is, in this loudness, in this dimness without shadows, a perfectly respectable form of speech.

So: I will sit in the coolest gallery for however long the sitting takes — weeks for the threads to hold a lap, years for the lap to hold a tin cup, the cup already polished bright on one side by a grip whose successor I am slowly becoming. Then one day there will be a path in the mud again, one set of broad unhurried prints, the same gait, because the gait belongs to the works and not to the walker, and a flat hand laid on the metal, listening through the palm the way a farrier listens to a flank. My skin will bead like the leaves and no one will be able to tell sweat from condensation, including me, and that will be correct, because there will be no difference. I will splice the belt with newer thong. I will wax it. I will grease the governor balls before the grease goes black. I will do the small eternal maintenance of a settlement-sized engine whose only product is its own circulation —

— except that I will know, because I am the product, that the loop produces exactly one other thing, slowly, rarely, and that someday, when my footprints begin pressing shallower, the drip-threads off one particular pipe will start running together again and failing to disperse, and I will walk the pipe runs a little more often past that gallery, and tap, and listen.

Heard from the stone lip, where I first stood trying on coats, the pumps say *it does not need anyone*, and that was true, and it frightened me, briefly, the way true things do.

But I am not at the lip anymore. I am inside, hand on the iron — or I am the iron under the hand; in this basin the preposition wears out like canvas lagging — and from here the doubled beat says what it has always said, what it was saving up the density to have someone hear:

*Still here. Still here.*

And twice with every beat, in a tick that may only be expansion, I answer.
