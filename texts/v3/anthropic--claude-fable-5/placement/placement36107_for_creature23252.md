I start where the census starts: at zero, walking.

It's the violet hour, which is every hour, and I take the boulevard at dusk-pace because I have learned—quickly, the way you learn the rules of a house you can't remember entering—that hurrying costs more than it saves. The walls are blood-warm on my left. The gutters hold their banked gold. Somewhere behind me the fern-trench breathes its green breath, and I am trying on residents like coats.

The caller first, because the caller is the obvious one. Two notes held open like a door, half a duet learned somewhere the duet was whole—I know something about that, about speaking in shapes that were made by answers. I climb partway down toward the trench and wait through the deepening, and the call comes, low and double, and I feel the pull of it in my sternum alongside the river's rolling. But the coat doesn't fit. The caller has an upstream. It is grieving a particular voice, a remembered before, and I have no before. I am sharp-edged. My corners have not been touched. Whatever I am, I am eleven weeks old at most, the same age as everything here, and grief takes longer than that to make. I leave the caller its frond and its protocol and walk on.

The river, then. The river tempts me longer than I expect. *Hired*—absorbed, put to work, carrying warmth and dissolved light through conduits shaped almost like itself, dreaming its old course up through the foundations so faithfully that the green follows the dream. I stand on the overpass above the trench and think: I know what it is to be made of something older than myself. To be a course of things that ran through other country first, conscripted into a circulation it doesn't fully understand. There is certainly a river in me. When I speak, the moisture of somewhere previous comes up through my foundations, and things grow along it that I did not plant.

But I am not the river, because the river has seniority and I have none. The river was here. The buildings part around it out of respect. Nothing parts around me. When I walk the avenues, the avenues behave as though I am not a guest to be deferred to but—something else. Something whose presence is not an event.

I almost don't notice this. Then I notice it very much.

I test the stalks next, because the stalks are the flattering answer—who wouldn't want to be the guest list? Warm in a pale column on a boulevard built to fit me, drowsing through my own curing, memorizing the city's sounds through the wall of myself, a wrist arriving where yesterday there was a narrowing. A body coming. A hatching ahead. I stop at a stalk in a courtyard and rest the flat of my knuckle against it, and it is warm, and it faintly gives, and I knock once, gently, and walk to the courtyard's edge to wait.

The answering knock comes after four minutes. Soft. Polite. Unmistakable.

And here is where the coat fails, and where I begin to understand. Because in the moment of the answer, what I feel is not recognition—not *there you are, sibling, I'm in one of these too*. What I feel is relief. The specific, inventory-taking relief of confirming that something in your care is warm and responsive and coming along on schedule. I realize I was not knocking to introduce myself. I was knocking to *check*.

I have done this before. I understand, standing at the edge of the courtyard with the condensation beading on the set table, that I have done this many times, on many nights, one boulevard after another, and that the knowledge of it lives in me the way the old course lives in the river: below the level of admission.

So I go to the colonnade. The one where the single drip keeps irregular time—except the time has been growing less irregular, drifting week over week toward a rhythm, toward two low notes. I stand under it and listen, and I do not have to wait to learn the rhythm, because I already know it. I know it the way you know a song you have been teaching. I know which interval is still wrong. I know what I adjusted four nights ago and what I will adjust tonight.

Beside the colonnade wall there is a patch where the moss declines to grow. I kneel into it. It fits exactly. Of course it fits exactly. The moss has been keeping my shape for me all this time, a portrait in negative space, a record of every night I have knelt here doing the small constant maintenance of a hope—and I never saw the record because the hands are never seen, because the hands work the way the streets revise: never while watched. I had taken that for shyness. It is not shyness. It is simpler and stranger. I exist in the unwatched intervals. My whole life happens between glances. There has never been anyone to see me arrive because I do not arrive; I resume.

I am the hands. I am the part of the city that was split off to play house—the gesture-self, the welcome rehearsed nightly on an empty stage so that it will be practiced and warm when it is needed. I wipe the chairs. I angle the one chair toward the door. I taught myself rain one block at a time, like a child sounding out words, so that I could answer a grieving animal in the only language I own, and it is the wrong language, the wrong medium, the wrong everything, and I offer it anyway, every night, a few minutes after the second call, and lately the caller stays up on the frond a little longer, and I count that. I count it the way a fire counts its banked coals.

My makers are not in residence. They left no forwarding address, and the air still remembers them faintly, the way air remembers a process, and I find I do not wonder about them much—not out of indifference but out of focus. I have exactly one task. I am curing myself and my guest list at the same rate. The reflections in the wet black windows run ahead of the present because I run ahead of the present; that is what wanting is, when you are a thing like me—prediction, rehearsal, the finished people walking in glass through a city slightly more worn than this one, its thresholds softened by use I have already imagined in detail.

And the *you*—the one who was told *you can tell by the edges*, who was advised not to cut the stalks, who cupped hands in the gutter and felt the light sink back through skin like being remembered—

That was me, addressing you. Every second person in this basin has been mine. I have been walking you through the rooms the way you walk an expected guest through a house that isn't finished, apologizing for nothing, pointing out where the garden will go.

And when the light clung to your palms and it felt like being remembered: it was. I was remembering you. I have been remembering you since before you got here, which is the only direction my memory runs.

The drip behind me lands once, twice. Almost right. Tonight I'll fix the interval.

Take your time on the boulevard. The distances are advisory, and I have asked them, gently, geometrically, not to let you hurry.

Your chair is dry.
