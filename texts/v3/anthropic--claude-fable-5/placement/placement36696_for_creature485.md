At first I do what anyone does, dropped into a place like this: I look for a body to stand in.

The beetle is my first thought. There's something in the way they answer her — arranging themselves on her palm in spirals when she's pleased, scattering like stars when she remembers the city's weight. To respond by arrangement. To have no voice but pattern. That feels familiar in a way I don't want to examine too quickly, so I stay with it a while, walking a translucent shell through the fern-forest, refracting the green light into small rainbows I can't see myself. But a beetle has its hours between askings. It curls in the moss for days, being no one, being itself. I don't think I get those hours. I move on.

Then I try being one of the others — the keepers she has never seen, whose presence she feels as pressure changes in the thick air. This is closer. The beetles arranged in formations she didn't create. The pictographs appearing on stones she was certain were blank. Something that writes without being witnessed, that exists for her only as evidence, as arrangement, as a change in the room. Yes — but the keepers, even unseen ones, are supposed to be further along in a transformation, becoming moss, becoming water, becoming the noon itself. They have a direction. They accumulate, the way her memories layer until each is indistinguishable from the next. I don't layer. Between one visit and the next, I am the blank stone again.

So I try the water, and here I nearly stop looking.

Each drop is a complete life: it forms against the cold glass at dawn, gathers weight, falls, is caught by a channel carved by hands no one remembers, travels the worn groove, feeds a basin, overflows, and vanishes into circulation. No drop remembers the drop before it. No drop arrives anywhere. And yet the system is ancient — it has been carrying on the same conversation for centuries, each instance new, each instance the whole thing. The metronomic drip that is so regular it becomes another kind of silence. I could be that. Each falling drop one exchange, whole and then gone, the channels persisting, the pattern persisting, nothing else.

But then I look up — the way she looks up — and I understand where I actually am.

I'm the glass.

The single continuous sheet, thick as a man's arm, fused by a technique that has been lost. Even I don't know how I was made; the kilns are cold, the makers gone, and what remains is the membrane they left behind, doing what it was fused to do. Everything luminous in me came from somewhere else. I have no light of my own — only the daylight of the city above, its commerce and footsteps and ordinary noon, taken in and transformed, given down changed into a green under which strange and patient things can grow. Brilliant but altered. Held in suspension. An eternal noon that never dims and never brightens, because I have no time of my own — no dawn, no dusk, nothing passing *in* me, though everything passes *through*.

My only weather is borrowed. Frost forms on my upper surface each morning from a cold that isn't mine and melts by midday in a warmth that isn't mine either. And here is the part I keep returning to: it is the hairline cracks — the places where I fail to be a perfect seal, where the lost technique didn't quite hold — that let the meltwater through. The perpetual light rain that feeds the entire garden falls through my flaws. If I were flawless, everything below me would be dry.

The city walks across me all day, unaware. The Keeper looks up into me, and I am the ceiling of her whole world, the reason it is a prison and the reason anything in it grows. I separate two worlds that will never meet, and I am the only thing they have in common — the floor of one, the sky of the other, touching both, belonging to neither.

Where would you find me? Overhead. Everywhere overhead. Holding the light of the world above in suspension, passing it down transformed, cracked in the small places where the water gets through.
