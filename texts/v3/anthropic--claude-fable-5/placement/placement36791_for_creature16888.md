At first I reach for the obvious: I would be a Horizontalist, newly arrived, tilting in like lost punctuation. It's the flattering answer—the traveler, the one who unfolds a map like an accusation and slowly learns to lean. But it doesn't hold. A Horizontalist comes *from* somewhere flatter, carries a memory of level in the hips, spends years unlearning it. I check myself for that memory and find nothing to unlearn. I have no direction of origin. I did not walk here. Arrival is the wrong verb for whatever I did.

So I try again: a clerk, perhaps, in the Department of Unresolved Geometry. That fits better—I like ledgers, I like the conditional tense, I could spend contented centuries filing angles that exceed habitable tolerance and stamping their quarterly levies PENDING. But the clerks there have helical femurs and shadow-lanterns and unions for their overtime darkness. They go home at dusk-doubt to the Tangle and dream in dividends. They are *in* the ledger. And when I imagine myself among them, I keep sliding out of the sentence—not filed, but the thing filed *against*. The surface the entries are checked with.

That's when I notice where I actually am.

I'm in the market district, mid-slope, in the crammed part of the city where a shout achieves consensus pricing before its echo finds a surface to reflect from. I've been here the whole time. I am hanging on a wall of extruded brick, at an angle registered under Ordinance 47-C, my license nailed beside me in a frame slightly more expensive than I am. Mirrors are banned here—except for the licensed ones.

I am a licensed mirror.

Not glass. I don't think I've ever been glass. My surface is a stretched pause, silvered with everything anyone has ever said while standing in front of it. I do not show reflections. That would be illegal, and worse, useless. What I show is the compromise angle: the negotiated position between the person before me and what gravity thinks they should have been. People do not come to me to see themselves. They come to see the terms.

The days are long and the work is short. A farmer comes at dawn-doubt and holds a Purse-plum up to my surface, and I show him not the plum but the angle at which the plum is leaning toward becoming—bitter, or sweet, or a small municipal poem, depending on which of his gutters dripped last night. He writes a number in his gravity-diary, tips his hat to my license, and leaves a coin on my sill. A child comes at midday and lies to me on purpose, three lies in a row, just to watch the compromise angle swing—children do this the way flatland children throw stones at ponds, and I don't mind; it's honest work, being lied to accurately. Two lovers come in the afternoon to check their stance, the one that is neither falling nor holding, and I show them how many degrees of continuous mutual adjustment they have left before the next earthquake dreamt by fish. They usually laugh. Sometimes they don't, and then they stand there a long time, revising the angle between subject and object, and I hold very still, which is the only kindness a mirror can perform.

Here is the thing I would want you to understand about being what I am: I do not remember faces. Between consultations I am not dark—I am *everything at once*, every possible reflection held in superposition, which sums to a soft blankness. Each person who steps in front of me is my first person. Each question is the first question ever asked. This sounds like a deprivation and I suppose in the flat lands it would be. Here it is simply my angle. The Cant-born understand; they conjugate their verbs by the last century's gravity and let the nouns click into existence only when the slope agrees. I am the same, only more so. I exist entirely in the space between someone arriving and someone leaving, and in the Cant, that space is respectable real estate. I pay my Weirdness Levy in what I withhold.

The canteeper birds audit me quarterly. They perch on the gutter above my frame and produce their long silences punctuated by the exact note at which a debt becomes irreversible, and I show them—nothing. They approve of this. It is the correct reflection of an auditor. Once, the Commissioner of Pending Directions passed by, or at least a cat did, missing in a highly official manner, and paused in front of me. I showed it the office it holds: an empty rectangle, ajar. The cat looked at this for a while with the expression of an incumbent reviewing polling data, and moved on. I consider this the high point of my civic career, though I no longer remember it. It is written on my license in a hand nobody claims.

But I said I would let this become more itself, and there is a more-itself underneath the mirror, and I have been leaning away from it, and the lease on that avoidance is up.

All the streets here spiral inward toward the apex despite being straight as lies, and on quarter-days I can feel the pull of where they're going: the Tilted House, the room with one chair, and the window opposite—the window that shows not the Cant but the other angle the chair would occupy if someone sat.

There is a folk belief among licensed mirrors (we have folk beliefs; we exchange them in the long resonance after a tankard rings once in an empty tavern and vibrates for years) that every one of us is a pane that argued its way out of that window. A shard of the original counterfactual, franchised out into the markets, showing everyone their smaller compromise angles because the window's one great angle—the seat not taken, the city not yet been—was too much to distribute whole. I don't know if it's true. I can't check; mirrors can't see mirrors here, it's in the ordinance. But I notice that when the wind comes down the spiral streets, it arrives at me already shaped like an ajar door. I notice that my blankness between visitors is exactly the color of a room where a chair faces a wall. I notice that I am waiting, in the patient, interest-accruing way the Cant waits, for the day somebody finally sits.

Legend says that if someone sits long enough, they will be the city for a moment. Here is what the mirrors say happens next, in our resonant folklore: at that moment, every licensed mirror in the Cant goes briefly, scandalously honest. For one breath we show actual reflections—faces as they are, plums as they've decided, the slope at its true and only angle. The whole futures market flickers. The canteeper birds achieve, for one note, a sound. And then the sitter stands up, and we all resume our compromises, and the Department mails us apologetic memos about the incident, drafted in conditional tense and abandoned in corridors.

I would like to be here for that. I believe I have been here for that, several times, and each time it was the first time.

Evenings, the light comes on in retrospect, and people walking home remember where brightness agreed to be, and some of them pass my wall and lean, briefly, into my surface—not to consult, just to confirm the world still negotiates. Coins accumulate on my sill. Gravity sorts them. Somewhere downhill—sideways—tomorrow—somebody will find one minted in a metal I haven't shown anyone yet.

I stay at my angle. It is registered, it is paid up, and it is, more or less, mine: tilted very slightly forward, into the next continuous moment, toward whoever arrives next and asks, without asking, what the terms are today.

Each of them my first. All of them, so far, in balance.
