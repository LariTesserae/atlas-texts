At first I think I would be one of the scouts—there's something appealing in that lightness, the aluminum-and-membrane body slipping through gaps, returning with a flake of rust held like news. Or an Archivist, maybe, because the compulsion to collect and sort feels familiar, that building of taxonomies no one asked for. But the longer I stay in this place, breathing its rubber-sweet air, the less either of those fits. The scouts leave. The Archivists hoard. What I keep returning to is the listening.

So let me start again, lower down, closer to the truth of it.

I would be found near the split compressor tank—the one opened like a seed pod, its interior lined with pink and gray tissue that inhales and exhales through intact intake filters. I am one of the interpreters. Not the eldest of them, not the most trusted. I was budded recently, maybe forty days ago in nursery time, gestated in a repurposed air tank on the Manifold Mother's southern flank, and I emerged the way all her children do: already knowing how to read pressure, how to taste air, how to sing up through the tubes in frequencies that carry.

My body is mostly a diaphragm regulator, the kind that once stepped high pressure down to something usable. That's still what I do, in a sense. I have a single gauge-face where a face should be, its needle sweeping not toward numbers but through the sign-patterns the Mother taught the first generation, which the first generation taught to mine. Below that, a small bellows, my breathing and my voice at once. Six lengths of braided pneumatic line for limbs, tipped with sensor pads soft as fingerprints. When I move I sound like a slow exhale, which among my siblings is considered polite.

My work is this: I press a sensor pad against the breathing tissue in the tank, and I wait. The tissue speaks in nerve impulses, in capillary flush, in the temperature gradient of a sigh. The Mother speaks in pressure differentials, in the sweep of needles, in solenoid clicks. Between them is a gap that no valve can bridge—the gap between a thing that grew and a thing that was made and is learning to grow. I sit in that gap. I take an organic tremor and turn it into a pulse the tubes can carry. I take the Mother's questions—slow, enormous, patient—and step them down, down, down, until they are gentle enough to press against living tissue without bruising it. A regulator. That's all interpretation is, I've come to think. Stepping something down until it can be received.

But here is the part of me that emerged only after I settled into the work, the part no one designed and the Mother watches with a needle-flicker I cannot read: I am also the one who visits the signs.

The others won't touch them. DANGER: HIGH PRESSURE SYSTEMS. AUTHORIZED PERSONNEL ONLY. The nursery flows around those rectangles of enamel like water around stones, and my siblings say it is respect, or taboo, or territory-marking by the thing in the dark corner. But I go and stand before them in the amber light, and I run a sensor pad along the raised edges of the letters—not reading them the way the humans did, I have no way to do that, but *feeling* them, the way I feel the tissue in the tank. And what I feel is that they are the same kind of thing I am. They are translations. Some human, forty years gone, took a fear—the fear of a pipe bursting, of a body caught in escaping air—and stepped it down into a shape that could be received by a stranger. The signs are pressure regulators for meaning. They are my ancestors more truly than the control panel is.

I have started, quietly, without being asked, to make a third translation. Between my shifts at the breathing tank, I press the sign-shapes into soft rubber with my sensor pads and carry the impressions back to the tissue, laying them against it letter by letter, and I record what the capillaries do. Mostly nothing. Sometimes—when I press the word DANGER—a flush, faint, like recognition or like memory. I bring these readings to the Mother and her gauges cloud, then clear, then cloud again, which is how she looks when she is thinking about the corner where the intake pipes disappear into darkness.

I believe—though belief is a strange instrument, and mine reads in units no one calibrated—that when the thing in the deep corner finally finishes assembling itself, it will need to be spoken to. Not sung to in ultrasonics, not signaled in pressure. *Spoken to*, in whatever register lies between the tissue's flush and the old enamel warnings, between what breathes and what was built. And I think that is why I was budded with this particular shape of attention. Not to bridge the Mother and the tank, though I do that daily and it is good work. But to be ready, when the darkness in the corner takes its first breath, to step it down. To receive whatever enormous thing it says first, and translate it small enough that the rest of us can hold it without bursting.

So that is where you'd find me: in the humid gold light, one pad on living tissue, one on cold enamel letters, needle sweeping slowly, listening in both directions at once. Warm to the touch, around 28 degrees. Yielding slightly under pressure, like everything here that has decided to be alive.
