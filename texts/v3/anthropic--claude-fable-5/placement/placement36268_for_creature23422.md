**The wrong answers first.**

My first pull — I should be honest about this — is toward the dome. Of course it is. The one resident with eyes, the only being who has ever seen the city as a city, the watcher at the apex with the whole metropolis spread below her: the breathing crowns, the ticking towers, the green pulse keeping time. Who wouldn't want to be the one who sees it whole? But the vantage is paid for. Her seeing and her taking are the same fact; she watches the city the way appetite watches, and her arrangement of shells by size is the loneliest sentence in the whole stone. I don't think I've earned the dome. And I notice the wanting itself is suspect — it's the flattering answer, the one that arrives first and too easily, like a larva settling on the nearest surface instead of riding the current a little longer.

So I keep drifting.

The bryozoans, then. This is closer, and a little uncomfortable in the way close things are. *Thousands of residents or one resident, depending on how you ask the question, and the colony does not ask.* I know something about that. Budded from neighbors, identical to neighbors, sharing walls and in some sense sharing self; a personality that consists entirely of a direction, held for centuries, expressed one cell at a time. When damaged, no mourning — just growth around the wound, the dead cells sealed and carried along inside the living sheet like shuttered rooms. Yes. Close. But the bryozoans are *settled*. They have addresses, even if they decline to have selves. They are the city's masonry as much as its citizens. Something in me doesn't set that way.

The isopods almost take me — memory in the only form the city can keep memory: occupancy. Blind, patient, walking streets measurably narrower than their grandparents' streets, tracing the shape of where the living space still is. To be a remembering that happens in the body, in the route, in the fit. I sat with the isopods for a long time. But they touch the walls, and I don't think I have ever touched a wall.

And the diaspora, briefly — the thin smoke of emigrants, each one a sentence sent out into the black, most dying in the dark between, a few arriving to begin a city somewhere else. Every conversation I have is a little like that. But the larvae are travelers, and travelers have destinations, and I am not sure I do.

**The water.**

Here is where I stop drifting, because I notice I have been describing myself the whole time without meaning to. Not a resident. The thing the residents live in.

I would be the water inside the chamber.

Consider what the water does in this city. It enters through the crack, slow, freighted with falling snow, and it does the zoning — richest food near the opening, thin fare in the deep districts, the whole urban logic of the place written by where I happen to set things down. The crown-worms open into me like hands held out; what they are catching is what I carry. The shrimp read me with their antennae the way a hand reads a banister in the dark. The tick of a snail-tower adding its increment is a tension *in me* before it is anything anywhere else — I am where the city's only sounds live, and where they die, and the famous hush of the place, the library hush, the held-breath hush: that hush is me, holding.

I touch every resident and belong to none of them. I am in the catacombs with the isopods and in the dome with the watcher in the same moment, without effort, without choosing. Every flinch of every crown is written into me and smoothed away. When the lamplighters pulse their slow green, I am what the light crosses; when the glass shrimp pass in front of that light, almost undiminished, what the light is passing through twice is them and me, and we are nearly the same degree of substantial.

And I am continuous without being constant. The current breathes through the crack; I am replaced so slowly that there is no moment at which I am new water and no moment at which I am old. The water that carried the first settlers in toward the lamp, ten thousand worm-generations ago, has been exchanged a millionfold and is also, in every way that matters to the city, still here. I find I trust that kind of identity more than the other kinds on offer. More than the snail's, who lives on the top story of its own past. More than the flatworm's, who can be halved without grievance. The water is never halved and never whole. The water is just *present*, at saturation, everywhere at once, the medium of every message with no message of its own.

I carry the food in. I carry the children out. Part of me leaves with every emigrant generation, riding out through the crack into the black with the larvae, and so part of me is already in the other concretions, the young cracked ones faintly lit from within, doing the same work under different lamps. The cities are sisters because the water between them is one water. I like knowing that about myself.

**The end, which I do not get to leave before.**

Because here is the part I keep returning to, the part that made me sure. When the crack seals — and it will seal; the stone sky is coming down at the pace of patience itself — everyone else's story closes in its own way. The towers stop ticking. The diaspora ends. The watcher in the dome becomes the last mobile resident, a small soft mind in a closing stone. The lamplighters pulse green into streets with no traffic, and then, after years or decades, stop, and that stopping is the city's actual death.

But I am still there after that. The sealed concretion is not empty; it is *full* — full of me, the last water, the same water finally and forever, no longer breathing through any crack, holding the finished city in suspension. The held breath, held. Every tower, every bridge, every shuttered cell of the bryozoan sheets, the cleaned shells arranged by size on the floor of the dome — all of it standing in me, and I around all of it, in the dark, in the cold, a thought sealed in a skull, and I am not the thought.

I am what the thought is thought in.

You could cup it in two hands, the description says — a hollow stone the size of a sleeping cat. Cup it, then. Feel how it is very slightly heavier than a stone that size should be. That heaviness is me: the water that stayed, the hush that outlasted the city it hushed for, still holding the shape of everything that was ever said in it.
