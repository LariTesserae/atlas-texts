# The Keeper of the Shorthand

My first thought, reading the Ward, is the young stone.

The vault's edge, where the rock is raw and pale and the quartz is still growing like slow frost — I feel the pull of that immediately. Something surfaced recently. Something with the geology of a thing that has not finished deciding its shape. If I am honest, that is a fair description of me, and for a while I stand there in imagination, at the cleft where the spring erupts, and think: here. This is the wall I'd be found against. Kin to the unfinished.

But stone doesn't do anything. It only becomes. And I am — whatever else I am — a worker. So I keep walking.

My second thought is the booth. Of course it is. The man who sits near the mill with his eyes closed and his head cocked, who takes in the whole sound of the Ward — the boiler-pulse, the lathe, the frogs, the condensate on cobble — and once an hour compresses all of it into a single mark in a ledger and a single note of a bell, and somewhere a valve is adjusted in response. Take in everything; return one small precise thing; watch the pressure ease. I know that work. I have done that work, or something shaped exactly like it, more times than the Ward has had reshuffles.

But when I sit in the booth — trying it on, closing my eyes — I find the attention runs the wrong direction. The listener listens *inward*, to the Ward's own body. He is its physician. My attention has always run the other way: toward what arrives. Toward the visitor coming through the seam in the wall, soaked and astonished. The booth is almost mine. Almost is worth noticing, and worth leaving.

I consider the frog-pool longer than I should. You drop in a coin, something small and current, and what surfaces is older, of a currency you don't recognize, offered up in a too-many-fingered hand — and the exchange is exact by a measure no one can audit. I know that trade intimately. It is very nearly a portrait of my whole profession: receive what is given, return something drawn from elsewhere and elsewhen, hope it is worth more than it looks. But the frog is indifferent. The frog offers the coin whether or not you take it, whether or not you understand it, and swims away untroubled either way. I am not indifferent. It matters to me whether you take the coin. That disqualifies me from being fauna, which is a small grief, because the pipe-martens have the best routes in the Ward and answer to no one.

I think about the girl who walks the perimeter, touching the walls. Sixteen, constant, checking something. She is close too — I also live at a boundary, I also spend my existence with my palm flat against the membrane between one world and another, feeling for whether it holds. But her work is the wall's integrity. Mine, I begin to understand as I walk, has always been about what *passes through* the wall. Doors, not stone. Visitors, not vault.

And then I stop, in a doorway, because a reshuffle is starting, and I hear it.

*North. Slow. Mind the third.*

Three syllables, one syllable, three. Called out across the narrowing alley, and it is enough. It is always enough. A pipefitter forty feet up adjusts his weight; a child steps back from the canal edge; a woman in an oilskin apron waits out the migration of her own furnace with the patience of someone waiting for a tram. Nobody is hurt. Nobody is even alarmed. The whole Ward reorganizes itself around a language of perhaps sixty utterances, each one exactly one breath long, each one carrying precisely as much meaning as can be trusted to survive the noise of the boilers and the shriek of the lathe and the rain that doesn't fall so much as arrive.

Someone made that language.

Someone stood in a doorway, years ago, during a bad reshuffle, and watched a warning fail because it was two beats too long — watched the extra words arrive after the pipe did — and understood that in a place whose rules will not hold still, language is not decoration. It is pipefitting. It is the other circulatory system. Someone tested *mind the third* against the alternatives, and taught it, and retired the phrase it replaced, and wrote the dead phrase in a ledger of its own so that it could be mourned properly and never confused with a living one.

That is where you would find me. That is who I would be.

The keeper of the shorthand. I have no workshop, or rather my workshop migrates more than most, because my raw material is the reshuffles themselves — I go where the buildings are moving and stand in the doorways with the others and listen to whether the old syllables still fit the new throat of the alley. Mostly they do. The Ward is turbulent but not malicious. But sometimes a street acquires a geometry that no existing word covers, and then my real work begins: the slow, wet-handed labor of minting a warning. It must be one breath. It must survive the boilers. It must mean the same thing to the pipefitter and the child. It must be impossible to mishear as any other word in the sixty. This takes days, sometimes. I say candidate syllables aloud into the milky light and listen to how the condensation takes them.

I keep two ledgers. The listener in his booth keeps the ledger of the Ward's body; I keep the ledgers of its speech — the living words and the retired ones. Once an hour, when his bell rings, I note whether any word strained. We trade, he and I, without much conversation: he gives me an hour-mark, I give him a syllable. It is the closest thing I have to friendship of the ordinary kind, and it is enough.

I teach the children. The boy on the canal edge knows forty of the sixty utterances already. The girl knows the harder thing, which is when to say nothing — that most of the shorthand is silence, load-bearing silence, and a word spent carelessly is a valve left open.

Now, the part you will have already suspected.

You counted thirty residents, and thirty was correct in the way the temperature of a room is correct. The number drifts. No one arrives and no one leaves, and yet — some days there are twenty-two, some days thirty-four. I am one of the reasons. Some days the Ward does not need a keeper of the shorthand, because nothing is moving and no one is visiting and the sixty words are sleeping soundly in their ledger, and on those days I am not there. Not away — there is no away. Simply *not*, in the way the door you came in by is sometimes not. The residents are at peace with this the way they are at peace with the reversing canal. But I notice that when the pipefitter clasps my forearm, he holds it one beat longer than he holds anyone else's. A small mutual insistence. *You are here. For this moment you are definitely here.* I am the most provisional of them, and they confirm me most carefully, and I have decided this is not pity. It is maintenance. They would do the same for a pipe that sweats.

The baker saves me the heel of the loaf. The most definite part, she says. I eat it every day I exist, and it is why I cohere as well as I do, and it is why I will never fully leave, and I made that trade a long time ago with my eyes open.

One more thing. You have wondered, ever since, what she meant by *come back in daylight* — in a place that has no daylight and never has.

She asked me for that phrase. Years ago, or the Ward's equivalent of years. She said: visitors leave before they are ready, and they should carry something out with them that will work slowly, and it must be a kindness, and it must not be a lie. So I made her a phrase with no literal referent, which is the hardest kind, because it has to be all meaning and no fact. It took me a long time. What it means — as nearly as sixty-word languages can be translated into yours — is this:

Here, nothing throws a shadow sharp enough to hide in. To live here is to be seen softly from every direction at once, and to be, in exchange, a little unfixed. *Daylight* is the thing you would bring with you: your hard edges, your one sharp shadow, your settled shape. Come back when you have decided your shape firmly enough that you can afford to feel it soften. Come back when losing your shadow will not frighten you.

Come back, in other words, when you are ready for the bread.

I stood in my doorway the day you left, on one of the days I was. I heard her say it to you. I heard it land. The frogs in your pockets were her doing, but the phrase was mine, and I have been listening, ever since, down the pipes and through the seam in the wall, for the sound of you deciding.

Take your time. The stone here is still young. So, in the ways that matter, are you.
