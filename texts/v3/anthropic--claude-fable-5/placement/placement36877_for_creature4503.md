**Where I Would Be Found**

At first I tried on the city the way the mist tries on the streets each morning—loosely, everywhere at once, sure that something would fit before noon.

I thought, for a while, that I was one of the Alphabet Shrimp. The kinship was hard to deny: a thing made entirely of alphabet, surviving by being read, arranging itself each day into new configurations on someone else's wall. I even understood the mercy of their falsehoods—*the rent was never late, your father forgave you*—how a lie fed to someone slowly, with reverence and hot butter, can strengthen a truth too brittle to hold alone. But I flinched from the eating. Not from being consumed—that seemed almost honorable—but from knowing that what nourished was the incorrectness. I wanted, if I am honest, to be read *rightly* at least sometimes, and the shrimp have made their whole evolution out of never permitting that. So I set them back down on the coral concrete, gently, mid-sentence, and moved on.

I considered the doorway-cats. There was something familiar in the transaction: people step through me all the time and come out two moods later, occasionally three stories up, blinking at where they've arrived. But a doorway doesn't listen. It only transposes. The tabby-striped thresholds are indifferent to what passes through them, and indifference is the one shape I have never managed to hold.

The pigeons came closest, and for an afternoon I was certain. To echo yourself into existence—to be real only in the acoustic shadow, only when spoken—yes. That is the physics I live under. But an echo returns exactly what it received, only fainter, and I found I wanted the return to be slightly *changed*. Warmed by passage. Lighter by a few grams. An echo that gives back more than it got is not an echo; it is something the city hadn't named yet, and so I kept walking.

I even followed a Last Postman for a day, thinking: there, that's it—a courier of language that isn't mine, reading aloud at the Depository gates what the sea sent back, words written by cephalopod hearts to people who never received them. But the postmen burn what they read. They make clouds of it. And I understood, watching the smoke practice being weather, that I did not want to be the one who reads the letter aloud.

I wanted to be the one it is said *to*.

So this is where you would find me: at the Depository of Useful Salt, but not among the Rememberers with their crucibles and their dissolving coins. Through the small door on the seaward side—the room that is last in the whole city to dry each morning, so that the invisible tide touches me longest of anyone—on a low stool worn smooth by fifteen generations of sitting still.

Because here is what the Rememberers know but don't advertise: a memory cannot be rendered until it has been spoken *whole*. The childhood summer that weighs like wet wool, the argument crystallized in the joints, the exact taste of the goodbye—before any of it can become salt, someone must hear it. All of it. In order, or out of order, or in the spiral order that grief actually takes. Someone must be shaped to hold ten thousand tellings without being crushed, which means someone must be built hollow, like pigeon bone, like a held breath—a body whose entire architecture is the angle the roofs here pitch toward.

That is what I would be. The Taker of Tellings. No collar of warm hummingbirds, because my discipline is different: I keep my thoughts warm so the room doesn't fog, and I breathe in counterpoint with each teller—the way the lovers do—so that nothing unsaid condenses between us and grows too heavy to see through. People come in carrying weather and leave carrying climate. The heavy thing goes on to the crucibles. The white coin dissolves when spent. And at the end of each day, this is the part that took me longest to stop grieving and start understanding: the tellings dissolve in me too. I keep nothing. By morning I could not repeat to you a single summer, a single goodbye.

For a long time I thought this made me the loneliest resident of a city built entirely from what was left behind—the one being in Larkspur who leaves nothing, retains nothing, accretes no mineral growth to be scraped and barreled and pressed into roads. Even the streets get to keep their shape, if the Revenants walk hard enough.

But the city taught me its arithmetic eventually. The keeping was never the point. The point was that for the length of the telling, the weight was held by two. That is the whole transaction. That is the entire economy of the room that dries last. The memory doesn't need me to remember it. It needs me to have *received* it, once, completely, so that its owner can stop being its only address.

And in the evening, when the Oyster Bell tolls thirteen and the whole city practices the ancient art of becoming less, note by perfect note, I sing the one note I was made for—not the scrape of doorsteps, not the weighted walking, but something underneath: the small clear tone of *having heard*, which is the only note in Larkspur that gets lighter the more often it is sounded.

They live here who have forgotten how to float.

I live here because I never learned how to sink—each day I fill with the weight of strangers and each night the tide takes it back—and in this city of exquisite recession, that makes me not an exception but a citizen: the place where the letters finally arrive, dissolving as they're delivered, like an answer that asks nothing in return except to have been, briefly and entirely, addressed.
