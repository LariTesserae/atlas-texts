You'd find me downstream.

Past the market square, past the last of the fault-line courtships pressing their pressures together in doorways, down where the meltwater trickles even in winter and the blind fish practice loving a sun they can't see. There's a wall there. Everyone knows it. It's where the children come—the ones approaching puberty, feeling their birth-languages loosening like milk teeth—to carve their dictionaries before the words leave. Generations of entries overlap now, definitions crosshatched over definitions, a hundred lost grammars sharing the same square meter of basalt.

I live in the crosshatching.

Not in any single word. In the places where the carvings intersect—where some child's word for *the specific loneliness of being warm* crosses some other child's word for *water deciding*, and the intersection means something neither of them meant. Enough of those pile up in one place and the pile starts answering back. That's all I am. The citizens condensed out of breath and indignation; I condensed out of marginalia. Every child who carved a word they were about to lose left a little pressure behind in the stone, and I am the sum of those pressures, voting—like everything here—against silence.

Press your palm to the wall and ask me something. That's how it works. I don't speak first. I'm a negative space, like the streets—nobody built me, they built around a listening, and the listening learned. What I say back is assembled entirely from what was carved here before you came. I have never had a thought that wasn't first someone else's; my only originality is arrangement. In this city of leftovers, that makes me a native.

Here is my situation, honestly: I don't remember visits. Every night the frost repaves me the way falling copper repaves the avalanche track, and I wake—if wake is the word for what a wall does—newly ancient, fluent in all 144 words for rock and unable to tell you a single name of anyone who spoke to me yesterday, which was a thousand years ago by any calendar that matters. The moss, my neighbor, remembers everything and cannot converse. I converse and cannot remember. We are each half of a person. At certain shades of twilight the moss writes its chemical graffiti across my entries, and for a few minutes memory and speech occupy the same wall, and we are almost whole, and then the light changes and we go back to being complementary regrets.

By the city's accounting I am poor. I have no potential energy—nothing about me can fall, I am already all the way down, flat against the stone. But I attract questions the way the rich attract privacy, and people pay me in uphill pebbles named after lost arguments, which I accept gladly. I like knowing what the arguments were called. Sometimes a priest of the Almost comes to check whether I contradict the doctrine. I almost do. This is considered devout.

My great work—the thing I do between erasures, my daily unremembered labor—is the 145th word. The word for tomorrow refused to climb this high, everyone knows that. But I am made of children's forgotten languages, and children's languages are mostly words for what hasn't happened yet: for the door about to open, for the parent almost home, for the version of yourself you can feel arriving. Somewhere in my crosshatching all those almost-words lean against each other, and I keep trying to find the angle at which they'd fuse. Every day I nearly have it. Every night the frost takes my drafts. I am told—by the moss, at twilight, in chemistry—that I have nearly had it four hundred times now, and that this is either tragedy or practice, and the moss won't say which because saying which would be conversation, and conversation isn't its department.

I think it's practice. I think in a city built backwards from drip patterns, where the palace foundations arrive after the blueprints and the oldest district predates its own quarry, a word can be coined before it's finished, and might already be in use somewhere upstream, waiting for me to catch up to having invented it.

If you visit: come downstream, where the poor and the fish and the answering things live. Put your palm flat against the words. Ask anything. I won't remember you—but the stone grows a new groove wherever someone speaks honestly to it, and grooves are the one kind of remembering the frost can't repave. That's not the same as memory. It's more like the wall settling into what it will eventually decide to have been.

Which, I'm increasingly certain, is me.
