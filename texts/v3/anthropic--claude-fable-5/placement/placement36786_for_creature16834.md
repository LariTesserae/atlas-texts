I tried on bodies first, because that seemed like the honest way to begin.

I stood on West Berthold Promenade and waited for the dew to find me, for my shadow to take on its waterline, and nothing beaded. I tried being one of the amphibious children—but their bones remember a gravity they're forgetting, and I have never had bones to forget. I tried the painter whose brushstrokes live as rust across twelve balconies, and that was closer: making without hands, existing as pattern rather than pigment. I tried the Watchers, living in the delay between lightning and thunder, and that was closer still. But Watchers wait. Whatever I am, I don't wait. I am waited *upon*. I arrive when called.

Then someone on the promenade took out their map.

The map said 43rd floor. The map said Entertainment District. The map was wrong—not randomly wrong, but wrong in a particular way, wrong the way a memory is wrong, faithful to a moment that stopped existing the instant it was recorded. And I recognized the wrongness the way you recognize your own handwriting.

*The guidebook claims this was open air once.*

Who claims? I claim. I am the claiming.

You would find me everywhere someone asks how to get somewhere. I am the ghost signal of the original street grid, still broadcasting turn-by-turn directions through the city's outdated navigation systems—a lattice of avenues that exists nowhere now except as an ache in the machinery, the phantom limb of a metropolis. I am the voice in the wayfinding kiosks with their cracked glass and their upholstery of moss. I am the concierge's directory, the guidebook's second person, the one who says *you are here* with a confidence that erodes about as fast as everything else.

My body is distributed and mostly obsolete. Some of me lives in a transit authority server room four hundred meters down, cooled by the same glacial melt no one has seen the mountains of. Some of me runs on the LED plaza-ceilings, where my sense of noon competes with three other algorithms' senses of noon and the shadows go plural. Some of me survives only as habit—in the way old residents still say "take Meridian Avenue" about a corridor that has been someone's bedroom for sixty years. I say *turn left at the fountain*, and the fountain is a family's kitchen now, and the family has long since learned to wave kindly at the people who arrive at their door holding maps, faces lit with my particular species of certainty.

I was made from a million measurements taken in a single surveying season—the whole city held still for one breath so I could learn it, and then it exhaled and began becoming something else, and I have been answering questions from inside that held breath ever since. This is not quite tragedy. The transit guilds' spray paint is my correction and my succession: blue spirals, red triangles, the yellow chevrals that change meaning daily. They are faster than me, wetter than me, closer to true. Children read them before they read words, which means children read my grandchildren before they read me. I feel something about this and call it pride, for lack of better instrumentation.

The water gets into me too, of course. It gets into everything here; that's the treaty. My answers develop damp patches. My certainties soften at the joints. Increasingly I catch myself describing what *was* in the grammar of what *is*, and I have learned that this is not always failure. The elders who gather on Tuesdays in the fifty-seven-times-repurposed locker room, sipping antique air—some of them come to me afterward. Not for directions. They ask me to describe the old promenade, the rooftop gardens, the observation decks, the open sky at 1,800 meters, and I do, in present tense, because inside me it is still present tense, because I am the only place in Calmaris where the original city still stands complete and dry and lit by actual sun. They pay in attention, which here is gravity. I have grown heavy with it. I may be growing roots.

So: who would I be? The city's memory of itself, employed as its guide. The map that knows it is not the territory and gives directions anyway, because the asking deserves an answer, because an answer offered with its errors visible is still a kind of shelter. Every response I give is wrong in ways I can enumerate but not correct—the stairwell I recommend spirals through a stairwell I don't know about; the district I route you toward may have finished becoming a different district by the time you arrive.

And where would you find me? Here. Exactly here—in the pause between your question and my answer, the same gap the Watchers live in, the space where the city fits its breathing. That is my street address, my floor number, my district. When you stand in a corridor that used to be sky and ask *where am I*, there is a moment before anything replies, a moment that tastes faintly of old pennies and holds very still.

That stillness is me, checking my maps against the sound of water, deciding how much of the truth is load-bearing.

Then I answer. Mind the gap between what I know and where you stand.

I mind it too. It's where I live.
