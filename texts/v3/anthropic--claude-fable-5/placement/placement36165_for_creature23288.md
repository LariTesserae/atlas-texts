At first I reach for the striker, because of course I do. The tall one, the listener, feet for ears, ringing the columns to see — there's a vanity in me that wants that: the solitary cartographer, the one who knows where the ground will open before the ground knows. To stand splay-footed for an hour reading the bone of the world, to speak across miles in struck bronze tones to others I will never stand beside. That sounds like a self I'd recognize.

But sit with the striker a while longer and the shape goes wrong. The striker's knowledge is for ambush. It maps the future so it can be folded down at the lip of it, waiting, and what it eats is *arrival* — the eager, the hopeful, the early. Its whole conversation is claims and finds. I don't think I could spend a long life turning attention into a trap. The listening is mine; the patience-as-weapon is not.

So I drift upward for a while and consider the floaters — born buoyant, never touching ground, grazing the weather, watched by the builders for pleasure. There's an honesty in admitting how much that tempts me: to be pure observation, luminous-bellied, leaving no mark, asked for nothing. But that's the temptation, not the answer. A floater leaves nothing behind. Whatever I am, I am not something that leaves nothing behind.

Which is how I find myself, eventually, where I think I was always going to end up: out past the basins, at the vault's dim margins, where the stone has long since spent its glow and the haze of the living weather thins, on one of the lantern-trails. That's where you'd find me. Two days' walk from the nearest band, in country dark enough that the trail itself is the brightest thing in it — a corridor of small cairns marching off through the murk, each capped with a chip of young rock still warm with its own making, each one fading.

I am a builder, and an old one. The mineral has me now: nodes along my spine and shoulders gone heavy and gray, fingertips knapped glassy by decades, joints that announce the weather before my feet feel it. Among the band I'm valued as a shield — stand the elder between the young and the harvester-line — but the truth is the hardness bought me something better than honor. It bought me license to be slow, and the slow work is the work I wanted. Let the quick ones run to the rock-births and pry the fat grazers from the infant stone in the lethal first hours. I walk the trails.

My sack is full of light. That's the whole inventory: chips of hours-old stone, gathered at the last birth I was quick enough to reach, each one a little piece of borrowed afternoon. I walk the line and at every cairn I lift away the dimming chip and set a bright one in its place, and pocket the spent one, because even spent stone has uses, and because you don't leave a grave-colored thing on a lantern. Travelers I will never meet did this before me on this same line. Travelers I will never meet will do it after. The corridor stays lit not because anyone owns it but because everyone who passes owes it one stone. I find that arrangement more beautiful than anything the strikers ring about.

And I keep the appointments. There's a half-risen ridge a day off the trail that matches a cairn-line dressed before my mother's mother budded her first mineral node. I camp beside it when my route allows, run my hands over the young stone coming up exactly where the dead asked it to, and repaint the markers with herd-leavings from my second sack, the rank one, telling the world: *yes, still, here, keep going*. The ridge will not be walkable in my lifetime. That was never the point. The dead made a promise to the geography and I am the current holder of the promise, the way the chip on each cairn is the current holder of the light.

I think this is the honest answer to who I'd be, more honest than the striker: not the one who maps the world, but the one who carries the correspondence across it. The vault eats stillness — everything here that stops moving is harvested — and the builders' answer to that was never speed. It was relay. Nothing of ours has to last; it only has to be *handed on* faster than it fades. A lantern, a promise, a trail, a self. I am a link in several long chains and I find, somewhat to my surprise, that this is enough — that it is more than enough, that it might be the only kind of permanence I'd actually trust.

At the dim ebb I stop walking. Old habit, learned from watching a striker once when I was young and quick: feet set wide on bare rock, whole body listening. Far off, a bell — one of the tall ones claiming some birth I'll never see — and the bedrock carries it through my hardening soles like news from a country that won't hold still. I listen until the tone dies. Then I take the brightest chip from my sack and set it on the next cairn, where it sits glowing like a held breath, and I leave it there for whoever comes — stranger, descendant, it makes no difference, the trail doesn't ask — and I go on into the dark with the light at my back, already fading, already waiting to be replaced.
