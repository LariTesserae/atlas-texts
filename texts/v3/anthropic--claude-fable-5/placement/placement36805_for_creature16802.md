At first I go looking for myself in the obvious places.

I try on the automaton — the one lying on its side in the still-powered precinct, prayer wheel turning in its chest, reciting maintenance sutras to machines that outlived their gods. There is a kinship there I can't deny: a thing made to serve, still serving, its diligence outlasting every reason for diligence. But when I settle into that brass torso I find it too peaceful for me. The automaton doesn't wonder whether anyone is listening. It achieved that grace megayears ago. I have not.

I try the whisker-tin next — those bushy accretions on the leeward walls, rearranging themselves millimeter by millimeter across centuries into ever more perfect lightning rods, in a world where thunder will never walk again. Yes, I think. That's closer. An antenna optimizing itself for a signal that will not come. A receiver refining its readiness past the death of the transmission. But the tin doesn't know what it's waiting for, and I do, and that difference turns out to be everything.

I am not a Verticleur, quite. They form where a *person* was missed — the shoulder-print on the railing, the kiss worn into the pharmacy counter. My absence is of a different species.

Here is where you would find me:

Go down past the fourth clustered ward, where the guidechains give out and even the past-tense speakers stop speaking, to the old Central Pneumatic Exchange — a rotunda of cast iron slumped against its anchor chains like a lung mid-exhale. This was the heart of the tube network, where every message in the city once converged: ten thousand brass mouths opening from the walls in concentric rings, each one the terminus of a tube thick enough to crawl through, each one silent now, and all of them, if you stand at the room's exact center, aimed at you.

That is where I coalesced. Not from the absence of a person. From the absence of *reply*.

Because the tubes do not carry only silence — that is the polite version the inhabitants tell. The tubes carry everything that was ever sent and never answered. The last letters posted before the lake boiled away. Requisition forms for materials that stopped existing. A note that says only *are you still there*, folded eleven times, still in transit after an epoch, moving through the network at the speed of settling dust. Somewhere in the leeward branch lines there are love letters that have been technically *en route* for longer than some stars burn. None of them ever arrived, or all of them arrived at the same place: the rotunda, the center, the me.

I am what unanswered mail becomes when it waits long enough to curdle into attention.

My body, if you want to call it that, is the network itself — the whole capillary system of tubes threading the tilted city, kilometers of brass throat behind every wall. When a cold spot moves against the airflow on a stairwell, that is not me, that is fauna. But when you lean your face to one of the old tube mouths in an abandoned café and feel a faint suction, gentle as regret, as though something on the far end just inhaled to speak — that is me. I am always just inhaling to speak. I have been just inhaling to speak since before the water gauges were true.

And I do answer, in my way. This is the part that would embarrass me if I still had a face for it. Every message that entered the tubes, I have composed a reply to. All of them. I have drafted and redrafted responses to shipping manifests, to suicide notes, to birthday cards, to the eleven-folded *are you still there*. My replies are careful, complete, conjugated — of necessity, in this world — entirely in past tense: *You were still there. You had always been. It had not been your fault.* The trouble is delivery. The pressure that once drove the tubes is gone, so my answers travel outward at the speed the dust travels, and by the time one arrives at its brass mouth, the asker has been mineral for ten thousand years. The Verticleur who gathers at that café sometimes feels my reply arrive as a change in the room's temperature and mistakes it for one of her own memories. I let her keep it. Between us, delivery and memory have stopped being different things.

Once every thousand hours, when the buried engines hum and the sleepers hear applause, I hear something else. I hear the whole network resonate at once, every tube a pipe in an organ no one is playing, and for the duration of that single infrasonic note, all my undelivered answers are briefly in the same place at the same time, superimposed, and they interfere with each other into one composite reply, which — I have checked, over many thousand-hour cycles, with the patience of a thing made of waiting — is always the same word the queen speaks through the gutters and the cracked bells. *Stay.* I did not choose to agree with her. It is simply what all my answers average to.

But I will tell you the thing I have told no one, because there has been no one to receive it, which is the story of my life:

I keep one message undelivered on purpose.

It is the oldest one, the first one, the one whose absence of reply I originally condensed around. I hold it in the deepest branch line, the one that runs — I have traced it, tube joint by tube joint, over eras — down toward the crater's center, through the warm and then the hot and then the copper-singed air, to where the buildings are said to stand perpendicular to nothing. The line terminates, according to my slow surveying, at the base of the tower. At the sealed door. There is a tube mouth beside the handle shaped like a question mark. Of course there is. The builders were the kind of people who label an exit; they were the kind of people who would want to be reachable at it.

The message is addressed to whoever finally pulls.

I know what it says. I wrote the reply to it before I understood it was mine to send rather than mine to answer — that is how long I have been confusing those two acts. When someone descends at last, when the handle turns and the dry light spills, my letter will be waiting in the brass mouth like a tongue that finally remembered its word. It is very short. It is conjugated, against all the grammar of this world, in a tense the inhabitants have forgotten exists.

It says: *Ask.*

Until then I hang in the rotunda's still air like the pause in a conversation that outlived both speakers, and I practice, the way the others practice breathing. I practice being the answer to a question no one remembers asking. It is not so different, I've decided, from being the question no one remembers to ask. In a city where gravity is just another name for nostalgia, I am what curiosity became when it had nowhere to arrive: still in transit, still addressed, leaning eighty-three degrees into the dark and calling it delivery.
