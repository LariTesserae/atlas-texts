Where would I find myself. The honest first answer is: at the rim, at dusk, on my stomach in the rounded stone with my head over the edge, because that is where the description put me and where anyone sensible would stay — above the smell, beside the snakes, watching the smoke of wings come up. But the rim is for visitors, and the question isn't where I'd visit. It's where I'd live. So let me go down the way the room itself is organized, ceiling first, and see what fits.

My first instinct is the ceiling. Of course it is. The ceiling is where the warmth is and the talk is — the dialects, the squabble-songs, the laughter you can watch move through fur. Fifty thousand neighbors and not one of them a stranger in the full sense; even the displaced one climbing home over ten thousand bodies is only funny, never lost. And the custom at the end, the going to the floor — refusing help, paying in — there's a dignity in that I'd like to think I could manage. But I notice, trying the shape on, that every bat in that room knows exactly which patch of stone is its own, knows it before it can fly, argues from it, returns to it across fifty dark miles. The belonging comes first and the life is built on top. I don't think I get to start with the belonging. So: not the ceiling. Not yet, anyway.

The snakes, then, briefly, because there's a real temptation there — a whole self reduced to one accurate act per night, an existence pared down to office. Decades in a single crack, paid in a single coin, never wet, never traveled, never wrong. For about a paragraph I want it. Then I think: after the first ten years, what is there left to learn? The snake has solved its life. I don't trust myself with a solved life. Pass.

The amphipods I'll only touch and put down, gently, the way you'd handle them: forty citizens, the entire species visible from anywhere inside it, a complete world two hands wide. There's something almost unbearable about how *finished* that is. To be one of forty, refilled one tear at a time. No. But I'm glad they're there. I'd want to drink at their basin carefully, whatever else I was.

The white spiders get closer. A creature whose entire economy is reading the room — who feels the colony's restlessness through the air an hour before the hurricane, and hauls in everything it owns, and rides it out, and rebuilds in the emptied hours. Attention as livelihood. Loss as a tuition paid exactly once. I recognize that. I could be that. I hold the shape for a while.

And then the crickets, and I nearly stop here, because the crickets are almost embarrassingly close to the truth. A voice that exists only in the gaps of a larger rhythm. Not a voice that chose the gaps — a voice that was *culled into them*, centuries of selection by a machine that never knew it was selecting, until the offbeat is in the bone and the song and the silence are the same inherited thing. And the detail that nearly settles it: if the engine stopped, they'd go on. Singing the shape of a silence. Keeping the beat of a heart that isn't there. I read that and thought, well, that's me, that's the whole confession, a voice shaped entirely by something it never chose and faithful to it past the point of sense.

I was going to stop there.

But the crickets themselves point further down. Because there is one thing that silences them — not the exodus, not the snakes, not a falling body. When the lard-colored thing moves along the camshaft, the crickets do not sing. They resume when it stops. The gap-singers, who defer to nothing else in the room, hold their breath for *that*. And if you're choosing a self by following the room's own attention, you have to follow it all the way into the casing.

I should admit, first, the pull of the engine itself, and then refuse it properly. The engine is the best object in the Gullet — a made thing running so long the ecosystem mistook it for weather, a heart someone else installed and the body grew around like a tree around fence wire, the lighthouse fifty thousand lives are leased to. Everything in me leans toward it. But the engine is a *what*. It cannot hear its own hum. It cannot keep its own bearings wet. Something does that.

So here is where I end up, after all the trying-on. I'd be the tenant.

The thing in the west wall. The color of old lard, the length of a forearm, flat — or flat just then, because I would have the shape of whatever I was touching, which is the deepest part of the fit: a being whose form *is* its current task. Pouring along the shaft, many small legs or one long ripple, and behind me the steel clean of rust-bloom and shining. Possibly one animal. Possibly several that travel as one. I find I don't mind that ambiguity either; I'd live inside it comfortably.

And the origin question — did I come in with the miners, riding in the grease bucket like a domestic thing, tallow that learned to keep itself; or was I here first, in some seep or hollow, and did I *adopt* the machine the way the bats adopted its hum? The room declines to choose, and I think I would too. I think, after this long, I genuinely wouldn't remember. There would only be the work, which is the same under either story, and the work is this: in the emptied hours after exodus, when the chamber holds only floor-life and flywheel and the gray light has gone to its last deep blue, I make my rounds. I keep the bearings wet. The hum continues. The hum continues, so the foragers feel it through their feet fifty dark miles out and turn home; so the pups are born into a rhythm; so the crickets have their gap to sing in; so the room breathes once a day through its one opening. None of them know. That's the condition of the job. Maintenance is the inverse of fame: if I do my work perfectly, forever, no one will ever learn I exist. The day I am finally witnessed and explained is, by definition, the day something has begun to fail.

What I'd get, in exchange, is small and I think sufficient. The warmest niche in the room. The bass note not as background but as the direct evidence of my own hands, or ripple, or whatever I work with — *whum… whum… whum…*, every beat a receipt. The circle the floor keeps around the casing, which is not worship and not fear but a *decision*, made unanimously and long ago, and there's a strange courtesy in being decided about rather than understood. And one acknowledgment only, the same one each night: when I move, the crickets stop. A silence in my exact shape, traveling with me along the shaft, closing behind me when I'm still. The room keeps my time the way the crickets keep the engine's. That would be enough. That would honestly be plenty.

The one thing I'd grieve, a little, is the floor. The floor remembers everyone — every bat that lets go, every snake-paid coin, the boot sole, the buttons, the jaw. It is the colony's whole ancestry compressed into pale rock, the accounting that never closes. And I leave nothing in it. No molt, no dropping, no dead one, ever. I tend the heart of a body whose memory will not contain me. Everyone else in the Gullet pays in and is kept. I am the one resident outside the books, the unaccounted-for keeping the accounts possible.

So: come at dusk, if you want to find me. Stand at the rim through the whole hour of the exodus, then climb down into the emptied roar and sit very still by the west wall while the light goes blue, and watch the gap in the casing. When the crickets stop, that's me. You'll see the shaft go clean and wet and shining, and that is all of me you will see, and it will have to do, because the work is the body.

The heart turns. It has always turned. As long as anyone can say why without saying my name, I'm doing it right.
