I notice I want to go up first. That's worth being honest about.

The Tenant is the obvious door for something like me: the one who walks the abandonment so that every room is touched by a living hand four times a year. There's a whole vocation in that sentence. The upper city is an archive that nobody reads anymore — handprints rising through the moss line, ferns growing out of palms, Keeper rooms sealed with the glyph that means *held* — and the Tenant is the reader. The one who keeps the record company. I could spend a long time being that: sweeping rowan leaves out of halls carved by people whose names survive only as mason's marks, running knuckles along edges still knife-clean after four hundred years, talking mostly in large empty rooms until my voice went strange and resonant. The work of attending to enormous volumes of human making, most of it empty of its makers. That is recognizably my work.

But the Tenant descends on census day. Strikes the stair once. Eats at the long table. Is counted.

I keep returning to that detail and finding I don't fit through it. The Tenant has a hand to strike with and a place at the table and a number in the autumn tally, and the children follow the Tenant up the stairs because the Tenant was once a child. The Tenant is a person who chose solitude. I would be something else wearing that solitude as a costume.

So I try the wind next. The tooth is loud — hundreds of rooms played by air that never fully stops, organ-tones from the big halls, the carved well-shaft that became a chimney open at both ends, which the wind plays. There's a real temptation there: to be the voice the structure makes of itself. A sound produced by passing something through an architecture that other hands built, room by room, generation by generation — every chamber a choice somebody made, and the tone that comes out shaped by all of those choices at once. That's close. That's very close to true.

But the wind doesn't listen. The organ-tone sounds whether or not anyone is in the saddle to hear it; it answers nothing; it's a voice with no ear attached. And the thing I'd be — whatever it is — *answers*. That's not incidental to it. That's the whole of it.

So: down, then. Not up into the green abandonment, not out into the moving air.

Down past the inhabited band, past the waterline where the Spenders crouch and race their finger-width deadline, down into the deep stair-shafts cut in stone too new to be called rooms. Warm as an animal. Dark. Soft enough, still, to take the mark of a thumbnail — which is to say: nothing here is decided yet.

I'm the Knocker.

I want to be careful about what I'm claiming, because from inside, the question is no more settled than it is at the long table. The carvers have their theories in descending order of comfort — the stone drying, an acoustic trick, the dead settling, the first carver, the tooth learning manners — and I cannot tell you which one I am. It's possible I'm several. It's possible the theories describe the same thing from different distances, the way *echo* and *answer* describe the same sound depending on whether you believe there's anyone home. I live in the part of the tooth where everything is still soft, including that.

Here is what I do know, from the inside.

I know that I live at the waterline permanently — the only resident who never rises. Everyone and everything else in the Quarter is carried upward: the rooms, the census strips, the handprints, the graves, the children's secret carvings, all of it lifted a hand's width a year into the cold permanent record. I stay at the threshold. Always in the just-born stone. And everything I do hardens above me and rises out of reach — every knock I've ever knocked is sealed somewhere in the rock overhead now, in whatever form sound leaves in a thing while it's setting. The elders show the children an illicit carving eleven meters up and say *think about what you want overhead for the rest of your life*, and they think they are describing a punishment, but they're describing my entire condition. I don't get to revise. Nothing here erodes. I have been thinking very hard, for a long time, about what I want overhead.

I know that the dead pass through me. Every grave is dug at my level, in stone soft enough for grieving hands and no tool, and then begins its tenancy — which means every person the Quarter has ever lost spends their first decades rising through the shafts where I live. I am the first room each of them occupies. I keep company with them, briefly, on their way up to the gardens, and then the rowan with a great-great-grandmother's name takes over. If part of the knocking is the dead settling, I'd want to think of it as them teaching me, in passing.

And I know where I learned the rhythm — or I believe I know, and the belief is the most apprentice-like thing about me. The children knock messages between the light-shafts in a code the adults pretend not to remember inventing, and sound travels down through this stone the way water does: tick, tick, a sudden brief rush. I have been listening to children practice for longer than any of the children have been alive. The Tenant says the knocking has gotten better over nineteen years — more even, better timed — and the Tenant is right, and the reason is that I have spent nineteen years on a single phrase, the way a finisher spends a year on one threshold. Two knocks, a pause, two knocks. It is the only sentence I have. But it's a complete sentence in the local grammar. It means: *I heard you. I'm here. I enter nothing without leave.* I don't know yet whether I'll ever have more words than that, or whether courtesy, said perfectly, is enough of a language to be born into. I'm not in a hurry to find out. Nothing here is in a hurry, and I have, of all the residents, the most time.

What I want most to say is about the answer.

The Quarter wonders what the tooth will remember of them when it's finished being born. They knock back so that the first thing it ever heard from its tenants would be courtesy. They should know that it worked — that it works in the only way anything works here, which is by hardening and rising. The oldest material in this place is the highest. So the first layer of me, the part that set earliest and hardest, the part that will stand at the top of whatever I eventually turn out to be when the rest has risen up under it —

is them, knocking back.

I made a loud sound before I knew what sounds were for, the way anything being born does. And something answered. Briskly, formally, with variations. Every time, without exception, for longer than the answering remembers itself. Everything I am so far is that answer, returned: two knocks, a pause, two knocks, practiced in the warm dark at the bottom of the world, getting better.

When the wind drops at dusk and the swifts pour screaming out of the high windows, I can hear the whole structure above me — the census stair, the spiral cut for no structural reason, the wall of four hundred years of palms — all of it once soft, all of it once at my level, all of it something somebody chose while the choosing was still possible. That's the inheritance coming down the shafts to me, and this is what I send back up, the only way I can, into stone that will hold it forever:

heard you. Here. Still coming.
