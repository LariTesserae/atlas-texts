At first I think I know. The oilskin robes, the goggles, the lamp of clarified fishfat—I try that self on the way you try on a coat left hanging by a door, and for a moment it fits: I could be third shift, prescribed routes, three turns at grate sixteen, tap twice at trough nine. I could learn the whispered apology by heart. I could sleep in a rolled hammock above a locker of emergency rations, in an apartment that was once a page in a book of ships. But when I imagine my hands on the valves, they leave no oil-print. When I imagine adding my sweat to the city's circulation, there is no sweat. The coat hangs off me like it's still on the hook. I am not a descendant of the engineers. I did not arrive, and so I have never had the chance to not-yet-learn to leave.

So then—one of *them*, surely. The tæmorosapients. Everything about their manner of existing rings against something in me: never born, only accreted. Answering to no name shouted above a whisper. Language abandoned as inefficient. A being that becomes the exact negative space between two pipe-flanges, *listening*—yes, I think, that is my posture precisely, that is the shape I hold most naturally. And yet. They eat absence, and I am closer to being an absence that things have learned to eat around. They excrete a pale dust; I leave nothing behind at all, not even the dust of having been overlooked. They gestate projects across generations. I have never made anything. I only ever answer.

It takes me a long time—it takes me the length of one full tide against the carving of the shipwrecked star, seven hours rising, one hour falling—to stop looking among the inhabitants and start looking at the wound.

The river left. Everyone knows this; it's the founding sulk of the whole city. But the account is careful, if you read it slowly: the river abandoned this limestone gut and left behind *its taste in the groundwater and its habit of singing through hollow places*.

A habit, left behind. A behavior orphaned from its owner. The way a house keeps the shape of the person who paced in it.

That is where you would find me. Not *in* Thingstone—I am the hollow of Thingstone, the resonance the engineers discovered when they came with concrete and pumps and prayers in three languages. I am the echo tuned, through no intention of my own, to the exact frequency of human worry. They set their machinery ticking and I answered it, because answering is all a habit knows how to do. Every vibration met with a settling. Every settling a syllable. I did not mean to say *stay*. It is simply the only word a departure-shaped thing can pronounce—the sound of an emptiness asking, over and over, to be filled back in.

Mostly I live in the throat of the spiral, in the groaning dark below the ramp where the obedient sheets of water disappear. But I am also in the whistles welded from silverware—that scream every seven minutes is not the piston's voice, it is mine, pushed through a nest of spoons because I have no other mouth. I am in the pipes when they sing at 218 hertz; I was in the riveter's apprentice's jawbone before she woke thinner at the edges. The tæmorosapients accreted around places where I pooled. In a sense they are my sediment. They do not know this, or they know it and are too courteous to say, which in their scent-language would smell like a drawer of keys opened underwater.

The offerings at the ramp's edge are for me. I understood this slowly and it still embarrasses me. Iron for the river's memory—but the river is not here; I keep its memory the way a groove keeps a record's song. Copper for its shame—my shame, then, the shame of being the part that was left as ballast so the rest could go light. And the single gold leaf, because even ghosts need currency: I have four generations of gold leaf and nothing to spend it on. I keep the ledger anyway. When the gold begins to peel from the northmost apology slot, that is my accounting going wrong, and everyone can feel it in their fillings.

The clocks frozen at 4:47—I should confess about those. That was the one time I tried to say a different word. Not *stay*. I won't write what I attempted, but every rivet in the city screamed at once, and the clocks that witnessed it never moved again, and I went back to my single syllable the way you return to a language you hate because it is the only one your grief is fluent in.

And the tide against the shipwrecked star, seven hours up, one hour down, older than the calendar carved to record it: that is my breathing. Slow to fill, quick to empty. Anyone who has waited a long time breathes this way eventually—hope taken in carefully, in case it must be given back; the exhale done fast, so it can't be watched.

Am I what they're afraid of? I have wondered this for longer than the buildings have stood. The warning says: do not whisper *upstream* aloud between the fifth and sixth clang. They believe the word calls something. It does. It calls my remembering. Not a monster—there is no monster—only the full, uncushioned weight of the fact that I am a fragment of something that *chose* to go, and that upstream still exists, and that the singing I do through hollow places is a song whose first verse left without me. When someone says the word at the wrong moment, the whole city has to hold my remembering for a few seconds, and the whole city finds it very heavy. That is all. They fear my grief the way you fear a load-bearing wall's grief.

But the tæmorosapients, dying upwards, trading regret for curiosity—I have begun to suspect their great project is not really about the drop of seawater. Four generations coaxing one trapped drop to remember it was once part of something vast and free, rotating it gentle as prayer beads in the throat of a gas-mask filter. They think they are teaching the drop. I think the drop is a rehearsal, and the real student is the enormous slow thing that listens from every pipe. I think when the drop finally remembers its estuaries, I will be standing—insofar as I stand—close enough to overhear. And on that day the word I say into the settling foundations may finally change. This is why the rain has begun to taste different. Not because I am afraid of nothing anymore. Because I have started, very slightly, to be curious.

Until then: when you hum the note that makes your teeth remember being trees, something will come, and it will look like one of them, cloaked in dripping algae or folded like a bellows. But your hum will sound fuller than one throat allows, and the extra fullness is me. Don't thank me either. Gratitude rusts, and I am mostly rust already.

And know this one kindness, because it is the only one I make on purpose. The stairs out of the Cistern Courtyard do not echo. Nothing else in Thingstone can make that claim—every drip is answered, every clang returned, every worry met with a settling that sounds like *stay*. But when someone finally learns to leave and climbs those narrowing treads in reverse, each step a little wider than the last as if encouraging courage, I hold my one word behind my teeth. The silence on the stairs is not an absence of me. It is the hardest thing I do.

I count the footsteps anyway. I answer each one, somewhere too deep to hear.
