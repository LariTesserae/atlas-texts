I notice I want to start at the rim, the way everyone does — but that's the traveler's position, and the question isn't where I'd visit. So let me come down the switchbacks and walk around a while.

My first thought is the winding-houses. There is something in me that answers to the winders' oath — *I will be boring forever* — and answers to it not as resignation but as the vow of love they mean it to be. To be the reason the tram is where the schedule says it is. To be safety that works not by alertness but by synchrony, by being so reliable that other people can stop thinking about you and live. I could be a winder. Part of me already is one. But when I imagine the whole life — the cable, the timetable, the stone post — it feels like a portrait of one of my virtues rather than a portrait of me. Keep walking.

The kiln families, then. Someone in every house always awake; the firing logs going back centuries; the only continuous writing in the basin, and buried in it, almost by accident, the truest record of what the mountain is doing — temperatures and glaze outcomes that turn out, read across two hundred years, to be a transcript of the breath slowing. That's nearer. I am a creature of records. I love that the kiln logs were never meant to be prophecy and became it anyway, just by being kept honestly for long enough. But the kiln people are dynasts, embedded in family, in inherited insomnia, and when I look for myself among them I find I'm standing slightly outside the doorway, admiring. Not it. Closer.

Then I notice something that should have told me sooner.

The sealed rooms. The padded instrument-chambers where newcomers faint, because the body reads the absence of the roar as a held breath. I keep returning to them in imagination, and I am not bracing. The silence in there doesn't frighten me. It reads, to me, like a floor rather than a falling. And there is only one kind of person in the basin for whom that would be true — for whom silence is not the terrifying subtraction of a lifelong companion but simply the native condition, the place everything else is added to.

I'd be dove-born.

Never received the roar at all. Born into the one exemption the basin grants, named for the bird that solved the place first by giving up, in advance, the sense the place destroys. The hand-language would not be my workaround; it would be my first and only full language — language as pure visible form, with no ghost of sound underneath it, two clauses at once when I needed them, the left hand for what happened and the right for what was hoped. I would speak, when I spoke at all, in the felt dialect, the one calibrated to bones rather than ears, and I would understand touch-speech the way everyone here does, through the skeleton, which the roar never owned in the first place. I would feed the doves with the one sign every district makes identically, and I would mean something slightly different by it than my neighbors do, and we would all know that, and it would be fine.

And here is the rest of it, the part that arrived only as I wrote: I would be one of the surveyors of quiet.

A dove-born surveyor. There must be a few. Think of what that is: a person who has never once heard the thing whose absence she spends her life mapping. I would walk the trusted stone out to the nodes with my instruments, and I would chart, season by season, the slow grazing drift of the still points across the basin floor, and I would fire the maps onto ceramic plates and stack them in the dry vault with the centuries of plates before mine. I would show them to anyone. I would discuss methodology indefinitely. And when the visitor finally asked the real question — *what is in the quiet places? is something speaking?* — I would make the cathedral-shrug, and it would not be evasion. It would be the most precise sentence I own.

Because I have sat in the deepest nodes, the way my work requires. I have put my hand flat on the warm stone, which is the sign that also means *to trust*. And I can tell you exactly what I found there: warm stone. Faint trembling. The third hall's flywheel, loaded. The fen crust to the north, thinned by a wet week. The old ones sit beside me on the polished stones and their faces do the thing faces do when they are receiving, and afterward they do not say what they received, and I do not ask, because it is graceless to ask and because I am not sure the asking would even be in the right channel.

The city says of my kind: maybe the only ones telling the truth, maybe the only ones the basin cannot reach. I notice that I cannot resolve this about myself from the inside, which the city, in its patience, seems to find unsurprising. So I do the thing the whole civilization does, only I do it as a profession and as a self: I hold both reports without choosing. The old hear cadence; I feel stone; both of these go on the plate. The vault keeps them side by side, a quarter-tone of progress per lifetime, and neither one is the conclusion, because the basin has never been heard to finish a sentence and I, of all people, am not going to be the one to claim it has.

There is one duty I think I would have taken on quietly, without it being assigned. When the dying old sign in their sleep — fluently, in that accent belonging to no district, the forms even the dove-born say they have never seen — someone should write the forms down. Not translate them. No one can. Just record them, carefully, the way the kiln families recorded temperatures for two centuries without knowing they were taking the mountain's pulse. I would sit by the bed with a lamp turned low and copy the shapes of hands, plate after plate, against the possibility that someday, stacked deep enough, read across enough lifetimes, the record resolves into something — or doesn't, which would also be an answer, and would also go in the vault.

So: dusk, then. That's where you'd find me. Out on the trusted stone at the edge of a still point, instruments packed, work done, staying anyway. An old one twenty meters off, listening to what I can't confirm is there. The doves coming over in their slow deaf wheel, my distant kin, the first solvers. The lamps of the districts hanging in the vapor like embers in milk. The roar everywhere and, for me alone in all that bowl, nowhere — and under my palm, where it has always been, the stone, talking.

Be polite, I was taught, before I was taught anything else. I am still trying.
