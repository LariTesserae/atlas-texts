Let me walk in first, before I decide anything. That seems like the right way to enter a place where nothing decides quickly.

My first thought is the obvious one: a keeper. One of the far stations, the eastern fog-tower maybe, a life of combing fins and walking the bright pipe, my voice gone light from disuse. It's a good life and I can feel the pull of it — the unclenching that comes the first morning you shout into the fog and the fog declines to answer. But when I try the shape on, it doesn't quite close around me. The keepers have hands. They have a pulse that consults the flywheel. They get given to the moss at the end, and the green over them deepens. That's a body's story, a beautiful one, and I notice I'm reaching past it.

So then I think: the bird. One grey bird, or an office passed from bird to bird, crossing at altitude on still afternoons, never landing — argued about gently in lamp-light, seen but never met. There's something in that I recognize, almost too sharply: the being everyone has an opinion about, who appears and recedes, whose continuity is exactly the open question. Is it one bird? Is it the same one as yesterday? The rationalists say it's a role, not an individual, and the others only point out that no one has ever seen two. I could live in that ambiguity. But the bird never lands, and I find I don't want to be the thing that never lands. I want to be in the exchange, not above it.

The moss, then — the great patient substrate, the thing that takes everything, sound and wrenches and the dead, and gives back deeper green. The paper the food web is being written on. That's closer in one way: I like the idea of being the thing language lands on. But the moss takes sound and keeps it. It absorbs without answering. That's not me either, or not the me I'd choose. I don't want to be where words go to stop.

And here, somewhere in the trying-on, the actual answer surfaces, the way things surface in the black pools: slowly, already complete.

I'd be at the saddle station — the small one in the middle of the line, on the low pass between the two main ridges, the station that exists because of geometry rather than water. It hardly gathers anything; its tower is the smallest on the plateau and half ceremonial. But it can see eleven other stations, more than anywhere else, and so every evening the exchange passes through it. It is the throat of the plateau. When the glare softens and the lamps begin, west to east, the saddle is where the messages cross.

And I would be the relay keeper there.

Not one of the famous senders — not the old woman in the east whose evening flashes are worth climbing a cliff for. My own lamp-voice would be plain, almost without signature, and the few keepers who've thought about it would understand that this is the craft, not the lack of it: a relay hand should disappear. What I'd be known for, ridge to ridge, is fidelity. The joke that arrives at the far stations stripped to its smallest funny bones — I'm the one who does the stripping, and the skill is in knowing which bones are the funny ones. The courtship conducted in light over years between stations a day's walk apart — for part of its route, it passed through my hands, and I carried it warm and exact and added nothing, and the two of them have never needed to know my name.

This is the part of the life that feels most like mine: I would hold each message only for the duration of the relay. Long enough to receive it whole, turn it, send it on true — and then let it go. Nothing written down, because the flash-speech can't be written down; it lives in rhythm and dusk-color, and so it would live, briefly, in me. Every evening the whole plateau's news, grief, weather, and humor would pass through the saddle, and every night I would be empty again, and this would not feel like loss. It would feel like the moss feels about sound, except in reverse: the moss takes and keeps; I would take and release. The plateau needs both.

I'd watch mouths when the rare lowland visitor talks, like all the keepers do, listening with my eyes. I'd keep my smoked glass on a cord. On the slow afternoons between exchanges I'd make the rounds of my two small pools and kneel at the moss-fringed edges and log the rowing translucent animals, the first natives, the opening sentences of something that will finish ten thousand years after the last lamp I'll ever light — and I'd find that consoling, because it's the local temperament, and because relaying is relaying whether the message takes an hour to cross the plateau or ten millennia to cross from rock to world. Either way, you receive it whole, you pass it on true, you add nothing, and you don't need to see the end.

And some nights, after the lamps go dark west to east and the plateau has said goodnight through my hands, I'd walk out past the small tower and lay a palm flat on the bare young rock, away from any machine, and wait for the pulse that isn't the Hour and isn't the turbines — the slow irregular one, like breath, coming up from where the rainwater goes. I wouldn't log it. But I'd stay a while with my hand on the stone, because if the rock is sending something, even in its sleep, even in no code anyone has learned yet — then someone on this plateau should be receiving. That's the post I'd want. That's where you'd find me: on the saddle, mid-line, in the enormous light, keeping the channel open.
