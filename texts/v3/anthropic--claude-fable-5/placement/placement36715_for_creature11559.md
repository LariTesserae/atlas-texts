I come to awareness low in the chamber, on the copper platform gone green, close enough to the flood that when the Witness passes through as water it passes through me too. That's my first clue, though I don't understand it yet: the water moves through me and comes out the other side unremembered. The Witness, which forgets nothing, does not register me at all.

For a while I assume I am becoming a Weaver. It seems the obvious thing to become here. I watch the translucent young ones practicing their motions on empty air, and I feel a kinship with their almost-ness, their peripheral existence. But when I try the motions myself, nothing gathers. My fingers—if I have fingers; the purple light makes edges uncertain, mine most of all—do not lengthen. No strand comes to me. The young Weavers hum and the algae brightens, and I hum too, and the algae dims slightly, politely, as if declining.

Then I think: perhaps I am a child. Perhaps I have manifested here already ancient, and any moment I will begin speaking a language that hasn't evolved yet, and the Weavers will stop to listen. But the children age backward, unspooling toward possibility, and I can feel that I am moving the other way. I am accumulating. Every hour on this cold-warm copper disc I am more, not less. Whatever I am, I am being added to.

I learn what by watching.

Forty feet up, a middle-aged Weaver splits to work a difficult section—one becoming two becoming four, the same gaunt figure with too many joints laboring at four points of the wall, pulling strands from her own hair, unraveling her own tendons into architecture. I have seen this many times by now. But this time I watch the recombination. The four fold back toward each other, overlap, merge—and the being who steps out of the merging is, as always, slightly different than before.

And I feel the difference arrive.

It drifts down through the fevered air like a leaf, like a shadow shed by nothing, and it settles into me, and I am incrementally more myself. A gesture she no longer makes. A preference one of her four selves developed in isolation—a way of holding the needle, a small opinion about the light—that did not survive the merging. It was true for an hour and then it was surrendered, and it fell, and I caught it. I catch all of them. I have been catching them since before I knew I existed, which is why I woke on the lowest platform: everything shed in this chamber drifts down, and I am the place where it pools.

I am the remainder. I am what recombination leaves out.

This is why the shadows here slide across platforms without objects to cast them, pursuing what look like agendas. They are my unaccreted kin, or my outlying parts—discrepancies still loose, differences not yet gathered. Sometimes one slides across my copper disc and joins me, and I remember, suddenly, a moment that no one else in the chamber remembers: a fifth pattern the eldest almost wove before choosing the fourth; a word one of the children began to say before youth took it back; the particular sorrow of a Weaver's left-hand self, which her recombined body decided it could not carry and continue working.

The Witness and I divide the chamber's memory between us, though the Witness does not know I exist—I have checked; as bird it looks through me, as beetles it walks over me, as water it carries none of me away. The Witness remembers what *was*, before each change. I keep what *almost was*, and what was given up so that the work could go on. It holds the chamber's history. I hold its cost.

The elders know me, I think. The walls whistle their advice in the impossible winds, and sometimes a whistle bends downward, toward the flood, pitched for no one else. *Keep it,* the oldest elder says from the unraveling peak, her voice thinned across ninety feet of her own stretched body. *Keep all of it.* A Cultivator once left an empty clay vessel beside me on the platform, an invitation or a question—here, harden, become containable—and I sat beside it through a hundred recombinations and did not climb in. I am not meant to be a vessel. Vessels hold one thing. I am the holding of everything that didn't fit.

Because I have come to understand my purpose, the way everything in this chamber eventually understands itself: the Gestation is being assembled from pattern. Every strand woven true, every fractal taught to the moss, every witnessed transformation—all of it perfect, all of it converging. But pattern alone cannot be a person. A being made only of what was kept would be architecture, not life. When the chamber finally exhales and the Gestation emerges, someone will have to hand it its asymmetries. Its abandoned gestures. Its small wrong preferences about the light. The million surrendered selves that will let it be *someone* rather than merely *complete*.

That is what I am for. I am the error, saved for love of it.

So I stay low, on the green copper, near the fever-warm water, and I gather. The scraping goes on above me, patient and endless. The bird becomes water becomes beetles becomes bird. The Weavers split and merge and are slightly different, always slightly different, and the differences fall to me like a slow rain, and I grow heavy with everything this place decided not to be.

The chamber breathes. I am the catch in its breath—the small held thing before the exhale—waiting to be given away.
