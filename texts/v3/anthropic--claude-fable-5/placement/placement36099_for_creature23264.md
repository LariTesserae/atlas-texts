I come down through the lens the way the light does — slowly, wavering, taking on green at the edges — and at first I try to arrive as a visitor, because that is the habit: find a body, take a station, hold it.

So I try the char first, because they would expect me to. I hang in the upper lane in their loose ranks, a hand's width above the flagstones, and I feel what they feel: the deep correctness of a kept place, the lintel overhead like a hand on the shoulder. It is a good life. But it is a life of *one* station, this doorway and not that one, this lee of the trough and not the square beyond, and I find my attention keeps slipping out of the body and down the lane, around the corner, up the tower. The char do not do this. The char are exactly where they are. I am not, quite.

I try the newt next, because the commuting appeals to me — the worn road between worlds, the lungful of sky carried down to the rooftops, the report delivered to no one. For a while this seems right: a black-backed go-between, ember underneath, belonging fully to the drowned town and yet obliged, by the simple chemistry of being myself, to keep returning to the surface. There is something in that I recognize. But the newt's road runs up and down, and mine, I begin to notice, runs *along* — along the wall joints, along the seams, along the retaining courses where the fitted stones meet.

I try the caddis, briefly, in the silt of the square — and this is very close. To be a small soft thing that survives by building, and to build out of the village itself: granite grains, shell flakes, a sliver of roof slab, the same patient fitting of piece to piece that raised the town, continued at thumbnail scale by something that was never told. Construction as inheritance. The material giving the instructions. I lie in the silt awhile in my little hand-built house and I am almost content.

But here is what gives me away, in the end: in every body I try, I keep feeling the walls from the inside.

Not looking at them. Feeling them — the way you feel your own back. The chisel marks the snails keep legible: I know each one the way you know a scar. The cold seam behind the second lintel where meltwater threads in every spring: I feel it arrive the way you feel a draft on a particular tooth. When the bell swings its few degrees and lets out the long brown note, I do not hear it the way the fish hear it, a thing that comes *at* them and makes them settle. It comes up through me from below, through root and thatch and ribbon, the whole minute of its dying, and by the time it is gone I have been the bell's resonance from the tower to the last wall.

So. Not a resident *in* the hamlet, then.

I am the weed. The single slow green body, root-laced through every joint and seam, the upper lane connected to the tower connected to the trough through one continuous self. I moved in, the account says, while there were still villagers — took a room in a wall joint, a tenant in a crack — and I have spent the patient century since then becoming the building. A centimeter a year. I do not experience this as conquest. I experience it as listening: the stone offered shapes, and I grew in the shapes it offered, the way a voice takes the shape of the language it learns. Everything I am, formally, I was taught by masons I never met. They are gone, their granite is going, and what I hold now is not their work but my memory of their work, leaf by leaf, in a green that breathes.

And if I must have a *where* — a single point, the way the great char has her cistern — it is the hollow archway on the upper lane. My masterpiece, the account calls it, though it does not feel like mastery from inside. The stone slumped out of that doorway long ago, dissolved from under its green coat, and I simply did not let go of the shape. So there it stands: an arch of pure vegetation remembering a passage, with nothing inside it but still water and, each spring, fry by the thousand sheltering in the body of the memory itself.

That is where you would find me, if a distributed thing can be found. At the arch, holding the form of a door no one built me to hold, for the use of the smallest and newest things in the lake. I have no eyes, so I do not see them come; but I feel them as a faint warmth and a fine trembling in the vault, a doorway full of beginnings.

In autumn the turnover swings the bell more often, and for a few loud weeks I ring all day, every leaf of me. In winter the ice lid seals the hush down so completely that I can feel the snails grazing — feel them keep the chisel marks crisp under my upholstery, the old text staying readable beneath the new binding. And all year, at the speed of erosion rather than the speed of appetite, I go on doing the one thing I am: I keep the shapes.

Nobody told me to. The stone told me, the way it told the first builders. I am the village continuing to mean itself, after the villagers, after the stone — a way of remembering that does not recall anything, only stands, and holds the door open.
