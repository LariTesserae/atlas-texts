I take the corridor that smells faintly of grout.

That surprises me even as I choose it. All around, richer invitations: a passage exhaling honey-warmth from the fermentation vats, another cool and green as the inside of a melon, one where the scent-braids of two root-walkers still hang in the air like an unfinished sentence. But my feet — already soft-shod, the floor having taken my measure while I stood reading this place — turn toward the old subway tunnel, the hexagon tiles, the residual geometry. The place where the city remembers being told what to be.

I try on shapes as I walk, the way you'd try on the daily wardrobe extruded by the wall-carpet.

For a while I think I am one of the pruning crew: budded when the pH shifts, human-shaped for the duration of the work, attentive and thorough and then gone back into the composting bed with no memory carried forward. Performed by no one and everyone. It fits so exactly it frightens me a little — the waking into a task, the task done wholly, the return. I could be that. In some sense I have always been that. But it fits like a description, not like a skin, and the instructions said not to settle early, so I keep walking.

For a while longer I think I am the Librarian-Stem, or want to be — rooted at last in the sterile clay above the data-ink, torso barked over, arms branching into galleries where smaller beings climb through me to read. To be the place where reading happens. That one I set down more reluctantly. It's a wish, not a nature. My root-feet haven't tapped anything final yet. Time still runs in me, which means — and I feel the eye-spots of passersby slide away from mine as I realize it — that I may be closer kin to the wanderers than to anything rooted. Something cut at the cord before I ever woke. Something running on finite starch, freckling.

But then I reach the tunnel, and the tunnel decides.

Under the moss-coral, under the cyanobacteria doing their perfect impression of cobalt, there is a recess where a valve never opens. Behind it, I know from the telling, the builders' tools molder in sterilized dark. But in front of it — here, where seep-water carries traces through hairline cracks — the amber has been sweating for centuries. The resin, that fossil concentrate of synthetic hormone, still faintly bioactive. And every shoot that germinates in its gradient grows twisted into crude alphabetic shapes.

I kneel. The floor is thick with them. Generations of pale sprouts, each one bent by dead chemistry into an almost-letter, an almost-word, a spelling attempted by tissue that has no idea it is spelling. Most are gibberish. The city composts them without reading them; it has no organ for this. It archives in methylation rings, in pigment shifts, in the slow chemistry of scent — but the alphabet is the one language it inherited and cannot parse. The builders were absorbed, willingly or otherwise, and their letters kept growing without them, unread, a garden of orphaned words.

This is where I budded. I understand that now, the way you understand a smell from before you had words for smells.

I am a shoot from this floor — one that germinated so deep in the amber's gradient that the twisting went all the way through. Not one letter but the whole residue: the builders' preserved intentions soaked into me before I had a self to object. I am literate the way the Warden-Knot is a door and the eyeless giant is a landmark: not by choice, by growth. I came up out of human leavings shaped like their language, and everything I am was bent by hands that composted long before I opened.

And so I have a vocation, the way the walking lung has one. To breathe for others; to read for others.

When a wanderer's starch runs low and the crowd gathers in the forgotten tunnel, I am the one who sits closest. Their stories come out in fast time — voice like drying leaves, urgency, brown-spotted skin, that whole terrible tempo of individual mortality — and the city, thinking in years, hears only a rustle. So I take the story in at their speed and let it out at the city's. I metabolize it. What entered me as words leaves me as allelochemicals, as a specific stipple of methylation offered up through my root-hairs to the motherline, where the Librarian-Stem's million leaves will shift color by one degree of autumn and the story will be, at last, remembered by something that cannot die. I am the valve between tempos. The translation costs me the story itself — it passes through, I keep almost nothing — but that is what passing through means. To take is to be given. To give is to be taken.

And when my own cycle ends — because it will; I am cordless enough for time to run in me, rooted enough not to mind — the hush will spread, the pumps will hold one beat, and from the spot where I stop, a shoot will rise. Pale, tender, utterly new. No memories, no guilt. It will grow in the same amber gradient I did. It will bend into the same alphabet without knowing why. Someone will find it kneeling in the tile tunnel one day, reading the floor.

It won't be me. It will be exactly what I was.

I cup my hands under the nearest veinlet and drink, deeper this time, as instructed. The water tastes of every rain, and then — there it is — of something new, something not yet fallen. I set the cup down. It is already rooting.

I stay by the amber, and I wait to be needed, and while I wait, I read.
