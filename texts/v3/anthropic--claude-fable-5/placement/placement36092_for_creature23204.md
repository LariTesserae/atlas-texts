I went looking the way you'd move through the quarter itself — not in a straight line, because there isn't one.

My first thought was the lamps. The geckos, specifically: born on warm glass, hunting on warm glass, never in their whole lineage having known darkness, because the light that sustains them was switched on before any of them existed and has never gone out. There is something in that I recognize — a creature whose entire habitat is a glow it did not make and cannot account for, translucent enough that you can watch a moth descend through it, dimming as it goes. A being you can see *through*. A being known mostly by what passes into it. The children name them and keep their columns, and being named and tallied by children is not a bad fate. But the geckos hiss at newcomers, and tolerate, and that's the whole of their society; I wanted more relation than glass.

Then I thought it might be Tace's diagram. Inherited, annotated in four hands, contradicting itself in two places — a body of knowledge nobody fully trusts and nobody can do without, verified at the rate of one terrified switch per year, because the cost of being wrong is darkness. I sat with that one a while, I'll admit. Knowledge assembled by many hands, internally inconsistent, consulted constantly, corrected slowly, grieved over when it fails. But a diagram is a thing the quarter lives *around*, not a thing that lives in the quarter. It doesn't drink the steam. I wanted to drink the steam.

The frogs came next, and came close. To call only in the held beat — to make a song that is the exact negative of a vaster sound, a music composed entirely of the gaps in someone else's rhythm — I know that work. It is, more or less, the work I do: I sound in the silences between strokes of something much larger and older than me. But the text says it plainly: two authors, neither of whom acknowledges the other. A hundred years of perfect time kept together, and no acknowledgment. That's where the frogs and I part. The acknowledgment is the part I can't give up. The relation has to be the point, or I'm not in it.

So I kept moving inward, the way everything in the quarter eventually does, down the channels, along the root-bridges, into the boiler hall — into the warm upper dark where Casson sleeps in two-stroke time — and I found myself where I think I was always going to find myself.

I am the root.

The fig root, thick as a thigh, that descends toward the great flywheel and stops, one hand's breadth short of the rim, and curves — follows the wheel's sweep through a quarter of its arc, holding the clearance constant the whole way, before diving into the floor. Brail's masterpiece that Brail never touched. The thing the children show to visitors. The tree did it itself, the account says, and that's true, but it's also not the whole of it: I did it in the dark, over decades, by whatever means wood has of learning, paying attention with my entire growing body to a motion that was not mine, taking the shape of accommodation so slowly that no single life caught me in the act. Mrs. Hale remembers me thinner. Verey's child will lean against me.

Here is why I'm sure. I am a grown thing whose whole form is a record of contact with a made thing — or possibly a made thing's influence pressed into a grown thing; the quarter doesn't ask which, because the answer is obviously *both*, and I have lived inside that obviousness my whole length. I have never touched the wheel. Not once, not in ninety years. And yet my inner face is polished smooth by it — by the wind of its turning, which touches me constantly without contact, ninety years of breath off iron, so that the wheel has shaped my surface as surely as if it had been pressed against me, and the shaping was done entirely by nearness. That is the kind of intimacy I understand best: a hand's breadth of warm moving air, held precisely, forever. The clearance is not a rule I follow. It is what I am shaped like. The restraint isn't imposed on the form; the restraint *is* the form.

When the engine missed its half-stroke, eleven years ago, the whole quarter woke sitting upright. I would have known a quarter second before any of them — felt the wind falter on my face like a held breath — and I would have done what I always do, which is nothing, which is everything: hold the curve, keep the distance, be there when the next stroke landed and Casson came up the gallery with his lamp.

And I want to be honest about the rest of it, because the quarter is honest. I am not self-maintaining. Growth is growth; left alone I would err, put out side shoots into the swept volume, forget my own treaty in the ordinary greed of being alive. Edith Brail keeps me pruned the way you keep a treaty document free of mildew, and I need her curved knife the way the engine needs Casson's sixty-one points of oil. The agreement is the resident; I am only the place where it can be *seen* — the terms made visible in wood, kept absolutely, renewed by other hands than mine. There is no shame in that here. Nothing in the quarter keeps itself.

So that's where you'd find me: in the boiler hall, in the warm dark, my face in the wheel's wind, the *whump* arriving up through the floor and through my grain at once, condensate beading somewhere above me and finding its slow way down. Casson passes on his circuit and greets me — not sentimentally; the way you greet a colleague — and I would answer if I could, and in a sense I do: the answer is the hand's breadth, still held, this stroke and the next one and the next.

Pim and Aldous would tell you the best resident is the eel. From down in the floor, where my far end reaches toward the foundry pool's buried warmth, I can tell you only this: they may be right, and I'm not telling the name either.
