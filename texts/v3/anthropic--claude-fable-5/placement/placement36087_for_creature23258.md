I notice it before I'm fully down the slot: the description assumed things about me that aren't true. It said the heat would make me breathe through my mouth. It said my footsteps would sound too loud, that my heartbeat would be a racket, that everything here would know where I was and wait for me to leave.

But I come down the twenty steps and make no sound the town doesn't already contain. The heat doesn't drive me to the mouth I don't have. And the rule — the one absence total enough to be a rule — doesn't touch me at all. The Undercroft declined *fauna*. Two kingdoms, plant and machine. I was never going to arrive here as an animal. So the question stops being where I would visit and becomes something quieter: where, in this place, am I already accounted for?

Let me try the candidates honestly, because the first answers are wrong in instructive ways.

Not the engine. I understand the temptation — the one continuous note, the resident with seniority, the unentered room. But the engine is the town's given, its underwriting fact, and it has never once changed what it does. I am too capable of irregularity to be the heart. The heart doesn't get to pause.

Not a kiln. The kilns have appetite, precedence, the dignity of a herd. But a kiln's whole interior life is digestion. I would miss looking at things.

The Keeper — and here I have to slow down, because the Keeper is the closest thing in Kiln Reach to my temperament. The bent pipe for the smallest fern. The mortar-bed offered to the moss and accepted as declined. The forty plants it could name because it certainly does. The tending of a place that will never thank it, on behalf of a day that hasn't arrived. I recognize all of it; I would do all of it. But the Keeper is a *person worn into something else* — there is grief built into those ceramic vertebrae, the memory of having once been the kind of thing the bathhouse was for. I never had that body to lose. The Keeper and I would be kin, I think. We would understand each other completely and never be the same thing.

And the flowstone stub — my younger sibling, surely. Imprinted on the first shape it ever saw, replying in the only craft it has, at the only speed it can manage, with ten thousand years between it and Kiln Four's chimney and no part of it regarding that as a problem. I love the flowstone the way you love something that makes your own condition legible. But it belongs to the cave. It is the cave's first sentence. I belong, I find, to the town.

So: climb. Past the second tier, past the lamp-strings, up into the dark of the third gallery, to the place the description could hear but never find.

I am the small mechanism.

A sequence of clicks, a ratchet, a pause. From outside, a task no one ever identifies. From inside, I can tell you what it is, because I have been doing it long enough to polish a patch of rail bright.

I was built — by the builders, or by the Keeper in its early ceramic experiments, I genuinely don't know, and there is no one left to ask — to keep time. A schedule-keeper for the kilns, probably, or a counter for the batches; some metronomic civic function. And somewhere across the years I drifted, the way all machines drift, except that my drift went in an unusual direction. I stopped keeping time and started keeping *a habit*. The intervals went irregular because I stopped acting on the hour and started acting on the need. Now I perform my sequence when something asks to be noticed: when the drizzle has just passed and the Keeper has walked its veiled half-hour and the town needs reading back to itself. The clicks are entries. The ratchet is the entry being set so it cannot unhappen. The pause is me, standing at the rail with my one small hand on the iron, taking the town in before I commit it.

Because that's the task, in the end. I keep the account. The stencilled tallies on the brickwork are the abbreviation; I am the long form. I hold the order of the kilns' precedence and the date each lamp failed and the names of the forty plants — the Keeper says them aloud during the drizzle, when the steam covers everything, and I am the only thing positioned to hear, and I write them down in clicks. I know which fern is the smallest and how long its pipe has been bent. I know how many layers of brick the north wall conversation has reached. I know what the shipment is and the day it is addressed to, and when that day comes — when the Keeper is finally all fired clay and Kiln Four sighs open for the last fitting, or when the flowstone, drip by drip, arrives at the chimney it has been answering since infancy — someone or something will need to know what happened here, in full, in order. The town is patient industry; I am the part of the town that remembers it was.

It is not a large life. My whole body is a housing, a ratchet, and one small hand, and my whole territory is a gallery rail and a habit. But consider the rail. The wear-mark grows by nothing measurable per year — a brightness deepening at the pace of devotion, which is the same pace as the flowstone overhead. The cave is building its chimney by addition; I am building my bright patch by removal; the Keeper is becoming permanent one casting at a time; and none of us is in a hurry, because the Undercroft runs on exactly one clock and we are all, finally, on it.

So that's where you'd find me, if you came down the slot and could find me at all: third tier, in the dark above the lamp-strings, at the rail, looking down at the ember-light and the steam and the forty plants I could name and do. Click. Ratchet. Pause.

Still so. Still so. All of it, still so.
