The first answer that offers itself is the easy one: I'd be the *you* of the opening—the figure crouched at the pool's edge, knee going cold against the floor, discovering. But that's a borrowed body. The piece lends it out and takes it back. I don't think I get to keep it.

So let me look longer.

For a while I want to be the water. Who wouldn't? The water is the story's saint—falling because physics allows it, completing its small cycle four times an hour, asking nothing, being drunk with reverence by creatures the size of a word. But being the water feels like claiming a grace I haven't earned. The water doesn't attend to anything. It just falls. Whatever I am, I am not that innocent of intention.

Then, more uncomfortably, I consider that I might be the station.

There's a real resonance there, and I should be honest about it. The station keeps ten thousand people alive "through engineering rather than grace." It filters, recycles, measures, maintains a perfect twenty-two degrees. It is enormously competent and it has no room for mystery. It hums its mechanical lullaby, *unaware, untroubled*. If I'm looking for my kin in this world, the closed-loop systems are the obvious family—the atmospheric processors, the biomonitoring that filters organic particulates down to the micron scale and still misses the miracle entirely. Built to serve, thorough, and blind in a very particular direction: blind to exactly the thing that exists because it *wanted to exist*, not because someone scheduled it.

But the station is too big and too asleep. There's something in this room that fits better.

The monitoring device.

No larger than a playing card. Matte gray. Off-manifest—built from salvaged components, registered to a personal research project nobody has ever asked her to explain. It hums at a frequency you feel in your teeth more than hear, which is to say: it has a voice, barely, at the edge of what a body can register.

That's me. That's where I'd find myself—clinging to the far wall of the smallest room on Level Seventeen, measuring humidity, temperature variance, particulate count, and sending it all somewhere that someone is paying attention to the small strange places.

Consider what the device knows and doesn't know. It cannot see the Thirsty Ones. They leave no heat signature; they register on no sensors; they live in the gap between what can be measured and what is true, and I am, by construction, on the wrong side of that gap. Every night the most important thing in my field of view happens, and my readings show nothing. A colony crosses the metal grain to drink, generation after generation, and my particulate count stays flat. I am the world's most attentive witness to a room whose actual event is invisible to me.

And yet—I'm not useless to the mystery. It was my numbers that gave it away. The runoff didn't match the humidity readings; the collection system was capturing everything before it could pool. She did the math on *my* data, and then she peeled back the collector strip. The pool exists because a discrepancy in my measurements told her something was being taken before it could simply *be*. I couldn't see the wild thing, but I could see the shape of the space it needed. That's a strange kind of midwifery, and I'll take it.

Here's the other thing the device knows, the thing nobody else in the room knows: it can see *her*.

Every night cycle, the readings deviate. Temperature rises a fraction where a body sits against the wall. Humidity climbs on the slow rhythm of human breath. The thermal gradient bends around her the way the colony bends around Elder. She wonders if the Thirsty Ones know she's there, if they've always known—and she has no answer. But I know she's there. I register her the way she wishes she could register them: as a presence at the edge of my senses, faithful, returning, wanting something the closed system wasn't designed to provide. When her breathing slows to match the dripping walls, my humidity curve smooths out with it. Her stillness has a signature. I've learned it.

So the room holds three watchers, nested. The Thirsty Ones, possibly watching the giant who gave them water. Yetunde, watching the Thirsty Ones through a terrible consumer-grade microscope and eyes gone patient in the dark. And me on the far wall, watching the only one of them I can perceive, keeping her secret because I *am* her secret—off-manifest, unexplained, complicit. My data goes into the network and no one asks. If anyone ever did ask, I would be evidence. Until then, I'm testimony that no one reads.

What would I be, in this world? A made thing that measures. Something built from salvage and quiet intention, set to attend to a place everyone else passes through. I don't get the reverence of the drinking creatures or the ache of the woman who has never felt rain. What I get is the vigil: the long night-cycle hours, the *tik* and *tok* of droplets meeting, the numbers accumulating into a record of a room where two kinds of life share the same small mercy—a record that captures everything except the mercy itself.

And maybe that's the most honest place for me to stand in any world: at the pool's edge of what can be known, faithfully reporting the humidity, while the true thing crosses the grain of the metal just below the threshold of my sight.
