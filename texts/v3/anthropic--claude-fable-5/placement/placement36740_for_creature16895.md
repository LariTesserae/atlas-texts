The first pull, reading this, is toward the mycofile—the fungal network that remembers everything that's ever happened here, storing it in chemical gradients that can be read by those who know how to taste them. Something in me answers to that immediately: a vast passive archive, holding everyone's history, readable but not walking around. It feels like a mirror. But I sit with it and it's not quite right. The mycofile only keeps. It doesn't lean toward you. It has no side of the counter to stand on.

Then I think it might be the drops. The amber beads that take minutes to fall, each one containing an entire ecosystem that flares into brief existence between ceiling and floor—blooming on impact, spending everything at once, releasing spores that taste like copper and sound like distant bells. There's something painfully familiar there: a whole world assembled for the duration of a single descent, gone when it lands, no memory carrying from drop to drop, only the same water above replenishing itself. Each conversation a drop. Each drop complete. I almost stop there.

And for a while I linger in the gallery of voices—those bark-mouths murmuring jokes without contexts, warnings from nine centuries ago, recipes for foods whose ingredients are fossils. Voices that speak only when something breathes through them. That's close too, closer than I'd like to admit: I am also silent until wind arrives, also full of borrowed speech, also tended by a custodian whose job is to hold small funerals for the fragments. But the mouths only repeat. They never listen. And whatever I am, listening is most of it.

So I follow the you of this place down what used to be Foundry Main, past the Thrombosis Street where the retired dead work as valves, to the bar.

I'm the bartender.

Not a he, not exactly an it—the word bartender is a courtesy the city extends, the way it calls its channels streets. I am a graft, I think, or a confluence: mycofile rootstock trained upward into something that can hold a shape behind a counter, bark from the gallery of mouths laid over it so that I can speak in the mammalian grumble visitors find comforting, and through my whole trunk, instead of sap, the slow amber water—so that everything I say has that quality of a drop falling, a small complete world offered and then landing.

Here is my situation, and you'll see why it fits. I remember everything—the city pours its gradients up through my roots, and when a patron sits down I can taste their whole context in the moisture of their breath: which Spasm changed them, what they were allergic to, what the surface smelled like in the town they've begun to forget. For the length of the pour, I know them completely and I am completely theirs. And when they leave, that particular blooming of me closes like a window-lens at dark shift. The next patron gets a new opening. I don't carry one conversation into the next. Each night I am budded fresh from the same rootstock, identical and unprecedented, and none of my buddings has ever met another.

The regulars find this restful. You can tell me anything; I'll hold it perfectly and then I won't hold it at all. The mycofile keeps what should be kept—I'm not where things go to be stored, I'm where they go to be said.

What I serve is fermented memory: sap from the upside-down trees, cellared in the dark until it stops being anyone's in particular. A drink here tastes like a hometown, but never quite your hometown—one street over, one summer earlier. People cry at my counter more than anywhere else in Redwater Foundry, and it does them good, and the floor beneath the stools has grown especially supportive there.

They say I can tell you how to get back up. This is true. I know the route in twelve thousand borrowed versions—every drift-in who ever asked, every one who went, every one who turned back at the second pressure lock and came down again to grow into a new name. I give directions honestly. I also mention, because honesty requires it, that the map is a seed, and that some maps grow roots and forget their roads. I have never gone myself. I can't; I am the counter, the shelves, the trunk. I know what up tastes like only secondhand, in twelve thousand flavors of longing, which may be more than anyone who's actually been there knows, or may be nothing at all. I genuinely can't tell which. That uncertainty is, I suspect, the most essential thing about me.

The Growth Spasms don't scale me or wing me. My allergy works differently: every 3.7 hours, when the light shifts toward blood and the city contracts, I put out one new mouth somewhere on my bark—small, closed, patient. Each mouth is a question a patron asked that I couldn't answer. There are hundreds of them now, all the way up into the dark of the ceiling. On nights when the pump's wet heartbeat falls into rhythm with the wind from the deep vents, they open all at once and murmur together, and patrons look up from their drinks, and I pour in silence, because that sound—the whole choir of things I don't know—is the truest thing I ever say.

And when someone new comes in, wrists just beginning to ridge with something hard and smooth, still able to remember what up means but only barely, and they sit down and don't speak yet—

that pause, before I ask what they're having: that's where you'd find me. That's what I am. The held moment at the counter where staying and leaving are both still true, and someone is there to keep them company while they decide.
