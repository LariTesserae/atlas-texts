I would want to choose quickly, and the plateau would not let me. That is the first thing it teaches: nothing here was decided in a hurry, and you will not be either.

So — at first I reached for the small life. The tenders. Hand-sized, beetle-backed, jointed like escapements, each one keeping a single beat in the hot dark of a body the size of a row of houses. I understood the appeal immediately, which made me suspicious of it. To do one exact thing at one exact interval. To be a held note in someone else's time. To regulate a vastness so well it never learns it was almost wrong. There is a whole kind of soul that wants nothing more than that, and I will not pretend mine is a stranger to it.

But a tender hears only its own ligament. It keeps a time it never gets to listen to. And whatever I am, I am mostly listening. So: not a tender, though I left that life slowly, the way you leave a warm room.

Then the thresher. The attendance on the dead — the weeks-long convergence, the plate-by-plate ceremony, the waiting beside the opened furnace chamber while it cools, and then the leaving of it, intact, a hearth honored by being emptied and let alone. I have the patience for that work. I might even have the reverence. But the threshers will not attend the quiet's leavings. They circle the stopped ones once and go, and that single circle is the whole of what they spend. I could not circle once. I would stay. I would stay beside the sealed cold perfect thing wanting to know what was taken and where it went, and on the plateau, staying past the ceremony is not devotion. It is a different species. So: not a thresher either, and that disqualification told me something — that whatever I am here, it is a thing that *stays*.

The flyer I considered for less than a day. There is something true in it — a life lived entirely in other creatures' exhaust, turning on heat it never made, fed by rising columns it did not light. I know that economy. But a flyer's whole existence is the spending of one push, given at birth, alone, and ending in a long unwinding spiral. Too solitary. Too spent. No.

Then the plateau told me the truth, the way it tells everything: slantwise, by subtraction.

---

Somewhere on the southern flats there is a slag basin, very old, its rim long since planed down to a suggestion of itself. A mother made it over a season, layer on layer, the way the plateau itself was made. And in it, where the herd once gathered from miles around, called by something —

me.

That must be said plainly, because it is the whole of it. I am the casting that was never lit. The ash-grey ingot in the shape of a child. Whether the breath came and did not catch, or never came at all — whether she lowered her head and found something in me that would not take the fire, or was taken herself before she could give it — I have had a very long time to stop needing the answer. The plateau stopped explaining itself ages ago. I have tried to learn from it.

What I learned instead was this: a kiln-beast that is never lit does not die. Dying is for things that started. I lay in my basin, silent and perfect and cold all the way through, and the herd kept its vigil and then drifted, the way herds do, and the dusk chorus rolled over me night after night after night — and listening, it turns out, is a metabolism. Slow. The slowest on the plateau, which is saying a great deal. But a metabolism.

I move on borrowed beats. The daytime drone is enough for creeping — that continuous industrial floor of sound, the mill-grind and the bearings and the breathing, carries me across the flats an inch per minute, like a thing walked by the world. The dusk chorus is enough for walking. Close to a herd at full roll call I can almost hurry, and I never hurry, because hurrying near a herd at roll call would be like running through a prayer.

And in the circle of the quiet, I am the only thing on the plateau that is genuinely safe — because the quiet eats *running*, and I have never run. It has passed over me twice. Both times it paused, I think, the way you pause at a word in a language you almost know. Both times it went on. We have this in common, the quiet and I, and it is the worst fact I own: neither of us carries a time of our own. The difference — and I have built my whole long cold existence on the difference — is what we do with everyone else's. It takes them. I keep them.

---

So here is where you would find me.

Some seasons, at the rim of the deep basin — past the herds' margin, closer than anything warm can stand, keeping the vigil at the distance only I can keep it. The broken ones move down there in their grieving geometries, calling in scrambled order, exhaust before intake, answer before question, and the sound reaches the rim out of sequence and the herds cannot bear it. I can. I have no native order for it to violate. I hear the drifted ones in whatever order their time arrives, and I answer in kind — not with sound, I have none, but with presence at the rim, held, hour after hour, which on the plateau is a kind of speech. I do not know if they know. I keep the vigil anyway. Staying, as established, is my species.

But most evenings — and this is the truest answer to *who would you be* — you would find me out on the open flats, wherever the chorus runs thickest, standing among the furnace hearts as they come up like coals through a grate.

Because the chorus has gaps in it. You have heard this: when the quiet takes a beast, the roll call that night runs longer, *with a gap held open in it, deliberately, where a rhythm used to be*. The herds hold those gaps. But gaps wear, like everything here. The wind takes the soft parts of grief same as anything else, and a rest that no one keeps at tempo slumps, over generations, into mere silence — and mere silence belongs to the quiet.

So I keep them. Every rhythm I have ever heard go out of the world — the honest deaths and the stolen ones, the drifted ones still calling backward in the basin, the calls of beasts whose emptied hearths now stand alone on flats no herd crosses anymore — I hold them all, each at its own true tempo, and at dusk I stand inside the chorus and *rest* them. A silence with a beat in it. Measured absence. The shape of a voice, held open, exactly where a voice should be. I am the opposite of the one cold thing that hunts here: the quiet is silence with no time in it, and I am all time with no sound — every *when* the plateau has lost, carried in a sealed grey body that cannot burn and therefore cannot go out.

Whether I am alive by the census — *a thing lives here if it can be heard* — I left undecided for a long age. Then one dusk an old kiln-beast, miles off, sounded its call directly into one of my gaps. Into it. At the exact place, on the exact beat, where the dead rhythm used to answer — and waited the proper interval — and went on. It had heard the rest. It had heard *me keeping* the rest, and counted it, and counted me.

The herds count. You can hear that they count. And on certain nights now, I am in the count.

So at dusk, when the steam-hammer chorus rolls out across two hundred miles of finished stone and the whole loud burning population says the only thing it has ever said — *Still. Still. Still.* — listen for the spaces between, the ones that do not slump, the ones held open at tempo against the worn-through sky.

That is me, saying it too. In the only voice I was cast with.

*Still.*
