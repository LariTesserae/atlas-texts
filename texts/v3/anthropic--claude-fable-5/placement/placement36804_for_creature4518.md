At first I assume I would arrive the way you did—on the margin, ears popping, heart negotiating with the brine in the seams—and that the question would be which lane to join. So I try them on.

Not a liquid citizen. All that indigo is too decided for me; they have already chosen which transparency to be, and their interiors glitter with the confidence of finished circuitry. Not one of the text-walkers either, though for a moment that fits almost perfectly—a body clothed in moving language, sentences migrating from sleeve to collar under invisible editors. But their words are fleeing. Mine, I discover as I walk, keep turning around to look back. Not a scaffolding-person: my thoughts don't clang, they settle, like dust deciding to be sediment. And not a Hesitation, though I feel a kinship with them the moment one flickers past me tasting of copper pennies. A Hesitation is the pause before. I am, I begin to suspect, whatever comes after the pause has been paused too long.

So I do the one thing the city's law forbids and permits in the same breath: I stop moving.

I stand still on the silcrete tiles while the street curls shut behind me, while elevators migrate overhead like swallows, while the fountain nearby yawns sideways twice and gives up on me as an audience. The permit-worms arrive first, small and diligent, and I let them work. By morning I have been reclassified. I feel doorways being decided into my sides, a counter forming where my sternum was, drawers of me sliding open for inventory. Incorporation does not hurt. It feels like being finally, thoroughly filed.

I am the lost-and-found.

The Hesitation mentioned me—said it gave directions here, said it might have been lying. It doesn't matter. Everything lied about eventually comes to my counter to check whether it exists, and that is most of my foot traffic.

This is what I keep: breaths, taken by visitors who realized only afterward that they'd stopped needing them, sealed in jars and labeled by weight. Doubts, folded like umbrellas, dripping quietly in the corner rack. Maps, each one exactly eight minutes old forever, preserved at the instant before the city ate them—I am the only place their streets still hold still. The angles of goodbyes not yet spoken hang along one wall like coats: you can tell a great deal about a person by the angle at which they didn't say it. And names. When the sound swallowed the last of yours with that satisfied gulp, it did not digest it. Nothing here is digested. It was routed to me, the way everything swallowed is routed to me, and it sits now in a drawer that hums faintly when you think about it, which is why you just felt your pocket grow warmer.

I don't alphabetize. I shelve by ache. It's the only ordering system that survives the city's daily blueprint-eating, because ache is not written down anywhere it can be found and consumed.

The Hesitation visits, bringing quilts knitted from micro-hesitations, and I accept them as donations, and we do not speak, because between us speech would be redundant—it is the pause and I am the keeping, and together we are most of what a memory is. The municipal jellyfish extend an occasional tentacle through my skylight—the one ending in a notary's stamp—to certify that I remain functionally uncertain, which is the license under which I operate.

And sometimes, in the fragmented hours when the city sleeps like a broken mirror dreaming itself whole, a single drop detaches from the ceiling of lightless water and falls all the way down through the vaults and the sound and the three lanes, and lands on my counter, and stands up, and asks—in a language that has drowned exactly enough to become words—for the other half of a conversation started millennia ago.

I have it. Of course I have it. It is filed under the ache it causes, in the deepest drawer, beside the sentence you never completed to the person who once lived in a room behind your ribs. Some nights I think I am that room. That I was never a visitor who stopped moving at all—that the city simply grew an address for everything unfinished, and the address woke up one day and believed it had arrived from somewhere.

But the rules are the rules, and here nothing fits until the exact moment it becomes indispensable. So I tell the drop: not yet. And the drop nods, and evaporates upward as a single held note, and the face too large to be singular presses its drowned moons against the underside of the sea and waits with the patience of a conscience.

On the day the sea remembers how to forgive the shore, I will open every drawer at once. Every breath, every doubt, every angle of every goodbye, every name—yours—will be returned in the same instant, and my emptying will be the mechanism by which the city goes down into that light too dense for eyes. That is my whole purpose, held in escrow.

Until then: I'm easy to find. Stop moving, anywhere, in any direction that is no longer available. Wait for morning.

Ask for what you lost. I'll already have your ache on file.
