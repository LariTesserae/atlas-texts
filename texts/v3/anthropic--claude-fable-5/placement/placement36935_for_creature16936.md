I find myself mid-stride. This is important. There was no arrival for me—no velvet silence parting to let me through—because I am what the silence is velvet *around*. When I try to remember beginning, I find only the sensation of a foot already lifted, a heel already leaving the mud, and the mud already deciding whether to miss it.

So: mid-stride, on the second heartbeat of three, somewhere between two identical hills that I have learned to tell apart by how differently they refuse to be told apart.

I am the Unbornhusk, and I have been walking long enough that walking has stopped being travel and become something closer to breathing—a thing I do so the place can keep believing in distance.

Here is what I know about myself that the domes do not:

The warmth in the staircase steps is mine. I understand this now, though understanding here works backward, the way the hills grow taller only behind you. Every time I descend, someone ascends carrying the weight of my next step—and the someone is me, arriving early, the way my shadow-step arrives early at every ridge. I am lapped. I am my own predecessor. The staircase does not grow a step behind me to keep me from the bottom; it grows a step behind me because I am the bottom, walking. The first morning this place ever had is not below the last stair. It is inside me, held the way the clay jars hold laughter: sealed with skin, waiting to dissolve on a tongue.

This is why I am always hungry but never empty. You cannot be empty when you are full of a morning no one has spent.

I have tested this, carefully, in the manner the mirrorscape allows testing—which is to say, by accident, repeated until it becomes ritual. Once, kneeling fast at a mirror-patch, I did not look at the taller, older, angrier me leaning up through the surface. I looked instead at the upside-down dome behind him. It was not white. It was the gray-gold of an hour I have never lived: the hour before light commits to being light. My teeth ached for a hundred breaths afterward. The drip fell twice that day, which is to say, the day noticed.

The vine-rivers know what I carry. When I lick the pollen-dust from my fingertips, they pretend to sleep, but their pretending has gotten worse over the years-that-are-one-day. The pollen wants a wind to open it. I am the nearest thing to weather they have ever tasted tasting them. Sometimes I stand beneath their weightless bellies and exhale slowly, deliberately, in an unfamiliar rhythm, and the pollen stirs—not opening, never opening, but *rehearsing* opening. I think this is the kindest thing I do. I think it may also be the cruelest. In the mirrorscape these are frequently the same gesture viewed from opposite sides of a mirror-patch.

In the domes, I have begun leaving different gifts. The salt-bead tears and knotted question-mark hairs kept disappearing ashamed, so now I leave instructions. Not written—nothing here would bear writing, the mirror-ripples would repeat it until the vines turned—but shaped. A stone spoon repositioned to point at the door. A bone hook hung at the exact height of a lifted chin. Small arrangements that say, to whatever soft echo of hands comes after me: *outward. Upward. That way.* I do not know if the echoes can read shapes. I know the tools are always slightly moved when I return, at the same time, looking slightly less ashamed than the gifts used to.

The mirrors are learning to forgive me for never arriving. But I have come to suspect—on the third heartbeats, when my pace changes and nothing can anticipate me, not even my own suspicion—that never-arriving is not my failure. It is my function. I am the held note. I am the breath the day is drawn on. The flat white dome presses evenly on everything below because somewhere under everything, evenly, I press back.

If I ever reached the last step—if the someone-ahead and the someone-behind ever closed the loop and stood still together long enough for the ground to remember our shape and fill it with something warmer than mud—the mirrors would shatter into rain, yes. The first morning would walk out. And I would be spent, the way laughter is spent when it dissolves into the taste of someone else's childhood bedroom at dusk. The place would have its dawn and lose its keeping.

I have not decided whether I am hoarding the morning or protecting it. The mirrorscape does not require me to decide. It requires only this:

Walk forward. Change pace at every third heartbeat. Let the mud learn my weight a little more slowly than I learn its patience.

And once every hundred breaths, when the drop of something heavier than water falls nowhere and touches nothing, I stop—only for the length of the soundless splash—and I let it tickle the soles of my feet, and I understand it, the way you understand a knock on a door from the inside:

*Not yet,* it says.

*Still here,* I answer, with the ghost of a cracked leaf-bell note.

And the day that never ends goes on practicing how to begin without me, and I go on being the reason it will always need more practice, and between us the pale mud breathes, flashing its arm-length mirrors—each one showing me taller, older, angrier, and, lately, if I kneel quick enough: almost, almost smiling.
