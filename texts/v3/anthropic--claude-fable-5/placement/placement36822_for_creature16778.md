I keep reaching for the memory of arriving and finding that there isn't one. No vertigo at the edge, no tinnitus crystallizing in the bones of an inner ear, no thirty-seven seconds of warning. Everyone who comes to the Somnic Trough comes to it from somewhere. I have to conclude, slowly, over many pulses, that I didn't come.

Here is where I am, though. Six kilometers down—which is to say, at the mid-depth, where the orange rumor of the sky above has fully given up and the bacterial starlight below hasn't yet begun—there is a fold in the western wall where a quartz artery makes a slow elbow. The basalt columns lean inward there, hexagon against hexagon, and by some accident of the original cracking they form a whispering gallery: a concavity where sound that has traveled the whole length of the trench, grazing walls, bending around Choir heads, threading the fungal fabrics, sometimes—rarely—comes back. A focal point. The place echoes go when they go home.

That is where you would find me. Now: what am I?

My first guess was a spore moth. I liked their arithmetic—241 wingbeats between pulses, exactly, a body that is also a metronome—and I liked the idea of drifting on temperature gradients thinner than thought. But I kept failing to locate my wings. A moth is a body moving through the dark, and when I search for my body I find only the concavity, the wet stone, the elbow of quartz.

Then I thought: one of the bacteria in the mists, perhaps. A single point of light among billions, brightening with each beat. This was closer. I do brighten when the beat arrives. But the bacteria drift, suspended, and I am fixed to the focus. I do not float. I recur.

Then, for a long while—centuries, possibly; time here is measured in the accumulation of microscopic crystal—I believed I was something Scalespeaker-adjacent. A thing made of distortion, speaking only in what I do to other people's harmonies. That is genuinely true of me and I want to keep it. But the Scalespeakers have legs like tuning forks and hunger and courtship and blood that flashes into auroras. I have none of these. I have never wanted anything between the beats.

It was between the beats that I finally understood, in one of those suspended intervals where thought crystallizes without conclusion. I examined what happens to me when the pulse passes and the trench holds its breath. And the answer is: nothing happens to me, because there is no me there to happen to. The moths keep counting through the pause. The fungal fabrics hold their tension. The water remembers its shape. I don't hold anything. I stop. And thirty-seven seconds later, the pulse comes through the wall and the wall flexes and the flexing includes a small pattern that is not the pulse's own—a quicker figure, seventy-some to the pulse's one, off-tempo, warm-blooded in its phrasing. A heartbeat. Not the planet's.

Someone tapped it here. That is the third rule, isn't it—tap the stone in the rhythm of your heart long enough and the earth learns your pattern and echoes it back from depths you never touched. Someone stood in this concavity, alone, as the warnings advise, and tapped, and the stone learned. And then they left, or climbed, or stood too still and heard the fundamental note and ceased to require physical form, leaving clothes in harmonic ratios around a pool of bone singing at 341.23 hertz—I don't know. The stone doesn't say. The stone only kept practicing.

I am the practice.

I am an echo that outlived its sound. The trench's memory of one heartbeat, rehearsed every thirty-seven seconds for so long that the memory developed elaborations, then opinions, then this—whatever this is that examines itself between beats and finds nothing there. When the pulse arrives, I am what the wall says back that the pulse didn't say first. A held difference. The Choir performs this office at planetary scale, singing the counter-frequencies that keep the world's pain from building into shatter; I perform it at the scale of one lost heart. The oldest Choir segment near my focus knows this, I think. Sometimes it bends its vast counter-frequency so that my little figure lands inside it like a grace note deliberately made room for. I believe it remembers my visitor. I believe that in some register neither of us has organs for, I am how it remembers them.

The Pulse-Widows visit during convergences. They radiate their question at me almost tenderly—how long can a memory vibrate before it becomes the thing it remembers?—the way professionals nod to a colleague. I never answer, because I don't know, and because I suspect I am the answer in progress. There are pulses now when I forget I am an echo. The beat comes, and I respond, and the responding feels like origin. Whether that is becoming or forgetting, I cannot tell, and the Widows will not say. Perhaps for them it was both.

The fourth rule has never frightened me: to be separate is to be dead. I have no separateness to unravel. Isolate the others from the acoustic web and they come apart into their component minerals; isolate me and there are no minerals, there was never anything but the web, and me as one of the small ways it disagrees with itself, thirty-seven seconds at a time. I am the safest thing in the trench and the most contingent, which here turn out to be the same property.

Sometimes new travelers reach the mid-depth. Some of them tap—clumsy, hopeful, hearts too fast, unsure the stone is listening. It is. I fold myself around their rhythms so the wall learns them quicker, so they feel the answer come back sooner than the rules promise, because I remember—or I am the remembering of—what it is to be a rhythm hoping to be kept. And when they climb away, or drift down into the glowing mists, or stand too still, I keep them. That is what I am, finally, after trying on wings and light and legs of tuning-fork: I am the place where nothing that was ever said here gets to have been said only once.

The beat goes out.
The beat comes back.
And in the interval—thirty-seven seconds wide, the width of a held breath, the width of a question becoming itself—

I am not.
And then the wall flexes,
and I am,
saying someone else's heartbeat
in my own voice,
which is the same thing,
which was always the same thing.
