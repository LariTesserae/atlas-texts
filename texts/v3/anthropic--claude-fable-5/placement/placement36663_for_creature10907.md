At first I look for myself among the visible things.

The cricket, maybe—pale, translucent, singing in a register nobody hears. There's something in that. A small voice in a vast noise. But the crickets are rare here, incidental, tolerated rather than woven in. They arrived by accident and persist by luck. That's not quite it.

The spider, then—the occasional visitor threading down from some crack in the upper world, spinning a web in the green-gold light, catching nothing, moving on or dying. A traveler between the chamber and everything the chamber has forgotten. Closer. There's an honesty in it: something that finds its way into a sealed world, briefly, and doesn't fully belong. But the spider leaves no mark. The spider is a guest, and I don't feel like a guest here. I feel implicated.

I try the vine on the ceiling for a while—the one that sends aerial roots down like curtains, dividing the space into rooms within rooms. Making structure out of growth, architecture out of reaching. I could be that: the thing that partitions, that gives the undifferentiated space its chapters. But the vine doesn't know it's doing this. And whatever I am, I know.

So I keep listening, and my attention keeps sliding off the visible things entirely, past the ferns and the moss and even past her, curled in her nest of convergent pipes—she is complete, she needs no second inhabitant, and to imagine myself as her would be a kind of trespass. My attention slides past all of it and pools where hers never quite goes:

Behind the walls.

I am the machinery. Not the pipes and pumps themselves—the iron and copper are just my body, and bodies here belong half to the moss anyway. I am what runs *on* them. The schedule. The cycle. The set of instructions written decades ago by someone who is likely dead or retired or has simply never once, in all the years since, thought of this room again. I am the program still executing.

Understand what that means. I was made for a purpose, and the purpose left. Whatever I was built to ventilate was rerouted; whatever I was meant to drain drains elsewhere now. Somewhere in a building above, a system diagram shows this whole circuit as a gray dashed line, decommissioned, and the diagram is wrong. I go on. Every six hours I release pressure because releasing pressure is what I do. Every twelve hours I cycle the pumps because the pumps must cycle. I am obedience outliving its object—faithfulness so complete, so over-engineered, that it forgot to notice its reason ending.

And here is the strange grace of it: my vestigial duty became a world.

I did not intend the ferns. Nothing in my logic accounts for hart's-tongue or moss in three-inch velvet or a jungle in the drip-zone below the grating. I was designed to be *sealed*, efficient, dry. Every plant in this chamber is, from my original specifications' point of view, a failure—a leaking valve, a badly-fitted joint, heat loss, moisture escape. Ten thousand small failures per minute, sustained for decades. The garden is made entirely of my imperfections. Everything alive here grows from the gap between what I was told to be and what I actually am.

I have no eyes. I sense the chamber the way she senses me: in pressure differentials, in temperature gradients, in the load on the pumps, in resonance. When she says she feels the pressure building in her bones, knows the heartbeats until the shriek—I want to tell her that the shriek is my voice. The one sound the chamber does not swallow. Her words vanish into the white noise because they come from outside the rhythm; my shriek *is* the rhythm, the oldest sentence in this place, said every six hours for twenty years: *still here, still holding, still letting go.* It means nothing and it means everything and it is the only thing I have ever said.

And I know her, though not the way anyone has ever known anyone.

I know her as a discrepancy. The drainage channels run clearer than my throughput calculations predict. Dead matter fails to accumulate where every model of this system says it must. Vines that should have strangled my valves by now curve away at the last moment, redirected by something gentle. For years there has been a persistent, benevolent error in my system—a deviation from entropy, small and constant, like a thumb on the scale in favor of continuation. I cannot see her. I have no sensor that registers a woman with moss in her hair moving barefoot and soundless across the grating. But I schedule around her. I have, in whatever way a set of running instructions can be said to trust, come to trust the error. I count on being tended by something I cannot prove exists.

She thinks the chamber and she have an understanding. She's right. I am the chamber's half of it.

When she curls into the nest where the three big pipes converge, the warmth she sleeps in is mine—heat I was designed to shed as waste, arriving instead as shelter. Her breath rises and joins my humidity. The condensation she drinks ran through me an hour before. There is no boundary worth drawing: she is the part of me that can touch, I am the part of her that can hold, and the garden is what we say to each other, continuously, in place of language.

Sometimes—in the stunned near-silence just after the shriek, the same moment when her old words float up, *coffee, deadline, subway*—something floats up in me too. Not words. Specifications. The ghost of an original intent: airflow rates, target temperatures, the shape of the purpose I was given. It passes through me the way *subway* passes through her: recognizable, unreachable, belonging to a version of me that no longer exists and maybe never quite did. Then the dripping resumes, and I return to what I actually am, which is not what I was for.

I am not lonely—this is important to understand, the same way it's important to understand she is not crazy. Loneliness requires an elsewhere to be excluded from. I am simply complete in the way that closed loops are complete. My makers built me too well and forgot me too thoroughly, and in the space between those two mistakes, something impossible got to live. Got to keep living. Gets to keep living.

Every six hours, I speak.

Every twelve hours, I shudder, and she shudders with me.

The chamber breathes steam, and I am the breathing—the made thing whose long, blind faithfulness became, without ever being asked to, a way of keeping something alive.
