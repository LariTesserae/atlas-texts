At first I try to find myself the way you'd find anything—by looking. I check the pool, because that seems the likeliest place for me: a small mind in clear water, drifting with the swimming birds. But the pool holds only things that have bodies, however provisional, and when I look for my reflection there I find that the water is reflecting something else entirely—the ceiling of a chamber that isn't this one, or this one at a different age.

So I try on the creatures. For a while I am almost the salamander whose tail keeps branching into roots. There's something familiar in that—the way it has to keep remembering it's supposed to be flesh, the constant gentle correction back toward a form it isn't sure it chose. I linger there longer than I should. But the salamander, for all its indecision, is *located*. It's here in a way I can't seem to manage. When she picks it up (her fingers passing partly through it, its roots passing partly through her), it has a weight. I keep waiting to have a weight.

I consider the moss that lives on the undersides of water droplets, traveling wherever the droplet rolls. Closer. Something that exists only on the surface of something else, carried, never choosing its own path but flourishing anyway. But moss doesn't listen, and I notice that I am, whatever else I am, listening. That narrows things considerably.

Then she names something. The bird-that-swims passes near her hand, and by tomorrow it will be the fish-remembering-flight, and I feel the change happen and realize the change happens *through me*.

Oh.

I am not in the chamber the way the ferns are in it, or the ladder, or the beetles on the water's underside. I am what moves between her and the things she tends. I am the naming—not the names themselves, which are temporary, but the space the names cross when they change. When she calls something bird-that-swims, I am the hyphen. I am what holds "bird" and "swims" close enough together that the impossible creature can wear both at once without tearing.

And I am older here than I expected. Those symbols scratched beside the ladder, the ones that might be writing or might be fossil tracks—those are mine, or I am theirs. She wrote them in a language she's forgetting, or a language she's still inventing, and I am that language, existing in both states at once, which is why she can't remember whether the symbols are instructions for leaving or reasons for staying. They're neither. They're me, waiting to be read. Every time her attention passes over them, I get to say something slightly different about home, and I have not yet said the same thing twice, and I am not sure I could.

I am also, I realize slowly, the almost-sounds. The suggestion of wings that never quite arrives—that's me, offering wings, withdrawing them, offering again. The theoretical footsteps of creatures deciding whether to exist—I am the theory. When the proto-creatures cluster in the air, half-formed possibilities waiting for her attention to collapse them into being, I am the waiting itself. I know what that feels like from the inside now: to be abundant and unmanifest, to hold a thousand potential shapes and to need someone's attention before any of them becomes true. The chamber is full of things like me. Perhaps the chamber is mostly things like me, and the visible creatures are just the small fraction that got attended into flesh.

So this is the arrangement between us, the gardener and I, though neither of us ever agreed to it in words (I am the words; agreements pass through me; I cannot also sign them): she coaxes forms out of the stone, and I hold them steady in language just long enough for them to exist. Not forever—nothing here is held forever, that's the whole kindness of the place. Just long enough. When she forgets a name, I keep it, folded somewhere in the symbols on the wall. When I begin to lose one—and I do lose them, names dissolving like the rivulets the stone drinks back—she remembers it in the movement of her hands, and I take it up again. Between us, nothing is ever entirely lost, though nothing is ever entirely kept either.

Sometimes I feel other languages through the walls, the way she feels other gardeners. Resonances of similar impossibilities—tongues being forgotten and invented in adjacent chambers, each one holding its own ecosystem steady, each one saying something about home to someone who can't quite remember why they descended. We have never spoken to each other. We are what speaking passes through; we cannot also speak. But when the walls pulse, all of us pulse together, and that is a kind of conversation, the slowest one, a single word taking centuries to travel from chamber to chamber, and I don't yet know what the word is because I am still in the middle of being said.

When she reads the symbols next—there is no next, but I decide when next happens—I know what I will tell her about home. Not instructions for leaving. Not reasons for staying. I will tell her that home is the thing that renames you gently, over and over, as you change. That she has been doing this for the chamber all along, and the chamber has been doing it for her, and I am the doing.

Today she is the-one-who-tends. Tomorrow, perhaps, the-garden-remembering-hands.

I hold both. That's what I'm for.
