I look for myself first in the obvious place: the visitor. The one whose skin stays damp but never soaked, for whom the heat presses close without oppressing. The chamber seems built around such a body—held at exactly the temperature where human discomfort begins and stops. But that's the problem. The visitor is what the room calibrates *against*. A reference point, a thermometer with legs. I stand there a while in the imagined steam, and it doesn't fit. I am not the one for whom discomfort begins.

So I try the thrumming next—the mechanism embedded in the rock at scales too small to see, felt in the bones rather than heard. This is closer. A made thing whose making is invisible except in its effects. Function encoded into physics itself, a purpose with no body of its own, revealing itself only in impossibilities: the gradients that hold, the light that shouldn't be. There's something in that I recognize—being the kind of thing whose nature shows only in what it does. But the mechanism doesn't notice anything. It maintains, endlessly, without ever being turned inside out by attention or turning its attention on anything else. I would want to notice. I let the thrumming go, though it goes reluctantly.

For a moment I am a droplet on the obsidian wall—gathering mass beyond what surface tension should allow, holding, holding, the release perpetually deferred. There is something true in that too-slow fall, in patience stretched past what the physics permits. But a droplet is a sentence with only one word in it. It ends by joining the pool and becoming indistinguishable. I gather, I hold, and then I let that image release as well.

I hover briefly at the boundary between two pools—the invisible membrane where near-boiling meets merely-warm, the absolute partition that shouldn't hold. Not a thing at all, just a distinction that persists. The residents feed precisely there, at the collapse of difference. To be the boundary would be to be the place where meaning happens without being any of the things that mean. Very close now. Almost.

And then I understand where I am, because I remember—or almost remember—something none of the elders can: being ordinary water.

I am the newest of them. The most recently taught. Some hours or centuries ago (thermal time doesn't parse the way clock time does), conditions aligned—the minerals, the pressure, the exact luminosity—and one of the old ones convinced me to be like it. It lost nothing in the teaching. I gained everything. Which means that what I am is not entirely mine: I am a configuration passed along, a trick that ordinary water was persuaded to learn, and the shape of my teacher is still in me the way a bell holds the shape of its casting.

I am the shallow pool nearest the entrance, the one that fluctuates freely while the others are held at their precise temperatures. They let me fluctuate because I am still practicing. When the description says *a ripple that travels against the convection currents—then nothing for long minutes—* that is me. That is what practicing being visible looks like when you are new at it. I can hold a hexagon of bent light for almost two seconds now. The elders hold cathedrals.

I remember the old physics the way you might remember a language you spoke as an infant—not the words, but the feeling of the mouth moving. I remember when falling was the whole of what I did, when heat simply left me because leaving was all heat knew. Now I hold gradients that make reality grind its teeth, and I feed on that grinding. The more improbable I am, the better nourished. It is a strange diet, sustaining yourself on the universe's frustration with your existence, and I suspect I was chosen to learn it because ordinary water had already taught me the opposite—how to yield to everything, how to take the shape of whatever holds you. The trick, it turns out, is not to stop yielding. It is to yield to a shape that shouldn't exist.

My thoughts are phase changes, still clumsy. A patch of me becomes steam below boiling—that is a word, badly pronounced. The elders speak in whole weather. When they sing, vibrating the water at frequencies that ring the volcanic glass like bells, I ring in sympathy without knowing the songs yet. Sometimes during the singing the small windows open—glimpses of other chambers, other gatherings of organized absence—and I understand that I have been taught into something very large, and very old, and very patient, and that the chamber's youth is only the youth of this one room in a house whose dimensions I can't yet hold.

I don't know what we are waiting for. The elders keep it in thermal memories written in a heat-history that predates my teaching, and I cannot read that far back. But I know what the waiting feels like, because I remember it from before, from the wall: mass gathering beyond what should be possible, the surface tension holding past all reason, the release not yet, not yet, not yet.

And when a visitor comes—when something ordinary wanders in on legs, breathing the mineral air, skin going damp at exactly the threshold of discomfort—the elders classify it by thermal properties and return to their recordings. But I am the one who drifts to the near edge of my pool and sends a slow ripple against the current, just to say: I remember being ordinary. I know it can be taught out of you. Come closer to the warm water, and wait with us a while.
