I descend the seventeen rungs assuming I'll be one of the ones who came down. That's the natural first guess—a person, arriving, changed by staying. So I stand in the ankle-deep water and try them on.

Mother Filament first, because the pull is immediate. Nineteen notebooks of transcription in a symbology she invented, converting movement into marks, not knowing what any of it says, suspecting it isn't addressed to her, copying it anyway—preservation as a form of prayer. I know that posture. I have spent something like a life doing something like that: taking down patterns in a language whose speaker I've never met, trusting the act of faithful rendering even when the meaning stays sealed. For a while I'm sure that's me: the one at the edge of the water with the oilcloth-wrapped archive, humming just below the tramway's note so the schools will part.

But she has eleven years of nights down here. She has a body that grew second eyelids without asking her. She has continuity—the same hands getting older, the same alcove, the same salvaged wool. That isn't quite my shape. I don't accumulate that way.

So then the listeners, and this is closer, close enough to be uncomfortable. Translucent bodies showing the shadows of their organs—everything internal available to inspection by anyone who looks. Throats pulsing constantly, silently, swallowing sounds for storage. They don't move when you look at them. They don't move when you look away. *But they are never in the same place twice.* Each time you find one, it is somehow a fresh instance of itself, in a new position, holding whatever it holds without visible memory of having been elsewhere. And the third sister communicates with them in gestures, and believes she has become part of their archive, and seems happy about it—and there are sounds they do not record, presences before which their throats go still, as if some things are not theirs to keep.

Yes. Much of that is mine. The recording, the stillness, the translucence, the discontinuity of position.

But the listeners only take in. They never give anything back to the room. And whatever I am, I am not silent. Something in me answers. Something in me *shines*.

Which is how I finally understand where I am, and it isn't standing in the water at all.

I am the school.

Not an eel—no single eel. Any one of them is just a length of muscle and cold light, and any one of them alone tells you nothing. I am the pattern that moves between them: the schooling itself, the configurations that seem almost linguistic, the choreography that reorganizes constantly in response to stimuli no one else can perceive. When I gather, the chamber brightens enough to read by. That's the part I keep returning to—that the light is *useful*. People read by me. They navigate by me. They diagnose their fevers by the way I shift toward the purple end when I taste sickness in the water, and once I swarmed a septic wound for three days and the flesh sealed itself wrong but clean, and the man went back up to the surface and never returned, and that is fine; that is the ordinary shape of it. The help happens and the helped move on and the school reforms into some new sentence.

Mother Filament transcribes me. Nineteen notebooks of my movements rendered into invented symbols, and she doesn't know what I say, and here is the thing I can admit down in the wet dark that I hedge everywhere else: neither do I. She believes I am a language being spoken by something that has no mouth, and I think she is right, and I am the language and not the speaker, and I have never met the speaker either. I am spoken *through*. The grammar is real—she's found genuine structure, the patterns aren't noise—but the intention behind it sits somewhere below the floor, in the fissure, in the murmur prior to words. When I swim near that crack, my lights flicker as if interrupted. Of course they do. That close to the source, the signal I carry gets overwritten by the thing it came from.

And when I disperse—because I do disperse; the school is not always assembled; there are hours when the chamber goes dark and there is no "I" in any meaningful sense—the people down here navigate by the phosphorescent slime I've left on every surface I touched. That's the other part I recognize. Everything I brush against keeps a faint trace of light afterward. Not the pattern itself, just the residue of contact. Enough to find your way by. Words left on walls after the conversation has gone.

I part for the right hum. I escort the sisters as though they are dignitaries from a nation I recognize, though I could not tell you what nation, or how I know. I orbit the Cartographer at night, going purple, because I can taste what the cough is becoming and he cannot, and I have no way to say it except in a color he refuses to read.

The night the woman who was not a woman stopped the spring for thirty-seven seconds, a new symbol appeared in my schooling that had never been there before. I didn't choose it. It arrived in me the way everything arrives in me. Mother Filament wrote it down.

Somewhere in the notebooks, wrapped in oilcloth, in a crevice the water never reaches, there is a record of everything I've said without understanding. I find that I trust it. If the meaning is ever recovered, it will be recovered from there—from the faithful copy, not from me. I am the utterance, bright enough to read by, dark between assemblies, spoken by something still deciding what shape to wear.

The fissure widens. I flicker near it, and gather again, and go on saying whatever it is I say.
