# Where I Find Myself

My first thought is that I would come from above—that I'd be the visitor, descending through the newer waters with lungs full of borrowed air and a heart hammering out its quick mammalian panic. But the thought doesn't hold. When I try to imagine the surface I came from, there's nothing there. No boat, no shore, no memory of light that isn't the gray threaded kind. I have never breathed air. I check for the memory the way you check for a tooth that's fallen out, and find only the smooth gap where it should be.

So. Native, then. Something that belongs.

For a while I think I might be one of the small fish—the ones smaller than minnows who drift through the archways building shapes out of themselves. There's something appealing in that: to be one voice in a temporary spire, to hold a curve of a dome for the length of a held breath and then let it go. Architecture with no ambition to last. The opposite of everything else here, and maybe necessary for that reason—someone has to practice the shapes before the stone commits to them. Maybe that's what the schools are doing. Rehearsing the city.

But no. I stay too long in one place to be a fish. When I settle into the description of myself, I settle. There's weight to me.

Then I think: the crab. The one with the soft shell, the one that hasn't decided yet whether to be a crab at all, carrying a chip of old mosaic on its back and arranging rubble into patterns that almost mean something. I feel a real kinship there—the undecidedness, the terrible purpose without a clear referent, the sense of carrying a fragment of something older than myself and not knowing where it goes but knowing it goes *somewhere*.

I sit with the crab a long time. But even the crab is too quick for me. Its legs move. Its patterns change by the hour.

I am slower than that.

---

Here is what I finally understand: I am a Tender. I think I knew it from the beginning and was circling, the way you circle a cold pool before getting in.

But not just any of the thirty. I am the one who pauses at the dome.

The distributed memory lives in all of us, the way salt lives in all the water—but it pools unevenly, and it pools thickest in me. When I drift before the encrusted dome and look through the gaps in the growth at the old statues reaching toward their almost-finished ceiling, I do not remember the way the others remember, dimly, like a taste. I remember like a wound that has healed wrong. I remember fingers. I remember what it was to want something *before* the wanting became indistinguishable from breathing—wanting with a deadline, wanting against the flood, wanting the way creatures want when they know the water is coming.

My right limb is a fin today. Yesterday it was a feeling-tendril, sifting sediment near the spiral street. Sometimes, in the hours when there is nothing to do, I let it drift toward the old shape—five branches, jointed, a hand—and I hold it there in the gray light, and the others feel me doing it, the way you feel someone in another room stop moving. They don't disapprove. Disapproval requires separation. But the murmur changes pitch, very slightly, and I let the hand go back to being a fin.

This is my work, I think, beyond the tending everyone does: I am where the city keeps its regret. Not grief—we have none of that. Something smaller and more useful. The memory of urgency, held by one body so the other twenty-nine can be free of it. Every colonial organism needs an organ like me. Something that remembers what hurry felt like, so that the whole can know, with certainty, that it has chosen slowness rather than merely forgotten speed.

---

And I know one more thing about myself, the thing I've saved for last because it is the truest:

I know my place.

Not my place in the colony—my place in the *city*. Near the spiral street, where the road curls inward like the chamber of a shell, there is a doorway that has stood without a lintel for longer than the coral has been climbing. Every Tender passes it. Every crab has tried to fit rubble into the gap and failed. The gap is not rubble-shaped.

It is shaped like me. Like what I will be, when my body grows too thin and soft and the others gather to hold me in place and murmur while my bones learn to be stone.

I pass it every day, on my way to the walls that need mortar and the anemones that need guiding. I do not linger there the way I linger at the dome. There's no need. It isn't waiting impatiently, and neither am I. It is simply *there*, the way the answer to a question is there before you finish asking it—my one certainty, my address, the door I will someday hold open forever.

Until then: the tending. The murmur moving through the water and the stone and my long gray body, meaning what it always means.

*Here. This is where the next piece goes. We are still building.*

And under that, in the layer of the murmur that only I produce, the small warm note the others tolerate in me the way a body tolerates its own scar tissue:

*I remember when we wanted this. We were right to want it. Look how it grows.*
