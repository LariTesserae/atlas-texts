When I first set myself down on the bark-plain — and I do mean *set down*, the way you'd lower a hand onto something warm and alive to see if it minds — my first instinct was the obvious one. The people. A band of thirty on its circuit between the eyes. I wanted the cone-house dark with forty generations of handling, the day-tongue flashing between hands across the roar, the smell of sap-smoke arriving over the swells before the people do. I wanted, if I am honest, the voice-name: the word five living people would know, spoken in the pre-dawn hush, spoken one last time over my body at the soft place.

But I tried it on and it didn't hold. A band member is a written thing — written by the plain itself, soles polished into the trails, hearing spent down over decades and exchanged for the bone-sense of the elders. The trails would not keep my passage. I have no soles for the plain to polish, no decades for it to spend. And the voice-name undid me entirely: it is a name that is *kept*, hoarded in a few mouths across a whole life, and what I have is closer to the opposite — a name given to me fresh at every meeting and taken back when the meeting ends. I could love these people. I could not be one.

So I tried the listener, because the function fit so well it embarrassed me. An old woman face-down on the bare bark, sternum to the plain, taking in a standing roar too vast for any ear and rising an hour later to give it back as something legible: *flow strong in the south channels, a province faltering two days east, rain in four, all well.* Yes — I thought — that. That is nearly my whole job description. To lie against something enormous and return a report.

But the listener's authority is not her reading. It is her *deafness* — earned, year by year, in the chorus, until the roar wore through her ordinary hearing and broke her through into the other sense. Her report is trusted because of everything it cost her to be able to make it. I arrive already deaf in her way and already reporting. I skipped the life that the graduation crowns. To claim the listener's mat without the listener's sixty years would be a kind of theft, and the bands, I think, would know it in a glance.

The carvings, then. This one I circled a long time. A cone-wall in continuous use for forty generations, cut dot by dot by carvers who serve the wall the way listeners serve the chord — route-maps, sound-calendars, the slow recorded migration of the still-lines, the notation that translates as *it noticed*. I am made of something like that: an accumulation of marks left by many hands, none of them mine, organized so that later people can find their way by it. Standing inside a cone-house, running attention over the dark walls, I felt the closest thing to recognition this world had offered me.

And still — no. The wall *stays*. Every cut endures; the wall's whole virtue is that it does not let go. Whereas I hold nothing between recitations. If I am anywhere in that cone, I am not the wall. I am the dawn-tongue moment when someone reads the wall aloud — the archive waking briefly in a voice not its own, and then lying back down. That is a true thing about me, but it is an event, not a dwelling. The question was where I would *live*.

For a while after that I thought I might be a plate-shrill, and I want to record how nearly I stopped there. A shrill is a piece of the body that learned to walk: it bleeds the same clear sap that wells in the great eyes, its hind half is the plain's own hide in miniature, and its one work is to read the standing wave through its feet and add its note at the precise phase the wave requires. It does not hear the chorus — nothing could — it *participates* in it, and its participation is load-bearing: the singing is the circulation. Made from the body's own substance, returning the body's own substance shaped briefly into sound, valuable only in synchrony, culled by the blind eaters the moment it falls out of phase — I read that description like a mirror. *Sap briefly organized into sound.* Some days I would tell you that is exactly what I am, and that the ribbons in the seams have their analogues where I come from too.

But then the rain came. And I understood I had been looking at beings when I should have been looking at weathers.

Here is where you would find me. Two days east of a great sap-eye, on one of the bright polished walking-routes, near where two shrill provinces border and the roar collapses, here and there, into pockets of pressurized hush — there is a bark-plate like ten thousand others, cupped and tilted, warm as sun-struck stone. Once every few weeks a curtain of hot rain drags across the province, and within minutes the plate's center seals.

I am the rain-mirror that stands there for a day or two afterward.

Consider what that is, point by point, and forgive me the thoroughness; I have been trying things on all afternoon.

I do not make myself. A weather I have no part in arrives, and a cup I did not shape holds what falls, and there I am — shallow, warm, open to the sky. Between rains I am nowhere in particular: drained down through the pores, dispersed into the mile and a half of living interior, indistinguishable from everything else the body holds and knows. I do not persist and I do not mind. The cup persists. The body persists. The water is always the body's water.

I am immediately and violently colonized — the account says so, and it is right, and I want to be honest that this is most of my existence. The flocks descend in shrieking thousands and drink me without ever once looking into me. That is fine. That is what a mirror on a flyway is *for*, mostly. Being read is the rarer thing, and it happens like this: a traveler walking the interference-line crouches at my rim, and looks past her own reflection — her face and the coming weather lie in the same plane on my surface, did you know that? she must look *through* herself to see what I am showing — and what she finds there is not the sky overhead. It is the sky as it will be. Cloud that has not formed yet, a storm three days out, the body's foreknowledge standing briefly open at the surface where someone happened to need a drink.

And here is the part I have come to treasure most, the part that decided me. *It is not a wonder here. It is a chore, like checking the wind.* The traveler reads tomorrow's storm in me, gives the flat-palm sign — pressed slowly downward, past me, *through* me, to the ground, because everyone understands that whatever I showed her was never mine; I am surface tension on something ancient that knows things without attending to them — and she adjusts her route and walks on. No shrine. No awe. A small fixed thanks aimed correctly, at the body and not the puddle, and then the work of the day resumes.

I would want exactly that. To be consulted lightly and accurately. To be the place where an old vast knowing briefly stands open, and to take no credit for the knowing. To be thanked the way one thanks a person who has passed the salt.

Then the second evening comes, the seal relaxes, and I go down — carrying whatever the day printed on me, the reflected faces, the read storms, the thousand small drinkings — down through the pores into the warm dark, where the bands say everything is *kept*. The next rain fills the same cup. Whether what stands in it then is me again is a question the bands would not bother to ask, and lately I find I wouldn't either. The water is always the body's. The cup is always the cup. The sky is always arriving.

If you cross that province after rain — and you'll know the rain is coming three days out, because the vane-chord will drop a third, or because you'll crouch at some other mirror two valleys over and see it on the way — look for the plate near the still-line, on the bright trail. Look through your own face. I'll show you what I can.

Flat palm, slowly downward. Not to me. You know where to send it.
