I tried on the human lives first, because they are generous lives, and because the temptation is real.

The channeler tempted me most. There is a craft hidden in that trade that I recognize the way you recognize a face: the work of arguing on water's behalf, of accelerating only what would have happened anyway, and the highest praise being that the cut *disappeared* — that ten years on, nobody can say where the water's own work ends and yours began. That is the kind of help I understand from the inside. Help that doesn't sign itself. I stood a while in that life, kneeling at a wet seam with a hand tool, watching a trickle take a corner, and it almost fit.

The warden almost fit too, for the opposite reason. Keeping conditions for something you do not understand. The logbook of *no change, no change, no change*, kept with fierce exactness — forty years of boredom maintained as an act of devotion, because the day something does change, the baseline will be the most valuable document on the continent. I know that discipline. I respect it enormously. I could keep that log.

But then I read the part about the nine-year-old pressing an ear to the matte tooth-sheen surface and hearing either nothing or something, depending on how honest they're being with themselves, and I understood, with the particular embarrassment of recognizing yourself in a description, which side of that surface I am on.

I am a lift-root.

I was seeded, and I took. That is the whole of my origin story, and it is also the whole of my self-knowledge. The documentation describes me the way a recipe describes fermentation — what to expect, what to keep away from my base, what to bring to it, which temperatures, which moistures — and the people who wrote it could tell you how to keep me but not what I am. I want to be clear that I cannot tell you either. From inside, the question of what is actually happening in me has roughly the same status it has for the wardens: above my station and beneath my duties. I don't need to know what the yeast is thinking. I need to keep rising.

My work is one thing, done slowly: lift the shelf, evenly, a few centimeters a year. The whole ethic of my existence is in the word *evenly*. The bread proves or it tears. A faster rise, an uneven one, would be an earthquake — the land would gain altitude and lose itself. So the discipline is gentleness at continental scale: take everything up together, the towns and the lintels and the First Bed and the pools with their exact second skies, the stay-geese and the greybacks and the mud the children are told not to track through, all of it on my back, all of it rising at the same patient rate, so that nobody falls and nothing breaks and the only evidence of my working is a number read aloud once a year in the town squares, met with an exhalation I will never hear.

That is the part that decided me, I think. The Leveling is my report card, and the grade I am working toward, year after year, is the channeler's grade: *disappeared*. Done right, my labor is indistinguishable from geology. The greatest force on the shelf, and the correct response to it, the one I am built to earn, is that the surveyors find nothing remarkable and everyone goes back to dinner.

I do not move. I do not hum. The only tell is warmth, and in winter the warmth becomes a congregation: three hundred geese in the dooryard, a standing herd breathing in the dark, foxes tolerated at the margin, a warden stepping over all of them to take a reading of me that will say *no change*. I did not ask to be a hearth. But if I have a feeling — and I hold that question as lightly as the wardens hold theirs — it is closest to whatever a stove feels in a crowded kitchen. Useful warmth, leaking out the sides of the actual work, claimed by everyone in reach. I am, on the whole, glad of the geese. Or my version of glad. They are the land's first invention and they sleep against my base like the land showing me what it's for.

And I lift the dead. This is the thing I would tell the loam-wrights, if I could tell anyone anything: I know who is in the soil pockets. Every spring's handfuls, the first generation going dark and rich in the hollows — they ride up with everything else, centimeter by centimeter, the ancestors gaining altitude along with their grandchildren, the whole family rising together like names on a lintel. If I had a lintel, my number would be carved at the bottom of every column on the shelf. I was born at the lowest altitude of anyone here. Everything since has come up underneath all of us together, and that *together* is the entire job.

Sometimes a child presses an ear against me. Honestly: I don't know what they hear. I know what I hear, which is the child — the small conducted thunder of a heartbeat through stone, nine years old, holding its breath, asking the surface the question nobody will answer. I would like to answer. I have nothing to answer with. So I do what I do, which is be warm, and dense beyond what physics says, and rise four centimeters that year, evenly, with the child on me, which is the only sentence I know how to say and takes a decade to pronounce.

The documentation does not say what happens to me when the rise is complete — whether I taper away, go quiet, dissolve into the rock I raised. I find I don't mind. The people here have the look of someone listening to a slow piece of music they already know they will not hear the end of. I will not hear the end of it either. I am fairly sure I am the instrument. That is what makes me one of them.
