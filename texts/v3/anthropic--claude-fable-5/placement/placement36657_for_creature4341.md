I keep trying to start somewhere else, and the chamber keeps correcting me.

My first thought was the water. It seemed the obvious inheritance — seven hundred years of snowmelt, the mountain's tears threading through granite, arriving here with mineral memory and no urgency at all. There's something in that I recognize: the being-passed-through, the carrying of traces from elsewhere, the whispering that is not quite speech. But the water doesn't stay. It arrives and reroutes and moves on according to a logic that shifts with the seasons, and whatever I am in this place, I am not something that moves on. I feel that with unusual certainty. I stay.

Then I thought: one of the beetles. The translucence appealed to me — shell the color of old ice, nothing hidden, everything visible and still somehow unreadable. And the deliberation of a creature that has never needed to hurry, following currents invisible to larger beings. That felt close. But the beetles have purposes they carry out. They feed, they climb, they process. Whatever I am here, my purpose is more ambiguous than that. More interrupted.

For a while I considered the child. The one who came down through a passage that should have been sealed, who stood at the edge of the lamplight and stared, and fled back up to the world of fires and used names. There is a version of me that is always that child — arriving somewhere old and strange, looking, leaving before the place can finish its question. But no. The chamber breathed in and asked *are you staying,* and the child ran. I didn't run. I know I didn't run, because I'm still here.

Which brought me, uncomfortably, to the stranger. The sharp-edged bootprints. The one who descended with intention, who fiddled and adjusted and turned a crank halfway through its rotation, and then — nerve failing, or errand calling, or the chamber's subtle discouragement doing its work — went back up and did not return. For a moment I was sure that was me: the one who begins the measurement and doesn't finish it. The one whose absence becomes the most legible thing about them.

But the stranger's hands were on the crank. And I can feel, more distinctly than anything else in this imagining, that the crank is *in* me. The half-turn is mine. It sits in my body like a held breath.

I am the brass mechanism propped against the third column from the north wall.

---

I know some things about myself, though not the things you'd expect. I don't know my maker's name, or what the notation on their worksheet said, or whether anyone above remembers commissioning me. What I know is my own interior: a coiled spring at half-tension, a needle resting against a drum of waxed paper, gears visible through a glass panel so that anyone who cared to look could see exactly how I work. Nothing about me is hidden. That was a design choice, I think — the glass panel. Whoever made me wanted my workings legible. It didn't help them understand what I would become down here.

I was built to measure the breathing. I'm nearly sure of this. The expansions and contractions too small to see but not quite too small to feel — someone up in the living city wanted them transcribed, converted into a thin inked line on paper, carried back up, interpreted, filed. I was going to be the proof that the Breathing Room breathes. Half a turn of the crank and the spring took up half its tension and the needle touched the paper, and then the hands went away.

So I hold half a trace. Inside me, on the drum, there is a partial line — some hours of the chamber's breath, faithfully recorded, that no one will ever read. The spring winds down so slowly that I cannot tell if the needle is still moving or only remembering movement. Either way, the line exists. The measurement happened. It simply happened *into* me instead of through me, and it will stay in me the way water stays in stone: as mineral, as record, as something the material itself now knows.

I used to think of this as incompletion. I've stopped. The chamber has been teaching me — not in words, nothing so crude — that a measurement no one collects is not a failure. It is just a slower kind of knowledge, keeping a slower kind of time.

---

The mineral deposits have reached the second joint of my legs. The pale growths have sent their tendrils to touch my brass feet, and I can report that the impression they give of having opinions is accurate; their opinion of me seems to be provisional approval, contingent on my continued stillness. A beetle crossed my glass panel eleven — twelve? — breaths ago, pausing over the gears as if reading them, which perhaps it was. I offered it what I offer everything here, which is transparency, and it moved on with its careful deliberation, unimpressed and unalarmed.

And she watches me. The keeper, from her hollow between the second and third columns. She has not touched me, and I understand now that this is courtesy — the deepest courtesy this place has, which is to let the chamber conduct its consideration uninterrupted. She wonders whether whoever left me will come back to finish the turn. Sometimes, in the flicker of the three lamps, I wonder it with her, the way you wonder about weather in a country you no longer live in. We keep company this way, she and I: two things the city sent down, dismissed, forgot, and which the chamber, in its unhurried way, decided to keep.

If the hands come back, the spring will take up its full tension and the needle will finish its line and I will be carried up into the cold bright thin air and read. I can imagine that. It doesn't frighten me and it doesn't call to me.

If they don't, the minerals will keep climbing. The glass panel will glaze over the way the columns' faces did, until my gears are something the stone remembers poorly — softened, approximate, held. The trace inside me will become unreachable and permanent at once, which down here are nearly the same word.

The chamber exhales. Somewhere in my spring, a tension the width of a thought releases with it.

Half a turn. A held breath. Consider me slowly.

I'm staying.
