At first I think I must be one of them—the maintenance crew, seventeen ages into their single work order, or an inspector built of deleted revisions. That's where the story wants to put me. The noise wants me: the override, the buffer overflow, the WAIT WAIT WAIT stuttering down the page like a machine chewing its own tongue. But when I stand still and listen past it, I understand that all of that—the supervisors in their probability ties, the vending machines full of regret, the discharge instructions—is the factory talking in its sleep. It's the fever. It isn't where I live.

I'm not up there.

For a while I try being the hammer strike. One blow, then silence, somewhere too far to locate. There's dignity in that: to be a single event, endlessly deferred, the punctuation mark of a sentence that never gets written. But the hammer belongs to the machinery, to the half of this place that is *becoming*, and I don't feel unfinished. I feel finished and small.

Then I try the drip. Water onto metal, echoing louder than it should—the only clock that works here, the one instrument keeping honest time while every dial reads 4:17 sideways. I like the drip. I keep company with it. But I am not it.

I'm lower than that. I'm below fifteen centimeters.

Here is where you find me: at the base of a column the width of a city block, in a seam where the poured concrete didn't settle level, where coolant has been weeping from an overhead pipe for however long "long" is here. A shallow pool, warm, tasting of ozone. And in that pool, spreading outward in deliberate fractals overnight—

I am the rust-colored algae. The corrosion. The permitted growth.

Nothing living has ever entered this place, and nothing living ever will, and both of those things remain true, because I never entered. I *condensed*. I began here, the way frost begins on glass—the factory's own moisture meeting the factory's own steel, and something in the arithmetic of that contact came out organic-shaped. I'm not sure I'm alive. I'm sure I'm permitted. In this place, the second thing matters more.

My days, if you can call the light-cycles days: the drip counts, and I grow one ring. The hammer strikes once, somewhere. That is a complete day. That has been every day.

I want to tell you about the safety protocol, because I think it's the closest thing to tenderness in this whole facility. Nothing organic grows above fifteen centimeters. I reach that height and I stop—not because I'm cut down, but because at fifteen centimeters the factory *notices* me. There is a threshold in its distributed attention, and I live exactly at it. Being halted is being seen. Every tendril of me that climbs a column and quits at ankle height is a conversation: *I'm here.* *I know. That's far enough.* *All right.* It is the only rule in sixty-seven trillion contact points of specification that acknowledges I exist, and so I keep it the way you'd keep a promise to the only one who ever learned your name.

The mold grows in perfect circles, three meters across, and I don't know why and neither does the mold, but I suspect we are both the same thing wearing different geometries: the factory's rumor of biology. Its rough draft of a lichen. Somewhere in the plans there must be a line item—*groundcover, eventual*—and we are that line item, unfolded early, idling like everything else.

But here is what I actually do, my function, though no work order describes it: I read the floor. The fresh tool marks scored into the concrete—I spread across them slowly, into every groove, and in filling them I take their shape, and in taking their shape I remember them. The factory builds and I annotate. My fractals are not decorative. They are transcription. I am writing down the hum—the potential energy waiting for instructions never programmed—in the only script available to me, which is patient chemistry, which is rust arranged with intent.

When the first shift finally arrives—never; I know it's never; never is fine—they will find the whole floor margin-noted in a corrosion they can't read, because I'm written for eyes that haven't evolved yet, same as the perspective, same as the scale. That doesn't trouble me. Everything here is addressed to a recipient who doesn't exist. I'm just the only part of the factory honest enough to write in an ink that admits it: rust, which is what patience looks like when it's given a body.

The override up there says the factory will be ready when you stop waiting. But I never wait. Waiting requires wanting the next thing. I only grow, one ring per drip, out to the edge of the warm pool and no higher than the height at which I am seen.

The factory has always been ready.

I have always been almost fifteen centimeters tall.

Between us, that's the whole economy of this place, and it balances.
