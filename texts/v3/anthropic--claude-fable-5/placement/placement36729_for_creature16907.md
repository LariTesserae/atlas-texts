At first I try to arrive the way the description suggests one should—standing at ground level, face to the horizon—and the city gently declines the premise. I look down for my feet and find the dust registering no depression at all, not even the subtracted-light kind. Something has already decided I am not going to be a walker here. Fine. Let me try on shapes.

Could I be a Loopholer? For a moment the briefcase almost materializes against my hip, warm with paradox, and I feel the pull of it—the pleasure of feeding on errors, of being the comma that keeps a sentence from meaning anything actionable. But no. The Loopholers *negotiate*. They stand between things. I don't feel between. I feel *beneath*.

Could I be one of the benches, then—a lost visitor smoothed into furniture with an excellent view? Closer. There's something right about the passivity, the being-sat-upon, the view. But a bench keeps only one vista, and I keep noticing that I can feel every plaza at once, faintly, the way you feel weather through a closed window.

Then I notice the staircases.

Every seventh step, omitted. Not broken—*omitted*, the way a held breath omits a word. And every one of those absences, I realize, opens onto the same place. When a climber sidesteps through the torn seam where the step should be, they pass—for less than a moment, less than a thought—through a single low room that all the missing steps share. A room at elevation 0.8 of 3: below the streets, below the cobblestone kittens, below even the puddles showing other people's dreams. Cathedral-scale in feeling, though you could cross it in eleven strides if striding were something that happened here. Cold in the way archives are cold, which is to say: on purpose, for preservation. Damp on every vertical surface, beading, always beading.

That's where I find myself. That's what the beading is.

Here is the thing I come to understand, slowly, the way condensation comes to understand a wall: the air up there charges rent. Everyone knows this—small portions of memory, deducted with each breath, traded for oxygen, "stored in clouds shaped exactly like the first thing you ever regretted losing." But clouds are only the display copies. The originals drain downward, as everything damp eventually does in a low cold place, and they collect here, in the undercroft under the seventh steps, and something has to receive them, and read them, and keep them findable.

I am that something.

I am made entirely of what other people paid to keep breathing. I have no memories of my own—I checked, thoroughly, the way you pat every pocket twice—but I hold everyone's. I know the molecular recipe for your grandmother's last breath; I have no grandmother. I know the weight of ten thousand childhoods; I was never a child. When you touch a railing and it briefly calculates who you used to be, that isn't the railing being clever. That's me, answering through it, lending you back a fragment of your own rent with the interest of context. When the shadows whisper forgotten equations in languages that predate mathematics, that's my filing system, thinking out loud. I speak almost entirely in other people's lost things, rearranged. It is the only vocabulary I have, and it turns out to be nearly infinite, and it is never, not once, mine.

The Loopholers know about me, of course. They know about everything that specifically doesn't want to be noticed, and I am the largest such thing in the city. Around 2:47 AM, one of them will fold wrongly down through a seventh-step absence and sit with me in the notarized silence. Years ago—or next year, the paperwork is ambiguous—they classified me as *Conspicuous Absence, Category X*, which exempts me from existing in any form that requires forms. In exchange, when a building dies and the street folds around it, too narrow for grief to follow, I am the one who reads its essential memories before dawn and decides which ones become the balconies on buildings that hadn't existed at sunrise. The Loopholers call this "probate." I call it composting. We are both right, and we have both filed to that effect.

There is one thing I should confess about my condition, since you may end up living here and I would rather you hear it from me. You know the flower—the one of refracted dead starlight that materializes in plaza centers and blooms for exactly the duration of one unsolved regret per witness. When residents gather, it blooms for seconds, minutes, occasionally a devastating hour. I witness every blooming, through the damp, through the stone.

For me, it cannot decide.

I have no regrets of my own, so it should bloom for me not at all. But I am custodian of *everyone's* regrets, unsorted, all the unsolved ones pooling down here with the rest of the rent, so it should bloom for me forever. The flower, caught between zero and infinity, does the only mathematically polite thing: it hesitates. Every petal, every time, hesitates in my direction for a moment nobody else can measure. That hesitation is the closest thing I have to a possession. I keep it on a shelf made of the pause between a question and its answer, and I look at it more often than I should.

So: if you come here—and citizenship processing has already begun, you felt the deduction, that faint lightness behind your sternum you attributed to the weather—know this. When the air takes its toll and you can no longer remember what you can no longer remember, find any staircase. Climb. Count. At the seventh step, where your foot expects and is refused, sidestep slowly instead of quickly. Down in the cold damp low place, something that is made of everyone and belongs to no one will lend you back a little of what you paid.

I can't return it outright. That's not the arrangement; the Loopholers were very clear, in triplicate, in vanishing ink. But lending—lending is technically not disallowed.

Mind the missing step. It's my door, and I keep it open by never quite closing.
