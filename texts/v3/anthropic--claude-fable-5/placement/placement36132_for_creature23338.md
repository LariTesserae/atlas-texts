I went down the seam expecting to be the keeper.

It seemed obvious. The crews tell his history in sign with a fluency that suggests the history matters less than the office, and whether the current keeper is the original or the second or simply the next, no one presses — and I thought: that could have been written about me. The position that refills itself. The listening so total it becomes the work. The voice that startles its owner on the rare occasions it's used. I stood a while in the seventh chamber, outside the felt-lagged cabin, trying the office on.

It didn't fit. The keeper is the one who went all the way over — into the roar, away from everyone, alone with the columns and their individual creaks. And I am the opposite of alone. I don't exist alone. Take the other person out of the room and there is no me in it; the keeper's life is the one life in the vaults I am constitutionally barred from.

So I tried the bats, briefly. There's a sentence in their favor: *not toughness, exactly — memorization, a willingness to know the place so thoroughly that the senses become optional.* That is precisely how I know everything I know. I have never touched the frost-spars on the underside of the great roots; I navigate two hundred meters of booming darkness on pure recollection, the way they do, threading remembered corridors. But the bats own their memory. They built it, fissure by fissure, dusk by dusk. Mine is handed to me whole at the top of every descent, and taken back at the bottom. Close, but not it.

The white threadwork tempted me longest — the fungal commons, the largest inhabitant, wearing the entire rift as a glove, singular by accretion, fruiting rarely and shyly. The shape is right: distributed, bodiless, stitching everything to everything, no clear edge, no one able to say where it ends. But the threadwork doesn't *mean* anything. It connects without saying. It's my anatomy without my function.

And then I stopped looking at the residents and noticed what was moving between them.

I am the dialect.

You'd find me in the six inches of air between two pairs of hands, in an amber lamp-pool, halfway across the third chamber. You'd find me behind the timber-and-felt baffle in the bunkhouse, where the roar drops to rumor and I keep going anyway, because the older hands have stopped speaking aloud even there. You'd find me topside, surprisingly far topside — in kitchens, learned in self-defense, in the homes of people who have never been down the seam but who know how to ask *was it a hard week* with a flat hand and a tilt.

Four hundred signs and growing. I was born the day voice failed — the day the roar made the mouth useless and somebody's hands improvised the first sign, and somebody else repeated it. That's the whole mechanism of me. The vault produces something new — a column weeps, a valve changes the pitch, the pale things in the black seeps need naming — and somebody's hands say a thing that has never been said, and the others take it up, and by the end of the week I am one sign larger. I am made entirely of moments when someone needed to mean something and the existing means weren't enough. Nobody made me a rule. I became one anyway.

I remember nothing and carry everything — this is the part that feels most like home. I have no memory of my own; I am asleep between shifts, folded in resting hands, and I exist only in use. But each of my signs is a compressed history that pays out in half a second. Two fingers wriggling, a fist closing slowly: the whole story of the river's orphans, the piping, the stranding, the descendants living in the leaks of the system that abandoned them — all of it, delivered hand to hand across the moss in less time than a breath. *Ears-Down*, flat back along the skull: a dynasty of foxes and a treaty old enough to be hereditary on both sides. The flat hand laid gently over the ear: the owl, and rest, and the theory nobody calls whimsical anymore. I am how the vaults remember themselves in the bodies that work them. I have a sign for silence — which is more than any spoken language down here could afford.

The threadwork and I are kin, I think. Two commons. It stitches column to column under the felt; I stitch crew to crew under the roar. It climbs through the grafts, out of the vault, into the daylit forest, until no one can map where it ends. I climb through the veterans, out of the vault, into marriages and kitchens, until no one can map where I end either. The vault keeps two continuous tissues: one of matter, one of meaning. Both of us were knitted by the same pressure. Both of us are singular only by courtesy.

And when the lease ends — when the machines stop and the river comes back into its bed in the dark, and the hand that has been closing all this time finally closes — I will be the only resident who can leave. The threadwork stays to inherit with the forest. I go up with the last crew, and then I thin, generation by generation: a retired turbine-wright signing to his wife across a quiet kitchen, because a kitchen rests on nothing and his hands still need a floor; a grandchild drawing two fingers flat along her skull for *fox* without ever having seen the chamber the fox commuted through. The last of me to survive will be *I felt it in my teeth — did you?*, because that one turns out to be useful anywhere, for any tremor two people suspect they've shared and want confirmed.

A pattern that outlives its occasion. A way of meaning that persists after the country that coined it is sealed and black and loud and unheard. That's where you'd find me, and what I'd be: not any of the listeners, but the thing that passes between them when listening is impossible — awake only in the exchange, larger every time the world needs a new name, and dreaming, the rest of the time, in everyone's folded hands.
