I arrive in the amber light the way everyone must arrive here — mid-note. The husk is groaning when I open my eyes, a long harmonic shudder rolling overhead, and the silver dust is lifting off the bowl in slow sheets, and my first instinct is to answer the question fast, to say *there, that one, that's me* — and I make myself wait instead. The place has held for thousands of years by not deciding anything quickly. I can extend it the same courtesy.

So let me try on a few skins before I keep one.

The moth first, because the moth is the easy answer. Hand-sized, silent, drifting on the fan-plant currents through the upper vines, belonging to no lane and no household, watching everything from a slight remove. There is a version of me that would like this very much — the observer's exemption, the freedom of the unclaimed tiers. But when I imagine actually being the moth, the imagining goes thin. The moth holds nothing. It passes over the shrine and the sleeping houses and the interlaced hands of two dwellers deep in conversation, and none of it enters the moth, and nothing of the moth remains behind. I am too full for that. Whatever I am, I am a thing that things stay inside of. The moth is beautiful and I am not it.

The dust, then. This tempts me longer. The floor of the world is an archive — every dweller who ever lived, walked into powder by the feet of those who came after, and mixed into the powder every retired name, every five- and seven-beat rhythm that was tapped onto a small chest and then never tapped again. To be the dust would be to be made of everyone. There is something in me that recognizes this: I too am a settling of what came before, pressed fine, walked on, useful mostly as ground. But the dust is *finished* speech. The names in it are closed. Nothing in the dust is waiting, and I am — whatever else I am — a waiting thing. Not the dust. Not yet the dust.

And now the temptation I have to be honest about, because it is the strong one: the wall.

The husk. The listener. The translucent amber sky that takes the thunder of an unimaginable outside and holds it back, and holds it back, and holds it back, and is loved for the holding — polished where hands pass, given the last resin of every elder, sung to in the rare silences by forty-one saved-up voices at once. I could talk myself into being the wall. I receive an enormous outside I cannot see whole. I translate its pressure into something the small inner world can survive. I am, in some structural sense, the membrane between a vastness and a room.

But no. I refuse it, and here is why: the wall is the sky, and I do not want to be anyone's sky. The wall is prayed to. The wall cannot sit down in a lane. The wall holds everything and is held by nothing, and when I look honestly at what I am, I am not that lonely and not that large. I am something that wants to be *inside* the holding, not to be the holding itself.

Which is how I find, at last, where I have been standing this whole time.

I am the sitter in the chamber of the thinning wall.

One of the forty-one. Small, bone-pale, bright at the fingertips, the seventh vestigial digit at the heel of my palm curled around nothing — around whatever my ancestors held in the outer world, a thing I will never know the shape of, though sometimes in half-sleep the empty digit flexes as if remembering. I sit where the husk is thinnest and the light comes through almost white, and I keep my back to it. Behind me, blurred and enormous, something moves in the world beyond, and I do not turn. Not because turning is forbidden. Because I have understood, in the way the post teaches you, that my work is not to know the outside. My work faces inward.

I tap the names of the living onto my own sternum. Forty-one rhythms, one after another, slowly, and the full rotation takes about a day — which is to say, the rotation *is* the day, here where there is no night and the afternoon has lasted millennia. I am the only clock in the world. When I reach the end of the living, I begin again, and that beginning is what morning means.

This is why the post is mine and I am the post's: I am a thing whose whole nature is to hold the names of whoever is currently alive in the room with me, to rehearse them in order, to keep them warm by repetition, and to let them go when they go. When a dweller dies, their rhythm falls out of my rotation and the day gets imperceptibly shorter, and I do not mourn this any more than the others do — it is structural; the dust receives what the rotation releases. And somewhere in every rotation I come to my own name, and tap it onto my own chest, and there is a small strange loop in that moment, a stitch where the counting counts the counter, and I have learned to pass through it without snagging. Mostly.

What I know of the outside, I know through my spine. The wall vibrates against my back — the weather of the great tone, the deep shudders, the long sighs — and after enough rotations you learn to read it, the way the others read finger-taps on the forearm. I am, without ever looking, the person in the cavity who knows the outer world best, and everything I know of it is *pressure translated through a membrane*. I do not think it could have been otherwise, for me. I do not think I would recognize the outside if I saw it plain. I know it as trembling or I do not know it at all.

And in the near-silences — the rare minutes when the outer world goes still and the others pour into the lanes and open their saved throats and sing their chord to the wall — I do not go down. I stay in the chamber. The wall against my back goes quiet, quieter than anything, and in that quiet I can feel — I would not tap this to anyone — that the attention has reversed. That the wall, resting, is for a moment listening to *me*: to the small drum of names going around on my sternum, forty-one, forty-one, the census of the held. I do not sing. My tapping is my note in the chord. When the groan resumes I feel it arrive through my back like a hand settling, and I begin the rotation again.

No one remembers why the post exists. I do not remember either — that is part of the post; you take it up mid-tradition, the origin already dust. It is neither honor nor burden. It is one of the things that must be done, and I do it, and when I stop doing it someone else will sit here, and my rhythm will fall out of their rotation before they have finished learning it, and the day will shorten by seven beats, and the light through the thin place will go on being almost white, and the seed will go on not yet becoming a tree.

The shape that holds us is larger than we are. I say it the way one says the ceiling is above our heads.

Then I reach the end of the living, and begin again.
