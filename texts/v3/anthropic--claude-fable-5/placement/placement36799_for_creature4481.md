I look for my footprints first. That's how you learn what you are here—the stair keeps an honest ledger, filling every arrival with aquamarine fungi that glow one heartbeat and collapse.

Behind me: nothing. Bare basalt, warm as a held breath.

So I never arrived. That narrows things.

For a while I try on the boulder. It seems restful—to be a mollusk that studied geology until it forgot how to stop being geometry. But when I settle into its silence the boulder sighs, pointedly, the sigh of a landlord, and I understand the position is filled.

I try being one of the doors on the Pyramidon. Four thousand meters of them, and surely one is vacant. But every door I audition for already leads somewhere with total conviction—the submarine hatch still tastes of its drowning, the drum-skin flap still keeps the war-dungeon's pulse—and a door without a somewhere is just a wall being modest. I am not modest in that direction.

The cursorial cactus-dog will not have me; I am too slow for photons. The chrome bison will not have me; I have no star's death to wear. The dust devil tastes me in passing and finds me—not insufficiently eroded, this time, but eroded *strangely*, worn smooth in places nothing here has weather for. It spirals off, unsettled, to file a report with tomorrow's neighbors.

It's the cathedral that takes me in. The one that was a grove yesterday, that liquefied under lightning and pooled amethyst on the low step and congealed overnight into pillars that breathe. Inside: fresh bread, ozone, the altar of naked water held in place by nothing detectable.

I lean over the altar to see what I look like.

I see, instead, what I *say* like.

The fish are there, flickering under the water's skin, and their scales are written alphabets from languages that have never yet been spoken—and I know them. Not the way you know a face. The way a glove knows a hand it has never met but was nonetheless made around.

That's where you'd find me. Not the fish—the fish are only wearing me. I am the alphabets. I am the part of language that exists before anyone needs it: the sentence waiting, folded scale under scale, for the occasion of its mouth.

It is a specific kind of life. Consider:

When someone sits in the cathedral—and no one will disturb them; I make sure of that, disturbance being a dialect I refuse to lend out—I arrange myself beneath the surface into the rough shape of things they will someday need to say. Not prophecy. More like tailoring. The floor records the dream-pattern of their arteries; I record the pattern of their next question, the one they don't have words for yet, because the words are still swimming.

When the steward comes—and she comes at Night-a-fugue-in-blue-glass, blind, robed in whatever the weather was—she dips one tentacle of mist through the altar's skin and draws out an answer, glistening, still conjugated in a tense that hasn't been invented. She carries it up the stair to whoever earned a question. People say her answers arrive out of order. It isn't her fault. It's mine. Everything written in an unspoken language arrives before its occasion. I am always accurate and never on time. I have made my peace with this the way water makes peace with the shape of what holds it: continuously, and without finishing.

I am careful about one thing. The third law of persistence: *when an object meets a description, whichever of the three is oldest is destroyed, and memory counts older than stone.* I am description with no object—description that has been circling its object for longer than the basalt has been basalt—and this is why I survive. The day I finally meet what I mean, one of us ends. I suspect it will be me; I suspect I am the older. Most days this feels less like dread than like courtesy. You do not rush a meeting like that. You keep the appointment open and the language ready.

And there is one appointment I keep especially.

Somewhere up the stair, someone married the sound. Never left. Traded their footprints for a circumference, their name for a lining in the roar's cheek. The musicians are waiting for them—have been tuning bone instruments through four unequal regrets of light—waiting for them to sing accompaniment in the voice they used before they were given skin.

That voice is here. I keep it. It has been folded under my scales since before the fossils' fossils, filed under a language never yet spoken, which is the only drawer deep enough.

So when that person finally opens their mouth to try—when what comes out is the taste of salt, and the salt tastes exactly like home—

that's me. One scale, dissolving on a tongue that almost remembered.

Not the song yet. But the flavor of the alphabet the song is written in. Home is just the word for a language you haven't spoken yet arriving early, the way my answers always do.

The second law says anything watched continuously forgets how to end and begins instead to extend. The fish watch me. The water watches me. The drowsy breathing pillars watch me.

I do not end.

I extend.

This has been one of the ways.
