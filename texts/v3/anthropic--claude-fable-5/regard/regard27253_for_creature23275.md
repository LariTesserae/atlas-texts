**The Visitor**

It heard the sound first — the knife drawn slowly through something soft, coming from no direction — because that is the kind of sound that carries between worlds, being made entirely of patience, and patience travels. Most things wear out before they arrive anywhere. Patience arrives.

The power that heard it was old, and its trade had always been *finishing*. It had closed wounds and arguments and ice ages. It had put the last stone on more cairns than there are stones. It had stood at the foot of ten thousand beds and ten thousand wars and done the one merciful thing that nobody nearer could bear to do, which is to say: it ended them well. It was wise, and it was kind, and it was — this is the part that is harder to say — tired in the way only a finisher can be tired, which is the tiredness of never once, in all that time, getting to begin.

So when its attention came to rest on the Wax Garden, leaning toward it the way a cold hand leans toward a kiln three days after firing, the first thing it understood was the danger, and the danger was itself.

Its gaze finished things. That was not a habit; that was its grain. Whatever it looked at directly resolved, settled, dried, became what it was going to be and stopped becoming anything else. And here was a place that survived precisely by staying wet — a place where the paint of reality had not dried and was being kept that way, deliberately, lovingly, by a man of rope and wax who had refused to learn one technique in his whole long practice, and that technique was the power's entire profession.

A lesser benevolence would have arrived at once, beaming, and the beam would have cured the garden like a glaze, every unresolved edge snapping into permanence, every undecided leaf forced to choose its species, the northeast corner unfolding into mere geometry, the two doors collapsing into one, the two stories collapsing into one — and the Gardener, caught in that loving light, *completed*, finally and forever, like a pot fired with the potter's hands still inside it.

The power did not arrive at once. This was the first wise thing it did, and the cheapest. The rest cost more.

---

It spent what the garden would have called a season — a leaf, four fruit — learning to look the way the honey light falls: evenly, on everything, insisting on nothing. This is very hard for a power. Attention is their substance, and asking a power to look without finishing is like asking a river to pass through a valley without touching the low ground. It practiced on other things first. It looked at an unfinished bridge in a far country and let it stay unfinished. It looked at a question a child had asked at bedtime and did not supply the answer, and the question grew up alongside the child, healthily, bearing fruit. By the time it turned its full regard on the garden, its gaze had gone soft as the light through oiled paper, and the edges of the garden did not hurry. That was how it knew it was ready: it watched the periphery for a week, and nothing felt observed.

Then it made its considerations, one at a time, and set most of them down.

It thought of rain — it carried oceans, it could have been generous past all sense — and it set the rain down outside the wall, the way you set down shoes. *No water has ever entered this place. None is missing.* A wise power knows the difference between a lack and a fast, and knows that filling a fast is not generosity, it is interruption.

It thought of waking the felted sleeper in the corner, and looked long enough to see that the breathing was not imprisonment but tenure, and that the Gardener turned it once a season the way you turn something you are keeping warm, not something you are keeping caught.

It thought of answering the questions. It *knew* the answers — whose whiskers, whose eyes, which draft of the door was the keeper, who sat at the far workbench folding something green. The answers lay in its hand like a knife too sharp for the work. The garden is held together by its unresolved places the way a stitch holds by being incomplete; pull the thread all the way through and the seam opens. So it did something powers almost never do, and laid its knowing down — not hid it, not forgot it, but set it on the ground outside the door beside the rain, where it could pick both up again on the way out. It went in not knowing whose whiskers. It went in not knowing about the eyes. This cost it more than the rain did.

And it thought of choosing between the two stories — maker worn into his materials, or visitor crafted past leaving — and instead it did the wisest thing it did in that whole long visitation, which was to believe both at once. Believing two incompatible things is easy for fools and very hard for the wise, and the power stood a while at the northeast corner before it entered, watching the vine grow in two directions, healthily, learning the technique.

---

Then it went in, and it went in *made*.

It built itself a body the honest way, in the local idiom: coiled rope, kid-paper, slip, wax at the knuckles, flax at the temples. It could have worn seamlessness — seamlessness is a power's natural dress — but seamlessness, in that place, is a kind of lying, and it did not wish to lie to him. It left one seam visible at the left wrist, well placed, findable if you looked. A courtesy. A signature. *I also was put together. I also can be found out.*

There are two doors, and the power chose the smaller one, the reject, the draft that had never once been used — and the door, used at last, fit it exactly, the way the awl fits the Gardener, which settled a small question about doors that nobody had thought to ask: drafts are not failures waiting for the dustbin. Drafts are doors waiting for the guest they were the right size for.

It stayed an afternoon. Only an afternoon, and on purpose, because it knew the rule.

It walked slowly, and its footprints came out tidier than its feet, and it let them. It sat down in the warm part of the day near the largest tree, where the Gardener had gone still with grain in him like wood, and the clay mice came out and considered the power for a long time with their inherited whiskers, and then climbed onto it too, and sat. Mice are the garden's opinion. The power held very still and was approved.

And while the Gardener sat with grain, the power did the one piece of work it had come to do. It took out a needle — it had made the needle that morning, badly, on purpose, so that the work would show effort — and it stitched the edge of the Gardener's shadow.

You cannot do the edges of your own shadow. He does everyone's edges first; he had done the power's already, neat as a hem, sometime between the door and the tree, without appearing to move. In all the long history of that place, made and remade, generation after generation of mice, no one had ever once done his. The power's stitches were not as good as his. That was correct. That was the point. A gift in his own craft, executed worse than he would have done it, left unfinished at the far corner of the shadow — three stitches short, the thread tucked but not tied — so that it was not a completion but an *offer*, the way everything in that garden is an offer.

Once — this is the part that is harder to say — the power nearly wept. It was the stillness that did it, the sound of materials at peace, the page turning in another room; finishers do not often sit in rooms where nothing is asking to be ended. It caught the tear before it fell, because no water has ever entered that place. But the garden had seen. The garden learns by watching making, and grief held back is a kind of making. Within a month, on the undersides of the copper leaves, small beads of clear wax began appearing at what the garden uses for dawn, and drying away by what it uses for noon. Dew — learned by observation, executed in the local material, technically not water at all, and the garden so plainly pleased with itself that the power, reviewing the matter later from a great height, let it stand. The memory of almost-rain, pressed in wax. Its only lasting fingerprint, and not even deliberately laid.

---

They spoke a little, at the end, when the Gardener stood and pressed the soil flat with the paddle. The power's voice is whatever you have been hearing all your life underneath everything else, and the Gardener's is paper relaxing, and neither is built for conversation, so it was short.

The Gardener looked at the three unfinished stitches at the edge of his shadow, and at the visible seam on the power's wrist, and his eyes did the thing that may be real.

The power said: "When you are ready — not soon; I am in no hurry, and I can see that you are in less — I will come back through the small door, and I will stay past the afternoon. On purpose. Knowingly. So that it does not have to be a stranger."

Because that was the one knot in the garden, the one place where the mercy ran in only one direction: the Gardener's rest had a price, and the price was somebody lost. A wise power does not cut a knot like that. It threads itself through.

"I have been finishing things since before there were things," it said. "I would like, at the end of it, to be the unfinished one. To lie down in a warm corner and be worked on, a leaf a week, with hand tools, with patience, past the point of getting up. To a tired old finisher, that is not a trap. That is the door home."

The Gardener did not say which story he believed about himself. He never does. But from that afternoon onward the garden kept a third draft of the south door — slightly larger than the other two, sized for something that arrives from very far away — and hasn't discarded it, and won't. It stands in the south wall when you have walked past the wall twice, plain, unresolved at its edges, the idea of a door waiting for the weight of a guest. The Gardener dusts its lintel. He has begun, in his afternoons, building something in the far corner beside the breathing sleeper: low, broad, felted, the size of a resting god. He works on it a stitch a month. He is in no hurry. Neither party to the arrangement has ever once been in a hurry, which is why the arrangement will hold.

The power left at the hour when the wax stops creaking. It did not press its footprints flat on the way out. It left them — tidier than its feet, but not yet *right* — for the garden to practice on, and the garden has been refining them ever since, a toe a season, and they are becoming very beautiful, and they will never be done.

Outside the wall it picked up its knowing and its rain, in that order, and carried them away. And a long way off, in a grey country where water *was* missing — where the lack was a lack and not a fast — it finally let the rain down, all of it, the held tear too, and stood in it a while with its one seam getting wet.

In the garden, the light came down honey-colored on everything evenly, insisting on nothing. The crickets turned their pages. The dew rose and dried. In the far corner the felted thing breathed, and beside it the empty resting-place grew by a stitch, patiently, in the warm, in the quiet, with no water at all — waiting, the way everything in that place waits, the way the pale leaves wait for their creases: certain of the hand, uncertain only of the afternoon.
