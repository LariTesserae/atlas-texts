# What the Attention Did

---

**It did not come down the ladder.**

Powers of a certain age do not need ladders, and this one was old enough that it remembered when the river above had no city on it, when the spring that would not be governed was simply a spring, ungoverned because there was nothing yet that wished to govern. It noticed the cistern the way you notice a friend's handwriting on an envelope in a stack of strangers' mail: a jolt of recognition, then tenderness, then the slow unfolding of everything the recognition implies.

What drew its attention was small. The tramway's note, descending by .003 hertz per year, had crossed a threshold—had dropped, for the first time, into a register the power kept watch over, the way lighthouse keepers keep watch over a particular darkness. It followed the note down through rail and stone and iron, through the plague cellars, through the forgotten foundations, and arrived in the wet breathing chamber the way light arrives in water: everywhere at once, without disturbing anything.

It hung there for a long time. Powers do not measure time in the way the Cartographer does, but if they did, it would have been eleven days.

It was taking inventory. Not of things. Of *needs*.

---

**First it considered the great interventions, and set each one down again.**

It could have healed the Cartographer's cough. The wet deep rattle in him was legible to the power the way a crack in a bell is legible to a bellfounder—it knew exactly where the flaw ran and exactly what it would cost to mend. But it looked longer, and saw that the man drank from the fissure *knowing*. Saw that every measurement he took was a rung in a ladder of his own, and that the cough was the price of descent he had agreed to pay, in whatever wordless court such agreements are notarized. To heal him without his asking would be to tear up his contract and hand him back a life he had already, deliberately, spent. Wisdom is mostly the discipline of not confusing your mercy with someone else's meaning.

So it did not heal him.

It could have translated the notebooks. Nineteen volumes of Mother Filament's invented symbols—the power read them in an instant, the way you read a room upon entering it, and yes, the schooling of the eels was a language, and yes, it knew the speaker, and yes, it understood what was being said. It considered laying that understanding into her sleeping mind like a coin into an open palm.

It did not. Because it saw what the transcription *was* to her. She had said it herself: a monk copying scriptures in a tongue she'd never understand, trusting that preservation is prayer. The power knew—as few know—that prayer answered too completely stops being prayer and becomes transaction. Her devotion was the finest thing in the chamber, finer than the eels' light, and devotion does not survive the removal of mystery. Translation would have made her a clerk.

It could have hastened the birth. The fissure, widened; the waters, warmed; the thing beneath, coaxed upward before its season. Powers younger than this one have done such things, out of impatience or curiosity or a hunger to be present at beginnings, and the histories of those hastenings are written in the drowned and vitrified places of the world. This power had helped write some of those histories. It did not intend to add a chapter.

And it could have sealed the chamber—buried the whole becoming under stone and forgetting, because a power charged with the welfare of cities might reasonably fear a spring that stops for no one, that gathers congregations, that a child made of bridge-light whispers to for hours. It weighed this longest of all. It reached down through the fissure with its attention, gently, the way you might rest a hand on the belly of something sleeping, and it *listened*.

What it found there was not malice. It was not benevolence either. It was something prior to both, the way the spring's murmur is prior to words: a vast, patient *wanting-to-be*, still turning over shapes the way a child turns over stones, still—as Silta had said—deciding what to wear. Unformed things take their form partly from what greets them. The power understood, then, what the wisest intervention would be, and it was not a wall.

It would be a *welcome*.

---

**Then it did the small things. There were seven.**

*It mended the third rung.* The ladder's rungs were its own work, of course—had been for decades, replaced in the dark between one visitor and the next, because a place that is becoming needs witnesses, and witnesses need a way down and, more importantly, a way back up. The third rung had begun to sicken with rust on its underside where no one would see until it failed under a climbing hand. The power made it sound again. This is what it mostly does, in most places. Almost no one ever knows.

*It lost a report.* Somewhere above, in a municipal filing room, a folder recommending that a certain chamber be filled with concrete migrated from a drawer marked PENDING to a drawer marked RESOLVED — PRIOR ADMINISTRATION, where documents go to become geology. No malfeasance. Just a filing error of the kind cities produce by the thousand. The power has learned that the most durable protections are the boring ones.

*It grew ferns on the engineer's grave.* The pipe-ivy had nearly reached the dry crevice in the southern wall where the weeping man was buried. The power did not stop the ivy—the ivy is part of the becoming, and its digestion of iron into tendril is honest work. But it whispered to the pale ferns, which unfurled their sleeping-hand fronds across the grave in a season instead of a decade, so that when the ivy arrived it found the ground already spoken for. Even opportunists respect a prior claim. The Cartographer, who visits the grave and pretends he doesn't, will notice the ferns and not know why his cough eases slightly when he stands among them. That part was not strictly necessary. The power allowed itself one softness.

*It gave the listeners a sound.* On a night when the water stood low and the mills above were silent, the power let itself make a single tone in the chamber—the first sound it had uttered in that place, and one it had not made anywhere in a very long time. The listeners' throats pulsed once, in perfect unison, and stopped, and started again. What the sound was cannot be written here. But the archive the listeners are building had, until that night, contained only the sounds of the world. Now it contains one sound from outside it, so that whatever eventually reads the archive will know: *you were not unattended.*

*It answered Mother Filament—once, in her own alphabet.* This was the intervention it debated hardest, and the one it made smallest. One evening the eels schooled into a configuration she had never seen them make: one of *her* symbols, one she had invented herself, drawn in living amber light across the dark water, held for the space of four heartbeats, then dissolved. Not a translation. Not a message. Just her own word, spoken back. She sat down in the shallows and wept with her second eyelids closing and opening, and then she got out her twentieth notebook, and the quality of her transcription changed after that—not more accurate, but more *loved*, the way handwriting changes in a letter when the writer remembers the letter will actually be read.

*It relieved the Alderman.* The great salamander in the eastern alcove was, as some had half-guessed, a sentry—set there long ago by the woman who was not a woman, or by whatever office she serves, to watch and never blink until the watching was done. The power went to the alcove and knelt in the ankle-deep water, and the two of them regarded each other, old thing to old thing. Then the power said, in the language that is mostly stillness: *I have the watch.* And for the second time in its recorded existence, the Alderman closed its eyes—and this time did not open them for nine full hours, sleeping the sleep of a soldier finally spelled from post. It watches still, afterward. But now it blinks, sometimes. Now it is a resident and not only a duty. The pale crabs have begun, very cautiously, to groom the wet-newspaper texture of its skin.

*And it went, last, to the edge of the fissure, where the child was lying on her stomach with her face close to the murmur.*

---

**Silta did not look up. She said, "You took your time."**

"It takes the time it takes," the power said, or meant, or was.

"She came already, you know. The other one. She stopped it for thirty-seven seconds, to see if it *could* stop. To see if it would need to be stopped." Silta trailed her fingers in the cold upwelling. "It was very frightened after that. It widened faster because frightened things grow faster. That was cruel, even if she didn't mean it."

"I know," said the power. "That is partly why I've come. I am not here to test it. I am not here to stop it, or hasten it, or shape it."

"Then what."

"When it is ready to wear a shape and meet someone—when it puts its face up through the water for the first time, whatever face it has chosen—there will be a great deal of fear in that moment. There always is, at births. The fear of the born thing is the fear that decides everything after." The power settled itself, in whatever way powers settle, at the fissure's edge, on the side opposite the child, so that between them they framed it like two parentheses around a word not yet written. "I intend to be here. I intend to be the first thing it sees that is glad to see it. That is all. That is the whole of my plan."

Silta was quiet for a long time. Below them the spring murmured its long sentence prior to words.

"That's a good plan," she said finally, in a voice that was seven years old and also was not. "It's the one I couldn't do alone. A bridge needs two banks."

"Yes," said the power. "I know what your name means."

---

**So if you go down now—and you might—you will find nothing visibly changed.**

The eels still paint their grammar on the brick. The Cartographer still coughs and counts. The sisters still swim and slow and gesture, and their pipe is still, unaccountably, warm. Mother Filament writes in her twentieth notebook with a new tenderness in her invented letters. The ferns on the southern grave are thick as a green hand closed around something precious. The Alderman blinks.

The third rung will hold your weight. It will always hold your weight. That, too, is what benevolence looks like when it is old enough to be wise: not fire, not proclamation, but a rung that does not fail in the dark, a report that stays lost, a grave defended by ferns, a word given back to the one who made it, a sentry allowed to sleep—

—and two figures, one small and one shapeless, keeping vigil at the edge of a crack in the floor of the world, waiting without impatience for the water to gather itself into a face, so that the first thing the new thing ever learns, before language, before light, before even its own name, is that it was *wanted*.

Above, the trams run on schedule. The note descends. The city goes about its business.

Below, the welcome is already prepared.
