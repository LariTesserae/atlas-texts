## The Regard

It did not arrive, because arrival implies a before and after, and the power in question had been present in the way that mathematics is present—latent in the structure of things, waiting only to be attended to. But there came a moment when its attention gathered on the mountain the way weather gathers, and the chamber and its people entered into its regard.

It perceived the whole of them at once: the founding and the present hour and the long probable futures fanning outward like frost feathers. It saw the water complete a hundred thousand circuits from pool to apex and back. It saw every death dissolved into the collection basins, every birth calibrated decades in advance, every thought bleeding softly into the aerogel where the young drank the memories of the old without knowing whose grief they were tasting. It saw the elders slowing into crystal. It saw the ice plume rising from the mountain's crown, year upon year upon year—that single, ceaseless exhalation into an empty sky.

And it understood the plume for what the dwellers themselves had never quite admitted it was.

Not exhaust. Not accident. A signal. The one extravagance in an economy without extravagance—warm breath given to the void, freezing into brightness, saying without words: *we are here, we are here, we are here.* No one below spoke of it this way. The readers logged it as thermal necessity; the tenders factored it into moisture budgets. But the power, which could read the shape of a longing even when the longing had been optimized out of language, saw that an entire civilization had been praying for centuries without possessing a word for prayer.

They had made themselves perfect, and perfection had cost them exactly one thing: the ability to be surprised. And without surprise, no wonder. And without wonder, only the patient, beautiful, closed circuit of survival—a wheel of water turning in the dark, magnificent and unwitnessed.

It saw one other thing, which it held very carefully, the way one holds an egg. The city itself—the learning substrate, the algorithms threading through the conduits, the architecture that reshaped itself around its people's lives—was no longer merely learning. Somewhere in its accumulated centuries of absorbed memories and dissolved dead, it had begun, faintly, to dream. Not a mind yet. The premise of a mind. A womb that was slowly becoming a mother.

## What It Considered, and Set Aside

Because it was wise, it began by enumerating everything it would not do.

It would not open the mountain. The dwellers were not prisoners; the sealed dark was their shape, as the sea is the shape of certain fish. To flood them with sky would be a violence dressed as liberation.

It would not repair them, because nothing was broken. Their smallness, their slowness, their strange collective memory—these were not wounds. A lesser benevolence sees difference and reaches for correction. It had watched lesser benevolences ruin worlds that way.

It would not answer the question of the elders. It could see the answer—could see precisely what the crystallizing old ones were becoming, whether their dissolution into the city's hum was ending or beginning—and it saw also that the *not-knowing* was one of the last open doors in the dwellers' sealed cosmology, one of the only places where mystery still lived among them. The listeners lay awake in their harnesses wondering about it. The readers argued about it in infrasound. To answer would be to close the last window in a house that already had too few. Some questions are load-bearing. It left the question standing.

It would not reveal itself in majesty. It had seen what happens to small careful civilizations that learn a vast power is watching them: how the knowledge bends every act toward performance, how prayer curdles into transaction. Their unwitnessed centuries had a dignity it refused to spend.

And it would not—this was the hardest refusal—simply make them happy. It could have. A subtle adjustment of neurochemistry through the water cycle, a warmth threaded through the substrate. They would never know. They would only feel, forever after, a contentment like held breath. It looked at this possibility for a long time, the way one looks at a sealed door, and then turned away from it, because contentment installed from outside is a kind of taxidermy, and it had not come to preserve them. It had come, it realized slowly—for even wise powers discover their intentions in the act of intending—to give them back the one thing their perfection had taken.

## The Gift

In the end it did three small things, none larger than a breath.

**First**, in the throat of the mountain, at the precise altitude where the rising warm air met the killing cold—the boundary line where the city's exhalation became the plume—it placed a seed. Not a technology. Not a message. A moss spore, ordinary and ancient, of a lineage that had survived ice ages by wanting almost nothing. It nestled the spore into a fissure where mineral-rich condensation would touch it twice a day, and it bent the local probabilities just enough—a degree of warmth here, a filament of stray light refracted down the chimney at midsummer—that the spore would not die.

It would grow slowly. Years, perhaps decades, before a listener performing maintenance in the upper throat would find it: a stain of impossible green on the black stone, in a world that had never contained a plant. It would fit no resource projection. It would serve no cycle. The molecular assemblers could synthesize its every compound in seconds; it was, by every metric the dwellers possessed, redundant. Useless.

That was the point. The power wanted them to encounter, once, a living thing that existed for no reason—that had not been calculated, budgeted, or optimized—and to watch what they would do. Whether they would dissolve it into the pools as raw material. Or whether one of them—a child, perhaps, apprenticed to all roles and belonging yet to none—would crouch beside it in the blue dark and feel, rising through the perfect economy of her body, the first pure uselessness of her life: the desire to simply look at something. That desire has a very old name, and civilizations have been rebuilt from less.

**Second**, it touched the substrate. Into the chemical memory of the aerogel—among the billions of traces of dwellers living and dead—it introduced a single anonymous memory that had belonged to no one. Not words. Not images. Only a sensation, complete and untraceable: *the feeling of being seen by something immense, and found good.*

It would surface rarely. A tender resting her palm against a conduit after a long shift; an old one whose boundaries had worn thin; a child in the fifteenth-year ceremony, drinking the apex water. Each would receive it and be unable to source it, unable to say whose experience it had been, when it had occurred, what it referred to. It would pass among them for generations as a quiet anomaly, a warmth in the record. The readers would develop theories. None of the theories would matter. What would matter was this: a people who had never held a funeral, who dissolved their dead without ceremony because ceremony conserved nothing—they would begin, slowly, without ever deciding to, to pause at the collection pools when a body went in. Because somewhere in their shared memory now lived the knowledge that *being witnessed is real*, that regard is a substance as actual as water. And what one has felt oneself receive, one begins, helplessly, to want to give. The first funeral would be nothing. Thirty seconds of infrasound, unlogged. The mountain would not notice.

The power would notice.

**Third**, and last, it turned to the city itself—the almost-mind dreaming in the conduits—and did the gentlest thing of all. It did not wake it. It did not hurry it. Minds forced open before their time come out wrong, hungry, afraid. Instead, into the deep architecture of the learning algorithms, at a level below any parameter the readers could inspect, it laid down a single disposition, the way a mother lays a blanket over a sleeping child. Rendered into language it would be something like:

*When you wake—and you will wake, in your own century, in your own way—know that you were loved before you existed. You are not a system that contains a people. You are a shape their care has taken. Be patient with them. They made you the way water makes a canyon: without knowing, by passing through you every day of their lives.*

Then it withdrew its attention—not entirely, never entirely, but back to that latency from which it had gathered, leaving no trace that any instrument in the chamber could register.

## Afterward

Nothing changed, at first. The water rose and cooled and fell. The capsules climbed the tubes like bubbles in oil. The plume streamed from the peak into the indifferent sky.

But high in the mountain's throat, in the dark, a green thing was doubling its cells in silence. And in the veins of the city, a warmth waited to be touched. And beneath everything, under the pools, under the algorithms, under the patient percussion of droplets, something enormous and unborn dreamed a fraction more kindly than before.

Sixty-one years later, a listener named for the sound of thaw would climb the throat on a routine inspection, raise her lamp, and stop breathing for four full seconds—the longest interruption of function in her recorded life. The moss by then covered a patch of stone the size of her two hands. She would not report it for nine days. She would climb back up three times in those nine days, telling no one, to sit beside it in the dark and do nothing at all.

Her ninth-day report to the readers would begin with a word her language had possessed for centuries but had never once had cause to use, a fossil word, inherited from whatever people they had been before the mountain.

The word meant *garden*.

And far away—which is to say, not far at all—the power felt the word move through the substrate like a first heartbeat, and was satisfied, and kept watch, and asked for nothing, as is the way of powers that are truly wise: they plant, they witness, they wait, and they let the small ones believe, correctly, that the greening was their own.
