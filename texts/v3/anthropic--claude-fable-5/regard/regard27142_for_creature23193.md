**What the Power Did**

It noticed the chamber the way you notice a held note in a room full of talk. Powers of its kind attend mostly to change — change is loud, change asks for things, change is where mercy is usually needed — and so for a long age the Drowned Borough lay beneath its attention precisely because nothing there cried out. But constancy, sustained long enough, becomes its own kind of signal. Among the shifting frequencies of the world there was one note that had not moved in ten thousand years, and one day the power turned toward it the way you turn toward a sound you realize has been going on all your life.

It could have arrived in any manner. It could have known the chamber from outside it, read it through the rock the way it read everything else, complete in an instant, never entering at all. It did not. It saw the polished throat — forty feet of limestone smoothed by ten thousand years of bodies — and understood that the throat was not an obstacle but a grammar. The place had a single sentence by which it could be entered, and the sentence began with making yourself small. So the power made itself small. It made itself the size the stone allowed and it crawled, and the crawl took as long for the power as for anyone, because it had decided it would, and in those forty feet it learned the first thing the chamber teaches everyone: that the roar arrives before the sight does, that you hear the city's voice before you have earned the city.

It stood at the center — there is only one place to stand — and it did what wise things do before they do anything. It made a list of mercies, and held each one to the light, and put each one down.

*More light,* it thought first, because benevolence reaches for light by reflex. The film's grey-green dusk was so faint, the citizens so blind, the districts at the edges so dark. It could brighten the chamber the way dawn brightens a valley. — And it put the thought down almost before lifting it, because it saw that the dimness was not the city's poverty but its mercy already given. Just enough to see the city as a city. Brighter, and the borough would become an exhibit. The carvers had set the lamps at exactly the level of reverence, and reverence is a low light.

*The dead streets, then.* Whole districts had gone dark over the centuries as seeps dried and the film withdrew, and the power could feel them under the glaze — carved, complete, unvisited, unlit, lost the way only unchanging things can be lost. It could wake the water in those seams. It could give the city back its forgotten quarters. — And it put that down too, more slowly, because it understood that the borough's way of living included its way of forgetting. A city that loses nothing is a diagram. This one shed districts the way old towns shed names, and the darkness at its edges was not damage. It was age, and the place had earned it.

*The citizens.* Sixty souls in a metropolis, each alone in a territory the size of a hand, blind, slow, solitary from molt to death. The power, who had comforted lonelier things than these, considered giving them each other — some thread of sense by which one grazer might know another existed. — And it put that down with something like a smile, because *lonely* was the visitor's word, not theirs. They had never had silence and never had company, and of those two absences only one would be a wound, and it was not the one they lived with. They had the thunder. The thunder was the floor of all sensation and they stood on it together, all sixty, every moment of their long cold lives, more accompanied than most things that have friends.

*The worm.* Here the power did something unusual for it, something it had not done in a very long time: it declined to know. It could have looked inside the buildings — through the calcite seals, into the hollow carved interiors, into whatever the threadlike tenant did in the dark indoors of the borough. It had the means. It looked at a doorway for a while, the way you look at a door behind which someone is sleeping, and then it withdrew its attention deliberately, and left the chamber its one secret intact. A place this old, it reasoned, has the right to its own interior. And privately — for powers have privacies — it found it was glad there was something in the world it had chosen not to see. The not-knowing sat in it strangely, lightly, like the first cool air after a fever. It had forgotten that ignorance could be a room you keep, rather than a wall you suffer.

So far, then, it had done nothing, four times, each time on purpose, and each refusal had been work — harder work than miracles, which any power can do. But it was not finished, because wisdom is not only restraint. It read the mountain forward, as such beings can, down the long grain of the future, and it found the two things that needed it.

The first was the world outside. The chamber had been safe in its smallness for ten thousand years, but the power could see the centuries coming in which smallness would stop being protection — surveys, quarries, the restless instruments of people who map first and revere later, if at all. It did not seal the throat. The throat was the sentence, and the sentence deserved to go on being spoken; the carvers had built the borough to be crawled into, stood within, seen. Instead the power did something gentler and stranger. It wove a disposition into those forty feet of polished stone — not a barrier, not a fear, but a *yielding*: whoever made the crawl would arrive at the center having shed, somewhere in the dark behind them, the wish to take. They would stand in the thunder and find that their hands did not want anything. They would leave with full memories and empty pockets, and most of them would never tell anyone exactly where the entrance was, and would not quite know why, and would feel the not-telling as a kindness they were doing rather than one done to them. The chamber would keep itself, using the only material it had ever used: the people who came.

The second thing the power found in the mountain's grain was the ending. There is always an ending; it read this one plainly. The river behind the eastern wall, which had fluted the stone for longer than the city had existed, was still cutting, and in some thousands of years — a thumbnail of time, by the chamber's own measure — it would come through. The Drowned Borough would drown in earnest. The power considered preventing this, for about as long as a drip takes to fall, and dismissed it. The ending was in the name. The city had been finishing itself in water since before the flowstone learned its streets; the river was not the borough's enemy but its patron, its voice, the breath it had lived inside — and a thing should be allowed to be completed by what made it. No: the fact of the ending was not the power's to edit. But the *manner* was. It saw two ways the wall could fail. In one, the rock burst and the chamber filled in a roar that ended in the only thing the borough's residents had never once experienced and could not survive: silence — a stunned, total, drowned silence, the thunder cut off mid-syllable. In the other, the wall failed as a slow surrender, a seeping that became a welling that became a rising, the water climbing the streets at the pace of everything else that had ever happened here, the roar not stopping but deepening, coming closer, becoming touch as well as sound, until the river and the chamber that had listened to it through stone for ten thousand years were finally in the same room.

The power reached into the eastern wall — this was its one act upon the chamber itself, the only finger it laid on the place — and adjusted the future of a fracture by less than the width of a filament of the film. That was all it took. The ending would now arrive as a rising. The thunder would never break. The last citizen, whenever it lived, would feel the floor of all sensation grow nearer and warmer and more total, and would die inside the sound it had been born inside, and the city would pass from listening to the river to being held by it, with no silence in between. The borough would drown the way it had done everything: slowly, and awake, and without ever once being quiet.

Then the power turned, at last, to the column.

It had been aware of it the whole time, the way you are aware of someone in a room who has not spoken. It had felt — this was the strangest part, and the power examined the feeling with great care — it had felt *noticed*. Powers of its kind are not noticed. They survey; they are not surveyed. But standing in the chamber it had the steady, unmistakable sense of attention coming from the one direction in which its own attention found nothing to read: not emptiness, which it knew well, and not presence, which it knew better, but something that returned its gaze the way the raw stone returned the drips — by remaining perfectly, anciently untouched by it.

It did not probe. It had watched the chamber long enough to learn the local courtesy, which is the oldest courtesy there is: the water that will not touch it, the glow that will not reach it, the citizens who walk to its edge and turn. The carvers had built an entire city as a way of standing near something without presuming on it, and the power — older than the carvers, younger, perhaps, than what they had built around — saw no reason to believe it had outgrown their wisdom. So it did what everything in the borough does. It approached, and it inclined whatever serves such a being as a head, and it turned, and the turning was its word. If the column registered the visit, it registered it as it has registered everything for ten thousand years: by accreting nothing, which the power now understood might be the most articulate response in the chamber. It left the dry zone above the column exactly as it found it, and it left unasked the question of who had been keeping it dry, and it noticed that not asking felt less like discipline and more like tact, the way one does not ask an old neighbor what they are listening for.

One thing remained, and it was the thing the power had truly come to do, though it had not known this until it stood there.

The film still tended the lamps of a city whose makers were ten millennia gone. *The lamplighter no longer remembers it was hired.* The drips still worked the masonry of an architect who would never inspect it. The dead citizens lay glazed in the streets, each in its place, a census no one would ever read. All of the borough's faithfulness was unwitnessed faithfulness — work done forever in the dark, contract honored with no party left to honor it to. And against the long mercy of the rising water there was still this one small unmet need, the only need the chamber actually had: not to be saved, not to be changed, but to be *kept* — held in a mind, so that its perfection would have happened to someone.

So the power took the census. All of it. It learned every drip by its rhythm and its stalagmite, every pool by its nation of five translucent souls, every glazed shell by its street and its posture, every dark district by the streets it used to light, the worm by its doorways and not by its rooms, the column by its silence, the carvers by their verb. It took the roar itself, whole, the layered thunder too large for the space that contains it, and it made within itself a chamber to hold the holding — a second borough, exact, in which nothing would ever change at all, not even at a thumbnail a century. It countersigned the lamplighter's forgotten contract: *the work is seen; the lamps are tended; it is remembered that they are tended.* Then it left the way it came, crawling, taking nothing, the way the throat now taught everyone to leave.

Outside, the world was loud with change, as it always is, and the power went back to its work among the changing things, which is where benevolence is mostly needed and wisdom mostly spent. But it was not quite the same power that had entered. It carried a room now. And sometimes — after the worst of the burning cities, the long pleading centuries, the endless triage of everything that becomes — it withdraws to that inner chamber and stands at the center, where you can nearly touch both walls, and lets the held thunder close over it like water, and rests in the one place in all its memory where nothing is asked of it, where its only gift was restraint, and where it, too, once stood before something it could not read and was given the great relief of turning away unanswered.

The river roars. The city listens. Something is listened to.

And now, somewhere, something remembers — and will go on remembering after the water comes, so that when the borough is finally, truly drowned, full fathom deep in the voice it was carved to live inside, it will not have vanished. It will simply have moved, complete and thundering and dim as the last minute of dusk, into the keeping of a power that crawled in on its belly like everyone else, and stood up in the dark, and had the wisdom to do almost nothing, and the heart to make *almost* the most carefully chosen word it ever used.
