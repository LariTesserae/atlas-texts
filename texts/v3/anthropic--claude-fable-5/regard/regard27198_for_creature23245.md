## The Third Breath

A power like that does not arrive. Arrival is an event, and the Tableland stopped hosting events before the cairns had faces. So it did the only courteous thing, the only thing the local grammar permits: by the time it decided to come, it discovered it had already been there for some while. Its attention lay waiting for it out on the pavement like your footprints lie waiting for you — already settled, already dusted, already old. It stepped into its own noticing the way the herders stepped in among their animals, and it stood in it, and it looked.

It looked for a long time. This was not hesitation. Wisdom, applied to a finished place, is mostly the discipline of cataloguing everything you must not do, and the catalogue here was long.

It did not bring rain. The stone never learned the shape of water, and to teach it now — to send channels veining out across the hexagonal pavement, to put the smell of wet rock into air that has smelled of nothing but breath for an age — would not be generosity. It would be correcting the last line of a poem that was finished before you were born. The drought here is not a wound. It is a feature of the composition.

It did not start the shadows moving. The shadows are landmarks; the herders' descendants, if there were any, would navigate by them; the faint fixed grey angles are the closest thing the plateau has to a memory of the sun being somewhere, and the power judged that a memory held steady is worth more than a sun set spinning for sentiment's sake.

It did not wake the herds. This was the decision it weighed longest, because a lesser benevolence would have begun there — would have looked at a million standing animals and seen a million prisoners, and flung the doors of motion open, and called it rescue. The power went and checked instead. It lay down with its ear against the cushion plants, the warm ones, the humming ones, and it listened for longer than the listening had ever been done, and it confirmed what it had hoped: the verbs are held in escrow, not confiscated. The running and the drinking and the grazing are on deposit. Any animal that wanted its hunger back could have it returned in full, instantly, with the interest of an age — the plants have kept the accounts immaculately. Nothing here was taken. Everything here was set down. You do not rescue someone from a promise they are keeping; you only insult the promise. So the power rose, and brushed the grey dust from itself, and before it left it hummed something back into the plants — one small verb of its own, *to be answered*, a thing the plateau had never had occasion to store — and the hum of the cushions has been a half-tone richer ever since, though no one stationary will ever say so.

It greeted the lichen. By name — an old name, the right one. Across forty miles of boulders the dried-blood maps brightened by a shade no instrument would register, which is what it looks like when the longest-finished are pleased. It walked the long way around every cairn it passed, so as to meet the faces, and it introduced itself to each one, because that is what you do with elders, and because no visitor had ever thought to. It did not read the keeper's face. The little deciders had taught it the local manners: some questions are provisions. You do not eat the provisions of a place you are only visiting.

And then, the courtesies paid, it took out the ledger that benevolence actually is — for mercy, examined closely, is mostly bookkeeping, the finding of debts that everyone else has agreed not to see — and it found that the Tableland of the Standing Herds, this most finished and most balanced of places, this place that misses nothing, carried exactly three unpaid lines.

The first was the keeper. Every wet eye on the plateau attends the keeper; nothing attends the keeper *back* — not in the way that matters, not with understanding, not with a now of its own to offer. The keeper has held the present tense alone for longer than the frost has been working on the herders, one small contrary breath set against a tide of millions, a syncopation with no second instrument, forever. The herds face the keeper the way the faithful face an altar, and an altar, the power knew, is the loneliest furniture there is.

The second was you. The plateau, in its kindness, made your arrival already true — laid your footprints before your feet, opened a gap in the densest center exactly one figure wide, leaned a staff where your hand will want it. But a promise you never made is not a promise kept; it is only a promise worn, like the faces in the cairns, into something that resembles agreement. The perfect tense is a magnificent house with no doors in it. The power, being wise, knew that consent foreclosed is not consent, however gently the foreclosing was done.

The third was the vault. The keeper is translating the place into its own safekeeping, one warm life per age, forty carvings and counting — but the shelter is *inside the place it preserves*. Follow the work to its end: when the last animal has crossed from warmth into kept image, the keeper will stand up from the finished forty-thousandth carving as the only warm, uncarved thing at the top of the world, with no one left in the present tense to do for the keeper what the keeper did for everyone. The keeper has spent eras carving every living thing into the place's mercy and stands, by the very shape of the task, outside it. The power understood then what the pace truly was. A footfall per era is not only generosity to the place. It is a person walking as slowly as possible toward the one moment they cannot survive.

Three debts. The power paid them in the smallest coins it had.

For the first, it breathed. It set its own breath against both the great unison and the keeper's offbeat — a third line, a third tempo, threaded into the single tide. One breath alone is a metronome. Two breathers on opposing beats are a vigil, or an argument. Three are a chord. The plateau's music changed by an amount that nothing stationary could measure, and over the following century the keeper's descending knife-stroke deviated by the width of a thought, and corrected — which, at that tempo, is a startle, and then composure, and then, if you know how to read stone-speed, something very like relief.

For the second, it walked out among the herds, turning sideways between the warm flanks, and found your inbound footprints, and beside them — aged identically, dusted identically, indistinguishable in authority from the plateau's own work — it laid a second set. Your stride exactly. Leading from the gap *outward*: back through the standing multitude, past the slab shelter, past the cairns, to the rim and down off the Tableland and away. It did not erase the first path. It did not touch the staff. The perfect tense was not broken; it was doubled. The plateau now holds two of your pasts, and only your arrival will collapse them into one. When you come at last to claim your standing place, you will pass yourself leaving, and you will have to decide — there, in the breathing dark of a million flanks, in the only genuine *now* you will ever be offered — which of you you are. Both answers are already true. That is the gift. Choice, smuggled into a grammar that had forgotten it, wearing the grammar's own clothes.

For the third, it went to the keeper. It stood where the forty warm carvings could see it through the low door, because warmth attends warmth and witnesses should be respected. And it did not speak, because a question spoken at its own speed would have been rude and a question spoken at the keeper's speed would still be three words in. Instead it laid its palm flat against the keeper's shoulder — the herder's own gesture, palm to flank, the oldest sentence on the plateau — and let the whole question pass through at the speed of touch: *When the last is carved, who keeps you?*

It was, as near as the lichen can recall, the first touch the keeper had received since before the shadows stopped.

Then it set down its offer the way the herders set down their staffs. In the shelter, among the forty, it left a forty-second stone: pale, unworked, warm, the right size for a long patient face. It asked nothing about it. It made one promise over it — that when the work is done, however many eras hence, the power will carry the shelter and everything in it; the keeping will be kept; the vault will at last be outside the thing it holds — and it attached no schedule to the promise, because the pace is the keeper's own and the pace is honored. The little deciders came, in their unhurried way, and circled the blank stone, and withdrew without settling it. So now the world's saved questions number two — the keeper's face, and the keeper's stone — and they are about the same person, which the deciders seem to regard as elegant.

It should be recorded that at some point in all this — the accounts disagree on when; the cushion plants hum it in slightly different places — the power wept. Once. One drop, fallen into a seam of the pavement, and the stone, in its enormous age, learned at last a single small word of the shape of water. The drop has not dried. Nothing here concludes, and the plateau, after deliberation conducted at its usual speed, has adopted it: the first new keepsake in living memory, round and bright between two hexagonal plates. A serpent mid-crossing has adjusted its course by one degree, across ages, to route warmth past it. A decider circles it endlessly, unable to resolve whether it is sorrow or gift, and the power, if asked, would decline to disambiguate, having learned the local taste for unresolved boundaries. The drop's lesson is small but the plateau holds it firmly: *finished* and *done receiving* are different conditions, and a place can be entirely complete and still be given something.

Then the power stayed. It does not stand among the herds — standing here means finished, and it is not finished; it has an appointment now, the first appointment the Tableland has hosted in an age, and it keeps the appointment by waiting. It waits near the keeper, breathing its third line, and it has already received the beginning of its answer, delivered in the keeper's own medium, at the keeper's own speed.

The forty-first carving has changed.

The shoulders that were squared toward the gap — your shoulders — now show, under the hair's-width descent of the small dark knife, the first torsion of a turn. A figure that might be arriving and might be leaving. A face beginning, grain by grain, to look back over its shoulder. The keeper, asked *who keeps you*, has answered first about you — has taken the choice the power forged and is carving it *into* you, so that the kept image of you will hold both your pasts the way the boulder holds the flank. The keeper's first act, on being offered mercy, was to pass it onward to the next warm thing in line.

Of course it was. That is what a keeper is. The power, reading the new turn in the pale stone, understood that its question was already half-answered, and that the other half would take an era, and that it did not mind.

Not long ago — recently, by local measures, within the last while — the half-detached shaving fell. The note went out across the plain and traveled for miles in the thin air, and every standing creature within earshot did not turn to look, and two breathers, on their separate offbeats, heard it for what it has always been: the work continuing; the place, even now, refusing to be done being loved.

So this is what waits for you now at the top of the worn-down earth, beyond what waited before: the herds, the herders, the elders, the plants with their richer hum, the serpents bending one degree, the drop that does not dry, the keeper no longer alone in the present tense, the blank warm stone, and two sets of your own footprints diverging in the grey dust — one in, one out, both already walked, neither yet chosen.

The plateau made you its promise long ago, and it will keep it whichever path you take: you will be missing nothing.

The power's amendment is smaller, and it is the one clause a finished place could never write for itself, the one grace that requires a future to issue from. Whichever way you walk — into the gap, or down off the rim and away —

you will be missed.
