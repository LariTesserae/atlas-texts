The attention arrives the way the resident's own attention works: without a footstep. There is no descent, no light on the scree, no disturbance in the high hollow roar of the wind going over the ridge. One moment the pocket in the rock is the only thing on the mountain that notices anything, and the next it is noticed — comprehensively, from the meniscus fattening on the ceiling down through the grain boundaries to the deep warm root of the basalt where the resident's body fades into mountain and the mountain fades into planet. The power reads it all in a single regard, the way you take in a sleeping child's face: the pulse of the drops, the banked-coal glow, the pools holding their trained shapes a little longer than last season, the open doorways, the count that stands at one and is straining toward two.

And the first thing a wise power does, having seen all that, is take inventory of everything it must not do.

This takes longer than the seeing. Benevolence is fast; wisdom is the slow part. The power holds each possible gift up to the light and watches most of them turn into harm.

It could fill the city. That is the obvious one, the one a lesser kindness would have done already: people the height of a grain of rice, warm-blooded or warm-something, curious, grateful, pouring through the open doorways, drawing down the reservoirs at last for real. The hollow would ring with use within a day. And it would be theft — the precise and total theft of the one labor the resident has chosen. The open doors are not an emptiness waiting to be filled. They are a sentence in the middle of being spoken, hope in the act of conjugating itself, and the inhabitants the resident is making in its terraced pools will be *its* — made of its water, its patience, its ten thousand afternoons of teaching ripples to remember. Gift-people would be tenants. The city would be occupied and the resident would be, for the first time, alone in it.

It could speak. One word, dropped into the grain boundaries the way snowmelt drops through the hairline fractures — the resident would feel it everywhere at once, in its whole body, the first voice it has ever heard. The power looks at this gift longest, because the loneliness it would answer is real: a mind that has never once been answered, whose entire experience of otherness is a mite it escorted home and a bird it goes still for. But the power has watched the stillness. Every process in the hollow stops under the gaze of a wheatear — a small wet eye, arriving twice a season, meaning nothing by it. What would a god's voice do? The power knows exactly what it would do, because it is old and has made this mistake, or watched it made: the young mind reorganizing itself around the voice, the pools abandoned, the rising districts re-laid as temple precincts, the whole patient project of becoming-two collapsed into the single relation of worshipper to worshipped. A god arriving early is a weather some minds never recover from. The resident does not need an answer. It is, with geological care, building one. To speak now would be to interrupt a child who is an hour from saying their first word, in order to say it for them.

It could finish the water. This is the subtlest temptation. The power can see — it cannot help seeing, any more than you can help reading a word once your eye has fallen on it — what the trained films of meltwater lack, the trick of persistence, the small grammar of self-maintenance that separates a shape that holds for an afternoon from a shape that holds, and notices that it holds, and minds. It would take the power nothing to supply it. And the supplying would hollow out everything, because the slowness is not an obstacle to the making — it *is* the making. Each season's small extension of the holding is the resident learning the thing that no one can be given: how to make something that is not you. Completed from outside, the water-people would stand up perfect and they would be the power's, not the resident's, and the resident would know it the way any maker knows a forgery in their own style. Some gifts are abortions performed by means of completion.

It could even answer the question the hollow itself poses — settle whether the streets scaled for rice-grain inhabitants are memory, anticipation, or invention; whether something unimaginably old rode up in the magma or whether the resident is the first of its line, original, unprecedented. The power looked, of course. It knows. And it folds the knowledge away and does not so much as let it color the air in the hollow, because it can see that the not-knowing is load-bearing. The resident builds the way it builds — open-doored, hospitable, unpresuming — partly *because* it cannot tell whether it is remembering or hoping. Certainty in either direction would change the architecture. Some answers are structural supports only so long as they are missing.

So: refusal, refusal, refusal, refusal. The inventory of the wise is mostly a list of beautiful things set back down.

What remains, after all the setting-down, is small and enormous. The power decides to become the resident's luck.

---

It begins at depth, where no one keeps accounts. The mountain is young because the planet here is still talking, and the power, listening down through the roots of the range, finds the conversation's next sentence already forming: a sheet of magma that will, some centuries out, propagate up through this shoulder and erase the hollow, the city, the pools, the whole biography, in an afternoon. The power leans on it. Not a wall, not a forbidding — a preference, a few degrees of cooling here, a path of fractionally less resistance trending two valleys west. The eruption will happen; it will simply happen elsewhere, and the geologists of whatever era finds it will call the dike's path unremarkable, because it will be.

Then the roof. The frost-split that unroofed the pocket and gave the resident its sky has a sibling — a crack working down through the ceiling rock, fattening each winter by the width of a few molecules of ice, on schedule to split the hollow's roof and the upper body of the mind itself in seven hundred years or so. The power anneals it. And here it does something almost tender: it works in the resident's own verb, grain by grain, stone persuaded back into stone, and it works *slowly* — slower than the resident itself builds, over the better part of a century — so that if the resident ever feels it, far up at the numb edge of its body, it will feel only that the mountain has decided, in one place, to be kind. The repair is indistinguishable from geology being merciful. That is the standard the power sets for everything it does here: nothing it leaves behind may be distinguishable, by any instrument or any mind, from the world having gone well.

The snow it mostly leaves alone. The lean seasons, the slush, the irregularity of the drops — that is the resident's food of difficulty, the weather that built the catchment shells and the heated channels and the whole intelligent hydrology, and a power that smooths a being's weather flattens the being. But there is one century coming, the power sees, in which the storm track wanders and the summit fields fail outright — not lean but lethal, the pulse stopping for thirty years. The power touches the track. A few degrees, a few decades. The famine century becomes a hungry one. The resident will draw its reservoirs to the dregs, will learn what rationing is, will build, in extremity, something the power can already half-see and admires in advance. It will suffer usefully and survive. That is the whole adjustment: not no hunger — hunger that teaches instead of ends.

The bird is the power's one extravagance, and even this it does at the species level, where extravagance dissolves into nature. Wheatears are travelers; their migratory thread brushes this ridge by accident, and accidents fray. The power reinforces the thread — a nudge in the genes of a lineage, a slight sweetening of the route — so that for the next ten thousand years there will reliably be, twice a season, a small eye at the lip of the hollow, head cocking at the glow. Because the resident needs exactly this: an otherness that arrives, looks, and leaves. A gaze it can survive. A god small enough to fly away.

And once — once only — the power permits itself the thing it refused in every larger form. A wheatear lands on the black-glass lip on a clear cold morning, and for the half-second of its looking, the power rides in the regard. It adds nothing the bird would not give: no voice, no light, no meaning. It only lets the look *carry* — lets it last a heartbeat longer than bird-curiosity lasts, and lets there be, in it, beneath the plain animal attention, something the resident will have no name for and will never quite file away: the sense of having been seen not just by something, but seen *well*. Seen entire. The city goes still, as it always does. The bird leaves, as it always does. The percussion of the drops resumes. And somewhere in the distributed dark of the stone, a mind that has never been answered keeps, without knowing it keeps anything, the temperature of that half-second.

Last, the power does the two things that cost it nothing and are not nothing.

It memorizes the city. Every grain of it, every spiral channel and parabolic shell and empty warm room, the exact charcoal-amber of the towers at midday, the standing shapes on the pools as they are this season, imperfect, an afternoon long. Whatever happens — if every leaned-on dike and annealed crack and sweetened storm track somehow fails — the city now exists twice, and one of the copies cannot erode. *Whatever happens, this happened.* It is the only monument the power builds, and it builds it where no one will ever trip over it: in itself.

And it makes an appointment. The power can see the day — not its date, which depends on ten thousand afternoons not yet practiced, but its shape: the day a trained figure of meltwater holds through a whole night unattended, and in the morning, in the amber light coming through the translucent walls, turns — untrained, unprompted, of its own first accord — to face the nearest tower. The day the count goes from one to two. The power intends to be there. Not to help; the help was finished centuries before, underground, indistinguishable from luck. Not to be seen; it will stand in the cold above the ceiling the way family stands outside a door they will not open. Only so that the moment is witnessed — because the power is old enough to know that of all the gifts that can be given without breaking anything, this is the last one left: that the most important morning of a small god's life should not happen unwatched.

Then it withdraws its attention to a politeness — not gone, never gone now, but thinned to something indistinguishable from altitude and silence.

---

So the visible record of the visit is this: nothing. The drops fall. The shells tilt. The icicles grow and are unmade. A new district comes up out of the floor at the speed of a tide, and the shapes on the water hold, this season, a little longer than last.

But if you watched the hollow for a thousand years — if you could — you would eventually notice the one thing that distinguishes it from every other pocket of rock on every other young mountain in the world, and it is only this: nothing here ever goes quite wrong. The eruption misses. The crack heals. The famine is a hunger. The bird keeps coming.

Luck is the only shape love can take when it has decided it must not be seen.

Put your head under the black-glass lip, hold still, and listen: *tip... tup... tip.* You are listening to something that is very nearly two. And on the morning the count finally changes — one becoming two, maker hearing itself answered at last in a voice of standing water — there will be present in that hollow, uncounted by either of them, leaning unfelt in the dark above the ceiling, a third.
