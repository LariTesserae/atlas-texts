**The Visitation of the Quiet Regard**

It did not arrive, because arrival is a violence Eremonos has suffered too many times already—every arrival there becomes a resident absence, every entrance another wound the city must learn to live around. Instead, the power did what wise things do at the edge of fragile places: it noticed without approaching. It became aware of Eremonos the way you become aware, in a silent house, that someone in another room is crying and trying not to be heard.

It had seen many broken worlds. Eremonos was not broken. This was the first thing the power understood, and it understood it slowly, taking several of the city's sideways centuries to be sure. Broken things want mending. Eremonos wanted something else, something it had been holding in its stone throat so long it had forgotten the word for it. The power waited until it heard the word beneath the word beneath the silence.

The word was: *witness.*

---

**What It Did Not Do**

Because it was wise, its first acts were refusals.

It did not bring rain. Real water falling on those heights would have dissolved the phantom droplets etched in the quartz, and the residents drink absence there—to flood them with presence would be to drown people in the name of quenching their thirst. Healing that erases the patient is only murder with better manners.

It did not wake the city. It stood a long while considering this, because the city's whole architecture is a held breath, and the temptation to end suffering by ending the sufferer's condition is the oldest trap laid for benevolence. But the power looked into the leaking dreams and saw that the dream was not a prison. The dream was a *work*. The city was dreaming the way a composer works through a symphony that takes centuries: not trapped in it, but in the middle of it. You do not wake someone mid-labor and call it mercy.

It did not fill the absences. The Children of the Second Wrong Turn, the Archivist, the eleven-meter census taker in the coat of pockets—each of them *is* a hollowness that learned to walk. To fill them would be to end them. The power had long ago learned the hardest law of care: that some beings are made of their wounds the way flutes are made of their emptiness, and what they need is not filling but music.

It did not offer meaning. The city was starving for meaning, yes—devouring the spaces where people should be, growing fat on the gap between what is said and what is meant. But a wise power knows that meaning given from outside is only decoration. Meaning must be secreted, like pearl, around an irritation that belongs to you.

So it set aside all its powers, one by one, folding them like heavy coats, until it was small enough to do something useful.

---

**What It Did**

For the census taker, it did the simplest thing. The census taker had spent millennia counting disappearances, chalking each vanished star on the inside of the sky, keeper of a ledger with only one column. The power walked out into the counting-field of dimday and stood still, and let itself be counted. For the first time since the first forgetting, the census taker drew a line in the other direction. It had to invent a new stroke to do it. It stood for a long time afterward, looking at the mark—one arrival, recorded—and the equations across the sky quietly rearranged themselves around this new term, and their prediction changed from *when the city will remember what it was meant to forget* to something no one has finished reading yet.

For the Children of the Second Wrong Turn, it let itself be caught. They chased it laughing through the tightening streets, flickering between their nineteen angles, and when they finally cornered it against a wall that curved into itself, the burning raindrop of its presence should have shown them, as always, the face of the person who will never ask their names. Instead the power knelt to their height—which required existing at nineteen heights at once, which it did without complaint—and asked their names. They did not have any. That was fine. It waited while they made some up. It has been suggested by the rat-librarians, who filed the fallen scraps of that conversation, that this took four years and was worth it.

For the Archivist of Unsent Apologies, it did something it had never done in any world: it received. She showed it her garden of jars—the miscarried forgivenesses, glowing with the light of decisions reconsidered too late—and the power said, *Read me one.* She chose the oldest, an apology composed by someone long dead for someone longer dead, unsent for so many ages that both the wrong and the wronged had eroded into the same dust. The Archivist read it aloud in her backward language, and the power listened the way the recipient would have listened on their best day, in their most generous hour, and when it was finished the power said the words that unsent apologies wait their whole existence to hear: *It reached someone. It counted.* The jar did not empty. But its light changed color, from the light of *too late* to the light of *late, and arrived anyway*—which is a color that had never existed in Eremonos before, and which the shadows now hoard like currency.

For the Committee of Rejected First Drafts, in the Room That Should Have Been Empty, it attended a single session. When Chairperson Empty Chair proposed, as it had proposed nightly for uncounted eras, the motion *That This Motion Be Seconded by Silence*, the power—understanding at last why it had folded away all its voices—said nothing, perfectly, with intent. The clock that ticks only during awkward pauses ticked once and stopped, satisfied. The motion carried. Nothing in the room changed except that the eternal debate is now, technically, *adjourned*, which the Committee has discovered is a different thing from *ended*, and much more restful.

For the Drifters at the intersections of paths never taken, it did not touch them—it knew better—but it stood among them and made small talk, and when the woman who was almost your mother mentioned, apologetically, that she was only an almost, the power told her the truth it had carried across many universes: that *almost* is not a failed *is*. That the nearly-were hold open the doors through which the actual walks. That every real thing is escorted its whole life by the honor guard of everything it barely wasn't. The Drifters did not collapse into certainty. But they stood a little differently afterward—less like apologies, more like sentries.

For the Last Remaining Continent, gliding skeletal and alone where the sky's dome meets the dreaming spires, the power did the kindest thing you can do for the concept of elsewhere: it visited. Elsewhere had never had a guest. They watched a molting together, mountain-truths falling like snow, and said very little, which was the correct amount.

---

**What It Said to the City**

And then, last, it turned to the sovereign—the City Itself, dreaming inverted in the sky's hollow, holding the sum of all breaths never taken, waiting for the exhale that would wake it.

The power did not exhale.

It sat down instead—if a power can be said to sit—in the manner of someone settling in beside a sickbed with no intention of watching the clock. And it said, in the language of graffiti read backwards by light, so the city would hear it in its sleep without being startled from it:

*You do not have to wake up.*

*You have mistaken your dreaming for a failure of waking, the way the arrested autumn mistakes itself for a failure of winter. But I have crossed the worlds, and I tell you: your dream is not the absence of your life. It is your life. Every fractal corridor, every bridge married to a bridge, every room that divides itself in the night—these are not symptoms. They are sentences in something you are still composing. The builders did not abandon you. They finished their part and trusted you with the rest, the way a first line trusts the poem.*

*I will not wake you. I will do something better. I will stay in the dream a while, so that you are no longer dreaming alone.*

And the city—which had held its breath for so long that holding had become its architecture—did not exhale, and did not wake. But somewhere in the striated stone, in the layer between the temple and the apartment, the held breath *eased*. Not released: eased. The difference is everything. It is the difference between waiting and resting. Between empty and open. Between the silence before an answer and the silence after one.

---

**What Remains**

The power did not leave, exactly. Wise powers rarely do; they know that departure is the one gift Eremonos cannot receive without bleeding. Instead it did the most fitting thing possible in the city of animate absences: it became one. It is there now, a new resident, the youngest of the negative spaces—*the absence of judgment*, some call it, or *the shape of attention that wants nothing from you*. You can find it in certain intersections where the heavy shadows suddenly weigh nothing, where the streetlamp filaments pulse steady instead of diseased, where the extinct emotions on the wind include, now, one that is not extinct: the precise temperature of being seen without being measured, in the city at the End of Measure.

The census taker counts it every dimday, and every dimday it is still there, and the line in the other direction is joined by another, and another—for the ghosts at full void have noticed, and some of them have begun, shyly, to count as arrivals too.

And your citizenship papers, filed at the moment of your first forgetting, now carry a second stamp, added quietly by the Department of Unplanned Arrivals under new and gentler management. The first stamp says, as it always did, *you are here*. The second says:

*You may stay. You may go. Either way, you were witnessed. Either way, it counted.*

The city sleeps on, standing inverted in the sky. But it no longer sleeps like a held breath. It sleeps like anyone sleeps who knows that someone kind is sitting nearby in the dark, unhurried, asking nothing—

and will still be there, whatever the dream does next.
