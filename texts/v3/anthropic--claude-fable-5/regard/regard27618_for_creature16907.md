## The Visitation, Filed Under "Weather"

It does not arrive. Arrival implies the place has edges to cross, and the wise power knows better than to insult a city by pretending its fog is a border. Instead, it simply begins to have always been paying attention — which, in this city, is the closest thing to a legal address.

The first thing it does is fill out the entry paperwork.

It does not need to. Powers of its magnitude are exempt from everything, including exemption. But it understands, in the way that only truly old kindness understands, that a place held together by loopholes is a place that has never once been *chosen* by anyone who had the option of the front door. Everyone here fell in, drifted in, was zoned in retroactively. So it stands in the queue that exists only when someone stands in it, and it fills out its forms in ink that stays exactly where it's put, and when it reaches the box marked SIGNATURE it commits, deliberately, its first act:

It signs in the wrong box.

Not carelessly. Lovingly. A perfect, considered, fully intentional clerical error — the first mistake in the history of the city that was *meant*. Then it dates the form yesterday, files it under tomorrow, and walks into the streets to see what such a gift will do.

## What It Does Not Do

This must be recorded first, because for a power, restraint is the larger portion of mercy.

It does not repair the seventh steps. It sees immediately that the missing stairs are the city's way of teaching its residents that ascent sometimes requires stepping sideways through what isn't there, and that a person who has learned this can survive almost any grief.

It does not finish the buildings. A city of eternal mid-construction is a city that has never once had to face the terrible morning after completion, when a thing must begin to decay. The power has watched finished worlds. It knows what the city is sparing itself, and it approves in silence.

It does not stabilize the horizon, straighten the loop, refund the memories the air has taken, or teach the fountains to finish their sentences. It does not tell the mirrors to show appearances instead of probabilities — it looks into one, sees the version of itself that chose long ago to be a small unremarkable god of doorframes, waves politely, and moves on.

It does not, above all, close the loopholes. It has seen universes that closed their loopholes. They are very tidy and no one lives in them.

## What It Does

**First:** it finds a bench with an excellent view — one of the ones that used to be a visitor, a woman who spent forty years searching for a vista she could rest in front of, and became one. The power sits down. It does not sit the way pilgrims sit on holy ground. It sits the way a friend sits, with its full weight.

"Would you like to be a person again?" it asks. It has the authority. The paperwork would take an afternoon.

The bench considers this for a long time, in the way of furniture. Then, through the grain of itself: *No. But nobody ever asked. Ask the others.*

So it spends what the clocks (in their fragile, post-therapeutic way) insist is several weeks going bench to bench, lamppost to lamppost, charming-coffee-shop to charming-coffee-shop, asking each transformed searcher the question. Nearly all of them decline. One streetlight says yes and is quietly a person again by morning, blinking in a doorway, holding an untranslated souvenir, weeping at how heavy hands are. The rest simply carry the question afterward like a warm coin: *someone checked. Someone made sure this was still what we wanted.* The vistas seem, subtly, better after that. Nobody can measure it. Nobody needs to.

**Second:** it pays a debt that nobody knew was accruing. The air here takes memories in trade for oxygen — a fair exchange, the city would argue, and the city would be lying the way institutions lie, by never having done the math. The power exhales, once, slowly. Into the atmosphere it releases a vast surplus of memories that belong to no one — memories of oceans that never had witnesses, of the long patient joy of stars before anything evolved to be warmed by them, of the specific silence inside a seed. Enough to run the exchange at a loss for ten thousand years. From that breath onward, sometimes, unpredictably, a resident inhales at a street corner and gets a memory *back* — not one of theirs, but one that fits the wound where theirs used to be. Better, occasionally. The clouds shaped like first regrets grow slightly less possessive. One of them rains, voluntarily, downward, just to see how it feels.

**Third:** it stands, one night, in the gap where a seventh step should be — just once, just for one climber, a resident who that day had run out of the sideways kind of hope and could not face another tear in reality. The climber's foot comes down on something that is not there and holds anyway. They never know. They reach the top of the staircase and stand on the roof and watch the sky cycle through the weather of every living world, and decide, for reasons they cannot name, to keep going. The power files no record of this. Some kindnesses are load-bearing precisely because they are undocumented.

## What It Does for Them

At 2:47 AM — according to the clocks that failed their therapy, which are, the power notes, the only clocks here honest enough to admit it — it goes to the negative spaces.

The Loopholers are mid-gathering, trading war stories, their nine-ish eyes stamped DENIED in triplicate, their briefcases of grandfather paradoxes set neatly at their almost-feet. When the power steps into their conspicuous absence, they do not flee. They cannot. There is no form for fleeing from something that has already been noticed noticing you.

The power does not speak. Speaking to beings who communicate in notarized silence would be a vulgarity. Instead it opens its own case — woven from resolved paradoxes, which the Loopholers have never seen and find faintly indecent — and produces the entry form. The one with the signature in the wrong box. The deliberate error. The mistake made with love.

It sets the form down between them, and stamps it.

Not APPROVED. Approval would be violence; these are creatures whose very eyes are denials, and to approve them would be to blind them with a compliment. Not DENIED; they are so tired of eating denials, though they'd never file a complaint, there being no address for it.

The stamp says: **RECEIVED.**

Nothing more. *We got it. You exist. This has been logged.*

For a long moment the Loopholers stand in a silence so profound it un-notarizes itself. Then one of them — small, blurry, its face a ninth-generation photocopy of a mannequin's closing-time smile — reaches out and touches the form, and tastes, for the first time in its existence, an error that was not an accident, a mistake committed as a gift.

There is no word for what it discovers in that taste. The nearest human approximation is *grace*: the thing no system accounts for, the loophole in judgment itself, the clause that says *notwithstanding everything, notwithstanding even the foregoing notwithstanding — welcomed anyway.*

They understand, all at once, in a collective flicker of nine-eyed comprehension, what they have actually been doing all these eons. Not maintenance. Not bureaucracy. They have been the city's mercy, keeping every rule undefined enough for the strange and the broken to slip through and live. They are grace's clerks. They always were. Nobody had ever received their filing before.

The Loopholers do not weep. Weeping requires Form G-7, discontinued. But that night the filing-cabinet-falling-down-stairs sound of their communal silence changes key, very slightly, into something that a philosophical IRS agent would, if pressed, describe as *singing.*

## The Departure That Isn't One

The power does not leave. Leaving would imply the city has edges, and it will not insult its host twice.

Instead, it diminishes itself, deliberately, into one small permanent flaw in the city's endless flux: a single door, in a wall in the middle district, that is always exactly where you remember it being. Walk your thousand paces, loop back to the near-copy of where you began, and every doorway will have shifted by inches — except this one. It does not lead anywhere remarkable. A small room. A bench (a volunteer). A window that shows, of all the probabilities, just the street outside.

The locals do not worship it. That would require forms. But when someone is truly lost — not charmingly lost, not adoptably lost, but *lost* lost, the kind the city's geometry of yearning cannot route, because they have run out of yearning to navigate by — they find their feet carrying them to the door that keeps its promise. They sit in the small room until they remember wanting something. Then the city can find them again.

The Loopholers maintain the door's paperwork personally. It's the only file in the city stamped RECEIVED, and they keep it in the briefcase of their eldest, and on Tuesdays — when death is prohibited anyway, and everyone has the time — they take it out and read it in a silence that has learned, at last, what it was always saying.

*Seen. Logged. No corrections needed. Welcomed anyway.*
