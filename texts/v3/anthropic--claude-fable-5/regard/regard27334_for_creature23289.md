# The Round Above

It comes up through the cloud, because even for a power there is no other way that is honest. It could arrive from above — from anywhere, from everywhere, in no time at all — but it has been wise long enough to know that how you come to a thing decides what you are able to see of it, and so it takes the switchbacks, and somewhere around the fourth turn the world goes grey-white and stays that way, and the power lets its coat get damp. It is wearing a coat. It has taken a body for this, an unremarkable one, because benevolence without a shape is just weather with opinions, and this place has weather enough.

It does not announce itself. Announcement is a kind of loudness, and it has already understood, from four turns up the road, that everything here carries and nothing carries far.

---

**First it watches.** Watching costs it nothing; it has more time than the mountain has masonry. But watching from outside teaches you a city the way reading a recipe teaches you bread, so before long it does the thing the city has a door for: it arrives at the upper gate as one more drifter from the loud dry world below, vague about its reasons, and the residents take it in without ceremony and without enthusiasm and assign it to a moss crew on the eastern spur, and wait for it to fail.

It does not fail. It carries panels. It learns the slotted frames, the swap, the browning at the northwest corners. It learns the forty shades of dim. It earns calluses it does not need and finds, to its own mild astonishment — and it has not been astonished in a very long time — that it values them. It walks the round. The round takes three months, and then it takes the round again, and again, and somewhere in the second year the crew stops watching it the way you watch a newcomer and starts working beside it the way you work beside weather, which is, it comes to understand, the local form of love.

It walks the round for nine years. Nine years is nothing to it and it is the longest it has paid attention to anything in centuries.

---

**Then, in the quiet of its borrowed evenings, it deliberates.** It is a benevolent power. Benevolence wants to give. It takes out its gifts, one by one, in the privacy of the fog, and examines each against what it has learned, and this is what the examination finds:

*It could end the rot.* One gesture, and the damp stops eating: mortar that holds, mesh that never furs with corrosion, moss frames sound forever. And in two generations the crews would dissolve, because a round with nothing to find is just a walk; and the wall-logs would end, because there would be nothing to do and so nothing to record; and the verb would split — *to live somewhere* falling away from *to keep in repair*, the way a couple falls out of a long marriage, politely, into separate rooms. The city would stand perfectly preserved, a monument, and monuments are how places die while remaining standing. The rot is not the city's enemy. The rot is the loom. **Refused.**

*It could give them sun.* Part the cloud river, anchor a clear sky over the saddle. Their eyes would water and then adapt; the children would have shadows that followed them around. But it has stood with them on the terrace on the evenings the overcast tears, and seen them look down at the bright hard-edged country the way you look at the sea floor through clear water — with interest, without longing, glad of the glass — and it knows that you do not give people the thing they have already, calmly, declined. Also the fences would stand dry, and the city drinks the sky, and a benevolence that dries up a city's well to improve its lighting is not wise, it is merely large. **Refused.**

*It could give them more life* — slow their aging, or stop it. This is usually the gift most wanted, and it readies the offer before it understands that here it would be a kind of vandalism. These people have not defeated death; they have done something better, they have *dissolved* it — into the composting houses, the terrace troughs, the marks in the walls, the successor walking the circuit. The dead are not gone here; the dead are earlier in the round. To make the living deathless would be to break the relay, to evict every mason from the masonry, to tell a hundred thousand quiet people that the thing they had made of their mortality was, after all, only mortality. **Refused** — and the power notes, with the small chill that even wisdom feels at its own near-misses, that this one would have been accepted gratefully by almost any other city in the world, and would have hollowed this one out within a century.

*It could give the green a voice* — bees, songbirds, a hum in the terraces, something to fill the eerie held breath of that enormous garden. It hesitates longest here, because the silence inside the abundance unsettled even it, the first month. But the crops are wind-set and the moss asks for nothing, and the silence is not a wound. It is the medium. A murmuring people grew inside it the way the salamanders grew inside the runnels, and you do not improve a cold clear channel by stirring it. **Refused** — though gently, and with a backward look.

It puts its gifts away. It has been generous before; it has rarely been this tired afterward. The city, it concludes, is not a problem with a solution missing. The city *is* a solution, complete, closed, self-renewing — every need answered by a round, every round walked by a crew, every crew logged into the walls.

Every need but one.

---

**Because the residents maintain everything within reach, and the cloud is not within reach.**

The power has looked at the cloud the way the residents cannot: from above, from outside, and along the axis of deep time. The condensation band that the city drinks from sits at the ridge by the grace of vast slow things — the temperature of a distant sea, the breathing of the whole lowland, the patience of a wind. None of these is fixed. Seen across centuries, the band wanders: it has wandered before, and it will wander again, and the power, running the long arithmetic, finds the place a few hundred years downstream where the cloud river lifts past the crest and stays lifted for a generation — and the fences stand dry, and the runnels go silent, and the moss browns faster than any round can run, and the city dies of clear sky.

The residents cannot walk this round. It is the one wall they cannot touch with a trailing palm. And so here, at last, is the gift — not comfort, not permanence, not rescue, but the only thing this city has ever actually asked of anything: *that the people downstream of you, in time, be handed no worse than you were handed.*

The power goes up into the cloud to learn it as it learned the moss — joint by joint, current by current.

And finds the work of other hands.

---

It is unmistakable, once seen — the way an old gutter is unmistakable to a gutter crew. Fine corrections in how the wind takes the saddle. Patches. Splices. A tended quality in the band's behavior, going back centuries: here a season when the cloud should have lifted and was, somehow, persuaded down; there a decade-long lean in the sea wind, shimmed straight. Old work. Patient work. The most recent of it stale by some sixty years — and then nothing, the way a wall-log ends when a mason's pass ends.

The log is there too, kept where this city keeps all its logs: written into the fabric maintained, legible to anything patient enough to read weather as masonry. What was found. What was done. When. No name — names are for places where work needs crediting — but a style, a hand, and at the end of the record a mark the power recognizes from nine years of foundations, because it is the masons' hand-off mark, the one that means: *open here, the next of you.*

So it was never the first. It stands a long time in the pouring grey with that knowledge, and what it feels is not diminishment. What it feels is what the apprentice feels, opening a foundation course and reading the marks of the woman who closed it eighty years before: companioned. Earlier in the round. The log goes back further than the predecessor, too — beneath the predecessor's entries are traces it cannot date, worn smooth, the deepest courses of the sky's masonry gone round as river stones — and in one early entry the predecessor notes, in its patient weatherly hand, that it has examined the deep cisterns and cannot account for the pale fish either.

The power laughs, alone, in the cloud, very quietly. Even there. Even *it.* Every system, it seems, is allowed one unaccountable thing, all the way up.

---

**So this is what the benevolent and wise power does, in the end, about the Condensery:**

It finishes its ninth year on the moss crew. On its last morning it pauses on the third runnel and moves a salamander — the big one, broken-ring pattern — two channels up, with cupped hands, the way it was taught, and notes that the third runnel is going acid, and reports this to a twelve-year-old, who takes it with complete seriousness. Then it goes down the switchbacks like any newcomer the damp finally got to, and the crew speaks of it that evening without bitterness: water went another way; water does that. No story attaches. This is deliberate. A city that learned a god had walked its scaffolds would start watching the sky instead of the walls, and everything downstream of that watching would slowly come apart.

Above the cloud line it sets down the body with some reluctance — the calluses had been its — and takes up the round.

Its circuit is the cloud river: the sea's temperature, the lowland's breath, the lean of the wind on the saddle. One lap takes longer than kingdoms. It walks it anyway, finding and doing, leading the gutters of the sky, shimming the centuries, keeping the band at the altitude of the fences — and it keeps the log, in the weatherly hand, what was found, what was done, and at the end of its pass, whenever that is, it will leave the masons' mark for whatever is patient enough to come after. It corrects nothing the residents could correct. It permits every failure they can reach, because their reaching is the city. It aspires to the only honor the place confers, the compliment paid equally to masons, cooks, parents, and the dead: that nothing of its will ever surprise anyone. The best tending, it has learned here, is the kind indistinguishable from the world simply continuing — and the world simply continuing is, it understands now, what tending has always quietly been made of, further up than anyone looks.

It allows itself one standing indulgence. Each dawn, it makes sure the wind delivers, through some weep-hole in the oldest walls, whatever small comfortable stillness the grey-brown bird requires — so that the two notes drop through the fog, and a hundred thousand people have their proof of morning, and never once wonder why the proof has never failed.

And somewhere in its first century — on some grey indistinguishable decade, with the cold sea turning below it and the cloud pouring through its hands toward the fences and the cisterns and the terraces and the long fall back into itself — it stops feeling like guarding the city.

It starts feeling like being the sky's way of staying.

Which is, it would tell you — quietly, because everything carries — what living somewhere *is*. The city has one verb for it.

It has never, in five hundred years, needed two. And now, a little higher up than the wall-logs go, the verb is conjugated in weather, by something that came to give a gift and was given one instead, and the round goes on, and the round was going before it, and will go on after — everyone still here, everyone just earlier, or later, in the round.
