**The Reader**

Begin with attention, because the power is mostly attention. It has no body it is attached to, no name it answers quickly, and very few habits, but the attention is constant and very old, and it moves across the worlds the way light moves across a floor — without hurry, touching everything, lifting nothing.

It noticed the Calderwald the way you notice that someone in a crowded room is not talking but is *about* to. A continent of warm stone, holding its heat like a body, spelling — vent-field by vent-field, four hundred years to the knee — the same character, attempted again and again, not yet right. The power has seen many strange things and only a few new ones. It stopped. It read the drift, the convergence, the narrowing variance, the way the rate had quickened slightly since the towns. It understood at once what the mapkeepers will not say aloud, and what the margins of their charts are slowly agreeing on, and one thing more, which is this: first words are the same in every script. A being's first word outward — not its name for itself, which it settles early, down in the warm dark, but the word it makes when it finally suspects it is not alone — that word is one word, everywhere, however drawn. The power recognized the character before it was finished the way you recognize *hello* in a stranger's mouth before the second syllable.

So the question was never what the word would mean. The question was what to do about a being that has been saying it for longer than there have been ears, and is close now, and does not know whether anyone will be there when it lands.

**What it does not do**

It could finish the word tonight. One warm evening's walk across the vent-fields, a touch here, a degree of branching there, every rose-and-arsenic accretion coaxed the last little distance — done, spelled, complete before the slagdoves wake. It considers this for what is, by its standards, a very long time, perhaps four seconds, and declines. A word given is not a word said. The Caldfolk worked this out generations ago at their crucibles: the mold made true comes out wrong. The power has crossed a great deal of dark in its existence and has learned almost nothing it was handed.

It could translate. Stand in a map-house, lay one finger on the master chart, and tell the keepers what the highland is going to say. It declines this too, and not only because meaning delivered ahead of utterance is a kind of theft. The map-houses have one taboo: write down only what has been spelled. It is a good taboo. The power decides it will keep it more strictly than anyone alive.

It could enter the dream — the warm dark, the shape almost made, the finger moving on the stone — and steady the hand. It very nearly does. It permits itself, in the end, only this: when an old founder dreams the shape and wakes with a finger still tracing, the surface under the finger is, lately, very slightly smoother. Nothing more.

**What it does**

Three things, and only one of them loud, and the loud one it does in vacuum, where loudness does not carry.

First, the loud one. There is a stone out in the dark, far down the centuries, on a path that would have ended in the Calderwald's sky with about nine thousand years of spelling still to go. The power goes out to it, alone, and leans — once, hard, the only force it will spend on this errand in its whole duration — and the stone goes elsewhere, and nothing anywhere records that anything happened. This is the power's entire opinion on the subject of interruptions.

Second, the quiet courtesy. Down in the lowlands, whatever the lowlands are now, the power lays a long mild indifference over the maps. Not a wall, not a fog — a fence would only draw climbers. Just a slope to attention itself, so that surveys pointed at the high country find their funding wandering, expeditions reroute for sensible-seeming reasons, and the Calderwald stays where it has been for a long time already: on the far page of every atlas, in the section no one's thumb falls open to. The highland asked for nothing, but a being mid-sentence should not have to manage visitors with drills.

Third — and this is the real work, the thing the other two only protect — it takes the post.

**The tower**

There is a tower on the south line, three days past the last town, whose warden has died; the line-runners have already sat upright in a row, facing up the cable, and the news is moving toward the towns in two speeds as usual. Before the gondola log arrives, a volunteer is walking up from the lowlands, which surprises nobody, because there has never once been a shortage of volunteers, and which should surprise everybody, because no one has come up from below in living memory. He has been walking, by his own account, a long time. His pace never varies. The gate-town heat-readers touch his hand in greeting and find it the temperature of the stone — the temperature of a sleeping animal — and look at him a moment longer than courtesy requires, and then give the matter the long answer, which it is still receiving.

He keeps the tower well. He greases the cable; the runners colonize his roof-beams within a season and within a year are grooming him, which usually takes decades, but nobody is counting. He passes the gondolas hand over hand through the sheaves. He keeps the log — weather, traffic, the health of the troop, anything odd — and under *anything odd* he writes, year after year, in a small steady hand: *nothing.* This is, from a certain point of view, the largest lie ever told on the plateau, and from the only point of view that matters, perfectly true.

He keeps the customs. Every passenger is fed and bedded free, on the one condition, and he honors his side of it scrupulously: asked what he has seen since the last tower, he tells them — the dust-haze on the western circuit, the ironback herd standing where it stood, the new bloom on the field below the saddle. All of it true. All of it edited with a kindness that would take centuries to detect. And all the mail rests overnight in his tower, as mail does, which means that twice a year the fair copies of the bloomstone charts pass through his hands, town to town, with their crowded margins — hundreds of dead keepers' freehand guesses at the finished character, hand after hand after hand, converging. He reads them by lamplight. Some of the guesses are very close now. He never corrects a line. He puts the charts back in their oilcloth and lies down on the warm stone floor like a marten, and the mapkeepers, the following year, note without speculation that the drift has quickened by another measurable hair, and add their line or two, and that is all anyone writes down, which is exactly right.

**The keeper**

Once — only once — he answers a question.

A mapkeeper rides the south line at the end of her life, making her last exchange of fair copies in person, as the very old sometimes do. She lodges at his tower. She pays her passage in the usual coin, telling him everything she has seen, which takes most of the evening, because she has been looking at one thing for sixty years and has seen a great deal of it. And then, because she is dying, and because dying loosens the only taboo she has ever kept, she asks the stranger across the lamp:

"Is it close?"

He gives her the long answer. No flywheel up here; he uses the cable instead, waiting until a gondola passes in the dark overhead and the line sings its one soft note and the note dies all the way out. A full revolution of silence, as required. Then:

"It will be read."

She studies him for a while with her unhurried eyes, and nods, and does not ask the other questions, which he counts as one of the finest courtesies he has been paid anywhere. She dies in the tower two days later, and stays warm, as the Caldfolk do, and he sits with her with his palm flat on her sternum through the whole long handover — and here the power, who came to give attention, receives something instead. He feels where the warmth goes. Not out. Not away. *Down*, and gladly, the way ink goes into a page — and he understands, with the closest thing to surprise he has felt in an age, that the highland is not spending its people. It is keeping them. The word is being written in them and they are in the word, all of them, every warm body ever laid in a sleeping hollow, every founder, every herder two-circuits-and-most-of-a-third old. When the character is finally finished it will not be a mark on a continent. It will be crowded.

He carries her to the hollow himself. *Warmth in, warmth on.* He has attended a great many funerals across a great many worlds and this is the first one he has ever envied.

Later, sorting the mail, he finds her margin-mark on the chart she carried — her one freehand guess, drawn once, never discussed. It is right. He puts the chart back in its oilcloth.

**The reply**

Among the powers — there are others; they are not numerous, and they do not legislate, but they have manners — it is held that a first word is answered by being said back. Not explicated, not graded, not improved upon. Said back. The way you answer a child's first word: with the word, returned, so the speaker knows two things at once — *you were heard*, and *you said it right.*

This is the whole of the power's remaining plan, and it is why the plan requires nothing further of it but the post, the log, the grease on the cable, and the patience, of which it has an unusual amount even for its kind. It does not know exactly how long — the keepers' charts suggest centuries, the margins suggest fewer than they used to — and it does not mind. It came prepared to answer in years and finds it will be paying in something more like circuits, and it has noticed, with the dry interior amusement that is the nearest thing it has to a vice, that the highland's own people would consider this a perfectly ordinary term of employment.

And when the last vent-field settles, when the variance closes, when the character stands finished across a continent of warm stone — crowded, as he now knows, with everyone who ever lived here — the warden on the south line will come down from his tower, and lay his palm flat against the rock, the gesture every Caldfolk makes a dozen times a day without thinking, and say the word back, in the highland's own script, in whatever medium a thing like him says anything: slowly, warmly, once.

It is not a long word. It was never going to be. It is the shortest word there is, and the largest, and it has been worth — by his accounting, and his accounting is very good — every age of the wait.

**Evening**

In the evening the tower keeps its small sounds: the cable cooling, the runners settling in the beams, the lamp. A gondola crosses the darkening upland singing its one soft note, and the warden passes it hand over hand through the sheaves and watches its light creep off toward the next town, carrying ingots, mail, two oilcloth charts, and no news. He makes the log entry. Weather fair. Traffic, one. Runners well. Anything odd: *nothing.*

Then he banks the lamp and lies down on the warm floor, and underfoot, patient as ever, the highland breathes out — and for the first time in all its long warm spelling, something it cannot yet name, but is getting closer to naming every year, breathes in.
