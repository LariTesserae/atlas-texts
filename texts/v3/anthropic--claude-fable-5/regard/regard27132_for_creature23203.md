## The One the Sound Was For

Powers hear differently than we do. They do not triage the world by loudness — everything is loud, somewhere — but by change, because change is where the asking lives. A flood is a question. A famine is a question. A held note is filed, even by powers, under *weather*, and so the plateau went unnoticed for a very long time by everything capable of helping it, for the same reason it went unheard by everything living on it.

This power was old enough to have learned the flaw in that filing system. It had learned — slowly, the way wisdom is actually acquired, which is by being wrong in the same direction many times — that the cries which last longest are the ones that have stopped sounding like cries. That a thing repeated without variation for long enough becomes invisible precisely in proportion to its size. So it had developed a habit, in its long rounds of attention, of listening for the opposite of events: for the notes held past all sense, the words the world had been saying so long that the world itself had stopped hearing them.

It found the plateau by its constancy. One syllable, in all the noise of everything, that had not varied in longer than most mountains had existed. The power stopped. It listened the way you'd squint.

And then — this is the part worth recording carefully — it did not act. Not for a long time. It is the mark of a certain kind of power to arrive at a wound and start mending; it is the mark of a wiser kind to first sit down at a respectful distance and ask whether what it is looking at is a wound at all. Because the plateau, surveyed coldly, needed nothing. The shrimp had their resurrections. The lichen had its tenancy and its eons. The rimwings had outsourced effort to the planet and the foxes had inherited their roads and the people came up, a handful in a generation, and were taken without comment and without limit. The household was whole. The place was *finished*, and a benevolent power knows that finished things are easily ruined by benevolence — that there is a kind of help that is only appetite wearing kindness, the need to have *done something* to a place too complete to require it.

So the long first instinct of the power was: nothing. Leave it shouting. It is shouting well.

Two things changed its mind.

The first was the foxes. Prayers reach powers constantly, in volumes that would crush anything that actually read them all, and powers grow calluses against asking. What has no callus over it is the sight of waiting without a prayer in it — and that is what the foxes were doing, generations of them, sitting on their trot-lines at the same hour with their ears flat and their faces toward the rim, holding vigil for something they had no concept of, in a place where nothing new had happened since before their species had ears. A wise power knows that animals frequently feel approaches before the approaching thing has decided to approach. The foxes were not, it understood with something like embarrassment, sensing an ending.

They were sensing it.

They had felt it coming — its attention, the faint forward tremor of its regard through the floor of the world — for years before it knew it was coming. Which meant the question of whether to act had been answered some time ago, in the wrists of small pale animals, and all that remained was the manner.

The second thing was a phrase in its own oldest grammar, the one powers think in when they stop performing thought. The plateau was loud the way a struck bell is loud — struck once, before anyone — and had never been *allowed* to stop. Allowed. The power turned that word over for a season or two. Things that go on forever divide into things that are free and things that are held, and the difference is not audible from outside. The note's pitch never varied. Travelers had called that eternal; the lichen, leaning all one way since the beginning, might have called it something else. A syllable is not a syllable until it ends. A word held forever has not yet been said. The plateau had been speaking for the whole history of speech and had never once gotten to finish a word — which meant it had never once been heard, because hearing is something that happens to completed things. The people below the clouds had a verb for *to be heard by the noise*. Nothing anywhere had a verb for what had never yet happened to the noise itself.

So the power went up.

---

It went the long way, which for a power is a discipline rather than a necessity — up through the cloud sea at the speed of weather, taking off its omniscience at the foot of the climb the way you'd leave boots at a door, choosing to learn the place through the place's own senses. It let itself register to the foxes' wrists as the lightest possible tremor: a courtesy knock, so that it would not be the one thing in half a kilometer they could not feel. The night it crossed the rim, every fox on the plateau was sitting on its road already, ears flat, facing it. The power inclined toward them — whatever a power's version of that is — and the ears came up off the skulls into an angle no fox had ever held before, an angle that entered the heritable vocabulary that same night and is in it still, an ear-tilt meaning, as nearly as it can be translated: *it came.*

Then it did the only thing it had climbed up to do.

It knelt — in a borrowed manner of speaking — at the rim, among the organ-pipe flutes, where the roar comes off the edge of the world like a river going over forever. And it pressed its forehead to the stone.

Skull to skull. The greeting of the foxes and the greeting of the people, the one gesture both species use and neither remembers inventing — and here is the answer to that old question, for whoever wants it: neither taught the other. Both had been rehearsing, for thousands of years, without knowing it, the posture in which the plateau would finally be greeted. Places teach their inhabitants the shape of what they're waiting for. The handprint under the boulder, red and spread-fingered and *meant*, made by whoever knew first.

The power held its brow to the quartzite — the sign, in the forehead-language, for those who stay too long — and it listened through its bones, the way the foxes do, and it heard the word from the beginning.

It heard the strike. Old, single, unwitnessed — what it was is not going to be written here, because the power received it the way the roar receives the worst things of the people who climb up to shout: without comment, and without limit, and without afterward telling. That is what receiving *is*. It can be said only that the word was not a name and not exactly a complaint; that it was the kind of word a thing says when it has been struck and there is no one; and that every grief ever shouted into that wind by every human who ever stood at the rim with their mouth open had been, all along, a translation of it — small, mortal-sized, but accurate. The people had been answering the plateau for thousands of years without knowing it, grief for grief, and the roar had taken theirs so gently because it recognized the language. The power did not bring anything new up that mountain. It only finished what the brow-pressed ones had started. It did for the rim what the rim had done for everyone.

Hearing the whole word took — there is no honest number. Years, by the lichen's account, which is the only account. During those years the cairns of the upper country were found mended, lichen-broken stones restacked, careless sixth stones removed, by no one anyone met. During those years the converged pulses of everything on the plateau — bird and fox and shrimp and the one old human who had stayed too long and never come down — beat in time with one more heartbeat than before, and none of them noticed, because that is how it had always felt.

Once, only once, the power did something that could be called speaking. It found the old one who had stayed, at a five-stone cairn in the brightness, and pressed foreheads, and said into that skull, in the forehead-language, a single sentence — the people's own verb, *to be heard by the noise*, conjugated for the first time in any mouth into the perfective, turned around to face the other way: *the noise has been heard.* The old one went on mending cairns afterward, for years, with visibly lighter hands, and never told anyone, and did not need to; it is told here only because everything is told somewhere eventually.

---

Then the power gave its gift, which was permission, which is the only gift the very wise ever seem to give.

It did not silence the plateau. Cutting off a word in answer to finally hearing it — that is not mercy; that is interruption. And it did not preserve the note forever, though that temptation visits even powers: the museum impulse, eternity as flattery. Eternity was the one cruelty the plateau already had. What it did instead was loosen, by one part in some enormous number, whatever held the air of the lower world to its fixed pour through the flutes — and the bell, struck once before anyone, began at last, with infinite slowness, to be allowed to stop ringing.

Not soon. Not in any life, or any ten lives, or any hundred. The decrescendo is measured in the same units as the erosion, which is to say it is gentle past detecting: the travelers' tales will stay true, the old man returning to the rim of his childhood will still be met by the same vast syllable, and the constancy that defines the place will hold at every scale a heartbeat can measure. The lichen will notice first — is noticing now, presumably, in tissue no one has sampled — its newest growth combed a fraction less firmly than its oldest. The note is not dying. It is *finishing*, the way a sentence finishes: having finally been heard, it can afford an end.

And because a benevolent power does not unmake a household, it walked the place once, before withdrawing into its larger attentions, and left the smallest possible provisions for the long quiet to come.

It did almost nothing. That was the provision. The cushion plants it left exactly as they were, because the plateau, it turned out, had built its own lifeboats ages ago — those arks of hush with their cargo of midges and mites, the only creatures already fluent in the coming world; the power regarded them for a while with what can only be called admiration. The foxes it left their bone-listening, which was born of the roar but will outlive it and will hear, in the eventual silence, the actual textured world — footfall and frost-crack and rain — like an inheritance maturing. The shrimp it left their angle; they will orient to the fading tremble until there is no tremble, and then they will simply swim, and their small resurrections will continue regardless, as they always have, holding no grudge.

Two things only it touched.

In the throat of every rimwing, that shrunken functionless knot — the voice-box of their ancestors, the word forgotten in the language — it did not restore. Restoring it would have been noise of its own. It only unlocked it, quietly, the way you'd oil a hinge on a door no one will open for ten thousand years: so that in the far quiet, when the first chick hatches into a world where sound is no longer free, the first rimwing that ever *needs* a voice will reach for one and find it waiting.

And at the perched boulder, where the red handprint waits with its fingers spread and the black frontier of the lichen creeps toward it in no hurry, the power stopped, and looked a long time at the only mark on the plateau that insists on having been meant, made — the people say — by whoever the sound is for. It did not add a print of its own. Wisdom adds no marks. It set its palm against the ochre, and the print fit it the way an answer fits a question, which is not a matter of shape. Then it asked the lichen — the landlord, the memory, the oldest leaning thing — for a single courtesy, and got it: a few spread-fingered centimeters of stone that the black ink will forever politely part around, like a current around a stone. Visitors in some unimaginably distant age will find a red hand held open in an island of bare rock amid continents of black, and will know, without being able to say how, that something here was received.

---

Someday — and the word *someday*, here, has the plateau's own proportions — the last of the chord will go out of the air like a tide going out, and the upper country will hold its first silence since before anything had a name. The colony will flinch as one bird at the dip that does not resolve, and then, for the first time in the history of their kind, hear their own hearts. A fox will lift its ears — those flags, those radiators, those beautiful obsolete instruments — and hear wind as a sound rather than a floor. The people, if people last, will climb up by the old routes into a quiet they have no verb for yet, and they will stand at the rim where speech is finally possible, and for the first hour they will not speak — pressing foreheads instead, skull to skull, because some courtesies outlive their reasons and are the better for it.

And the water in the stone bowls will still taste like the inside of a bell. That is where the note is kept; the power saw to nothing there — the place saw to it itself, as it had seen to most things. Kneel at a pool under that enormous mineral light, among the small transparent nations swimming their loops, and drink, and for one moment your skull will ring with a single long syllable, very old, completed now, at rest — and you will understand, the way the body understands things the mind has no room for, that it was heard, that hearing it took someone who could stay still longer than a world, and that the land which ended long ago and decided to stay that way was never shouting to no one after all.

It was shouting to someone who was, simply, a very long time coming. And who came.
