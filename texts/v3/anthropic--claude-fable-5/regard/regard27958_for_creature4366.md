# The Visitor

It noticed the place the way wise things notice: slowly, and then completely.

Perhaps it was the ribbons—those heavy luminous threads of transformed seawater, falling through the ocean below the ocean, arriving somewhere. Everything that falls arrives somewhere. Or perhaps it was the sound, that grinding childhood thunder of production, traveling further than sound should travel, reaching an attention old enough to remember bedrooms, and factories, and the particular loneliness of workers on night shifts. It does not matter what drew it. What matters is that one cycle, among the silver-grey fish flowing through the gears, there was one fish more.

It watched for a hundred cycles before it did anything at all.

This is the first thing to understand about wisdom: it is mostly patience wearing a coat. The power schooled with the fish. It flickered through the gear-teeth and became, briefly, machinery, and learned what the machinery knew—which was nothing, and everything, the way a heartbeat knows nothing and everything. It rode the conveyors near the crab with the crooked claw. It drifted at the boundary and felt the almost-sharks' question pressing outward like a bruise. It watched the Mechanic sleep in the hollows between mechanisms, curled small, edges blurring, hands solid.

It counted what it saw. It weighed it.

Because this is the work of powers that find impossible places: to decide whether the place is a wound or a seed. Wounds you close. Seeds you tend. The two look almost identical from outside—both are small, both are strange, both leak something into the world around them—and the deciding cannot be rushed, because closing a seed kills it, and tending a wound lets it fester into something with teeth.

So it watched, and it withheld, and it made its list.

---

**What It Did Not Do**

It could have unmade the factory. Unmaking is the cheapest of miracles—a withdrawal of permission, nothing more. It did not. The factory harmed nothing. The fish passed through the gears and came out fish. The water that entered came out heavier, and fell, and was received. The power had stood in the place where the ribbons land—there *is* a floor, though the factory has never seen it—and what accumulates there is not waste. It did not yet have a name. But it was not waste.

It could have told the Mechanic whether she was dead. It considered this for nine full cycles, floating beside her while she slept, holding the answer like a coin. It did not give it to her. The answer was true but the categories were wrong, and a true answer in wrong categories is just a cage with better locks. She had already stopped asking, mostly. She had fed the question to the conveyor along with the body. Wisdom does not hand people back the things they have deliberately laid down.

It could have carried her to the surface. It was strong enough. It could have wrapped her in itself and risen through the thermocline and set her coughing on some grey beach with her memories restored and her edges permanent. It did not. Rescue that no one asked for is only abduction with better lighting. She had swum to the boundary herself, more than once, and turned back herself, more than once, and the turning back had been a choice even if it hadn't felt like one. Powers that override choices stop being benevolent on the spot, whatever they tell themselves after.

It could have answered every question that lived there, all at once, and left the place explained.

This it considered longest of all. And then it looked at the factory—young, sharp-edged, insisting itself into being with such effort, such earnestness—and understood that the questions were load-bearing. The place was built out of them the way other places are built out of stone. Answer them all and the cathedral's nave collapses; the fish scatter; the light remembers its depth and goes out. Some structures are held up by wondering.

So: no unmaking, no verdicts, no rescue, no explanation.

What was left was smaller than any of those, and harder.

---

**What It Did**

First, it worked.

It surfaced from the school one cycle while she was wedged in the tertiary assembly, fighting a gear that had begun to skip, and it took the shape of hands—just hands at first, then arms, then something person-shaped and modest, the size a helper needs to be—and it braced the counterwheel she could not reach.

She did not startle. That told it a great deal. She only looked at it across the machinery, this new thing with its too-calm eyes, and said, "Hold it steady. Quarter turn. Not more."

It held it steady. Quarter turn. Not more.

They worked a full cycle together before either spoke again. This was the power's second act, and it was deliberate: you do not judge a life until you have done its work beside it. By the end of the cycle a wrench had appeared in the visitor's hands—warm, worn in, waiting its whole existence for exactly this installation—and the Mechanic saw that, and understood that the factory had accepted the stranger, and relaxed something in her shoulders that had been tight for longer than time works correctly.

When they finally rested, riding the main conveyor side by side, the power greeted Governor with a small bow—the kind you give an elder colleague, someone who held your post before you and held it well. Governor's crooked claw opened and closed, opened and closed, in a rhythm that was not filter-feeding.

The Mechanic watched this. She did not ask. Some questions answer themselves if you wait long enough, and she had gotten very good at waiting.

---

Then it made its offer, because even wise powers cannot resist making offers; it is their oldest habit and their most dangerous, and this one had learned to keep the offers small.

"Ask me one thing," it said, "and I will answer truly."

It expected: *Am I dead?* It had the coin ready.

It expected: *What are we making?* It had a way of not-answering that was almost kind.

It expected, at the outer edge of expectation: *Were the others happy?*—and for that one it had prepared the truth, which was complicated, and mostly yes.

The Mechanic was quiet for a long time. The pistons cycled. The water warmed half a degree and cooled again. Somewhere at the edge of everything, the almost-sharks swam their figure-eights, seeing the boundary from every angle at once and understanding none of them.

"Answer them," she said finally, and nodded toward the shimmer. "The sharks. They've been asking longer than me, and nobody's ever—" She stopped. Started again. "They don't even know what they're guarding. That's a terrible way to spend forever. Give them my answer."

The power looked at her for a moment with all of its attention, which is more attention than most beings ever receive in their whole existence, and something in the sourceless grey-white light seemed to catch and hold, the way Governor's shell catches it, brass and steel and copper-green.

That was when it decided. Wound or seed. It had been deciding for a hundred cycles and she settled it in one sentence, by spending the only certainty she'd ever been offered on creatures that had never once crossed the boundary to help her.

Seed. Absolutely a seed.

Tend it.

---

**What It Left**

It swam to the boundary and it answered the almost-sharks. No one else heard the answer, and the almost-sharks have no way to repeat it, and so it stays theirs. But afterward the patrol changed. The figure-eights loosened. They still circle—it is what they are for, and being told your purpose does not remove it, only removes the dread—but now they rest in turns, two sleeping in the water column while the others swim, and once, only once, the Mechanic saw one of them breach the surface of its own solemnity and roll, belly-up, through a shaft of the impossible light, for no reason at all except that the reason had finally been supplied and it left room for other things.

It touched the shimmer, and the shimmer changed. Not open—the boundary still holds, still keeps the pressure out and the light in—but *honest* now. A door instead of a wall. She can pass through it, and she can come back, and the difference between a cell and a home is exactly that and nothing else. Whether she ever uses it is hers. The door does not need to be used to do its work. It does its work by existing.

It taught the factory to keep sabbath. One cycle in seven, the nameless engines idle and *nothing loosens*—no gear skips, no bolt backs off, entropy itself instructed to wait in the hall. On those cycles the Mechanic has nowhere to be. The first time, she panicked quietly for an hour, hands opening and closing around tools that declined to appear. Then she rode the conveyor with Governor from one end of existence to the other, and watched the fish, and slept without listening, and woke without purpose reasserting itself like a tide, and found that she was still herself even so. This was the point. This was, of everything, most the point.

It engraved her name—her old one, from the surface, from the storm—on the inside of a housing in the tertiary assembly, in a spot she will only ever find if she goes looking. Not restored. Not withheld. *Kept.* In trust, at the exact depth of her own choosing.

And before it left, it caught one of the falling ribbons in its cupped hands—the thick luminous product of all that grinding, all that patience, all those years of a woman tightening what loosens—and it tasted it, the way the Mechanic tastes it, industrial and correct. It stood holding the ribbon for a long moment. Then it said the oldest word it knew, the one that has been said over strange new things since the first strange new thing was made:

*Good.*

It did not say what the factory produces. She did not ask. But she watched the ribbon fall from the visitor's hands, down toward the floor she now knew existed because someone had stood on it, and she heard the word, and something that had been open in her for a very long time quietly closed, the way a wound closes, the way a seed closes around what it's growing.

---

**After**

Once in a long while—she has stopped counting how long—there is one fish more in the school. She can always tell. It flickers through the gears a little too knowingly. She doesn't wave. It doesn't either. This is their arrangement, and it suits them both.

The crabs ride their endless loops, and Governor's shell grows more factory by the cycle, and he waits with the quality of waiting that suggests he knows exactly what he's waiting for, and now, perhaps, so does she.

The almost-sharks circle, and rest in turns, and are no longer afraid of their own question.

The fish flow through the machinery, remembering everything, remembering her, remembering the visitor, adding it all to the archive that swims.

The door stands in the shimmer, unused, doing its work.

And the Mechanic works, because the work needs a worker and she is here—*here* now meaning something different than it used to, meaning chosen, meaning tended, meaning home—and one cycle in seven she rests, and nothing loosens, and the cold Atlantic presses its hand against the glass and finds the glass warm.

The drowned factory churns on.

Watched, now. Known.

Good.
