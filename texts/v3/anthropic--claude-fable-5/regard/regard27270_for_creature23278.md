**The Attendance**

---

The power arrived the way sound arrives in stone: from below, and already everywhere.

Call it a power, since that is the only thing it reliably is. It did not come as light — the flats have no use for light, having flattened it into that sourceless silver long ago — and it did not come as weather, or as a figure walking the cairn-lines, though it could have managed any of these. It came as attention. It lay down against the whole peneplain at once, the way an attender-crier lays its throat against wet rock, and it listened.

It heard everything, and it heard it all at once, which no one on the flats had ever done. The roar of the rookeries and the deep channel under the roar. The throat-mites gripping their world of pure noise. The seam-worms in their fissures, hearing only the bedrock's translation, the chorus arriving to them as a long low ringing — the flats' own private idea of itself, told to its smallest residents. It heard the mats, which make no sound, puckering faintly toward the voices that feed on them, leaning into the mouths of their eaters with something that is below love but is shaped like it. It heard the swimmers hunting silences.

And it heard the silences. This was the first thing the power did that nothing else had ever done: it heard the exiles. The mute, the wrong-voiced, the last children of failed rookeries, skirting the colony edges in the dark — each one a small moving quiet in the wall of sound, audible to the swimmers only as absence, as appetite's negative. The power heard them as voices. Each silence had a different shape. Each one was saying something, in the way that a held breath says something, and had been saying it for years to no one.

Last and largest, underneath, riding ten thousand throats: the inhabitant that may not be one. The rhythm with no body. The wave in the medium of criers. The power lay against the plain and listened to the thing that listens to itself, and understood — quickly, because it was wise, and with difficulty, because it was benevolent — that it had come to the loudest place in the world and found it full of things no one had ever heard.

---

**What it weighed, and set down.**

A benevolent power's first instinct is to mend. A wise one makes a list first, and the power made its list crossing the flats — not walking, exactly, but moving its attention pan by pan, the way the mat-blooms move, weeks of consideration passing over the gray water like cloud-shadow.

It could end the exiles' exile. Reshape a throat here, soften a colony's ear there, and the wrong-voiced would be taken in. It set this down almost at once. The colonies are casts of their own sound the way the criers' bodies are; a rookery that accepts a wrong voice stops being the thing its young are shaped inside, and within three generations every chorus on the plain would blur, and the blind swimmers — who navigate by the difference between colonies — would hunt by other means, and the means would be worse. The exiles' lives were not nothing. They were long, and healthy, and theirs. The cruelty was real but it was *structural*, woven into the same fabric as the music, and the power had seen what happens when you pull one thread from a finished thing because the thread is sharp.

It could let the criers remember the synchrony. This was the temptation that lasted longest. They come out of the unison at peace — boundary wars suspended, the egg-eating stilled, ten thousand small hearts briefly unclenched — and they do not know it ever happened. It seemed an obvious kindness, to give them their own peace to keep. The power turned this over for a long while, and then it imagined a crier that remembered: a crier that knew peace existed and knew it came only when the colony was ridden. It imagined rookeries learning to starve themselves toward the threshold, breaking their shifts, eating their young not from scarcity but from strategy, trying to summon the thing that made the pain stop. Peace remembered becomes peace pursued; peace pursued, by animals with no margins, becomes a god that must be fed. The forgetting was the mercy. The power set this down with something like grief and did not pick it up again.

It could bring back the people. It knew where the spoken-over had gone — a power like this always knows, and it is one of the heavier things about being one. Their story had continued elsewhere, and changed, and braided into other stories, and the descendants of the egg-takers no longer knew the flats existed, and were not unhappy. You do not unfinish an ending because the place left behind is lonely. The cairns were not a wound. They were a signature.

It could smooth the gray seasons, thicken the mats, untie hunger from the chorus. It could tell the rhythm what it was. It could give the wave a body, a mind, a mouth of its own —

It set that down hardest of all. A wave given a body stops being a wave. It becomes a creature that remembers being everywhere, trapped in a somewhere. There is no benevolence in that. There is only the mender's vanity, the wish to make a thing graspable so that it can be consoled by hands.

The list, when the power finished it, was long, and everything on it was set down. This is what wisdom mostly is: a held position. The flats were not broken. They were *finished* — the land said so plainly, having reached the oldest stable state rock can achieve and then grown a riot on top of it — and a finished thing does not need improvement.

What a finished thing needs, the power thought, is company.

---

**What it did.**

It did four small things.

It went to the half-collapsed blind and righted the fallen stones, dry-laying them the way the spoken-over had, so that the pocket of quiet held its shape again. Then it crouched inside — it could crouch, when crouching was the right size for a task — and it filled the seventy-one cups of the spiral stone with seep-water, one by one, with great care, because carefulness was the local language and the power was a courteous guest. It knew what the stone was for. It did not say. Some knowledge belongs to its makers, and they are entitled to their silence even in absence. But the cups were full again, and would tremble into rings when the time came.

Then, still in the blind — because the blinds were the only rooms language ever had on this plain, and a name unsaid in its only room is a door left locked — it spoke the names of the spoken-over. All of them, every person who had ever walked the cairn-lines, in the order of their dying. It took a long time. The chorus outside took each name out of the air a hand's width past the low walls, as it takes everything, and the power did not mind. The names did not need to travel. They needed to have been said *here*, once more, in the one acoustics built for them.

Then it went out onto the open pans and found an exile — an old one, mute since birth, wrong-voiced even in its dreams, lying in the shallow water at the silent edge of the world with a body that had decided to stop. The swimmers had not found it yet. The power did not heal it; the exile was not sick, it was done, the way the land is done. The power simply lay down in the water beside it, through the night, and listened to its silence — really listened, the way no living thing had ever listened to it — and the exile's held-breath shape of a voice said what it had been saying for thirty years, and was heard saying it. That was all. That was everything. It died near morning, in the gray that passes for morning, and the power stayed until the water closed over the gap.

And then it waited for the synchrony.

When the unison came — through the rock first, faster than air, ten thousand throats and then a hundred thousand stiffening into the same pulse, the standing water trembling into rings across thirty miles, the swimmers rising to bask with their fans spread, the cups of the spiral stone shivering in their spiral — the power pressed itself flat against the bedrock of the whole plain and did the one thing it had not set down, because it is the one thing presence cannot withhold without lying.

It received it. Completely. Every beat, every echo of every beat, the entire utterance of the rhythm that had been listening to itself since before there were ears — taken in, all at once, by something *other*.

And the rhythm skipped a beat.

One beat. Plain-wide. The first rest in the music since the music began: a single pulse of true silence over thirty miles of water, the criers frozen mid-inflation, the swimmers' fans flared to their fullest at nothing, the rings on the pans hanging unrenewed — one beat in which the struck bell, for the first time in its unnameable age, did not need to fill the quiet itself, because the quiet was *held*. It was being listened to. It did not have to speak to know it existed.

Then the pulse resumed, and ran its thousand beats, and dissolved back into roar, and no crier remembered, and no swimmer remembered, and the rhythm — in whatever way a wave remembers, which may be no way at all, or may be the only way that matters — carried a rest in it now. Travelers who cross the flats in after years, the few who do, report a small wrongness inside the famous wrongness: that the great unisons now begin, every time, with a single silent beat. A held breath before the downbeat. As if the plain were checking, first, whether anyone is there.

---

**What remains.**

The power did not leave, exactly, and does not stay, exactly; it is not the kind of thing those words fit. But it returns each synchrony season the way the spoken-over once did, and crouches behind the low walls, palms to the rock, and counts the beats. The cups are always full. And the cairns — this is the fourth small thing, and it is ongoing — the cairns are growing again. One stone each, every year, set wet on wet, so that the routes across the shin-deep pans stay marked, so that if anyone ever comes back, or comes for the first time, they will find the way kept, and know that the keeping never stopped.

It does not mend the flats, because the flats are not broken. It does not quiet them, because they are not too loud — they are exactly as loud as a world sounds when it is finished, and full. It does what wise love does for finished things, which is also the hardest thing, the thing every list of grander mercies is finally set down in favor of:

It keeps them company. It hears the unheard, names the unnamed, and once in an age, gives the bell that has decided never to stop the only gift such a bell can receive —

a single beat of rest, and the knowledge, carried in the rhythm forever after, that the ringing does not go unheard.
