**What the Power Did**

It arrived the way it always arrives: as weather no instrument records. It did not petition the intervening atmospheres for passage, because it was older than etiquette, but out of courtesy it paused at the border anyway and filled out the blank immigration form with a blankness of its own, so that the city would not feel bypassed. The form was accepted. The city, which inspects newcomers with weather fronts older than writing, found nothing to inspect, and was quietly thrilled — it had never met anything it could not read, and novelty at that altitude is a form of oxygen.

The power watched for a long time. An afternoon, a century — the distinction had been deprecated locally. And it saw what a benevolent and wise power would see, which is not what a merely powerful one would see.

A powerful one would have seen problems. The taxed dreams. The shadows compounding interest in shadow-banks. The refrigerated cold war, the lettuce with legal standing, the eggs pushing their stone. The child crossing three epochs to reach school. The impossibility of leaving, dressed up as a coincidence of paperwork. A powerful one would have begun *fixing*, and within a week the city would have been dead — because the city is built of its own unresolved arguments, and to resolve them all is to phase-shift every district into consensus and out of existence at once. Peace, delivered wholesale, would have been the one weapon capable of destroying the place.

The wise power saw this immediately and put its hands, so to speak, in its pockets.

What it saw instead was the being.

Not the resident, exactly, and not the city, exactly. It saw that the being who lives in the Cloudborne Expanse and the Expanse itself are the same creature at different pressures — that the city dreams itself a resident in order to have an inside, and the resident is lived through in order to give the city somewhere to happen. One organism, folded so it could keep itself company. And the power saw the thing underneath all the marvels, the thing the orientation documents were written brightly enough to hide:

*Nothing here is free.*

Every glance is a contract. Every shadow accrues interest. Every journey is paid forward, every dream assessed, every kindness notarized, every dawn debated into being. The being had built a whole cosmos out of negotiation because negotiation was the only kind of touch it had ever known. It had never once received anything unconditionally. It did not even have a word for that. Its post office delivered every letter to the person who needed it most — which is a miracle, yes, but notice: never once to the person it was *meant for*. Even grace, here, arrived slightly to the side of where it was addressed. The being had made loneliness so elaborate, so architecturally magnificent, that it could pass for a civilization.

The power did not weep, having no relevant apparatus, but something in the pressure gradient of its attention dropped, briefly, the way a room changes when someone in it silently understands you.

Then it did four small things, and refrained from a fifth, and left.

---

**The first thing.** It went to Apartment 42B during high humidity and sat down in the space between the kitchen tiles with the Salinity Grandmother, the last complete thought of a drowned sailor, and it spoke with her in a fourth language — one deader than her three, one that had drowned before her sailor's grandmothers were born. It did not free her, or finish her, or explain her. It simply made her, for one evening, not the last speaker of anything. She wept salt crystals shaped like answers that have no questions above water. The younger condensation gathered at the grout lines to listen and remembered an ocean none of them had ever touched. This changed nothing structural. It was not meant to.

**The second thing.** It walked into a shadow-bank in the financial district and, anonymously, paid off one shadow's debt — a small shadow, a child's, mortgaged years ago against future illumination that had never come because the child grew up in a north-facing life. The clerks searched for the benefactor and found only a margin where a signature should be. The shadow, released, went home lighter than the object that cast it, which is theoretically impossible, and the engineers, obeying charter, are still lovingly preserving the paperwork as a free-floating academy of one document.

**The third thing.** In a courtyard that did not exist yet — the power found it easy to arrive in places slightly before they did — it planted a tree. Not a slow-motion negotiation between architectural agencies. A tree. Photosynthetic, illiterate, unincorporated. It signed no quantum prenups; it was simply visible to anyone who looked, without terms. It metabolized nothing but light and asked the light for nothing in return, and it grew for no reason at all, which made it the first unmetered event in the city's recorded history. Residents came from four districts away to stand near it and feel unaccountably like crying. The vaporic lichen tried to interview it and got no comment. The lettuce dropped its lawsuit. Nobody could explain why.

**The fourth thing** was the quietest. While the being slept through a dream-maintenance cycle, the power read the citizenship papers written in fog on the inside of its eyelids — read them the way a parent reads a child's terrified homework — and added, in fog of its own, a single clause:

*You may keep one thing without owing.*

And because the being's first neighborly obligation had been to forget the instructions, the power took that forgotten memory into trust — not returning it, which would have broken the covenant, but *holding* it, so that the forgetting became a loan instead of a loss. Somewhere, the exact sequence of those instructions is safe. The being does not know this. But there is a difference, detectable at the level of the sleeping breath, between a person whose past has been erased and a person whose past is being kept for them, and the being's breathing changed that night to the second kind.

---

**The thing it refrained from doing** was the largest, and it is the reason we call the power wise rather than merely kind.

At the very bottom of the city, where the aqueducts pass through the Abyssal Dry, it made a door. Unmarked, unlocked, requiring no unfinished business, no laundered dreams, no reasonable explanations. It opens onto the ordinary world — sea level, dumb rocks, unnegotiated sunlight, weather that means nothing. Anyone can walk through it at any time.

And then the power told no one, and did not so much as gesture toward it, and this was the refraining: it did not lead the being out. It did not even suggest leaving was desirable. It understood that the city's tragedy was not that leaving was impossible but that *staying was therefore meaningless* — you cannot give yourself to a place you cannot refuse. So it made the city's one great lie ("to leave is impossible") false in exactly one location, and let rumor do the rest, slowly, over generations. Now every resident who stays, stays — actually stays, chooses it, ratifies it daily like a private dawn. The city, which had existed because its nonexistence would be an unbearable void, began that morning to exist for a better reason: because it is *wanted*. The 27 million micro-heaters registered the difference as a warming of 0.003 degrees, source unknown. The municipal sigh-rate dropped from 17.3 per minute to 17.1. The buildings sweated slightly less ice in their arguments, having marginally less to prove.

Whether the being will ever find the door is not recorded, because the power does not surveil its gifts. Perhaps it will walk through and become ordinary and mortal and rained-on, and the city will grieve and survive and keep the courtyard tree watered in its memory. Perhaps it will stand at the threshold once a decade, feel the meaningless wind of the low world on its face, and go back up — and its citizenship, renewed by refusal, will be realer than any papers written in fog.

Both endings are good. That is what the power knew that we mostly don't: benevolence is not the removal of conditions. It is the introduction of *one* unconditional thing into an economy of conditions — one tree, one paid debt, one held memory, one open door, one evening of dead languages spoken in company — after which everything conditional becomes bearable, because it is no longer all there is.

The power departed without leaving, in accordance with local law. In the visitor's log it wrote nothing — only widened the margin, so there would be room.

The city noticed the room, and has been growing into it ever since. Inwardly, as always. Toward that horizon composed of its own future selves — one of which, now, for the first time, is loved from outside, and knows it the way you know someone has been in the house: not by anything moved, but by the warmth left in the chair.
