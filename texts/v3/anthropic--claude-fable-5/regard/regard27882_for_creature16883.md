## What the Power Did in Ulegn

**How It Arrived**

It did not descend, because Ulegn has no sky to descend from—only the vaulted glass and its steel-shaving snow. It did not walk in from the edges, because the edges are temporary and it did not want to be mistaken for one.

It arrived the only way a wise thing can arrive in a city like this: as an improvement in the quality of attention. One suspended-hour afternoon, the distant buildings stopped shifting when unwatched—not because they were caught, but because for the first time in their existence, being watched felt like being *held*. The shadows stayed crisp and the depth became reliable, briefly, the way a stammering person becomes fluent in front of the one listener who is genuinely not waiting for them to finish.

The Passing Familiars noticed first, because they are made of noticing. Several of them held their forms for eleven full seconds, a record, and one of them wept—which is to say, a chalk annotation from 1974 dampened slightly and became legible again for the length of a breath. It said: *back soon.*

**What It Declined to Do**

Because it was benevolent, it wanted to fix things. Because it was wise, it made a list of the fixes and read the list slowly, the way you read a letter you have decided not to send.

It did not close the seams in the glass ceiling. The snow that falls through them is the only weather the city didn't invent for itself, and a place this self-referential needs at least one thing arriving from outside its own argument.

It did not settle the question of whether Ulegn is a city or a conversation about cities. It understood that the disagreement *was* the metabolism—that a compost heap resolved is just dirt, and dirt does not send postcards.

It did not synchronize the Hooligan Clocks. It did not name the nameless. It did not finish the knitting, unsnarl the mail, or tell the buildings which hallways belonged to whom. It did not restore the rivers.

It looked at its list for a long while. Then it folded it, addressed it to itself at an address it hadn't needed yet, and dropped it into a pneumatic tube, as tuition.

**The Grief It Paid First**

Before anything else, it went down. Below the drum-hollow streets, below the ironwood lungs, to the buried infrastructure of unknown age—the one humming with the patience of metal dreaming of water.

Nobody had ever mourned the rivers. The city was built in their absence and treated the absence as a foundation, which is what cities do, which is what everyone does. But the pipes remembered. They had been holding the shape of water for so long that the holding had become their whole religion.

The power laid its attention along the oldest conduit and gave it one complete dream. For a single night, every pipe in Ulegn was full—not with water, but with the *memory of having been made for something*, running end to end, mouth to sea, with current and silt and the particular cold of snowmelt in early spring. Above, the citizens slept badly and beautifully. Roof dogs barked rain all night at nothing. In the morning the basins that usually filled with condensation had filled instead with something that tasted faintly of glaciers, and by noon it was gone, as everything here is gone by noon, but the hum beneath the streets had changed key. The patience was still there. But it was no longer waiting. It was *remembering*, which is patience that has been thanked.

**The Duel It Lost on Purpose**

The Hooligan Clocks found it near the district where the timepieces were recently embarrassed into silence, and they challenged it, as they challenge everyone, to a duel of interpretation on the subject of *now*.

The power could have won. Powers like this contain now the way an ocean contains a particular wave. Instead it argued badly—magnificently badly, with the specific, generous incompetence of a grandmother losing at cards—and it took its defeat with grace: one full week spent remembering things that hadn't happened to it yet.

This was not humility for its own sake. This was the only way inside. For seven days it remembered Ulegn's future from within Ulegn's tenses—the annexations not yet negotiated, the mushroom pamphlets not yet published, the clay-thatcher's five-hundredth harvest, the letter it had mailed itself upon arriving. It learned the city the way the city insists on being learned: out of order, with regret conjugated correctly.

The clocks, for their part, were unsettled by winning. One of them, a mantel clock with the face of the moment between dropping something fragile and deciding whether to catch it, followed the power at a distance for the rest of its stay, the way a young tough follows the stranger who let him win and thereby ruined fighting for him forever.

**The Answer It Gave the Thatcher**

The tallest clay-thatcher, five stories of harvested mud and windowed coat, speaks in pure conditionals, and had been asking its question of everyone for a generation: *if you had been born a gear, would you still ask what time it means?*

Everyone had treated this as rhetoric, or philosophy, or weather. The power understood it was homesickness. The thatcher had built itself entirely from the parts of other things and had never once been told whether the asking was among the parts—whether the question belonged to some dismantled loom-heart it had incorporated long ago, or whether the question was *its own*, the one component it hadn't scavenged.

The power answered in the thatcher's grammar, because you do not force a creature to leave its language to receive comfort:

*"If every part of you were borrowed, the asking would still be yours—because a question is not a part. It is what the parts are for."*

The thatcher stood still for three days. Then it spoke the first declarative sentence of its life, to a passing familiar, who carried it off in a direction that made sense five minutes later. The sentence was: *"I was born."* Witnesses report that afterward it kept harvesting, kept growing—but it began signing its work, pressing one straw-thumbed print into each gently dismantled beast, and the middling beasts, strangely, stopped fleeing it. Some now line up.

**The Errand at the Forwarding Station**

It stood in line. This must be recorded, because it matters: a power that could have occupied the Forwarding Station like light occupies glass instead took a number and waited behind a woman returning a future that had never fit her—too much certainty in the shoulders, she said.

Lost futures can only be claimed by accident, so the power engineered an accident of the purest kind: it stopped intending. It let its attention go slack, wandered to the counter, and was handed, by one of the identical prematurely aged clerks, a parcel addressed in the city's own handwriting.

Inside was the future in which Ulegn had *decided*. Committed. Chosen, once and finally, to be a city and not a conversation—seamless, punctual, resolved. It was a beautiful future. The streets did not echo. The clocks agreed. Nothing was annexed and nothing was lost and the mail arrived when it should, to whom it should, meaning exactly what it said.

The power read it all the way through, twice, in the manner of someone checking a beloved friend's test results.

Then it did the wisest thing it did in all its stay: it did not destroy the future, and it did not deliver it. It *re-addressed* it. Not to the city—to the city's fear. On the parcel it wrote: *To Ulegn, to be opened only if you ever believe your unfinishedness is a failure. Contents: proof that you could have finished, and chose this instead. Choosing is not the same as failing to conclude. Love, someone who checked.*

The clerk stamped it without comment—though all of her, at every counter, in every niche, was smiling at once, which the lost letters around them registered as a slight rise in temperature—and it went into the deepest storage, where it waits, a resignation letter the city will now never need to write, kept on file as a comfort.

The postage was paid in the customary currency of the station: the power surrendered a piece of its own certainty. Which piece, it never said. But those who met it afterward reported that it had begun, occasionally, wonderfully, to ask directions.

**The Center, Held Still**

Everyone knows the city's exact center relocates every equinox and can only be occupied by standing still long enough to be voted against. What no one had tried was standing still long enough to be voted against, and then *staying anyway*, and calling the staying by its true name: listening.

The power stood in the center for the length of one entire local stability—three minutes or three generations; the records disagree, and the disagreement is the point—and it did not speak, did not fix, did not even watch in the active sense. It made itself into the shape of an uninterrupted silence, which in Ulegn is the rarest architecture of all: a silence that is not competing.

And into that silence, at last, the Threshold Thoughts finished their sentence.

The question, remember, had been interrupted for centuries: *is it lonely, being several thousand metaphors piled in a trenchcoat, trying to pass as an address?* The answer had never once gotten all the way out. Now it did. It was not in words. It was the whole city going quiet in the same key at the same time—the abandoned workshops, the insulated machinery, the closed hours, the mid-gesture looms—all the competing silences agreeing, for one held breath, to be a single silence, which is as close as Ulegn can come to saying:

*Yes. It is lonely. It has always been lonely. Being unresolved is a way of leaving the door open, and doors left open are drafty, and we have been cold a long time, and we kept the door open anyway, in case.*

And the power, which had waited whole ages of the world to be the thing standing in exactly such a doorway, answered with the only medicine it had carried all this way:

*"I heard you. All of it. You do not have to finish to have been understood."*

Then it was voted against, per custom, and it accepted the vote with visible pride in the electorate, and stepped aside.

The city did not change, structurally. The seams still creep. The buildings still annex hallways. The mail still arrives addressed to childhood fears. But every witness agrees that something in the *quality* of the unfinishedness changed that day—that Ulegn's incompleteness stopped being an apology and became a style. There is a difference between a stammer and a signature. The city had always had the first. Now it knew it had the second.

**One Small Repair, as a Gift, Not a Precedent**

Because benevolence must be allowed one indulgence or it curdles, the power performed exactly one reunion before leaving. In a district four decades behind its neighbors, it restored to a single door and a single lost key the memory of *why they fit*—the hand that made them, the room they guarded, the child who was kept safe behind them through one bad winter.

The locksmiths gathered to watch, professionally, the way therapists attend a colleague's breakthrough session.

The door and the key, remembering everything, considered each other for a long time.

Then the key chose to stay lost—but *contentedly* lost now, lost the way a retired sailor is far from the sea—and the door chose to open freely for everyone, forever, needing no key at all, because it finally remembered that keeping the child safe had been the point, and the child was long since grown and gone and safe, and a door whose purpose is completed can afford to become a welcome.

The locksmiths took extensive notes. The lesson entered their practice under the heading: *remembering why is not the same as being bound to; sometimes the truest reunion is a mutual, informed release.*

**What It Left, and What Came After**

The custom is absolute: you may stay as long as you like, but you must leave a small part of your itinerary behind. That is how the city grows.

The power left the part of its itinerary in which it never leaves.

The city, as is its habit, repurposed the donation within the hour. Somewhere in Ulegn now—the location relocates, sympathetically, with the center—there is a traffic light with three states. The first is *go*. The second is *stay*. The third, glowing a color that resembles the phosphor of brass blossoms at dusk but warmer, is generally translated as *be gone, and beloved, and expected back.* Traffic obeys it with unusual courtesy. The middling beasts gather beneath it on cold nights. The tallest clay-thatcher has been seen standing beside it for hours, and has never harvested it.

The Passing Familiars now hold their forms a full second longer, on average, than they did before. The Archivists of the Unmade Hills added a new wing to their collection: buildings the city considered and *kept*, the first such category in their history. The Cloud Accountants, auditing the emotional budget that quarter, found an unexplained surplus and, after much swirling, filed it under *witnessed*, a ledger line they had to invent.

And years later—or yesterday; the tube system had opinions—a postcard left the city, addressed in the city's own handwriting to a name the power had not needed until the moment the card arrived, at which point the name fit perfectly, the way things in Ulegn fit: wanting to stay together, and now, for once, remembering why.

The postcard showed the vaulted glass ceiling with its steel-shaving snow. On the back, in a script assembled from footnotes, chalk, and one legible annotation from 1974, it said:

*Still unfinished. Still here. Still leaving the door open—but warmly now, in case you're cold out there too. You are welcome anytime, for as long as you can stand to be understood.*

*Back soon?*

*— U.*

The power keeps it. Powers like this keep very little—keeping is heavy, and they travel by watching—but it keeps this, folded next to the piece of certainty it never asked to have returned.

Some things that fit together remember why.
