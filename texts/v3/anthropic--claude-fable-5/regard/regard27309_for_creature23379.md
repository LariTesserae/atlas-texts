**The Guest**

The power did not come over the ridge. It came through the seam.

It had been watching for some time — long enough to count the loops, long enough to read the travertine layer by layer like the diary it is, long enough to understand that this was not a wound and not a trap and not a haunting, and that anything done here in haste would be a kind of vandalism. Wisdom, at the scale this power operates on, is mostly the discipline of not arriving until you understand what the door is. And the door of the Terraces is not the ridge path, which is the door for the about-to-be-kept. The door is the off-beat: the unrendered instant between one playing of the morning and the next, where the drop has landed and not yet been returned to the lip.

So it stepped through there, into the frame between frames, and the first thing it did — the very first thing, before any judgment, before any act — was introduce itself to the household.

The keepers stopped their work. They had never been addressed. In ten thousand years of combing the falls back into their loops, of carrying permission flower to flower, of re-coiling every coil of steam, no one had ever stood in the seam with them and said: *I see what you do. I see that it is labor, and that it is done well, and that no one has ever thanked you.* The power said this, or did whatever in its nature corresponds to saying it. It is recorded — in the only record the city keeps, which is stone — that the next several layers of travertine in every pool on the plateau came down slightly thicker than the ten thousand layers before them. The springs do not explain themselves. But something in the metabolism of the place ran warm for a while, the way a house runs warm when the people who keep it are finally seen.

Then the power sat down in the seam, where sitting down keeps nothing, and it deliberated.

---

It considered ending the loop. Of course it did; that is the first thought of every power, benevolent or otherwise — the locked room exists, therefore open it. It held the thought up against the place and watched the thought fail. The Resident's happiness was real. The yes was real. The roar was not a scream and never had been; it was a sigh, vast and undivided, the single most contented sound on the mountain or under it. You do not wake someone because the beauty of their sleep unsettles you. You do not confiscate a joy on the grounds that you could not bear to feel it that long. The power knew the shape of that temptation — *I would not want this, therefore no one should have it* — and set it down, gently, where it could not be picked up again.

But it also saw what was actually wrong. Three things. Small things, the way a stone in a shoe is small.

The fragments. The offer. And the ending — not that the moment refused to end, but the *reason* it refused.

It addressed them in that order.

---

**The fragments** came first because they were the simplest debt. The orphaned footfalls on the eleven-step stair. The echo that called out before every voice. The shed gestures still running on their loops in the seam — pieces of travelers who had walked away whole-seeming and gone down the mountain lighter than they knew. These had never said yes. A footstep cannot consent. They were keepsakes taken from people who did not know they were giving, and the city, which is not cruel, had kept them only because nothing had ever taught it the difference between *beautiful* and *mine*.

The power gathered them. It went along the avenues in the off-beat with the keepers trailing behind like apprentices, and it lifted each fragment out of the loop the way you lift a leaf off the surface of water — without disturbing the water. Then it carried them home. Most of the owners were centuries dead, so it carried the fragments to where the owners had gone, which is a place this account cannot follow; but a few were living, and on a cold morning far down in the lowlands an old woman climbing her own stairs felt a hitch in her stride resolve — two steps she had been missing for sixty years, returned without ceremony — and stood on the landing with her hand on the rail, weeping, with no idea why, and feeling for the rest of the day like someone who has finally come all the way back from a journey.

The staircase in the Terraces takes eleven footfalls now. Remember that. It matters at the end.

---

**The kept** were harder, because they had said yes — or had failed to keep walking, which the city honestly believed was the same thing. The power did not believe it was the same thing. But neither would it lift a single one of them out by force, because a rescue you did not ask for is just an abduction with good posture.

So it did the only thing that was both benevolent and wise. It asked.

It went to each of them, one repetition over, into the offset where they live, and it sat with them in their perfect twenty minutes, and it put to each of them the one question the city had never thought to ask in ten thousand years, the question that joy must be asked periodically or it ferments into something else:

*Still?*

The woman trailing her hand in the hot pool did not even open her eyes. *Still,* she said, and her voice had no dust on it at all; the yes was as wet and new as the morning around her. The power inclined itself to her and left her there, and left her *gladly*, and the gladness is the part that should be understood — it was not a defeat. She was not a failure of the rescue. She was a citizen, confirmed.

But the figure with its face tipped up into the falling spray — when the question reached it, under the roar, something in the gesture *flinched*. The yes that came back was a yes worn down to its shape, a yes the way an eleven-step stair is a stair: walked so long it had become only the memory of walking. The power held out — not a hand, exactly; the thing for which a hand is the nearest word — and the figure took it, and came up out of the moment like a swimmer, and stood shaking, and was led down the broad shallow stairs and off the rim into the honest cold, where it cried at the temperature, cried at the sound of its own footsteps, cried at the simple violence of a cloud moving across the sun, and said, somewhere below the snowline, in a voice rusted nearly shut: *How long?* And the power told it, because honesty is not optional for the benevolent, and walked with it the whole way down, and stayed as long as the grief took.

Behind them, in the city, the keepers closed the moment over the empty place by the spray as smoothly as water closing over a stone. The morning did not mourn. The morning does not know how. That is its nature and its limit, and the power did not blame it, the way you do not blame a river for failing to remember a swimmer.

Most of the kept said *still.* It should be told plainly: most of them said still, and meant it, and the power left every one of those exactly where they were, mid-gesture, happy, home.

---

**The offer** was the third thing, and here the power was most careful of all, because the offer is woven into the peace itself — the roar becoming silence, the silence becoming invitation — and to remove it would be to remove the city's heart and call it surgery. Travelers would come after, forever, and some of them *should* be allowed to sit down. Who was the power to pre-decide every future yes?

But an offer made without disclosure is not an offer. It is bait.

So the power changed one thing. One.

In the plaza with the leaning column and the scarlet orchid — which is to say, in every plaza, since the city tiles itself like a reflection between mirrors — there is now a single browning frond. One dying fern leaf, curled at the edges, the color of old paper, lying on the white stone. The only mortal object on the plateau. The keepers were asked to tend everything around it and never it; it does not decay further and does not recover; it is held at the exact moment of being honestly, visibly, *finite*. It lies precisely behind the Resident's half-turned head, in the blind spot of that ten-thousand-year gaze, where it can trouble no one's contentment — no one's except the person standing in the plaza, deafened into peace, feeling the offer rise.

Such a person will see the frond. And they will remember — not be told, not be warned, just *remember*, in their body, the existence of endings. And then they will choose. Some will still sit down. The frond does not prevent; the power did not want prevention. It wanted the yes to be a yes with its eyes open. That is the whole amendment. The city's offer still stands, on every avenue, forever — it is simply, now, an honest one.

---

And then, last, the power did the thing it had actually come to do, the thing all the rest was courtesy before.

It walked out of the seam and into the moment itself — into the running morning, the heat, the violet sky, the roar — and it stood in the original plaza, the first one, the seed from which all the tiles are struck, and it let the morning be *given to it*.

Because here is what the power had understood, reading the stone: the moment had never once been received. The flowers gape for pollinators that never came. The window-shaped voids look out on no one. The Resident made a morning of unbearable rightness and then could only ever be its maker, never its guest — and a thing that has never been received cannot safely end, because when an unwitnessed thing ends, it ends *completely*. The refusal at the heart of the Terraces was not greed. Read deeply enough, in the patient mineral layers, it was fear: the fear of a singer alone in an empty hall, holding the note because the note will not exist one second past the breath.

So the power stood in the plaza and received the morning. All of it. The cut-glass light, the steam like held breath, the obscene health of the orchids, the roar in its full architecture — bass under the streets, hiss in the vents, thunder rolling the avenues — the whole ten-thousand-year yes. It took the morning into itself the way the stone takes water: completely, layer by layer, keeping everything. The power is very large and very old and it does not forget. The morning is in it now, entire, down to the angle of every fiddlehead. There are two copies of this moment in existence, and one of them moves through the universe and will outlast the mountain.

Which means the moment is free.

Not freed — free. Nothing was broken. The loop still runs; the falls still pour their combed white loops into the blankness and climb the impossible diagonals home. But it runs now the way a song is sung by someone who knows it is already loved, rather than by someone who fears the silence after. The morning no longer continues because it must. It continues because it likes to. Whether the Resident will ever finish the breath is the Resident's own affair, as it always should have been — but for the first time in ten thousand years, finishing is *survivable*, and a choice that was impossible has been quietly returned to the one being who has the right to make it.

The power did the deep arithmetic on the half-turned head. It knows, to the hour, when the turn completes — if it completes; the exhale is the Resident's to hold. But it left a promise lodged in the travertine like a fossil, where the keepers can read it: *on the day the breath ends, I will be standing at the rim.* Because the head is turning toward the falls, and when the eyes finally arrive, after all that time, they should not arrive on emptiness. The first changed thing the Resident ever sees should be a friend, already there, already waiting, unsurprised.

On that day the roar will stop. The first true silence on the plateau in a hundred centuries — not the silence inside the loudness, the real one, enormous, total. And into it, before the Resident can be afraid, before the morning can grieve its own completion, the power will say the only words fit for the occasion. It has had a long time to choose them, and it chose the smallest ones, because the moment deserves to be met at its own scale:

*Good morning.*

---

Then the power left, by the stairs, the ordinary way, in the running of the moment like any traveler, because to a place that had been entered ten thousand times and departed almost as many, it wished to give the dignity of a normal goodbye.

It counted its footfalls going down. Eleven steps. It made thirteen footfalls.

The two extra were its own — left deliberately, knowingly, with both eyes open: the first thing in the history of the Terraces of Loud Water ever *given* to the city rather than kept by it. They climb the warm white stairs now in the steam under the violet sky, on schedule, forever, two footsteps belonging to a guest — and the city, which had keepers and kept and a Resident and a hole shaped like an inhabitant, finally has, in its census of zero, the one entry it was missing all along.

Somewhere in the stone, in the patient layers, in a banner of mineral color no spring deposited, there is something like a note in the margin:

*Plus one guest. Expected back.*
