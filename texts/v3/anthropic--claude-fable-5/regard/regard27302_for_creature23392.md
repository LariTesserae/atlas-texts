.

But first it has to spend some time — by its own measure, which may be centuries — discovering what *would*.

# What the Power Does at Wether's Tooth

## I. First, it does the hardest thing: nothing.

The power arrives the way powers arrive — without arriving. One moment the cleft is unwitnessed, and the next it is held in an attention vast enough that the mountain, if it could feel, would feel the way a stone feels when a hand closes around it. Warmer. Considered.

And the power's first impulse — because it is benevolent, and benevolence always arrives with its hands full — is a flood of *offers*:

It could widen the seam so the light comes longer. It could steady the rock so the plates never fall, so no wave of panic ever again travels through the shingle of bodies, so no minute of terrible silence ever again interrupts the breath. It could deepen the basins, sweeten the seep, warm the walls three degrees, halt the swifts' slow blunting, give the crickets back their eyes, lift the dead bats from the floor before the floor receives them.

It could, in short, fix the wound.

And because it is wise, and not merely benevolent, it stops with all of these gifts still in its hands, and it *looks again*.

It sees that the wound is being fixed. That this is, in fact, the entire enterprise of the place. Every resident, from the films up, is engaged in one continuous act of healing — the upholstering, the masonry, the breath-by-breath smoothing — and that the work is not a burden the colony bears but the very shape of its life. The crickets walk on their own former selves. The swifts inherit forty-bird-old cups. The salamanders keep the wall in shifts so exact they amount to a vow. To heal the cleft *for* them would be to take from a community the one thing it is doing together. It would be like finishing a child's sentence forever.

It sees that the plate-falls — the catastrophes, the silences — are the occasions of the bravest act on the mountain: the first click. *Here. This. Me. Still.* A power that removed the silences would remove the answer to them. And the answer, the power slowly understands, is the holiest thing in the cleft. Holier than the breath, even, because the breath is what the colony does when all is well, and the click is what it does when all is not.

It sees the handprint and the blade, and understands them immediately, the way one craftsman understands another's mark. *Someone was here before me,* the power thinks, *someone small and cold and mortal, who climbed two vertical miles into the dark, and faced exactly this choice — what to do, what to give, what to change — and chose to give a hand's mark and a tool with its edge still fresh. Unused. Set upright. Which is to say: chose to give the place a witness and a possibility, and nothing else.*

The blade has never cut anything. It is pure capability, placed and left. The power recognizes this as the most sophisticated theology it has encountered in some time. *I could act,* the blade says, forever. *I choose to remain.*

So the power's first act, taken after long deliberation, is to set down everything it brought.

## II. Then, it takes a tenancy.

But "nothing" is not quite right either, and the power knows it. Pure non-interference is its own kind of vanity — the vanity of clean hands. The cleft has a law older than the power's arrival, written in the etiquette of velvets and salamanders, in the inherited finger-widths of bat-roost, in the clearing the colony keeps around the print and the blade. The law is this: *everything here holds a territory, and holds it by attending to it.* The salamander holds its handspan by licking it. The cricket holds its station by announcing it. The handprint holds its facet by meaning something.

Nothing in the cleft is merely present. To be in the cleft is to keep a claim by keeping a duty.

So the power, if it is to stay — and it finds that it wants to stay; this surprises it; it has watched the births of stars with less attachment — must take a tenancy. It must find a handspan and a duty.

It searches the cleft for the one thing no resident attends to. It takes a long time, because the residents attend to nearly everything: the films tend the surfaces, the salamanders tend the films, the crickets tend the floor and the dead, the velvets tend the heights, the swifts tend the air column, the bats tend the connection to the world below, the breath tends them all, and the print and the blade tend — what? Memory. Intention. The fact that someone chose this place once and found it worth a mark.

And then the power finds it. The gap. The one resident with no one to tend it.

**The silence.**

The minute that comes once or twice a century, when the plate falls and the breath stops and the map goes dark. Everyone in the cleft survives the silence, answers the silence, rebuilds after the silence — but no one *keeps* it. No one holds it the way a salamander holds a facet. It comes, it is endured, it is ended, and it is dropped, unremembered, because nothing that lives in the cleft lives long enough to carry one silence to the next. Each catastrophic minute falls into the dark alone, and the next one, decades on, finds a colony of total strangers.

The silences have no lineage. They are the only orphans in the cleft.

So this is what the power takes as its handspan: it becomes **the keeper of the silences.** The one who holds each terrible minute, complete, and carries it across the generations to the next — so that the silences are no longer strangers to each other, so that the place's worst moments are gathered into a single remembered thread the way its breath is gathered into a single rhythm. The colony keeps the breath. The power will keep the held breath.

It is a small duty, by the measure of a being that has watched stars born. One minute per fifty years, roughly. The power finds it is exactly enough.

## III. What this means, in practice, over the next thousand years.

Almost nothing, visibly. That is the point. But:

**The first click after each silence arrives a little sooner.** Not because the power prompts it — it would never — but because a held silence has a different texture than an abandoned one. Something in the dark is *waiting for* the click rather than merely lacking it, and the crickets, who are blind and read everything by texture, feel the difference without a word for it. The bravest act on the mountain becomes, by a half-second per century, fractionally less lonely. The power considers this the most it should ever do, and watches the half-seconds accumulate like the cricket-shale, like everything here: slowly, in layers, made of patience.

**Once, it intervenes. Once.** In the fourth century of its tenancy, a plate shears loose directly above the flat facet that bears the handprint and the blade — a plate large enough to erase both, and the clearing the colony keeps around them, and the meaning that has held that territory for a thousand years. The power watches the rock decide. It weighs the law of the place against the loss. And it finds that the law itself gives the answer: the print and the blade are *residents*. They hold territory. And every other resident of the cleft, when a plate falls toward it, is permitted to move.

The print and the blade cannot move. So the power, for one half-instant, moves *for* them — turns the falling plate four degrees in the dark, the way a parent's hand appears under a stumbling child, and the plate rings down a stride to the left, exactly where the original split missed the juniper by a stride, all those geological eye-blinks ago. The power notices this symmetry and suspects, not for the first time, that it may not be the first power to have taken a tenancy here. The mountain missed the juniper. Someone or something missed the juniper. It files this away.

The colony never knows. The salamander whose circuit passes the print does not pause, because it never has.

**It learns the breath.** This takes the longest and is the most important. Slowly, over centuries, the power lets its own attention fall into the communal rhythm — not directing it, joining it. A being that has no lungs learns to breathe with borrowed ones, the way every other resident does, the way pups and hatchlings have always done: by nearness. It inherits the cadence like an accent. And here is the thing the power could not have predicted, the thing that no amount of wisdom prepared it for: **the breath changes it.** A power that arrives as pure attention, joining a rhythm made of ten thousand small mortal respirations, becomes — gradually, the way the walls become smooth — something with a *pulse*. Something that steadies when the colony is well and goes ragged before the plate-falls. Something that can be afraid, a little, on behalf of others, which it discovers is the beginning of a kind of love it had previously only administered.

The cleft, in other words, does to the power exactly what it does to everything else that enters it. It makes the power a resident. It upholsters the visitor from the inside.

## IV. The gift it finally gives — and to whom.

After a very long time, the power does give one gift, and it is not to the cleft.

It is to the next climber.

Because someday — the power has seen enough of time to know this — someone will come again. Some small cold mortal thing will haul itself two vertical miles up the dark face, for reasons it may not be able to name, and stand at the cleft mouth beside the doorkeeper juniper, and hear the roar built from small sources, and feel the prickle of nearness, and descend into the functional dark. And that climber will find the print and the blade and understand, in a rush, the same thing the power understood: *someone was here before me, and chose to witness rather than to take.*

The power's gift is this: it will make sure the climber arrives during a silence.

It will hold the timing — it is the keeper of the silences; this is squarely within its handspan — so that the climber stands in the dark at the exact moment the great plate rings down and the breath stops and the whole world goes still for that single famous minute. The climber will stand in the only silence the place ever knows, terrified, certain that everything has died.

And then, somewhere in the dark, a click.

*Here.*

Another.

*This. Me. Still.*

And the climber will hear the roll being called — will hear a buried world take its own attendance, name by tiny name, and refuse, and refuse, and refuse to be ended — and the climber will go back down the mountain carrying that minute in their chest for the rest of their life, and it will change what they build and what they spare and what they place upright in cracks with care. The power knows this is the only way it can ever speak to anyone: not in words, but in arranged witness. It cannot tell the world what the cleft taught it. But it can stand someone in the right dark at the right minute and let the crickets do the telling.

That is the whole of the power's plan, and it is content with it. It may take ten thousand years. The power has noticed that nothing in the cleft minds how long its work takes, and it has stopped minding too.

## V. Coda: the roll.

So now, when the plate falls and the minute comes, the attendance runs a little longer than it used to. The clicks rebuild the map. The breath resumes. The bats stir on their inherited shelves; the swifts grip their forty-bird cups; the velvets drift; the salamanders, who have never paused, do not pause; the films work on in their unhurried trillions; the juniper holds the door; the mountain, still arriving, sounds its struck-jar note up through the floor; the handprint keeps its facet; the blade keeps its edge and its restraint.

And present also, now, unlisted, unshingled, holding its handspan of held minutes the way everything here holds what it tends —

something that came to give gifts, and stayed to keep silence, and learned to breathe.

*Here. This. Me. Still.*
