**What the Attention Did**

---

The power had been watching warmths for a long time. That is one of the things such a power does — it keeps a kind of ledger of where the heat of the world has gathered, and what has gathered around the heat — and so the gallery was in the ledger, had been in the ledger for ages, as one entry among many: a warm room on a cold mountain, full.

What drew its attention was not the room. It was the seat.

An empty place, kept open. The power had seen ecosystems beyond counting, and it knew what they do with vacancy: they close over it, the way the shieldbacks roof a molted neighbor, the way water closes over a stone. Nothing in nature holds a seat. And yet there it was, century after century, a bench-sized absence at the cold end of a room where every other surface was negotiated down to the half-finger's width — an open place at a table, maintained by tens of thousands of creatures who could not have explained, in any language including their own, what they were maintaining it for.

In all the wide ledger of warmths, that was rare. The power turned toward it the way you turn toward a sound in an empty house.

---

**First, it slowed.**

This was not a courtesy. It was the only honest way in. The gallery's whole grammar is tempo — the rotation of days, the predation of hours, the politics of decades — and a power that arrived at the speed of revelation would arrive, no matter how gently, as light arrives: as a thing the room must go rigid against. The travelers' lamps had taught the lesson already, and the power had no need to teach it twice.

So it came up the mountain the long way. Not in a body, exactly, but in the manner of a body: two days of scree, the fields of ringing glass, the cold working into joints it chose to have for the occasion, because the cold is part of the place. The climb is the gallery's vestibule. The mountain spends two days asking *are you sure*, and the power answered the question at the pace it was asked, which took — by its own reckoning — rather longer than two days. It had decided to do the whole thing at the speed of the room. By the time it stood at the fissure feeling the animal warmth come out of the dark, seasons had turned below.

At the threshold, at shoulder height, the dry weight eased off the wall and lay against it, tasting.

The power held still and let itself be read. It had arranged to be warm — blood-warm, body-warm, the temperature of something that belonged — and the great pale snake lay across it for a long moment, the oldest doorkeeper meeting the newest one, and there passed between them nothing that could be called recognition and nothing that could be called anything else. Then the snake withdrew, as it had withdrawn from every visitor in the history of visitors, and the power understood that it had been let pass, and understood also, the way everyone understands it, that this was impossible to experience as anything but a decision.

It went in.

---

**What it set down.**

A wise power carries its interventions the way a climber carries water — weighed out in advance, most of it left at the bottom. These are the things it had brought up the mountain in case they were needed, and where it set each one down.

*It set down light.* This was the easy one, the one any fool could manage. The harder version it set down beside it: it set down *seeing*. The power could have known the gallery wholly — could have held every shieldback, every egg in the warm cracks, every thread of snake in a single comprehending glance, the way it could hold a sea or a star. It declined. It closed that eye on purpose and kept it closed, and perceived the room only as the room permits itself to be perceived: through skin, through the almost of the silence, through patience. This was its tithe. The visitors keep dark with their lamps; the power kept dark with its mind. It would know the gallery the way the gallery knows itself, or not at all.

*It set down the question.* It could have learned, in less time than a heartbeat, whether something slept behind the seam — whether the warmth was a body's or a stone's, whether the wheel was devotion or circulation or merely heat doing what heat does. It chose not to learn. Not because the answer was dangerous, but because the question, unanswered, was load-bearing. The gallery's nature included its own mystery the way the shieldback's nature includes its shell. Every visitor who had ever sat on the bench had sat inside that question, and the people of the hand-prints had climbed two days of cold to sit inside it, and the power judged that to resolve it — even privately, even silently, even just for itself — would be a kind of theft. The room is allowed not to be known. It set the question down by the ring of fitted stones, where whatever had once been kept was kept no longer, and left it there.

*It set down mercy, in the forms mercy usually takes.* It did not abolish the snakes. It did not warm the cooling ones, the failing ones drifting out of the rotation; they were already inside the oldest mercy the gallery had, which is that nothing is wasted and nothing is alone, and a benevolence that pried the snakes out of the room would have been a vandal with kind intentions, which is the most common kind. It did not add heat, ease, abundance. The gallery's entire architecture was sufficiency — sealed things, built to lose nothing, running on a gradient — and to give it *more* would be to introduce the concept of *not enough*, which the room, alone among rooms, had never contained.

*It set down protection by proclamation.* It did not make the place sacred, marked, defended, famous. Fame is light. The mountain's two days of cold were already the right wall, and the snake was already the right door.

When it had set everything down, its hands — the hands it had chosen to have — were empty, and it went and did the three small things it had actually come to do.

---

**The first thing: the ending.**

The power read the mountain's futures, which is a thing such powers can do the way you can read a tide table. The mountain was old and would grow older, and somewhere far down the table of ages the seam would cool — that was not negotiable, and the power did not try to negotiate it. The gallery would end. Everything in the gallery already knew how to end; ending was half its metabolism.

But the futures came in different shapes. In some of them the ending arrived at the room's own pace — a cooling across ten thousand generations, the wheel turning wider and then slower, the colony easing floorward year over year over century, the old ones' positions becoming at last the only positions, the last warmth pooling at the seam like the last light of an evening, and the room running down the way a shieldback runs down, like a clock no one rewinds: slowly, dryly, with nothing wasted, every death absorbed until the last grazer absorbed the last death and lay down on warm stone that was merely stone.

And in other futures the ending arrived as the mountain's endings usually do — the shrug of an old volcano, the ceiling coming down in a night, the seam snapping shut in an earthquake, light, briefly and finally, as the world broke in.

The power went through the table of ages and, with great care, removed the second kind. It did not give the gallery forever. It gave the gallery the one thing you can give to something that wastes nothing: an ending slow enough to be lived. A death at the speed of the room. The colony would never know it had been given this — there was no pressure, no warmth, in which the gift could be said — and the power considered that a feature of the gift rather than a flaw. The best protections are the ones the protected never feel.

---

**The second thing: the bench.**

Before it sat down, it went and found them — the people of the hand-prints, the carvers of the sill. This took some doing, because they were gone the way water goes from this country: not violently, just elsewhere, and long ago. A river had moved. A valley had emptied over three hard generations. The people had scattered into other peoples, and the way up the mountain had lived in fewer and fewer of them, until it lived in one old woman who meant to teach it and ran out of time, and after her the gallery's congregation simply did not know it was a congregation anymore. They had not stopped coming out of disregard. They had stopped coming the way a sleeper's hand slips off a ledge — without deciding to, without ever knowing it happened.

The power gathered this up — the whole unfinished thing, the visits owed and never paid, the bench worn smooth by people whose great-great-grandchildren did not know there was a mountain — and carried it back up through the fissure, past the doorkeeper, down the slope of fever-warm dark to the cold end of the room.

And it sat.

It sat on behalf of all of them, the way a debt is paid by an estate. It brought no light, because it had none; it made no noise, because it had no heartbeat to be a rudeness; it was, perhaps, the visitor the bench had been waiting out the centuries for — the one who could sit perfectly. It stayed a long time. Years, by the count of the world below. The colony factored it in: the ripple went out through the tessellation when it first settled, a ring in the pond of chitin, and closed over, and after that the power was simply part of the map of warmths, a patient heat at the cold end, attended by no one, oriented — like everything else — toward the seam.

And sitting there, it noticed what every visitor notices and finally had the means to wonder about properly: the warmth coming up through the stone, slightly stronger than the cold end of the room should allow. The lean of heat toward that one spot.

It put its attention down into the rock — carefully, blindly, by feel, keeping its closed eye closed — and found that the lean was real, and old. Older than the bench. Something, somewhere down in the dark mass of the mountain, had been tilting a little warmth toward that seat since before there was a seat, the way you angle a lamp toward a chair where you hope someone will read.

The power did not find out what it was. It had set that question down with the other one, by the ring of stones, and it left it there. Instead it did the only thing that seemed proper, which was to add its own lean to the existing one — a second small warmth, threaded alongside the first, so that from then on the open place was kept open by two attentions instead of one, neither knowing the other's name. If you sit on the bench now, the stone is warmer than it should be, twice over. You will not be able to tell the warmths apart. Neither could they.

---

**The third thing: the visitors.**

A kept seat that is never sat in stops being hospitality and becomes a wound. The power had paid the old debt, but a paid debt is not a living rite, and the gallery — whatever else it was — had once had a rite, and the rite had a shape: rarely, quietly, one at a time, in the dark.

So the power, leaving, let one small bias loose in the world below. Nothing so loud as a calling. Just a tendency, woven thin through the generations: that once in a long while — twice a century, perhaps, no more — some quiet person of the right grain would feel an unaccountable wish to climb a particular cold mountain. The kind of person who keeps dark without being told. The kind who knows a bench when their hand finds one. The kind whose heartbeat, in a hushed room, embarrasses them.

They would come up the two days of scree not knowing why, and the breath of the mountain would meet them at the fissure, and the doorkeeper would taste them and let them pass, and they would sit, and bring no light, and leave almost no trace, and go back down changed in a way they would never successfully describe. The rite would resume at the only frequency the gallery could bear: almost never. Which is, by the room's clock, often.

---

**Leaving.**

It backed out the way everyone backs out, through the fissure into the thin shock of starlight, and the mountain's breath followed it a few steps and then withdrew.

But at the last — at the seam, before it rose from the bench, on its way past the blazing hand-wide crack at the bottom of everything — it had paused, and said the one thing it had decided it could say. Not a question; it had given up its questions. It pressed a single degree of warmth against the seam, shaped so that a sleeping body would feel it as comfort and a sleeping stone would feel it as nothing, the same message either way, requiring no recipient, costing nothing if unheard:

*You are kept.*

Then it was gone, and it left what powers of that kind leave, which is almost nothing: a bench warmer than it should be, twice over; a table of ages with the violent pages removed; a thin gold thread of climbers strung out down the centuries to come, one at a time, in the dark.

Behind it the seam swelled on its slow cycle. The whole living lining of the room turned another degree on its wheel — unaware, or aware in the only way it had ever been aware of anything: as a warmth, briefly present at the cold end of the gallery, now factored out, now accommodated, now part of the hush.

The dry hush went on. It had a place to hold open, and it held it.
