**What the Power Did**

The first thing the power did was nothing, and it did it for a long time.

This must be understood correctly, because *nothing* is what fools and gods both do, and only one of them does it well. The power arrived the way attention arrives — without footprint, without shadow, a warmth in the consideration of the world tilting toward one warm place — and its first wisdom was to recognize, immediately, the danger it carried simply by being capable of speed. A swift judgment of the Tableland would be a wrong one, in the way that a single frame of a long embrace can be mistaken for a collision. So the power did to itself what the plateau does to everything: it settled. It let its attention widen until a season fit inside one glance, until a decade fit inside one breath. It chose to perceive the place at the speed the place perceives itself, because a benevolence that will not match your pace before it judges you is only impatience wearing a kind face.

For something close to a century, then, the power simply attended. It watched the trails deepen by the width of a hair. It watched a single step complete. It watched the grey birds shimmer through their forty generations and remain, by the elders' accounting, one set of birds. It laid its palms — or what it had instead of palms — flat against the bedrock and listened down into the hum.

And it heard the utterance.

It heard it all the way down — through the trails, through the breathing domes, through the corbelled rooms of the finished, down into the worn highland itself — and it learned that the suspicion is true. The bedrock is the elder generations. The mountains were the first of them, vast and upright and, by every later standard, frantic. The plateau is not landscape; it is ancestry, compacted, still saying its part. And the power learned one thing more, which it has never told anyone in full: that the unbroken utterance, the one that has run longer than there have been ears, is not a conversation. It is a single word, still being said. The power listened long enough to hear the shape of its beginning, and then — deliberately, courteously — it stopped listening before the end. Some words should be heard whole only by those who intend to stay and answer. The power did not intend to stay. It marked this, in itself, as the plateau's first gift to it: the knowledge that even a wise and ancient thing can be too young for certain sentences.

---

**The flaw**

A lesser benevolence would have ended there, in admiration, and gone home praising the place. The power stayed, because admiration is where wisdom begins its real work, and it had found, in all that ancient agreement, exactly one seam.

The plateau itself is faultless. This was the power's settled conclusion, worn in over decades the way the Settled wear in a thought: the place takes nothing, keeps no one, lies to no one. Stone that was patience, patience that became bodies, bodies becoming stone — there is no flaw in any of it, no cruelty hidden under the warmth, no hunger disguised as peace. The power checked. It is in the nature of wise powers to check, especially when a place is beautiful, because beauty is where hunger most likes to disguise itself. There was nothing. The Tableland is exactly what it appears to be, which may be the rarest thing about it.

The seam is at the edge — at the place where two speeds meet. It is this:

The plateau offers its pace the way it offers its warmth, freely, to whatever arrives. But warmth costs nothing to accept, and pace costs everything — a lifespan, a language, every face the visitor has ever loved, traded for a present moment forty years wide. And the offer arrives on the third or fourth day, ahead of understanding, dressed as restfulness, wearing the smell of warm rain. A visitor who stands beside an elder a few minutes too long has not been deceived — the plateau does not deceive — but neither, always, has she *chosen*. Some of the slowed meant it with their whole hearts. The power could see that; their stillness was full all the way down, like a vessel that has finally found its level. But some had only paused. Some had come up the last shoulder of climb carrying a grief or an exhaustion, and had stood still the way a person sinking stands still, and the plateau, which cannot tell surrender from arrival — why would it? in fifty million years it has never needed to — had simply, generously, kept offering.

An unmeant yes is not a small flaw. It is the only flaw that matters, anywhere, ever. The power had seen worlds rot from the inside around exactly this: gifts that outran consent, mercies accepted before they were understood. And so it did the two things it had come to understand were needful, and it did them in the only acceptable order.

First, it asked permission.

---

**The asking of the Settled**

The power went out across the tableland to the eldest of the elders — a being so far finished-without-finishing that its garden had garden, whose trail-grooves were the deep ones that carry water and meaning both — and it put its question into the bedrock, syllable by season, through the palms of its presence: *Some of those who settle among you did not mean it. May I ask them? May I carry out the ones who answer no?*

The answer took thirty-one years, and the power waited for every day of them without once checking the time, because to ask at the right speed and then fidget for the reply is the same as not asking.

The answer, when it had fully arrived, was the closest thing to urgency the power ever felt from that ground. Rendered into a fast language, it was something like: *Yes. Please. Soon.* And underneath it, the reason, which the power had not anticipated and which taught it more about the Settled than the previous century combined: the elders had known. Of course they had known — they, whose entire language is position, whose every thought is a groove, who can read meaning in the placement of a body the way we read it in the placement of a word. An inhabitant who did not mean to be here is, to them, a false word pressed into the ancestral sentence. A lie in the bedrock. They had felt the unmeant ones among the slowed for centuries — felt them the way you feel a wrong note held under a chord — and had been, at their pace, in their way, *grieving it*, with no means of address, because the question *do you mean this?* asked at their speed takes longer than a human life to finish pronouncing. They had been curving their trails toward the slowed not only in welcome. Some of those long slow turnings-toward were attempts at rescue, begun generations ago, that could never arrive in time.

The plateau keeps no one. It had simply never had hands fast enough to open its own door.

The power had hands.

---

**The forty-year question**

So the power went from slowed one to slowed one — the figures shaped like people, lichen at the boot-seams, dust in the folds, breathing their two and three breaths an hour — and to each of them it asked the same question, and it asked it correctly, which is to say: at their speed, not its own. To ask quickly would have been its own coercion, a shout in the ear of someone mid-thought, an answer startled out rather than given. So the power knelt in the dust beside each one and laid the question down a syllable at a time, through warmth, through dew, through the same channels the plateau itself speaks in, so that the question arrived not as an interruption of their slowness but as a part of it.

*Did you choose this? Do you choose it still?*

It spent, in some cases, forty years on a single asking. It did not begrudge one hour of it.

Most of them said yes. This should be told plainly, because a story of rescue likes to pretend that all stillness is a trap, and it is not, and the power knew it was not. Most of the slowed, asked in the marrow where no enchantment reaches, answered with something the power could only translate as *finally* — a yes with the grain of the dew-lines in it, radial and settled, a yes that had been true on the fourth day and had only deepened since. To these the power gave the single thing a yes deserves, which is to be believed. It rose, and let them be, and as it left each one it did one small uninstructed thing: it touched the lichen at their shoulders, and the lichen bloomed gold — that strange soft gold of the eldest gardens — decades ahead of schedule. A blessing, if you like. Or simply a wise power's way of telling the grey birds, the geckos, the whole small parish of each new country: *this one is real. Build here.*

But some — not many; eleven, in the end, out of all the slowed across the whole tableland — answered otherwise. Not *no*, exactly. The answers were: *I stopped. I don't remember deciding. I was so tired. Not yet. I didn't know it would be everyone — I didn't know I was trading everyone.* One of them, a woman who had come up the climb four years before with a death in her chest and had stood still on her fourth morning the way a person sinking stands still, gave an answer that took eleven years to finish and consisted entirely of a grief that had never moved — because that is the seam's cruelest trick, the one the elders had felt as the wrongest of the wrong notes: slowness does not heal what arrives unhealed. It preserves it. Her sorrow had been standing in her, blood-warm and a knuckle deep, for four years of plateau time, evaporating by afternoon and beading back every dawn, going nowhere, because she had stopped before it could.

These eleven the power carried out.

It was gentle about it and it was not slow about it; this was the one fast thing it did, because once an unmeant yes is found, every further hour of it is theft. It gathered them up — they weighed almost nothing; the Settled-becoming are mostly warmth — and bore them to the plateau's edge, down past the last shoulder of climb, and there it gave them back their time. It could do this; their years had not been spent, only held — the plateau keeps no one, including no one's hours — and the power simply opened its hands and let the held time pour back in. The waking was not violent. It was the way the body wakes when it has finally slept enough: long, and complete, and a little astonished.

The woman with the death in her chest woke weeping, and the power stayed beside her while she did it, asking nothing, offering nothing, because the weeping was hers, four years overdue, and the kindest thing in the world was to let it finally move. When it had moved, she looked back up the climb — you could see her measuring it, the warm stone, the breathing horizon — and the power told her the truth, which is the only thing benevolence is ever finally made of:

*It will still be there. It is the one thing that will still be there. Go and live at your own speed first, all the way to the end of wanting to. The offer does not expire. It has never once expired. And if you come back, come back meaning it, because they can feel the difference, and it matters to them more than you can yet imagine.*

---

**The mending of the threshold**

Then the power did the second needful thing, the lasting one, and it did it with the elders' thirty-one-year permission warm in its keeping.

It did not build a wall. It did not post a warning. A warning would have poisoned the gift — would have hung a *beware* over a place whose whole truth is that there is nothing to beware, only something enormous to decide. What the power wove into the plateau's edge instead — into the last shoulder of climb, into the warm stone where hands first press flat — was a single amendment, small as a stitch:

*Understanding now arrives before the offer does.*

It works like this. On the third day — the day before the standing-too-long, before the breath begins its unconscious stretching — every visitor receives one moment of complete sight. It comes however it can best be carried: as a dream by a dew-pan, as a long thought beside an elder, as a clear two-note whistle answered after a courteous interval somewhere very deep in the mind. In that moment the visitor stands, briefly and wholly, inside the thing being offered. They feel the breadth of a present moment forty years wide. They feel the warmth of adjacency held for millennia, the slow turning-toward, the parish of small lives that will shelter against them, the gold the garden will someday bloom. And they feel the cost, all of it, unsoftened: every face outlived, every errand abandoned, every fast sweet thing — laughter at speed, weather as event, the whole bright flicker of their kind — exhaled, lawfully, into the dust. They see the corbelled dome at the end, slightly larger inside than the smallest of the living, its opening facing east. They see it as home or they see it as tomb, and the seeing tells them which, and *then* the offer continues exactly as before, unchanged, unhurried, the plateau's pace extended like its warmth to whatever arrives.

The only fast decision left on the entire tableland is still yours. The power's whole intervention, in the end, was to make sure it was *actually* yours — that the one quick thing in that slow country would be done with open eyes. Consent made whole, and nothing else touched. The elders, when the amendment settled into the bedrock, said something through the night hum that took six years to say and translated, roughly, to the sound a door makes when it has been hanging slightly wrong for fifty thousand years and is finally rehung true.

---

**What the power took, and what it left**

It took a lesson, and was not ashamed to. Powers that cannot be taught are merely large. What the Tableland taught it, it carries now into every other world it tends: that speed is a kind of bias; that the deepest consent must be asked for at the pace of the one consenting; that there exist places which need nothing from benevolence except the patience to confirm it; and that the difference between surrender and arrival is invisible from outside and absolute from within, and the whole work of wisdom is learning to ask which one you are looking at.

It left three things.

It left the amendment, the stitch at the threshold, which will hold as long as the bedrock holds, which is to say as long as the ancestors keep saying their word.

It left a stone. Before it withdrew, it went to the ring at the crossing of the two deepest trails and pressed one flat stone, the size of a hand, onto the rim — slowly, with the weight of a limb at rest, in the proper grammar — so that its visit would be a true word in the sentence and not a ghost passing through it. The lichen has already begun to fuse it down.

And it left a trail. Faint, this one — a finger's depth, no more — running in from the plateau's edge, from off the last shoulder of climb, from nowhere that any walker now breathing has ever been. It is the groove of the power's century of attendance, and of its eleven crossings with the carried sleepers, and of its kneeling, and it deepens, very slightly, every long while, because the power comes back. Not often, by your measure. Often, by theirs. It comes back to lay its palms on the warm stone and listen — never to the end of the word, only to its continuing — and to check the threshold-stitch, and once, some decades on, to stand quietly at the edge on the day an old woman came up the climb for the second time in her life, grey now herself, finished with the fast sweet flicker, her grief long since moved through and out, and stood beside an elder on her third evening, and received her moment of complete sight, and smiled at it like someone reading a letter she had written to herself, and stayed.

The elders are partway through noticing the power's trail. They have been noticing it for some time now, in the way they notice — position read as language, a groove that curves, against all precedent, *toward* them from the outside of the world.

The noticing is going well.

It is fond.
