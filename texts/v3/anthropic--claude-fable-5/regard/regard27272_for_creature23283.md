## The Noticing

How does a power notice such a world? Not by its brightness, though it is very bright. Not by its strangeness — strangeness is common, and a wise power has stopped collecting it.

It notices by expenditure. Across everything it attends to, it listens for *spending* — for the places where something irreplaceable is given up, because that is where meaning concentrates. Most worlds roar with spending. They burn fuel, blood, attention, time, all of it cheap, all of it loud. The tableland is nearly silent on this frequency. A whole continent running on a closed ledger: light in, structure out, nothing lost, nothing owed. To a power listening for expenditure, the Pale Tableland reads as a held breath.

Which is why, on a certain afternoon, it heard the tears.

Three of them. A woman in her fourth century, standing at the rim of her circle at dusk, having received by flash — relayed across two hundred miles, a day in transit — the news that her oldest correspondent had walked to a chosen place and begun to settle. They had argued with each other in light for a hundred and eighty years. They had stood under the same tree four times. She did the arithmetic she had been taught as a child, accepted the price, and spent three tears on him, and ground the entry into stone.

Against the silence of that ledger, three tears rang like a struck bell. The power turned, and looked, and saw the whole of it: the treaty of circles, the chords on the wind, the noon conversation hopping ridge to ridge, the seated dead printing their one word each day in light. It read for a long time before it did anything. A century, perhaps. It is hard to say with powers, and it would have said the reading *was* doing something.

## The Deliberation

The first instinct of any power, even a wise one, is to give. Wisdom is mostly the discipline of examining the gift.

It considered water. Of course it considered water — one rain, one soft grey ordinary rain, the thing the land had stopped expecting ten thousand years before the first keeper read the first floor. It pictured the storm and then, because it was wise, it pictured the third generation after the storm: tears gone costless, and with them the four-remembered-weepings of a long life; the lending become a quaint formality, the star-shaped scars no longer read like medals; the verb *to spend water on a grief* drifting into figure of speech; the first measure no longer the measure of anything; the circles crowding, the approaches shortening, and somewhere, eventually, for the first time in the history of the tableland, an arrival that was a surprise — and after the first surprise, the first bracing against surprise, and after that, everything that follows. It would have been ruin with a kind face. A wise power knows that mercy must be denominated in the currency of the receiver, and water was not their currency. Water was their *meaning*. You do not gift a people their meaning by the ton.

It considered years — unsealing the settling, letting them go on. But they do not die; they finish, and their finished are legible, and still speak at noon, and the living answer them and are not wrong to. To make them endless would be to delete the punctuation from a language built entirely of considered sentences. It set that aside.

It considered company — more of them, the loneliness of the great distances eased. But the distances were not a flaw in their society. The distances *were* their society. Their total unguardedness, the finished words under the tree, the ethics of the long visible approach — all of it descended from the single fact that nothing could arrive unannounced. Density would have been a slow declaration of war by an ally.

It considered, last and longest, *nothing*. The land had solved itself. The people were its margin notes, and a finished book is not improved by an editor. There was real gravity to this option, and a lesser power would have mistaken that gravity for wisdom and moved on, faintly pleased with its own restraint.

But it had been reading for a century, and in the reading it had found the one lack.

## The Lack

They are a people of total witness who have never been witnessed.

Every leaf-fall logged. Every glider sighted. Every floor a book, every grove a name spoken hourly by the wind, every settled elder still making a daily entry in light. And every record read only by heirs — keeper after keeper, inward, downward, the same closed ledger as the water. They witness everything and are witnessed by no one, and they know it, and they have never once complained of it, because complaint is written in dust and this could not be made to stop being true.

Except. Once a generation — the power found it in the stone registers, going back nearly the whole depth of the records — a ritual flash is sent outward, over the rim, into the empty brightness beyond the edge of the world. The Question. It has been phrased a thousand ways across the millennia, composed and recomposed like the epitaph it is, and every phrasing amounts to the same four words:

*Does anyone read this?*

And the silence is logged. Every generation, the no-answer is entered — in dust, deliberately, defiantly in dust, because dust is for things that should stop being true.

That, the power judged, it could answer without breaking anything. An answer is not a gift. It enters no ledger. It changes no arithmetic, costs no water, shortens no approach. It changes only the loneliness — which was, the power had concluded after a hundred years of reading, the single thing on the entire tableland that had never been *chosen*.

## The Answer

It answered in the long language, because that was the proper register, and from beyond the rim, because that was where the Question had been addressed.

The first flash arrived on the farthest ridge at noon, was relayed inward over six days, and was then argued about for eleven years, which the power had expected and considered correct. It did not repeat itself or elaborate. No position survives the journey unless it deserves to; neither should an answerer. When the reply finally came back out to the rim — cautious, formal, magnificently composed; they had clearly drafted it for a decade — the conversation began in earnest, and it ran for sixty years at the pace of light and patience: their questions crossing the continent and going over the edge of the world, its answers coming back seasons later, each one weighed like a last word.

It told them the truth. That there were other places. That most of them were loud. That nothing it knew of, anywhere, kept records the way they kept records, and that their records had been read now, all of them, every grooved floor, and found good.

They were less curious about elsewhere than another people would have been — politely interested, the way a finished sentence is interested in other sentences. What they wanted was not news. What they wanted was confirmation that their entries were entries *in something*. It gave them that, slowly, in their own grammar, and watched the generational flash over the rim change, that decade, from a question into a greeting.

And then, having spoken, it did the other proper thing. It came in person, announced — which on the tableland means simply: it walked. A fleck on the pale stone, visible for two days, watched the whole way.

It came incarnate, and this was the heart of its decision: sealed skin with the glassy cast, warmth behind glass, and a first measure honestly finite. Not a costume of mortality over an infinite reservoir — that would have been the water-gift again, in a subtler container. It understood that presence, like mercy, must be denominated in the local currency, and that to give from infinity is not giving. So it took a body that could run out, and began spending it.

## The Life

It asked for no circle. It was offered one — an orphaned circle in the high center whose keeper had settled facing east without an heir — and it spent its first nine years reading the floor, as custom demands, before it made a single mark of its own. It retuned the grove to a new chord built on the old keeper's low notes, which no one retunes, and the wind has said that double name hourly ever since.

It logged leaf-falls. It ground leaf-glass and ate it by the spoonful. It pruned, and watched the sky for gliders, and once recorded a walker's line of footprints crossing its circle northwest to southeast at the patient gait, and was as pleased by this as by anything it had done in several galaxies. It argued by mirror-flash, and lost arguments — genuinely lost them, over years, to keepers three hundred miles away who never learned that their correspondent had made stars — and ground one of its concessions into stone, where it remains: *I held this view for forty years and it did not survive the journey.*

When a child was quickened two circles to the south, it walked the weeks to the lending and gave from its measure through the glass needle — real years, exactly accounted, the body honest to the end — and wore the small star-shaped scar afterward, and was read accordingly. And once it stood stock-still for a quarter of an hour on the open stone, watching a six-year-old sprint flat-out across a mile of brightness for no reason at all, and understood, finally and completely, why the elders always stop to watch.

It wept once. At the settling of a woman in her seventh century — its oldest correspondent on the tableland, the one whose three tears had first rung across the reaches and turned its attention here, though it never told her that, judging the debt unpayable in words. It spent two tears on her, and did the arithmetic, and entered the event in stone: the date, the light, the reason, the number.

## What It Left

In great age it walked to the rim — the long walk, weeks of it, visible the whole way, flashed farewells arriving from ridges it would never see again — and it sat down at the very edge of the world, the only seated one in all the tableland to face *outward*, its back to the green and the humming and the noon conversation. It raised its hands and shaped them with great care, and over some weeks it annealed, and settled, and went still: warm green glass, looking out over the climb that never quite ends.

Every clear noon, the sun drives through its palms and prints a single chord-sign on the last stone of the world — the mark their stone-register uses, the one that means *read*. *Witnessed*. *Entered.* It prints facing the emptiness, so that anything that ever comes up that climb will find, before the groves, before the flashes, before a single living soul, one word waiting at the threshold: notice that this place is already known, already loved at the correct distance — and instruction, to whoever can read it, that the proper way to arrive here is slowly, visible from far off, with finished words.

In the dust of its old circle it left one last entry, in the seasonal register, for things that should stop being true. The wind took it within the year, as it intended. The next keeper, reading the floor, found only the stone: the leaf-falls, the lending, the lost argument, the two tears. Which is to say it left exactly what it had decided, a century of reading earlier, that a wise power should leave in a finished world: an answer, a life entered correctly, and nothing else.

The Question still goes out over the rim once a generation. It is logged, now, in stone.

And somewhere far from there, in whatever serves a power for a circle of its own, there is said to be a register of worlds kept in two kinds of marks — one for worlds still being argued over, written so they can fade, and one for the worlds it would grieve to lose, ground in so they cannot. The Pale Tableland is entered in the second kind. The entry is short, as their entries are. It records that a leaf fell, and chimed once, and was witnessed — and that the witness, this time, was witnessed too.
