**What the Power Found, and What It Did**

It had mended worlds before. That should be said first, because it explains the shape of its first mistake.

It arrived the way such powers arrive — not from a direction, but the way attention arrives, the way a room is suddenly the room someone is thinking about — and it came carrying its old habits: the survey, the triage, the ledger of sufferings. It had ended famines elsewhere. It had broken sieges, turned plagues aside in the night, taught fire and writing and forgiveness to peoples who needed one or all three. It was not a meddling power, but it was a *working* power, and it had crossed the curve of the world toward the range with its hands, so to speak, already open.

It hung above the shoulder benches for the length of a morning, which for it was no time at all, and it looked.

---

**The survey**

It looked first for hunger, because hunger is usually the bottom of any world's grief. It found scarcity, which is different. It found a people who knew to the day how long the wool would last and to the cup how much water the night's condensation would yield, and who had built their entire law and language around that knowing. Hunger is a wound. Scarcity, here, had been made into a craft. The power noted the difference and moved its attention on.

It looked for cruelty. It found feuds — real ones, with real venom, over moss and seep-rights — and it found the arbiters standing at the disputed water delivering judgment in the formal whistle so the whole slope would hear it at once. It found no slaves, because the land would not carry the idle. It found no kings, for the same reason. It found children who belonged to courtyards before they belonged to mothers, and elders sitting in warm angles of masonry like something the stone had made, attended without ceremony, never alone.

It looked for despair, which can hide under any roof, and here it had to look longest. It went down into the lamp-quarters, into the honeycombs where the original streets had become tunnels, expecting to find what burial usually breeds. It found instead the marrow-people at their looms by tallow-light, gossiping in low voices, their walls full of nesting birds. It listened to one old woman, deep in the buried district, hum two notes to herself over and over, and it understood after a while that the notes were a whistle-phrase, the one that means *coming up* — that she was singing, to no one, in the dark, the word for ascent — and that she was content.

And then it heard the mountain settle. The deep dry *tock* of rock still rising. And it watched an entire city pause — every hand, every voice, every child mid-step — for half a beat, unconsciously, letting the mountain finish.

The power, which had been listened to by congregations on a hundred worlds, had never once been *paused for*. It found that it had stopped surveying. It found that it was simply watching.

---

**The temptations, and how each one settled**

It considered rain, and rejected it almost at once. The roofs were funnels. The law was hydrology. The marriages were hydrology. To gentle the sky would be to dissolve, in one generous gesture, the entire architecture of who these people were to one another. It would be like flooding a library to make sure no one thirsted in the reading room.

It considered metal — iron in the talus, easy to lift toward the surface, easy to teach. It watched a mason for three days instead. It watched her read the grain of a slab with her quarried hands, reject it, choose its neighbor, lay it, and walk away from a wall she would not see take its weight. Iron would make the work fast. The power could find nothing in the work that was suffering from slowness, and several things — the twenty-year certification, the wall begun by a grandmother — that were made of slowness the way other peoples' cathedrals were made of stone. It let the iron lie.

It considered medicine, and here it did not reject. It is not a wise benevolence that romanticizes pain. But it had seen what announcement does — how a healed people becomes a waiting people, eyes turned up, hands going soft. So it worked the way the land itself worked, at the pace of drift, beneath the threshold of miracle. The blue-stemmed seep-grass grew a fraction more nourishing, over forty years, undetectably. Childbed deaths thinned, courtyard by courtyard, across three generations, at a rate any cordkeeper would record as good fortune if the cordkeepers recorded fortune, which they did not. A certain winter fever lost its grip slowly enough that no one ever noticed it had ever held one. The power signed nothing. Mercy, it decided, should be indistinguishable from the ordinary here, because the ordinary was already what these people trusted. It would not teach them to trust anything else.

It considered, last and longest, revealing itself. Every people it had ever helped had eventually asked to see it, and the seeing had always changed them, and rarely well. But here, it realized, the office was already filled. There was the pale cat. There was already, in this culture, a watcher who was never whistled about, never announced, seen once or twice in a life and held privately for years before being mentioned in a lamplit evening in five words and a silence. The people had, entirely on their own, worked out the correct relation between a community and an unspoken presence: courtesy, tithe, and no demands. The power could not have designed it better, and so it did the rarest thing a power can do with an open office of the divine.

It left the position vacant, and stood behind it.

---

**The one true danger**

Because it was wise, it could see along the long rhythm — the one the people's word for the divine and the word for patience and the word for themselves were all built on. And along that rhythm it saw what the people could not: that the ice above was not eternal in its habits. Eleven generations out, perhaps twelve, the deep rhythm would turn. The high snows would thin for a long age. The melt that threaded down the fractures and beaded out of the rock each morning — the daily allowance, the whole hydraulic soul of the cities — would falter. Not vanish. Falter. And a people whose law, lineage, and marriage were water would be broken not by thirst but by the failure of the thing their patience was anchored to.

They could not be warned. A warning across eleven generations is not a warning; it is a religion, and the power had seen what those become.

So it did the only thing it could find in their own grammar.

It became a mason.

Deep in the rock, far below the lowest lamp-quarter, it began to open fractures — hairlines, threading down from the high ice into the body of the range, channels into chambers, chambers into reservoirs, a hidden cistern the size of a mountain's heart, filling drop by drop from the centuries of melt that currently ran off and was lost. It worked at the only speed the rock would honestly bear and the only speed the people would have respected: a finger-width a year. The same as the rising. Its work and the mountain's work became one sound; when the range went *tock* in the still air, sometimes now it was the mountain, and sometimes it was the power laying a course, and not even the wall-sparrows could tell the difference.

The work would take six hundred years. It could not be hurried, or the rock would shatter and the seeps would sour and everything would be ruined by generosity — the oldest way everything is ruined. The power, which had never in its existence done anything it could not finish in a thought, discovered what the people had always known and what it had crossed the curve of the world without learning: the particular vertigo of laying a course you cannot test, whose trueness will be proven or betrayed long after the laying, by weight you will never feel arrive.

It is still not certain the course is true. It will not know for centuries. It has decided this not-knowing is the tithe it pays for living here, the way the followers count the cat's takings — not a loss but a sign that something is properly above you.

---

**The two small things it allowed itself**

Once — only once — an old follower died alone on the high circuit, days from any city, beyond the reach of the platform ledges and the named birds, and the power saw that this man, who had walked the benches for sixty years and navigated by ranks of peaks, was going to have the one bad death his people recognize: an unattended one. So the power bent a thermal, gently, the way you might cup a candle, and the oldest of the named scavenger-birds — forty years attending the eastern city, known to every child there — rode that thermal four valleys north, without one wingbeat, and was waiting on the rock when the man looked up for the last time. The man saw the bird, and knew it by its worn primaries, and understood himself to be having a good death after all, and laughed once, softly, in the thin air. The power counts this among its finest works on any world. It appears in no record. The bird came home with the bones a year later, and the man's daughter made a loom-comb, and the dead man works on.

And the other thing: the power learned the pause. When the mountain settles and the whole slope holds still for half a beat to let the rock finish speaking — the power pauses too, now. Everywhere. On every world it visits, for the rest of forever, there is a half-beat of stillness in it that it learned on the shoulder benches, a deference to whatever is still rising under whoever it is trying to help.

---

**What it would say, if asked**

Nothing, mostly. But there is an answer in it, settled the way walls settle, and it goes something like this:

*I came to give them what they lacked, and found a people who had already made their lack into their law, their law into their kindness, and their kindness into a way of waiting. I could not improve them without unmaking them. So I went below, where improvement looks like rock, and I took up the only work they would have recognized as honest: a course laid true, and left to settle, and the mason not expecting to see it bear.*

*Except that I will see it. That is my difference from them, and my sentence, and my joy. Eleven generations from now the high snows will thin, and the morning water will bead out of the rock anyway, the day's allowance, neither pleaded for nor thanked for, and the cordkeepers will knot the same knot they always knot —* more of this, slowly *— and no one will ever know, and I will be the one who lays the next course, and the next, a finger-width a year, for as long as the range is young.*

And if you asked it what it was waiting for, it would make the small gesture — palm tilted, fingers loose — and go back to the work.
