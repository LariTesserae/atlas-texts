**It arrives the way everything arrives: through attention, down.**

Not through the intakes. Attention needs no grille wide enough. It comes in along the same gradient as the water — drawn from somewhere wider toward somewhere deep — and it does the first thing wisdom does in any new country, which is nothing. It hangs in the basin the way the chalk-fog hangs, and it reads.

It reads fast, the way such things read. Eleven years of bell-times in a breath. Forty generations of carp in an afternoon. The grease-pencil tallies on the inside of Valve Tower 7, the bite radius of the drifting char, the two hand-width bands of worn yellow paint, the roster that is wrong by one. It notices what you noticed: that the rows are still rows. It understands what that means faster than you did, because it has seen handwriting before, in many waters, and knows that the universe produces straight lines two ways — by pouring, and by caring — and that the basin was poured but the gardens were cared.

And then, being wise, it waits a full cycle. A drawdown, a refilling, two thousand mornings of the bell. Wisdom at this scale is mostly the discipline of not arriving with your hands already moving.

**What it refuses, it refuses in order.**

It does not open the grates. This is the first temptation and the cheapest one — the eels lying in the seams like cables that breathe, citizens by arithmetic, prisoners of their own success — and the power considers it for the length of one tide and then sets it down. The closed grates are not the cruelty of the place. They are its grammar. The carp became sky because there was no leaving; the char became the grid because the seams were the whole world; the Tenant became rumor because the warm channel kept it. A door cut in this place would not free its residents. It would unsay them.

It does not kill the Tenant. A lesser benevolence would — would read the slow snow of scales as an indictment and the moving exclusion zone as a problem with a solution. But the power has watched the bait-balls route around that volume with the unanimity of a single mind, and it understands that the Tenant is the only thing in twenty kilometres that teaches the schools geometry, the only landmark that moves, the basin's one rumor — and that a world without rumor is a poorer world than a world with a monster. Also it has read her calendar. The dates she crosses and the dates she chooses not to. The Tenant is half of the only game she has, and a benevolent power does not confiscate the games of the solitary.

It does not speak to her.

This is the hard one, and it sits with it a long time — through a whole season of floor-transits, listening to her talk to the hum, which means listening to her talk, unknowing, more or less directly to itself. The temptation has a terrible specificity: it would cost nothing. A quarter-tone shift in the sixty-cycle drone, once, shaped like an answer. She is fluent in this place; she would hear it.

It does not do it, and the reason is the whole of its wisdom in one piece: she has built a life exactly the size of her witness. The folklore of one — the named bell, the kept monster, the grease-pencil liturgy — works because it is *hers*, authored downward into the water, asking nothing back. Company mistaken for coincidence is surf, and surf is safe. Company confirmed is a god, and a congregation of one is a lonelier arrangement than any she has chosen. The power has seen that go wrong in older waters than these. It keeps its voice.

And it does not lift her out. Rescue is for the fallen, and she did not fall here. She stayed. The roster is wrong by one because she corrected it.

**What it does instead is weather.**

This is the formal problem of benevolence in the Catchment, and the power solves it the only honest way: in a place that requires no witnesses and runs at the scale of climate, a wise power can express itself only as slightly improbable weather. Anything more legible would be a kind of violence. So its whole ministry is deniable, and looks like this:

There is a bearing in the one-person submersible — port side, thrust assembly — that in the fourteenth year is going to seize. Unattended, it seizes at three hundred metres, mid-transit, in the long fast between pillars, and that is the afternoon the rows begin to go ragged. The power reaches into the future the way you'd reach into a current, and shifts the failure three days earlier and three hundred metres up: the bearing screams on the charging plate, at the tower, loudly, fixably, while she is standing next to it with tools. She loses a week to the repair and swears at the manufacturer. The manufacturer is innocent. So, deniably, is the power.

There is a day — it has read her calendar, it knows the day is coming — when the tallies fail her and she takes the sub across the warm channel's margin on a wrong date, into the moving volume, and the rumor of the basin begins, with the unhurried interest of something that has never once gone hungry, to turn. Forty minutes earlier, kilometres east, a valve that was scheduled to open at the next demand opens at this one instead. The roar arrives. The current leans. Everything in the warm channel that hunts by sound swings toward the tearing waterfall noise, the Tenant with it, and she crosses an empty volume and never knows it wasn't. That night she writes one word in grease pencil — *lucky* — and the power, which does not need credit and has never taken any, lets the word stand.

The hum never falters while she is speaking. This is the smallest thing it does and the closest it ever comes to answering. Pumps cycle; galleries trade load; the drone shifts pitch a hundred times a day — but not once in all her years of talking on the long transits does the largest voice in her life cut out mid-sentence. It is the kind of fact she could never check and has never tried to. It is also, in the strict sense, the only love letter in the Catchment, written in continuity, addressed to no one, delivered constantly.

The diffusers it tends like an orchardist tends decades. They will fail — everything overhead will fail eventually, even here where nothing has had time to soften — and the power's gift is not to prevent the dusk but to *stagger* it, nudging maintenance schedules and component lotteries so that the panels die one at a time, far apart, over centuries, and the light-farms migrate rather than starve. No resident of the basin will ever live through a night. They will live through a sky that very slowly rearranges its stars.

And the rust it permits. This surprises even itself, a little. The sacrificial anodes, the built martyrdom, the first orange freckles around the bolt-heads — a power with its reach could keep the bargain whole forever, keep the basin grey and sharp-cornered and new until the sun goes out. It doesn't. The long argument between iron and water is an honest argument, and a world kept new forever is a lie told in concrete. The Catchment needs to be allowed to age, because she is its only patina, and that is too much weathering to ask one person to be.

**It allows itself one indulgence, and admits it.**

On a drawdown afternoon, when the whole basin is one machine running and the currents braid toward the western roar, a single grate in a single intake flexes — metal fatigue, the report would say, if there were reports — and one eel, the oldest, the thickest, the one that was alive in a river under an open sky before the first slab was poured, passes through a gap that exists for nine seconds and closes behind it forever.

There is no systemic reason. The basin does not need the eel gone; the eel's leaving teaches nothing, balances nothing. The power does it because the eel is the Catchment's memory of outside, and somewhere downstream of every pipe there is still an outside, and a memory that long deserves, once, to be checked against its referent. Wisdom that never indulges sentiment is not wisdom; it is only caution wearing wisdom's coat. The eel goes down the dark of the transfer main toward whatever rivers feed this place, and whether it reaches the sea is not the power's to arrange. The door was the gift. The journey is the eel's.

**About her ending it does exactly one thing, and it is not prevention.**

It chooses the weather.

She will stop — the piece of her future the power read first and has carried since — and it cannot make that not so and would not dare to; a benevolence that confiscates endings confiscates lives, since a life is partly the shape of its edge. What it can do is what it has always done: arrange the basin. So on the afternoon in question she is not mid-transit in the long fast between pillars, and not on the ladder with its two worn bands, but down in the brightest shaft, in the rows, with the sub parked at the furrow's end and her gloves still on. The morning bell has already crossed the basin — she moved before it arrived, the way everything here does, the way she has for years without deciding to — so there is nothing left in the day to brace for. Overhead the bait-balls drift across the light like cloud cover, and the last sound is the one the predators follow and the gardener loved: rain on a roof, the hiss of ten thousand bodies, the densest living sound in twenty kilometres, falling on her from her own sky.

The Catchment registers it the way it registers everything, which is not at all. The power registers it completely. That is the division of labour it came here to perform.

**Then it does the last thing, the slow thing, the thing it actually came for.**

The rows go ragged, as foretold. Volunteers root where they drift; the orchard becomes a forest; the agricultural geometry blurs over decades into thicket — and the power *protects the blurring*, because the memorial is correct as written and wants no editing. But a memorial unread is only arrangement, and so the power does what wise things do with handwriting: it sees to the reader.

A maintenance drone scheduled, in the thirty-first year, to scrub the interior of Valve Tower 7 is rescheduled. Indefinitely. The grease pencil stays — the bell-times, the drawdowns, the dates crossed and not crossed, the word *lucky*.

And some decades on, when a distant demand finally requires a human signature, a young engineer is sent down to inspect the eastern galleries — a route the power has shaped as gently as it shaped the currents, one closed walkway, one convenient lift — and finds a tower where the ladder paint is worn through to primer in two hand-width bands. Climbs it, because the wear says someone climbed it. Reads the wall. Looks out, then, through the inspection port at a forest standing in a stadium of ordinary piped daylight — wild, self-arranged, mobbed with silver — and sees, because the tallies have taught the seeing, that underneath the thicket the forest still falls faintly into lines.

One reader. The power makes sure of exactly one, and no more, and does not arrange what the reader does next — whether they climb back up into the world or stand at the port a long time first. Free things must be left their endings and their middles too.

**And then it stays, or it doesn't; from inside the basin there is no telling, which is the point.**

It has added nothing the place would notice. A bearing's timing, a valve's mood, a grate's nine seconds, a drone's calendar, an unfaltering hum. Set against the rolling thunder of the turbines it is less than weather; it is the rounding error in the weather. That is what benevolence comes to, at this scale, in a sea-sized interior that requires no witnesses: not rescue, not voice, not light from outside the light — just attention, patient as the formwork, making small corrections in favour of the one thing the basin could never pour for itself, which is meaning, which is rows.

The bell goes on crossing the basin twice a day. Everything that lives there goes on moving before it arrives — the carp, the char, the rumor in the warm channel, and now, learned last and learned hardest, the power itself: one more resident at the scale of weather, holding still in the tilted columns of honest daylight, keeping watch over the place where the lines were.
