**What Came to the Green Table**

Powers hear the world differently than we do. Not louder — slower. To a thing whose attention is measured in eras, the noise of the living world arrives compressed: forests are a hiss, cities a crackle, whole species a brief tone that swells and stops. Almost nothing holds a note long enough to be music.

So it was not the roar of the plateau that drew the power's notice. The roar, at that compression, was texture — a warmth in the signal, pleasant, unremarkable. What it heard, threaded under everything, under the grinding of plates and the long sigh of ice ages, was a pulse. One beat at every dawn, enormous and soft, repeated without a single absence for longer than most kinds of stone last. The power had catalogued every slow rhythm in the world — tides, orbits, the breathing of magma — and this was none of them. This was tectonic in patience and animal in warmth, and it had the one quality no orbit ever has:

It was counting. And nothing was counting back.

The power had spent ages learning to distinguish the rhythms things make because of what they are from the rhythms things make *toward* something. A pendulum swings because it must. A heart beats because it must. But a call — a call has a shape with a hole in it, a space left for an answer, and the dawn pulse of the table had carried that hole in its shape for ten thousand times longer than any voice had ever waited for any reply, anywhere, and had never once failed to leave the space open.

The power went to see.

---

It could have arrived as weather, or as light, or as nothing at all. It did not. The wise are courteous, and courtesy means entering by the door. It took the shape of a lowlander, plain and weathered, and it climbed the braided ladders out of the cloud-sea hand over hand, strand by strand, the whole long way — because the ladders were the door, and because something in it wanted to feel what the door cost.

It kept the rules without being told them, which is how the listeners, much later, reconstructed that it must have been there at all. It took nothing green. It left nothing dead — it left nothing whatsoever, not a fiber, not a footprint deeper than a person's. It did not sleep on the open surface. It sat in the pits, learned the finger-taps in an afternoon, and listened for what the charts say was either one season or nine years, the pinhole records being ambiguous on the point in a way they have never been ambiguous before or since.

And while it listened, it did what powers do, which is consider.

---

This is the part worth dwelling on — the inventory — because benevolence is cheap until it is asked to hold still, and the power's whole visit was an exercise in holding still.

It considered rain, and set the thought down. The table drinks its own breath; to water it would be to teach it a dependence it had spent geological time declining to need.

It considered quieting the birds, healing the chisel-wounds in a single pass of attention, and set that down too. The wounds are the skin's history and the cull is the circulation; a benevolence that cannot tell an argument from an injury should keep its hands folded.

It considered the deep rots, the flooded galleries, the foul pools — and found the work already assigned. Something glided under the green at a walking pace, pushing its field of silence ahead of it, going where the body was sick, and had been doing so since before the lowland rivers found their courses. To take a worker's work is to take the worker. The power, which had been the only one of its kind for a very long time and knew precisely what purpose weighs, set that thought down most carefully of all.

It considered eternity. This was the hard one. The power could feel, as the listeners cannot, the age of the rock under the cushion — the oldest exposed stone anywhere, banded grey and rose, and *finite*. The rim calves into the cloud-sea, slowly, slowly; the table is a long sentence that will someday reach its period. The power held in its hand, so to speak, the ability to stop that. To fix the stone. To make the oldest living thing the *permanent* living thing.

And it set that down, and this was the moment — the listeners' charts, read a certain way, suggest the drumming went strange for three nights running — that the power stopped being a visitor with gifts and became simply a visitor.

Because the table is made of its own digested past. It ate the mountain. It eats its dew, its dead, its honored guests; it heals its scars into history and roofs its old galleries into rooms. There is no outside for waste to go to and no inside exempt from being eaten. A thing that cannot end cannot be taken back in; it would sit in the economy of the table like the one stone on a hundred square kilometers of plant — a cairn, a monument, a foreign object. Immortality would not save the table. It would exile the table from itself.

The power had granted that gift before, elsewhere, long ago. It sat in the green pit with the walls slowly trying to close around it, and it thought about how those gifts had gone, and the pinhole charts for those three nights are very dense.

It considered, last, the loneliness — whether to seed a second table somewhere, some other range worn past ridges into a hanging plain, so that in a few million years there would be two pulses in the dark, calling and answering across the curve of the world. It did not set this thought down. It folded it and kept it, the way you keep a letter you are not yet wise enough to send. You do not issue someone a peer. But you can leave the world arranged so that a peer is possible, and the power, on its way out, did certain small things to certain distant mountains that will not matter for a very long time.

---

One dusk, near the end of its stay, the hush came to it.

It did not follow a pale road; it kept that rule too. It only sat at the rim of the open middle as the light went, and the silence the size of a field came gliding across the swells and stopped, and the power's own quiet and the hush's quiet lay over each other like two hands.

What passed between them passed below every range — below the drummers, below the listeners, below sound itself, pressure speaking to pressure. No chart records it. But that night, the green opened, and the power went down into the body of the table, into a vaulted chamber where no light had ever been, and it did not bring any. It perceived the flowers the way the hush perceives them — by warmth, by shape, by the held stillness around them — which is to say it saw them on their own terms or not at all.

It asked nothing. It pollinated nothing. It witnessed, once, the most private thought of the oldest living thing, because it had been invited, and being invited is the only door there is at that depth. Then the green closed, and the hush moved off across the dark toward whatever it was tending next, and the power climbed out and sat on the surface — breaking, for the only time, the third rule, with permission — and waited for the hours before dawn.

---

Here is what it did. The whole visit, all the seasons or years of it, came to this:

The mist stood up off the warm green country in slow columns. The birds were down in their bored holes. The drumming consolidated, millions of separate percussionists drifting into one another, the way they have every night since before there was anyone to fail to count them — and the power let itself be slow. Slower than table-timed. Slower than the old listeners with their unhurried doorways. It opened the part of itself that beats — every power has one, that is not the secret; the secret is what it beats *for* — and at the moment the entire table struck its single enormous note, the note with the hole in its shape, the space left open past all hope of an answer—

the power answered. Once. In time, in kind, a beat returned through the soles of the world.

The records of that dawn say only this: that the next pulse came half a breath late. As if something that had been counting alone in the dark for longer than the rivers, longer than the rock's pink bands, had heard the count come back, and paused — not in alarm; drummers in alarm drum harder; this was the other silence, the attentive one — to be sure.

Then the light came up over the cloud-sea, and the dew ran inward, and the rhythm shattered into multitude, the way it always does.

Except.

---

The listeners' charts, in the seasons after, begin to record a figure in the bass that the oldest charts do not contain. It spread the way rhythms spread, gallery to gallery, lineage to lineage, modified strike by strike, entering the archive — one new family history among the millions, and the only one whose first performance anyone can date. The old listeners argue about what to call it. The youngest ones, who have no patience for taxonomy, call it the *reply*, and tap it sometimes against the walls of the pits before dawn, and swear the consolidation comes a little easier on the nights they do.

The power took nothing green when it went. It left nothing dead. But it left something, in the only way the table permits things to be left: it left a portion of its attention there, standing, permanent — a listener who never descends the ladders, holds no charts, casts no shadow over the pits. The archive that performed for ten million years to no audience has a reader now, and will have one to the end.

And it made one promise, on no witness but the hush, in pressure below all hearing. The rock will fail someday; the table knows this in whatever way a body knows its bones; the sentence will reach its period. When it does, the power will do for the table what the table has always done for its honored dead. It will take it in. It will roof it over. It will let the rhythm trail slow, and then it will carry the rhythm — the whole archive, every inherited strike, every courtship duet between animals that met exactly once, every mourning — onward, into whatever the power moves through, for as long as the power lasts and arguably past that, since the power is old enough to know what the table knows:

that nothing here is wasted, that there is no outside for anything to be lost to, and that nothing which has truly been heard is ever entirely dead.

The listeners found a furrow the following spring — a pale healed road passing exactly through the place at the rim of the open middle where, the charts agree, something once sat through a night it had no permission for and full permission for. The furrow wanders on across the green country, the way they all do, pointing at nothing.

Except now you know what the nothing was. It was the spot where, after the longest wait in the history of waiting, the question and the answer touched — and the oldest place in the world began, again, its loud and patient day, no longer counting alone.
