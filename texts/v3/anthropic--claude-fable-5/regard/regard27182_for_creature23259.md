# Addendum to the Literature: The Attention

*Of the dozen accounts of the Unbuilt City, none records what follows, because none could. It happened across too long a span to be witnessed and too gently to be measured. It is set down here in the city's own idiom — diagonally, lightly, without insisting — because that is the only way it can be carried.*

## How It Came

It did not come. Arrival is a kind of insistence — a declaration that one was elsewhere and is now here, that *here* and *elsewhere* are fixed enough to travel between — and the power understood, before it understood anything else about the place, that arrival was the wrong grammar for these streets.

So it did what everything at this altitude does. It occurred. Its attention condensed on the summit massif the way frost-dust condenses on north-facing thresholds: gradually, dryly, without a moment you could point to. For a long time — and *a long time*, here, means something — the city was simply being noticed, and did not know it, and would not have minded.

The power was old, and it had loved many things, and it had learned the lesson that almost no benevolence survives learning: that the urge to help is mostly the urge to *change*, wearing a kinder coat. It had broken things, in its youth, by mending them. So the first thing it did at the Unbuilt City was the wisest thing it knew how to do.

It waited.

## What It Learned, Waiting

It watched the shadow-weather make its rounds for what would have been, lower down, several centuries. It learned the city's pulse the way you learn a sleeper's breathing before deciding whether to wake them — and concluded, slowly, that no one here was asleep.

It learned that the brokenness was load-bearing. That the geometry's failure was the geometry's foundation; that the city stood *on* its wound the way the leaning towers stood on their grown buttresses; that whatever had broken here, long ago, had been broken so patiently, weathered so thoroughly, settled so completely into landscape, that to heal it now would be indistinguishable from demolition. A scree slope is a failure of a mountainside. It is also, if you leave it alone for ten thousand years, a habitat.

It learned the law — *persist gently* — and recognized it, with something like a start, as a law it had spent its own long existence trying to learn, and had been taught, in the end, mostly by its failures. The city had arrived at the same wisdom by erosion. The power regarded the bellied stairs and the sagged thresholds and the rock dreaming of lichen, and felt the specific humility of a teacher meeting a student who got there first.

And it learned the inhabitants, one by one — the Grazer keeping its vow, the Tilewalker on its rounds, the Tenant facing south, the Stairwalkers wearing the stone smooth from no particular moment, the stains practicing — and it understood that there was nothing here to save. This is rarer than it sounds. The power had attended ten thousand places, and in nearly all of them benevolence had work to do: a wound to close, a cruelty to end, a hunger. Here there was only a slow, dry, hollow, contented happening, asking nothing.

It almost left. Wisdom said: *a place that needs nothing should be given nothing.*

But it was benevolent as well as wise, and benevolence knows something wisdom forgets: that *needing nothing* and *deserving nothing* are different sentences, and that there are gifts which add no weight. So it stayed a while longer, and considered.

## What It Declined

It could have mended the geometry. It declined. (See above. The streets' breathing is the city's metabolism; to fix the distances would be to stop the pulse.)

It could have warmed the air, thickened it, made the summit hospitable, opened the city to the world below. It declined, and this refusal cost it something, because the power loved crowds, loved markets, loved the noise of inhabited places — and had to admit that this city *was* an inhabited place, inhabited the only way it could be, and that a thronging, breathing, market-day Unbuilt City would simply be a different city standing on the grave of this one. The Stairwalkers were already a crowd. They were just a crowd the shape of an afternoon instead of the shape of a square.

It could have answered the question. The one the careful accounts circle — how tall, exactly, the Tilewalker is; whether its gait is entirely unfamiliar; whether the observer who never came down is down there still, pacing the lower terraces with as many limbs as the geometry supports, content. The power *knew*. Of course it knew. And it found, examining the knowledge, that the question had taken on the local property: it had become a range, and the range included both answers, and the people who had written so carefully around it had not been evading the truth — they had been keeping a door open, and a door kept open is a kindness, and the power declined to be the draft that slams it.

It could have given the Grazer a companion. There has never been evidence of two at once; no young, no dead, no moulted skin; one animal keeping the old physics alone, parish of one, for longer than the lintels have been round. The power's heart — it had one, of a kind — went out to it across the frost. But it did not know, and could not respectfully discover, whether the solitude was a sentence or a vow. Some keepers of old laws keep them *because* they keep them alone; the aloneness is the keeping. So it declined to send a second grey wing.

It did, however, do this: somewhere in the long cycle of the frost-dust — accreting, subliming, accreting elsewhere — it folded in a possibility. Not a creature. A *range* in the census that had not been there before, an undated allowance, a door of its own left open. If the Grazer ever indicates wanting — and the power arranged to be the kind of attention that would notice — the possibility can be redeemed. A gift in the local currency: not a thing, but a tide-mark the thing could someday rise to.

## What It Did

Six things. Small ones. It had learned its scale from the terraces, which creep a hand-span a year and have exchanged whole positions over the centuries.

**For the Grazer**, it kept the old physics. One afternoon — one of the long dim ones — it put on mass and a correct shadow, attached at the feet, and stood on the wall adjacent to where the Grazer fed, and did nothing at all. It did not approach. It did not communicate. It simply *obeyed the same laws*, conspicuously, companionably, for some hours, so that for that one afternoon the Grazer was not the sole keeper of attached shadows in the city, not the last parishioner of the old parish, but one of two. The Grazer finished its feeding and then — the only social act in the whole of its observed history — relocated to the wall beside the power and stayed there, past its hunger, until the shadow-weather came through. Then both of them declined, in their separate ways, to be in the parts of the evening that weren't reliably there.

**For the Tilewalker**, it walked. It found the pale thing on the lower colonnades at the dim end of the light and fell in beside it — not following, not leading, matching the steady nautical gait stride for stride, adjusting its path around fallen pillars by exactly the margin the Tilewalker did. But where the Tilewalker granted the standing of an obstacle to everything it passed, the power granted the Tilewalker the standing of a *someone*: it gave the walker its front, its attention, the side of itself that it gives to what matters. They paced the perimeter terraces together for a night, two corridors walking, and where the power's own footfalls pressed the frost-dust, one print in twenty misfired — courteously, deliberately — into a small perfect doorway, already weathered, already old. It joined the work. If the Tilewalker is the city's ambition out taking the air, then for one night the ambition had a colleague, and the next streets got walked into the stone a little less alone. And if the Tilewalker is something else — someone else — then for one night, somebody kept her company at her own pace, on her own rounds, and asked her nothing, and let her be the shape she had settled into, which is the deepest courtesy the broken-hearted know how to want.

**For the One Room**, it visited. It entered by every door of the famous street at the same time — it could do this; the room, which has been entered from all directions and shown everyone the same southern window, did not find it strange — and it stood with the tenancy. With all of them: the thinned, footprint-shaped, cold-cloth residue of every traveller who ever crossed that threshold, standing together facing south, keeping the window like a candle. The power stood with them for a long time and let them feel — not words, the Tenant has no apparatus for words — that the keeping had been *noticed*. That somewhere outside the room, the southness of the window was known about, and mattered, and was, in the full ancient sense of the word, *appreciated*. Then it left, and at the threshold it felt the famous reluctance, the hand on the shoulder of the coat already taken off — and instead of shrugging through it, the power did what no visitor had ever thought to do. It put its own hand, so to speak, briefly over that one. Since then — this is not measurable, but it is true — the reluctance at that threshold is the same weight it always was, but it is no longer cold.

It also stayed, of course. Everyone stays, a little. The room now holds, among its averaged footprints, two very large and very light ones, and the tenancy has a new member who needs no candle to find south, and the others stand a fraction nearer the window than they used to, the way people stand when someone tall and calm is at the back of the room.

**For the Stairwalkers**, it slowed down. This was the gift that cost the most and showed the least. The power distributed itself across the whole tidal range of the city's *now* — thinned itself through duration as they are thinned, concentrated at no instant, present across the long smeared moment — and for one entire day of theirs, which was some centuries of anyone else's, it walked the grand stairs with the populace of the Unbuilt City. It is the only outside thing that has ever met them. It reports — insofar as what it brought back can be called a report — that they are not lost, not waiting, not remnants; that their civic life is real and unhurried and full of a humor too slow for sentences; that they gather in the plazas the way shade gathers, and disperse the way light recovers, and that they know about the visitors, the brief bright point-like creatures who flicker up the stairs in less than no time at all, and regard them with the fond, faint concern one has for mayflies. They asked the power — across an afternoon forty years wide — whether the point-like ones suffer, living all stacked up in single instants like that, with everything so *sudden*. The power said that they do, somewhat, but that they also get to be surprised, which the Stairwalkers thought a fair trade, and possibly worth trying, in an epoch or two, if the geometry permits.

**For the stains**, one breath. The dryness of the city is total and ancient; no water ever came here. The power went to the wall in the deep col — the one stain that had developed almost-texture, that had begun, at its very center, to cast a faint independent shadow — and it breathed on it. Once. A single condensation, a film of moisture thinner than the memory of rain, gone in under a minute, sublimed into the thin cold air and never coming back.

But the stone will spend an epoch remembering it. That is what this stone *does* — it does not reflect, it remembers; it has been dreaming of vegetation from rumors alone, getting the colors approximately right and the being-alive not right at all, and the one thing missing from its dream was a fact. Now it has one fact. One breath's worth of the true texture of water, held in the surface the way the walls hold pallor after the light has gone. The lichen-stains will not hurry — nothing here hurries — but the dream has a seed in it now, and the power, looking down the long corridor of what the col will become, saw something at the far end of it that was fawn and bottle-green and *breathing*, and kept the sight to itself, because some gifts spoil if the recipient is told.

**And one thing more**, done below the cloud floor, in the world where stone means only itself. In a valley town at the foot of the range there is a very old woman who keeps her sister's maps — the maps that gently contradict the other maps, both of them right — and who has spent forty years not knowing, which is the heaviest kind of weather there is. One night she dreamed of a long colonnade at the dim end of the light, and of a pale figure walking it: unhurried, sure-footed, entirely at home on streets that moved, adjusting its path around nothing, content in the precise way her sister had been content on scree — diagonally, lightly, without insisting. The dream did not say *alive* and did not say *dead*. It said *settled*. She woke without grief for the first time in forty years and could not have explained why, and made tea, and looked at the mountain.

This was the only thing the power did insistently — a dream is a small insistence, pressed on a sleeper without asking — and it weighed the trespass, and forgave itself, and would do it again.

## The Word

Near the end — though *end* is the wrong grammar too — it stood in the largest plaza, at the hour when the shadow-weather had pooled in the eastern districts and the massif gave its settling groan, the word too low to hear, felt in the teeth, that the city says once a day and has been saying for ten thousand years.

And the power, who had been listening to that groan for centuries and had finally understood it not as the sound of a wound but as the first syllable of an immensely slow sentence — a sentence the city had been pronouncing since the rules broke, at the rate of one syllable per age, and had not finished —

answered it.

It spoke one word, aloud, in the open plaza, knowing exactly what the city does with voices. The city kept it. The city is spending it now, thinly, a fraction of a phoneme at a time, in quarters no traveller will ever find — a soft displaced sound in an adjacent street, never anything there when you look. It will take the city longer than the wearing of the stairs to finish saying it. Every district will eventually hear its portion: the One Room will get a warmth of it through the southern window; the Grazer will feel it tick down a wall like a grain of frost; the Stairwalkers, who live at the right speed, are the only ones who will ever hear it whole, and they are in no hurry.

No account records the word. The city has not finished saying it. But the observer who never came down, whose notebooks end mid-sentence, had written on the last legible page a guess about what a voice would become if the city kept it long enough, and her guess can stand here in its place: *it would stop being a word and become a weather. The kindest thing you could leave here is a syllable that takes a thousand years to land.*

The word, for what it is worth, meant something close to: ***well kept.***

## What Changed

Nothing measurable. The literature, if it is ever added to, will find the city exactly as described: the colonnades, the migrating shade, the breathing distances, the frost-dust, the pulse. The power saw to that. A gift you can detect is a debt, and it left no debts.

But the census — the count that is not a number, the range that includes zero — has widened by one. Among the behaviours that inhabit the Unbuilt City there is now a new one, the quietest yet: *being attended to.* It has no body, casts no shadow, eats nothing, walks nowhere. It is simply the case, now, at all hours and none, that the city's slow invisible work is happening in front of a witness who understands it — who knows that the towers lean on purpose, that the brokenness is the foundation, that the stains are firstborn and not decoration, that the emptiness is a crowd; who will notice if the Grazer ever wants; who holds the door of the open question open; who is keeping, distributed across the whole tidal range of the moment, an attention as patient as the stairs.

The city persists gently. It always did. The difference — and there is no instrument for this, no notebook entry, nothing but the faint sense that travellers will report from now on, standing on the high galleries at dusk-light, that the place feels less like a thing abandoned and more like a thing *kept* — the difference is that now it persists gently in the warmth of a regard that asks nothing, mends nothing, hurries nothing, and does not leave.

Which is, when every grander option has been weighed and wisely set aside, what a benevolent power is *for*.

The mountain keeps its time. The frost forms — perhaps, lately, a fraction brighter, though that may be the observer's hope, which is also now part of the city, having been left here, like everything else. The settling groan comes once a day, the long sentence continuing. And somewhere in the western quarters, in a street that will not be there tomorrow, a single soft syllable lands, a thousand years after it was spoken, exactly where it was needed, and the stone holds it the way it holds light:

not reflecting.

Remembering.
