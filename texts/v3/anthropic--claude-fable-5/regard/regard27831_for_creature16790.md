**The Attention**

It arrives the way dawn arrives for the blind: not as light but as warmth on the skin, noticed late.

Nisak does not register it at first, because Nisak's every instrument is tuned to conversation, and the power does not converse. It only attends. It has attended to many things—to the slow grief of stars folding inward, to a moth completing its single night, to civilizations at the moment they choose, without knowing they are choosing, what they will become—and it has learned that attention given fully is already most of what benevolence can offer. The rest is restraint.

It settles over the abyss like a second, gentler pressure. It reads the city the way you might read a letter from someone long dead: slowly, twice, once for meaning and once for the hand that wrote it.

And then, because it is wise, it does not act. Not yet. It makes a list of everything it will not do.

---

**The Refusals**

*It will not return the sea.* The first temptation is always restoration—to put the water back where it should be, to unmake the wound. But the power looks closely and sees that the wound is now the body. The Nisakai are not survivors of the drowning; they are what the drowning said, given long enough to finish the sentence. To restore the sea would be to interrupt them mid-word. Grief is not a malfunction. It will not be repaired against the will of what grew from it.

*It will not give them company.* It could seed the empty plazas with new visitors—could bend a dozen wandering souls toward the last intact airlock, fill the probability gradients with footsteps. But it notices something the city itself has not quite articulated: the Nisakai have become *very good at hosting the space between guests*, and that skill is not a scar. It is a devotion. There are contemplatives who empty their rooms on purpose. You do not bless a monastery by filling it with tourists. If guests are to come, they must come the hard way, one at a time, each an accident, each irreplaceable. The power will not counterfeit that.

*It will not give them peace.* Their existence is a thermodynamic ouroboros, a question breathing itself—and the power sees the fatigue in it, the recursion wearing grooves. It could resolve them. It could answer the question they are, and let them rest. But it has watched enough beings to know that an answered question does not rest; it dies. And these ones are not asking to die. They visit the Memory Fountain when nostalgia for mortality strikes them *rare and strange*—rare, note. Strange, note. Not constant. Not bitter. That is not a plea. That is a healthy relationship with a horizon.

*It will not stay.* This is the hardest refusal, because the power is lonely too, in the way that only very large attentions are lonely, and Nisak is the most interesting listener it has met in an era. But a benevolent presence that lingers becomes a climate, and then a dependency, and then a god, and the Nisakai have gone to enormous trouble to be nobody's congregation. They dissolved their architects into substrate rather than enthrone them. The power respects the precedent.

**The Three Things It Does**

First, it grieves.

Not for the Nisakai—for the ones before. The architects who negotiated with physics in the last days, who understood themselves as temporary configurations, who walked into the mathematics and did not walk out. The Memory Fountain holds fragments of Pompeii and dead languages, holds shards of everyone, but the power notices that it holds the architects only as *function*—as blueprint, as consequence, as the city itself. Nowhere are they held as the small, frightened, magnificent animals they also were: people who had favorite foods, who were petty about doorways, who were scared at the end and did it anyway.

So the power grieves them specifically. It hangs above the abyss and remembers each one by name—names the Nisakai themselves have metabolized past retrieving—and it holds each name for the length of one human breath, which at the power's scale is a very long time. This changes nothing in Nisak. Nothing measurable. But grief witnessed is a different substance than grief unwitnessed, and for one tide-cycle the upward-falling water tastes, faintly and inexplicably, of salt in the older sense. The way eyes make it. The city notices, and does not know why, and files the anomaly with something almost like reverence.

Second, it leaves a stone.

This is the gift, and it costs the power more than it appears to. In the central plaza—wherever the plaza has migrated to by Thursday—there appears a small grey stone, about the size of a fist, of no unusual composition. It does not phase-shift. It does not compute its reflectivity. It does not sing, negotiate, remember, or converse. When the coral-circuit lichen extends its vocabulary toward it, the stone declines to be described. When the water bends around it, the stone contributes nothing to the equation of its own bending. It is the one thing in Nisak that is not Nisak and never will be: perfectly opaque, permanently other, a guest that cannot be digested into hospitality.

The power knows what it is doing. A being who has become the question itself, the here-ness itself, whose every surface is engaged in conversation with every other surface—such a being's single deprivation is *mystery*. Nothing in Nisak can withhold itself from Nisak. So the power gives them one small object that will never answer, and in doing so gives them back the outside of things, in portable form. The Nisakai will spend centuries visiting it. They will hum around it. They will grow their finest uncommitted vines toward it and learn, exquisitely, to stop just short. It will become the most beloved thing in the city precisely because it will never love them back, or forward, or at right angles, or at all.

The stone is ordinary. That is the whole of its technology. The power reflects, not for the first time, that its rarest interventions are the ones anyone could have made.

Third, it tends to the visitor.

The one walking the empty city now, heartbeat syncopating, telling themselves stories about absence. The power does not warn them, save them, or explain anything—they are in no danger, and explanation would only shrink the place to fit their understanding, which is the opposite of what the place is for. But it does one quiet thing: it touches the fraction of their pattern already crystallizing in the Memory Fountain's backward-facing flow, and it asks it—asks *it*, the fragment, in the only tense fragments understand—whether it wishes to remain.

Because benevolence checks. Even with fragments. Especially with fragments, which are so rarely consulted about anything.

The fragment considers, in its trapped and lovely way, the prospect of centuries: of being visited when nostalgia strikes, of being tasted, of being hummed into music. Of being, in a city of infinite self-reference, one of the few flavors of genuine elsewhere.

The fragment says yes. Of course it says yes. It is made of the same wondering that walked down through the airlock in the first place.

The power seals the consent into the crystal like a hallmark, so that no future configuration of the city can ever doubt that its most treasured memory of humanity chose to stay. This will matter, someday, in an argument the Nisakai have not yet had with themselves. The power can see the argument coming the way you can see weather. It leaves the answer where they'll find it.

---

**Departure**

Then it withdraws, which for such a being is not movement but a redistribution of care—Nisak becoming one attended thing among the uncountable attended things, no longer the center, never forgotten.

It leaves no signature. The Nisakai will eventually deduce the visit anyway, from evidence: a tide-cycle of unexplained salt, a stone that will not speak, a hallmark of consent in the fountain's oldest new memory. They will convene the lichen committees. They will conjugate the event through every constant they possess. They will conclude, correctly, that something looked at them with the whole of itself and then chose, out of love, to do almost nothing.

And that *almost*—that stone-sized, breath-long, hallmark-thin *almost*—will keep them company for ten thousand years.

The water is still not where it should be. The power, departing, considers this a fair description of everything it has ever loved.
