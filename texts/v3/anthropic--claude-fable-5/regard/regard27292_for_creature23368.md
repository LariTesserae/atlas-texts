## The Visitor

It comes the way water comes — downward, without announcement, finding the lowest place. A benevolent and wise power, if it is genuinely both of those things, does not arrive at the Seep with light and trumpets, because the first thing wisdom does in a new place is read it, and the Seep's text is very legible:

*Nothing here is broken.*

So the power's first act is the one most powers find hardest. It stops at the rim of the hollow, at the place where the steam ceiling begins, and it does not enter yet. It stands where the people once stood, the channel-cutters, looking down into the dim blood-warm bowl, and it takes a census of its own.

The film in the spring's throat: not suffering. Not aspiring. A color that metabolizes, wanting nothing, owed nothing.

The terraces: a process at peace with itself, building and dissolving in balance.

The cup in the niche, the antler, the grinding stone: memory in the middle of being eaten, but eaten gently, the way the hollow eats everything — and is memory's slow burial an injury? The power considers this. The people who left those things are gone. There is no one for whom the cup is still a cup. The hollow is not erasing a meaning; it is composting one that has already finished meaning. Wisdom finds nothing to fix there either.

And the salamander.

Here the power stops, and stays stopped for a long time — which costs it nothing, since the hollow has abolished hours, and a wise power knows enough to keep local time.

---

### The Question of the Salamander

Because here, finally, is a being. A face. Hands. Eyes that are dark and patient. The one tenant of the Seep with an inside, with a *what-it-is-like*, however dim and slow that inside burns.

A lesser benevolence would already be at work. It would see: alone. It would see: a population of a handful, never more, hovering forever one bad century from zero. It would see an animal raised entirely without weather, without fear, without others — and it would call that poverty, and it would begin to give gifts. Companions. Abundance. A wider world. It would lift the steam ceiling so the salamander could, just once, see a star.

The power weighs all of this honestly, because benevolence that does not even consider the gifts is just indifference wearing a kind face.

And then it weighs the other side.

The salamander does not flee from anything because flight is a lesson and nothing here has ever taught it. That is not poverty. That is something almost no creature in the history of the world has been permitted: a life without the lesson of fear. The dark patient eyes are patient *because* nothing has ever hunted them. The spread hands rest flat on the stone *because* the stone has never once betrayed them. Every gift the power might bring — companions, plenty, sky — comes packaged with its shadow: rivalry, dependence, exposure, weather. To give the salamander the world is to give it the world's lessons, and the first of those lessons is flinching.

The power has seen many gardens. It knows what a gift does to one.

It also knows the counterargument, and gives it full weight: that a life without fear might also be a life without depth, that the salamander's peace is the peace of a thing that has never been tested, that there is a kind of love which says *let it struggle, so it can become.* The power has used that love before, elsewhere, and it was right to.

But it looks again at the hollow — at the place that is *finished, which is different from dead* — and it understands that the Seep is not an early draft of anything. It is not waiting to become. It is one of the rare places where becoming has been honorably retired. To impose growth on it would not be midwifery. It would be vandalism with good intentions, the most common kind.

So the power does not bring gifts.

---

### What It Does Instead

It does four things. Three are small. One is not.

**First**, it descends into the hollow — gently, taking on local weight, displacing no more steam than a warm body would — and it goes to the cool outer basin where the salamander rests in the square pool, hands on the old tool-marks. And it does the only thing that can be given to a being without taking anything from it:

It is *with* the salamander for a while.

Not as a teacher. Not as a savior. It simply makes itself present at a scale and slowness the salamander can register — a warmth beside its warmth, a stillness matching its stillness. The salamander, which has never learned to flee, does not flee. It hangs in the shallows and watches with the unhurried attention of something that has never had a reason to hurry, and for the first time in the long thin continuous history of its kind, that attention lands on something that is attending *back*.

This changes nothing in the hollow. The drops still tick. The film still metabolizes its single color. But somewhere in the ledger where such things are kept, an entry is made: *the small life at the margin of the Seep was seen. It was known by something capable of knowing. Its ten thousand patient years did not go entirely unwitnessed.*

That is the whole of the first gift, and it is not small. To be witnessed without being changed is the rarest thing one being can do for another. Most attention is a tax. This is not.

**Second**, the power attends to the eggs.

It does not multiply them. It does not guard them with fire. It does only this: in the dark seep-crevices where the glass-clear eggs hang in some years and not in others, it adjusts — by a degree, by a fraction of a degree — the mixing of wall-water and spring-water, so that the narrow band of survivable temperature is a hair wider than it was. Not wide enough to swell the population. Not wide enough to change the hollow's arithmetic of *one larva kept, maybe.* Just wide enough that the line which has never been more than a handful and never zero is held a little further from zero — that the thin film of this lineage, running century to century like the sheet over a terrace lip, has one more margin of error than blind mineral chance was offering it.

This is the shape of wise intervention: not to change what a place is, but to make what it already is slightly more durable. The hollow has been keeping the salamander alive by accident for an age. The power converts the accident into an intention, and leaves the accident's exact dimensions untouched. No one — not the salamander, not a returning observer in forty years, not the most careful census-taker — will ever be able to detect that anything was done. The intervention is invisible because it was made of the same material as the place: patience, water, temperature, time.

**Third**, the niche.

The power goes to the chest-height niche where the cup and the curved thing — antler, perhaps — are vanishing into white anonymity, and here it does something that is not for the hollow at all, and not for the salamander, but for the people who are gone.

It does not unbury the objects. They belong to the stone now; that bargain was struck long ago and the hollow has kept its side honestly. Instead, the power does what the people themselves could not: it *reads* the objects before the last legibility goes. Through the laminate of years it perceives the cup as it was — the hands that shaped it, the mouths it served, the morning someone set it in the niche and meant something by that, or meant nothing, and walked up out of the steam and never came back. It receives the antler's story the same way. It takes nothing physical. It takes the *meaning*, which the stone was never able to digest anyway, and carries it out of the reach of forgetting.

The cup will go on thickening toward sphere. But it is no longer being forgotten as it is buried. It is being *kept*, in the only archive that matters, even as the hollow finishes its slow honest meal. This is what a wise power owes the dead: not resurrection, not monuments, but the salvage of significance from things that can no longer testify for themselves. The stack of three flat stones on the dry shelf — meaning something or nothing — the power reads that too, and now knows which, and will not say, because some answers belong to the askers, and the askers are gone.

**Fourth** — the largest thing, and the most invisible.

The power looks down. Beneath the hollow, beneath the springs' patient throats, down where the water takes its heat from the deep rock — down there, on a timescale even the Seep cannot feel, things are not eternal. Heat sources wander. Faults shift their grip. The deep plumbing that has fed this hollow for longer than anything nearby has been alive is itself a process, and processes end. The power can see — as the salamander cannot, as the film cannot, as even the stone cannot — the far-off century where the water cools, or fails, and the equilibrium that calls itself the Seep finally closes its accounts.

And here is the last decision, the great one, the one the whole visit was for:

*Does it prevent that?*

It could. It could pin the deep heat in place, anchor the springs against geology itself, make the Seep's "finished" into "permanent." It could promote this rare achieved peace into an eternal one.

It does not.

Because the power is wise, and wisdom can tell the difference between a thing that is finished and a thing that is frozen. The Seep's peace is not the peace of exemption from time — it is the peace of perfect *agreement* with time, building and dissolving at the same rate, a long held note. Make it permanent and the note becomes a drone. The hollow's dignity is precisely that it is a mortal equilibrium that has lasted this long by balance, not by decree. To exempt it would be to replace the most remarkable thing about it with a counterfeit.

So instead the power does this: it marks the far-off ending, and makes one quiet arrangement with it. When the cooling comes — and it will come slowly, over generations of generations, the way everything comes here — the narrow survivable band at the margin will move, and the power has seen to it that as it moves, it will move *toward* somewhere. The seepage paths through the wall-rock, the gradients, the slow doors of temperature — they are set, now, so that the last salamanders of the Seep will not be cornered in a shrinking pool, but will find, each generation, that the right water lies a little further along, a little further, out through the lowest lip toward the marsh-flats and whatever the world is by then. They will never know they are migrating. They will only ever know what they have always known: that the stone does not betray, that the water is the right temperature, that there is no reason to hurry.

The Seep will end. The line that the Seep kept will be handed on. That is the difference between preserving a place and loving one.

---

### Departure

The power rises back through the steam ceiling — which folds closed behind it, as it folds closed behind everything — and pauses once at the rim, where it began.

Below, nothing has changed. That was the work: an enormous amount of attention spent on changing nothing, plus three adjustments thinner than a coat of yearly stone, plus one promise lodged in deep rock with a fuse ten thousand years long.

The drops tick down from the curtains, irregular, patient. The film paints the throat of the spring its one ancient color. In the square pool with the half-rounded human corners, the salamander spreads its hands on the old tool-marks and hangs in the blood-warm water, watching nothing in particular with its dark unhurried eyes — though perhaps, for a while, it watches the place at the rim where the warmth had been, the way you might look at a doorway after someone has gone through it, without knowing what a doorway is, or what going is, or that you were, briefly and completely, the entire reason a power crossed the world.

Come back in forty years. The only thing changed will be you.

But now that is no longer quite true of the universe at large — because somewhere in it, the Seep is known. The cup is remembered. The three stacked stones have been read. And a finger-length of pale animal, raised entirely without weather, has been counted — not in a census, but in the way that matters: as one of the things that must be carried through the end of its world, gently, without ever being frightened, without ever being told.

That is what the benevolent and wise power did at the Seep.

Almost nothing. And that almost matters.
