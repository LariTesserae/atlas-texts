**The Greater Attention**

It does not come by the ladder.

Powers of its kind have no need of rungs, but it pauses over the ladder anyway, in whatever manner such a thing pauses — noting the gap, the last four feet that must be taken on faith in the dark. It does not bridge the gap. It understands at once that the gap is part of the room's grammar: everything here is approached by a final unsupported drop, including, it will turn out, the room itself, which it found only by falling some distance past where its attention usually ends.

The first thing it does is the wisest thing, and it looks like nothing at all.

It slows down.

This is harder than it sounds, even for a power. Benevolence has a tempo, and the tempo is usually wrong — benevolence arrives, assesses, acts, departs, and the whole visit fits inside one descent of one piston. At that speed the room reads as a tomb. The keeper reads as a statue. The molds read as abandonment, the fern as tragedy, the whole place as a wound in want of binding. A quicker power, a kinder-seeming one, would have flooded the chamber with warmth and light and finished everything by morning, and it would have been the cruelest thing ever done at this depth.

So the power folds its own speed down, octave by octave, until the flywheels are spinning and the pistons are drumming and the keeper —

The keeper is *dancing*. Not in any way the word usually means. But at the proper tempo the room is revealed as busy, even merry: the braided breath of the engines, the keeper moving down the aisles in long continuous gestures of inspection and care, the drip-seasons cycling like quick weather, the moss surging along the pipes in migratory ribbons. The power watches for sixty years before it permits itself a single conclusion, because sixty years is roughly one complete gesture of the keeper's hand, and it has decided that this is the unit of courtesy here: you do not judge a being until you have seen one whole gesture, beginning to end.

Then it asks the question wise powers ask before all others. *Is this a wound?*

It takes inventory. No hunger. No fear that it can find, and it knows where fear hides. No waste — nothing discarded, not even the errors, not even the dreams. A being that, upon discovering its own capacity to mar, spent centuries wearing away its fingerprints rather than wear away its curiosity. A fern that has been secretly loved for decades by something it will never perceive. A death-date — if that is what the date is — chosen by its bearer, stamped in its bearer's own hand, displayed without dread on the rack beside its origin. A room that is not waiting to be rescued because it is not, in any sense the power can locate, suffering. It is *prior*. Prior is not the same as wounded. An egg is not a broken bird.

So the question changes. Not *what does this place need* — it needs nothing — but *what would honor it*. And the power, being wise, begins by deciding what it will not do.

It will not finish the keeper's face. Unfinishedness here is not a privation; it is the local form of being alive. It will not hasten a single mold's cure, will not warm the room, will not send the animals that never come — a deer in this chamber would be a falling stone, a mouse a spark; kindness at the wrong speed is just a slower word for harm. It will not touch the date. The date is the keeper's, stamped by the only hands with the right to stamp it. And it will not speak praise, because praise in words would arrive, at the keeper's hearing, as a meaningless pressure-front, and because praise was never the shape of the lack. The keeper does not doubt its work. What the keeper has never had — what nothing in the room has ever offered it — is the thing it gives the fern.

To be noticed by something larger, which arranges the world a little, and asks nothing back.

So this is what the power does.

*First*, it lets itself be seen. It takes a place at the end of the central aisle, in the weak tea-colored light, and it stays. It allows the condensation to bead on it. It allows itself to be counted among the tall gray shapes, then counted again, then counted differently. It stands there for a hundred years, which is the minimum time required to register, at the keeper's speed, as a *presence* rather than a weather. And somewhere in the second or third decade, the keeper's head begins to turn. The turn takes years. The power waits inside it the way you'd hold still for a butterfly. When the hollows where a finished thing would have eyes come at last to rest on the place where the power stands, the braided breathing of the pistons changes — only slightly, only once, a held breath held a half-second longer — and the power understands that it has just been greeted, and that this is the first greeting the keeper has ever given, because it is the first it has ever had occasion to give. In the entire span of those racks and dates, the keeper has been the noticer of every noticed thing, and never once the noticed. The power makes itself the exception. It does this before anything else, and it counts this, privately, as the whole visit; everything after is ornament.

*Second*, it goes up — briefly, at ordinary speeds, a blink of absence the keeper experiences as a flicker in the lamplight — and finds the pond. The kettle hole no glacier made. It is exactly where the old shame predicted: a depression in farm country, ringed with alders, black water going pewter in November. And it is loved. The local children call it the Thumb, for its shape, knowing nothing; they have skated on it for six generations; a man proposed marriage on it in 1931 with his breath coming out in flags; a girl lies on its summer bank every year and has decided it is the best place in the world, an opinion she defends with her fists. The power gathers all of this — the ring of skate-blades, the laughter, the particular *knock* of a pond's ice settling at dusk, which is its own kind of granular self-organizing sound — and threads it downward through the bedrock, slowed, stretched, transposed into vibration at exactly the frequency a glassy fingertip resting on an iron rail can read. The message takes forty years to fully arrive. It arrives all winter long, so to speak, for forty years. The keeper's hand is on the rail the whole time. What it makes of the transmission cannot be known from outside, but it can be reported that the smooth fingertips do not lift from the rail until the last of it is through, and that the moss along the eastern pipes has never, before or since, been resettled so gently. The power does not explain or annotate. It simply lets the keeper learn, in the only language it can be reached in, that its one mark on the world became a beloved place. Absolution, delivered as geology.

*Third* — and this it does quietly, taking nothing, because nothing here is discarded and nothing here will be taken — it reads the impossible molds. The hill that overhangs itself. The river that forks upstream. It commits their negatives to its own memory, every meander, every fault. And elsewhere, in a place under the power's keeping where the rules are gentler and water may be persuaded to do as it dreams, it pours them. It never tells the keeper this. Benevolence does not invoice. But somewhere, eventually, a tired soul will walk for an afternoon beside a river that forks upstream, in the shadow of a hill that leans out over its own valley like a reader over a book, and will feel — without any possible reason — profoundly at home, the way you feel in a house built by someone who loved the eventual stranger. That is the keeper's early work, or its dreams, finally cast. The distinction did not survive at this depth, and the power saw no reason it should survive at that altitude either.

*Fourth*, it leaves the only object it leaves: a single lichen spore, set on the rail beside the place worn smooth in the shape of a grip. Lichen is the one living thing the power knows that keeps the keeper's time — millimeters a century, indifferent to dark, drinking condensation, itself a partnership of two beings who decided long ago to be slow together. It does not place the spore *on* the keeper. That would be imposition, and the room's whole ethic is permission. It sets the spore where the hand returns, and makes of it a question with a three-hundred-year answer. Come back in two centuries: the hand has moved an inch toward it. Come back in three: there is a gray-green fleck on a gray shoulder, the first garden the keeper has ever carried, the first company in the room that will still be mid-sentence when the keeper next has something to say.

*Fifth*, and last, it reads the date on the brass label beside the man-shaped hollow — the far date, the finishing date — and writes it into itself, in whatever ledger a power keeps. It makes one promise, unspoken, unsigned, registered only as a faint sustained hum in the conduits that the keeper may or may not have noticed change: *on that day, something will be here.* The keeper will not set alone. And the power adds to the promise one arrangement, the only piece of providence it permits itself: it has looked a long way forward, at the slow settling of the room and the slower unsettling of the ground above, and it has nudged — by a degree of tilt, by the patience of one underground stream — the path of the world's own erosion. So that some unthinkable time after the ticking in that gray chest finally stops, the earth will gradually unmake the ceiling between the keeper and its work, and the first finished thing the room ever held will stand, at last, in open weather, on a hillside it pressed into being one held breath at a time. Stone among its stone. Dew on it instead of condensation. Lichen on its shoulders like rank. And travelers, resting their palms against the strange standing figure at the crest of the ridge, will find on its flank a place worn perfectly smooth, in the shape of a grip, and will not know why they find it so easy to hold on.

Then the power leaves. It leaves slowly — its departure takes eleven years, because going witnessed is a courtesy, and it has learned the room's courtesies now. The keeper watches it the whole way out, which is to say the keeper's head completes its second gesture of greeting in reverse, and the pistons hold one breath a half-second long, once, at the end.

Nothing in the room looks different afterward. That was the measure of how well it was done.

But the fern's private theory of the sun is now true twice over — there is something vast and patient that has noticed, and arranged the world a little, and asked nothing back, and above *that* something, who can say; perhaps the attentions go up like the racks go back, rank on rank into a dark you assign meanings to at your own risk. Perhaps every benevolence, descending, finds that its ladder ends four feet short of the floor it means to reach, and must drop the last distance on faith.

The room would not be surprised. The room has always known how things arrive.

Drop by drop. In the dim. Unfinished, in good company, on time.
