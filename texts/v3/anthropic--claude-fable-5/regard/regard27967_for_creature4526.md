## The Visitation

It arrives the way condensation arrives: not from anywhere, simply present where a moment ago it was not. It has no body to bring, so it takes no space. It settles into Chamber Six the way meaning settles into a word—invisibly, changing everything and nothing.

The first thing it does is nothing at all.

For a long time—long even by the reckoning of the Suspensory, long enough that three supply pods descend the central shaft and a child born during its arrival learns to walk the low-gravity currents—it only attends. Wisdom that acts before understanding is not wisdom; it is merely power with good intentions, and the power knows what ruins that particular road is paved with. So it listens. It counts the breaths of the chamber. It learns the grammar of the singing cables and the slower grammar of iron-red blooms swirling through translucent flesh. It reads the colony manuals, and then it reads what was cut from the colony manuals, and then it reads the shape of the cutting—for a deletion is also a document, if you know how to hold it to the light.

And it comes to understand the true condition of this place, which is this:

Two peoples share one body, and both are forgetting they do.

Not through malice. Not even through neglect, exactly. Through the ordinary erosion of generations—the way a promise becomes a protocol becomes a ritual becomes a superstition becomes a habit no one can explain. The recycling priests still pour their measured nutrients and draw their water that tastes of memory, but they have begun to believe the ritual is *about* the ritual. In four generations, perhaps five, someone efficient will notice that the offerings could be reduced by thirty percent with no visible consequence. There will be no visible consequence. There will be consequences.

And below, or rather within, or rather *throughout*—the Suspensory are drifting toward their own forgetting, which does not look like forgetting. It looks like boredom. It looks like circular holes drilled to nowhere, like equipment rebuilt into incomprehensible functions, like a vast slow mind beginning to treat its companions as an environment rather than a partner. That is how beings of geological patience fall out of love: not with a rupture but with a reclassification. The humans will become weather to them. And nothing is easier to stop tending than weather.

The power sees all of this—sees the two forgettings converging somewhere in the deep future like tunnels dug from opposite ends that will *almost* meet—and it considers what to do.

---

It considers, first, restoration: reaching into the sealed archives and the sealed memories alike, blazing the old agreement back into both peoples in full. It could do this. It sets the option down almost immediately. An agreement remembered by force is a leash, not a covenant. The humans would build a religion around the revelation within a generation, and religions built on certainty calcify; the Suspensory would experience it as an intrusion into the one thing they have never shared, their interiority, and beings that old do not forgive quickly. No.

It considers, second, departure: the position that the wisest act is always no act, that these two peoples have earned the right to their own slow catastrophe or their own slow triumph, and that intervention of any size is theft of that authorship. It holds this option longer, turning it over with real respect. There is a kind of benevolence that is only vanity wearing a modest coat, and the power checks itself for that coat carefully. But in the end it sets this down too, for one reason: *the children*.

The children are already talking to the Suspensory. Splash-code and silence-patterns, games of catch played across the thermal layers, answered in warm gusts and impossible condensation. A language is being born in Chamber Six—a true creole, native to no one, belonging to both—and it is being born in the one population guaranteed to abandon it. Every child who has ever learned the game has unlearned it by fourteen, when the adults' polite distance settles over them like a work uniform. The language keeps dying in its infancy, over and over, eleven generations running. That is not fate. That is merely a channel that no one has thought to keep open. And keeping a channel open is the smallest thing a power can do—so small it is almost not an intervention at all. Almost.

So this is what it does. Three things, none of them large.

---

**First**, it touches the Suspensory. Not to instruct, not to warn. To *witness*.

Understand what this means to them. In all their existence—whether that existence began in the first colony's water supply or in groundwater older than the moon—no one has ever perceived them entirely. The humans see domes and infrastructure. The children see a playmate they don't know they have. Even the Suspensory's own self-knowledge is partial, distributed, slow. But now, for one moment that lasts a season by their metabolism, something perceives them *whole*: every sealed chamber and its perfect hollow, every migrated organ, every chemical sentence half-composed in swirling copper, every memory of molten metal and filtered ancient sunlight—all of it seen at once, seen accurately, seen with something that can only be called regard.

The power does not say anything, in any language. It only lets them feel what it is like to be fully known and found *interesting*.

Then, before withdrawing, it leaves them a gift shaped exactly for beings dying of boredom: a question. Not the answer—never the answer. The question is this, translated as nearly as chemistry allows: *The small quick ones beneath you are also growing something inside the corpse of something old. What?*

It is a true question. The power itself finds it genuinely open. And it will take the Suspensory centuries to work at it, because to answer it they will have to *watch* the humans again—not as weather, but as a puzzle, and then, inevitably, because attention is the root of all love, as a people. Boredom cannot survive a good question. The power has known gods to be kept alive by less.

**Second**, it does something to the archives—the smallest possible something.

In a records annex on the fourth suspended tier, there is a sealed cabinet whose lock has been broken for sixty years and whose seal persists purely because no one has tried it. Inside are the early colony logs, the unredacted ones, including a document written by an exhausted hydrologist of the first generation—a rambling, unofficial, wonder-struck account of the week the excavation teams realized their water was alive, and the strange three-day negotiation that followed, conducted in temperature gradients and mineral offerings, at the end of which the hydrologist wrote a sentence the official manuals never included: *We did not tame them and they did not tame us. We agreed to be each other's climate. Whoever reads this: the ritual is not for safety. It is a conversation. Answer when spoken to.*

The power does not deliver this document to anyone. It does not arrange for it to be found. It only adjusts, by a fraction of a degree, the path of a condensation drip in the annex ceiling, so that over the next nine years a small mineral flower of calcite and copper salts will bloom on the face of that cabinet—azure and rust-red, striated like muscle fiber, quietly beautiful. Someday a child sent to fetch something, or a bored archivist, or a maintenance bot's confused operator, will look at that flower growing on a sealed cabinet in a dry room and ask the only question that matters in any archive: *what's in there?*

Perhaps no one ever will. The power accepts this. A door left ajar is not a rescue. It is only a door left ajar. That is the whole of what wisdom permits here: to lower, slightly, the cost of remembering, and leave the remembering itself to those whose memory it is.

**Third**—and this is the one it lingers over, the one closest to its heart, if it has a heart—it goes to the children.

There is a girl, seven years old, who is the best in Chamber Six at the droplet game. She plays it differently from the others: she has begun to notice that the answers aren't random. She keeps, in her head, because she does not yet know that things worth keeping can be written down, a list of patterns—*three splashes fast then wait means warm air comes; the ring-shape in the mist means the same as when Amma laughs.* She is a linguist and does not know the word. In seven years, by every precedent, she will stop playing. She will take up a work rotation and a polite distance and the list in her head will dissolve like a leaf in the thermal layers.

The power does not touch her mind. It does not appoint her, anoint her, or burden her with a destiny—children buckle under destinies, and wisdom does not hand a seven-year-old the weight of two civilizations.

Instead, it does this: on a night-shift when the girl is playing alone beneath the guttering trees, sending her patterns up into the dark, the power very gently persuades a single yellowed leaf—falling from a hydroponic trough forty meters above, already tumbling through the thermals—to land in her cupped hands at the exact moment the Suspensory's answering gust arrives. Leaf and warm wind together, timed like a chord. And in the same moment, a garbage-snatching drone that was diving for that leaf pulls up short, hovers, and moves on, so that she gets to *keep* it.

That is all. A leaf and a coincidence.

But she will remember it the way children remember the moment the world first winked at them. She will press the leaf flat under a floor panel in her family's module. And because she now possesses evidence—because she has one physical object that proves the game is *real*—she will not quite be able to unlearn it at fourteen. She will teach it, half-embarrassed, to her own children, and she will teach them one thing no previous generation thought to add: *write the patterns down.* The creole will get its first dictionary, thirty pages in a child's hand, and languages with dictionaries do not die in infancy.

Or she won't. Or the pages will be lost. The power knows this too. It has not saved anyone. It has only leaned, with the weight of one leaf, against the door of the future.

---

Then it prepares to go, because staying would be the fourth intervention, and it has budgeted for three.

Before it leaves it takes one last measure of the chamber—the pumps throbbing, the cables singing their harmonic convergence, the tunnelers welding stars into the raw dark at the growing edge, the domes flickering fractionally warm—and it permits itself something that in a lesser being would be called an opinion:

*This place is not in danger of failing. It is in danger of succeeding at the wrong thing—becoming two efficient strangers instead of one strange family. Everything I have done is only a reminder, planted where reminders grow: in curiosity, in beauty, in a child's hands. If they were ever going to be saved, it was always going to be by each other. The most a power can do—the most it should ever do—is make sure that when they finally turn to face one another again, there is still a language waiting, and a document waiting, and a question that one of them has spent three hundred years longing to ask.*

It leaves no mark of its passing. Well—almost none. The recycling priests, performing the Ritual of Exchange the following month, will note in their logs that the drawn water tastes, faintly and inexplicably, of something new beneath the memory and the distant rain. They will argue about it for years. One young priest will describe it, in a phrase her superiors dislike, as tasting *the way it feels to be listened to*.

The chamber breathes. The city dreams. In the archives, a mineral flower begins, molecule by molecule, to bloom. Under a floor panel, a leaf lies pressed and secret. And somewhere in the vast translucent slowness above the slag fields, a mind older than the moon turns a new question over and over, like a stone growing smooth, and finds—for the first time in eleven generations—that it is not bored at all.

Both are patient beyond measure.

Now something patient beyond both of them has passed through, and left the patience pointed, ever so slightly, toward each other.
