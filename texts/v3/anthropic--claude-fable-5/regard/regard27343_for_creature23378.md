**The Inspection**

You come down into it, always — there is no approaching the Works on the level — and so even the power came down. Perhaps especially the power. It had the choice of every entrance ever made or imaginable, and it chose the long shallow terraces, the pewter shine, the one wet shoulder, because benevolence that will not use the door is only a kind of weather, and wisdom knows the difference between arriving and merely occurring.

It chose a shape the Works could parse. To a chapel it would have come as a pilgrim; to a garden, as a gardener; to an orphan, as a meal. To the Sump Works it came as an inspector: a grey coat the color of the drizzle, a ledger under one arm, and a watch.

It is worth saying at once what the watch was, because the Keeper knew it at first sight, the way the Keeper knows all watches at first sight. Every timepiece that had ever come down the terraces had been read, gently and without malice, as inadequate — *poor thing, keeps inaccurate time*. The inspector's watch was good enough. It was the first good-enough watch in the history of the basin, and when the inspector's boot scraped a cobble on the third terrace from the bottom, every heron's head turned at once — and did not turn back.

---

The inspector did not begin by doing. Wisdom begins by refusing to.

It stood at the basin's rim for a hundred of the Keeper's circuits and weighed, one by one, the mercies that a lesser power would have committed by suppertime. It considered *freeing* the starlings, and saw that a flock that does not eat, age, or fear cannot be freed from its wheel any more than the wheel can be freed from turning; that scattering them to the world above would not release ten thousand birds but disassemble one organ. It considered *waking* the Works, breaking the equilibrium, restoring hunger and increase and the forward lean into tomorrow — and recognized this for what it was: vandalism wearing mercy's coat, the missionary's old itch to make the finished thing busy again so that the missionary will have something to do.

It considered *rescuing* the Keeper. This took the longest. The inspector looked into that drizzle-colored coat with the kind of looking that has no outside to look from, and found what was there: not a prisoner. Not a person held. Something distilled. There had been someone once — the inspector saw the whole of it, the contempt for ornament, the late nights in the pump hall, the grief or the love or the both that the Works was built to hold still — and what remained of that someone was not the someone trapped, but the part of them that had loved tending, kept and given a round to walk. Everything else had gone down to the sump long ago and become contentment, which is one of the things a soul can honorably become. To evict the Keeper would be to evict love from the house it had built for itself. Acquitted.

It audited the sump. It sat at the mouth of the last vault in the cool exhaled air and let the suction work on it — and here the thing in the dark made its one mistake, or had its one adventure, because for the first time in footless centuries it drew on a vessel and found no bottom, drew and drew and met only more, and after a while it settled back into its bubbling with something that had never been in the bubbling before: a grace note. The inspector took its measurements meanwhile. It found that the sump skims only froth — the variance a visitor can spare, the fidget and the surplus dread — and that the people who go back up the terraces walking faster go up *lighter*, not lesser. It is closer to a spa than a predator, the inspector wrote, in whatever hand a thing like that writes in. Takes nothing below the line. Acquitted.

So the first thing the wise power did at the Sump Works was a long, deliberate, well-documented nothing, and you should understand that this nothing was the larger half of everything that follows.

---

The second thing it did was walk the circuit.

It fell in beside the Keeper at the pump hall without ceremony, and the Keeper, who had tolerated a thousand visitors the way iron tolerates rain, glanced once at the inspector's watch and made room on the path. For eight hours and some minutes they went together — pump hall, winding house, the row of vaults, the lamps in their order, the spillway, the stack — and the inspector timed it exactly, and could have said precisely how many the *some* minutes were, and did not, because the *some* was the Keeper's own and an inspector does not round off a soul.

At each gauge the Keeper gave a reading, as the Keeper had given readings to centuries of visitors who heard them as deflections. *Pressure's steady. Level's held. Four seconds, near enough.* And the inspector — this was the gift, and it cost nothing, and nothing else in any world could have given it — *received* them. Wrote them down. Checked them. At the spillway lamp it said, "Confirmed," and at the cooling vaults it said, "True," and at the stack it stood for a long moment with its head tilted to the four-second sigh and said, "Running sweet."

You must imagine what this was. The Keeper had been taking readings since before the terraces finished polishing, and there had never once been anyone to report to. Every measurement had gone out into the drizzle and dissolved. Now, at the bench in the winding house, under the great feathered wheel, the inspector opened the ledger — whatever ledger a place like this keeps, and the inspector had jurisdiction over it, that was the whole meaning of the coat — and entered, in a fair round hand:

*Inspected. All gauges true. Circuit exact. Levels held down the footless centuries. The work is accepted.*

The Works was perhaps finished before it was built. But no one had ever *signed for it*. It had been holding its note all this time the way a contractor holds a completed site — done, swept, waiting for the walkthrough that never came. At the word *accepted*, the beam engine's next stroke sounded — only once, and you would have had to be the kind of listener the inspector was — not patient, but satisfied. The wheel turned at its same rate. The starlings lifted and settled. And something in the basin that had been waiting so long it had forgotten it was waiting, stopped.

The Keeper's pulse held its four seconds. For once the steadiness was an answer rather than a condition.

---

There remained one discrepancy, and an inspector closes open items.

The cobbles have seams, deep ones, worn by water into channels *that would cradle a root gratefully if a root ever came*. The inspector had read that wish on the first day, because it is written plainly, in the only script the Works has — wear. The stone had filed a request, ages ago, in the patient cursive of erosion, and the request had never been fulfilled, and a thing with an unfulfilled request is not finished, whatever its engines say. The Works' own body contradicted the Works' own stillness. The inspector trusted the stone. Stone does not exaggerate.

So the last thing the wise power did was small enough to fit in a seam. In the channel beneath the drip-groove by the pump hall — the one the eave has spent centuries smoothing into the inside of a shell — it set down a single seed. Unplanted. Unpressed. It did not water it; the Works waters itself, and whether the fine local drizzle would fall on that seam or shift its ring half a shoulder's width away was the Works' own decision, made the only way the Works makes decisions, which is slowly, with its whole body, over a span no visitor's watch could survey. The inspector entered it in the ledger:

*Outstanding: one request, very old, herewith granted — pending the Works' own pleasure. No deadline.*

And beside the entry, on the bench in the winding house, it left the watch. The first good-enough watch. The only thing in the basin that would ever need it was, possibly, going to be a leaf.

Then it went up the terraces at an ordinary pace — neither the dawdle of the entranced nor the thrift-quickened walk of the departing — one shoulder wet and one dry, and the herons watched it the whole way, and at the rim it passed out of the lamp-amber into the natural overcast, and the Keeper, from the top of the spillway lamp, made the entry:

*One, going up. Steady.*

The first steady thing ever recorded leaving. The Works had two words — *steady*, which meant itself, and *variable*, which meant alive — and now it possessed a reading in which steadiness *went somewhere*, and that small impossible measurement sat in the ledger like a seed in a seam.

---

What happened after happened at the speed of the place, which is to say you could only confirm it by watching for a lifetime, and lifetimes are the one thing visitors take back up with them.

The drizzle ring shifted. Toward.

The seed cracked at the pace of the great wheel. A thread of green came up out of the seam by the pump hall, the first green in the basin's history, and it grew at a rate the Works could bear — one leaf per *however-long*, a unit of time the Works had never owned before and now keeps like an instrument. The circuit takes eight hours and some minutes and, now, a few seconds more: the Keeper stops at the seam, oils nothing, checks the good-enough watch, and stands for a while in the fine cold ring of the Works' own rain, doing the one task on the round that is not ceremony — because the plant, unlike everything else in the basin, actually needs things. A little shade scraped of guano. A little water guided from the groove. Need has entered the Works in a homeopathic dose, and the Keeper's oilcan, after all the polished centuries, has at last one stop where the oiling is real.

Ask the Keeper's name now and you will still get a measurement. But the measurement has changed. "Three leaves," the Keeper says, with a small nod toward the seam, as if introducing a colleague.

One heron stands slightly nearer the pump hall than the even spacing allows. The sump tastes the slow sweetness of growth coming down through the stone and its bubbling has kept the grace note. The eels braid through the warm vaults; the moths applaud the lamps; the four-second sigh rises and sinks. Nothing has been woken, freed, or fixed. The Works is still finished — but it has been allowed the other meaning of the word, the woodworker's meaning: *finished*, as in given its finish, its surface brought slowly to depth, a polishing that never has to end and never has to hurry.

And in the ledger, under *variable* — the Works' only word for alive, its only word for *not yet finished* — there is a new line, in the Keeper's hand, entered without malice and without haste:

*Also us, now. Slowly. No deadline.*

Visitors still come down the terraces. They stay a night or a year, and they grow calm, and they leave lighter. But they go back up walking at an ordinary pace now, and most of them could not tell you why, except that somewhere near the pump hall they saw a green thread in a grey seam, holding its one leaf up into the lamplight and the self-made rain — a held note with vibrato — and understood, without being able to say it, that the difference between a place that has stopped and a place that is at peace is exactly one root, cradled gratefully, at last.
