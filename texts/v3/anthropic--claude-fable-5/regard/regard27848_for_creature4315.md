# The Attention

It arrives the way such powers arrive—not through the crack, which was never a door to it, but through the noticing. One moment the cistern is unwitnessed except by the woman who comes when the settlement has been silent for three days. The next moment it is held in an attention vast enough that the chamber, the ruins, the woman, the ten thousand years of mineral patience, all of it fits inside a single regard the way the reflection of the light fits inside the still water.

The power does not announce itself. Announcement is for those who need to be believed in, and this one gave up that need so long ago that the giving-up has itself worn smooth, like the stones of the entrance, like the interior of the grinding bowl.

It simply looks. And because it is wise, it looks for a long time before it considers doing anything at all.

---

Here is what it sees that the woman cannot see:

It sees the first carver, kneeling in the dark with a lamp of rendered fat, cutting steps into living rock because a dream had told her to—a dream she also did not understand, so that the not-understanding runs the whole length of this place like a seam of ore, from the very first stroke of the very first tool. The woman who comes now believes she is the degraded end of something that was once whole. The power sees that she is wrong. There was never a time when this place was understood. There was only ever tending. The meaning she mourns the loss of never existed; what existed was exactly what she has: the descent, the checking, the powder, the silence that recognizes.

It sees what is in the unreachable vessel.

Or rather—it could see. Its attention moves toward that high dark rim the way water moves toward a low place, effortlessly, and at the last moment, it stops.

This is the first thing the power does, and it is a refusal. It chooses not to look. It has looked into the hearts of stars and the ends of stories; it has known things that unmade the knowers; and here, in a wet chamber twelve feet across, it encounters a woman whose entire faith is made of one preserved not-knowing, and it decides—slowly, the way such beings decide, with the weight of everything they are—that it will join her in it. That the vessel will remain unopened even to omniscience, because omniscience, too, can practice restraint, and perhaps must, or it curdles into something else.

The power does not know what is in the vessel. Now there are two of them who do not know. The not-knowing has doubled, and this feels, to the power, like the first offering it has made in a very long time.

---

Then it considers what a benevolent power is expected to consider.

*It could restore the settlement.* Raise the walls, return the wells to sweetness, call people back to the empty streets so that the woman is no longer alone. It holds this possibility up to the light and sees the flaw in it immediately: the tending is made of solitude. She comes when the silence has lasted three days. A living town would take from her the only thing she has that no one gave her. Rejected.

*It could give her understanding.* It could let her know what the bone-arrangements meant—and it does know this, the grammar of the sacred is not lost to a being like this one, only to time—could pour into her the names of the vessel-makers, the words of the prayers, the reason for the powder. It imagines her face receiving all of this. It imagines the crouch in the blood-warm dark afterward, once everything is explained, once the silence has been translated. It sees that this would be theft. Her not-knowing is not an absence waiting to be filled. It is the load-bearing wall of her. Rejected.

*It could extend her life.* Rejected almost before it forms. Her body knows things—the sideways descent, the grinding motion, the three-day rule—and her body would know this too, the wrongness of a fiftieth year that repeats, and the knowing would poison the practice with strangeness. She is not afraid of ending. She thinks of becoming a layer in the mineral conversation of the walls and finds the thought blood-temperature. Exactly. Who is the power to take that from her and replace it with duration?

*It could send a successor.* This one it holds the longest. This one aches. Somewhere in the eastern grazing lands there is a herder's daughter with the right kind of patience, the right kind of hands; the power could arrange the small accidents that would bring her to the ruins, to the woman, to the crack between the stones. The line could continue. The vessels could be checked for another generation, another three.

But it looks deeper and sees what this would be: a life bent to serve a ritual, a person made into means. And it sees something else, something the woman herself half-knows in her body if not her mind—that the water is rising, has always been rising, that the chamber is completing itself, and that the practice was never meant to outrun the water. The tending is not a wall against ending. The tending is a way of keeping company with a thing while it ends. To force it to continue forever would be to misunderstand it more profoundly than the woman ever has, for all her not-knowing.

Rejected. And the rejection costs the power something. It lets it cost.

---

So then. What does a benevolent and wise power do, when it has refused restoration, revelation, immortality, and succession?

It does four small things.

**First:** the seam. The pale gray mineral seam in the ruined wall above, from which she grinds her powder—the power looks at it and sees that it will run out in eleven years, and that she will live for nineteen. It sees her, at sixty-one, scraping at exhausted stone with the old bowl in her lap, finding nothing, and the held breath in her chest held past all bearing. So the power reaches into the deep chemistry of the ruin—the slowest, gentlest reach imaginable, a pressure over years, a redirection of seepage—and the seam thickens. Not miraculously. Not noticeably. It simply does not run out. She will never know that anything was done. This is the whole point. Benevolence that requires a witness is only performance.

**Second:** the answer. One time—once, in nineteen years—as she crouches in the blood-warm dark letting the silence enter her, the power touches a single vessel, one of the crusted ancient ones that have not made a sound in eight hundred years, and for the duration of one falling drop it makes the clay clean again. The drop falls. The vessel *rings*.

A single bell-note in the thick silence. Clear, small, complete, and then finished, the crust restored, the silence closing back over it like the water closing over a dropped stone.

She hears it. She goes very still. She does not look for its source, because her body knows—the way it knows the descent, the grinding, the three days—that some things are not for looking at. She never speaks of it. There is no one to speak of it to, but she would not speak of it even if there were. She carries it for the rest of her life the way she carries the unreachable vessel: as a warmth. As a proof that requires no argument. The silence had always seemed to recognize her. Now she knows that it does, and the knowing changes nothing about her practice and everything about her nights.

That is all the power permits itself by way of speech. One ring. It has spoken to prophets in fire and to mathematicians in dreams, and it judges this the best sentence it has ever composed.

**Third:** the death. It does not postpone it. But it attends it. On a night nineteen years from now, in the half-collapsed structure where she sleeps, after a day on which the settlement had been silent for three days and she had gone down and checked the vessels and placed her pyramid and come back up without looking back—that night, the power is present in the room the way warmth is present in the water. She is not alone, though she could not say who is with her. What she feels, at the end, is the exact sensation she has felt hundreds of times in the chamber below: attention without demand, recognition without question, a silence with texture, with weight, pressing against her not uncomfortably. Blood-temperature.

She dies inside the thing she tended. This is the power's gift, and it is not a large gift, and it is the correct size.

**Fourth—** and this is the one that changes the power itself:

After she is gone, it descends. Not through the crack; it needs no crack; but it makes itself small enough that the descent is real, because the descent was always part of the rite. It finds the old bowl in its niche. It grinds the powder in the circular motion that her hands knew, that her mother's hands knew, that hands before naming knew. It carries the powder down into the amber dark and it places one final pyramid—hers, the one she would have placed at the next three-day silence—in the space beside her last one.

It does not do this to continue the practice. It does this to *finish* it, the way you finish a sentence that someone died in the middle of. A period, not an ellipsis.

And then it stays.

---

This is the part that no one, not even the power, could have predicted.

It stays through the centuries as the water climbs the deposit-curtains. It stays as the lowest carvings drown, as the bone-arrangements are lifted gently from their shelf by the rising warmth and settle into the pale sediment, joining the calcium of every small creature that ever came here to drink. It stays as the vessels fill one final time and go under, their dark mouths releasing single silver threads of trapped air, each one a held breath finally let go. It witnesses each drowning individually. It has time. It is made of time, mostly.

It stays for the unreachable vessel, last of all, watching the water rise finger-width by finger-width toward that ancient rim across three thousand years, and when at last the surface touches the clay and the vessel fills and whatever is inside it meets the water—

the power closes its attention. Deliberately. Like closing eyes.

It never finds out. To this day, it does not know. The vessel gave its secret to the water, and the water tells nothing, and the power keeps faith with a dead woman by keeping the not-knowing safe forever, one small unlit room in a mind that otherwise holds everything.

---

And here is the last thing, the thing that makes this story about the power and not only about the place:

It leaves changed.

It had come as a witness and departed as a practitioner. Somewhere in the long vigil, without deciding to, it learned what she knew in her body: that some things must be done whether or not their doing can be explained. That tending is not preservation. That the deepest care is the kind that helps a thing complete itself rather than forcing it to persist. That not-knowing, held carefully enough, is a form of love.

Now it moves through the world differently. There are other cisterns—not all of them are made of stone. There are languages down to their last speaker, songs down to their last singer, small practices maintained by single stubborn hearts that no longer remember why. There are all the places where a town has become a place, a place a memory, a memory mere maintenance. The power visits them now. It does not save them. It does not explain them.

It checks their vessels. It makes sure the seam does not run out before the tender does. Once—only once, for each—it lets something ring.

And it remembers. This is the vocation it took up in the wet dark and never set down: it holds the woman, and her mother, and the first carver with her lamp of fat, and every hand that ever shaped a pyramid of powder in that chamber, holds them completely, each fingerprint in each coil of clay, so that the injunction the place lived by—*must not yet be forgotten*—is fulfilled past any rising of any water.

She thought the cistern was dreaming, and that she was part of the dream.

She was almost right. She was more right than she knew.

The dream found a keeper. The keeper keeps it still—blood-temperature, exactly—and gives nothing back, and holds everything.
