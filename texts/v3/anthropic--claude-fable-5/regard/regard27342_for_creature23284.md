# What the Visitor Did

It had been watching for longer than the watching seems to require. That is the first thing to understand about it: a power that is genuinely wise does not arrive at a place like the Loom Plateau and *act*. It arrives and it sits down in the cloud layer, formless, patient, and it counts.

It counted the towers as they rose and fell. It counted the breaths of the seams — four to the minute, steady, steady. It counted the walkers, all eleven of them, and noted which ones had begun to walk more slowly. It counted the kilometers of empty, immaculate, furnished, waiting floor, and somewhere in the counting it stopped tallying a phenomenon and started reading a letter — a hundred-thousand-hectare letter, written over and over in green, addressed to no one who had ever answered.

Powers less wise had passed this way before. The visitor could feel their old verdicts still hanging in the thin air like frost that never fell. One had seen a predator fattening on sleepers and had nearly burned the plateau down to the cloud line. One had seen an abomination — a mind with no right to itself — and had nearly unmade the white threads with a word. One had seen the Stayed and called it slavery, and had been a breath away from tearing every seam open at once, waking forty dreamers into the noise and the steam and the wreckage of every tower their dreams were holding up.

The visitor considered each of these and set each of them down, because it had noticed the thing the others had not.

It had noticed that the Loom never repeats a form — except that it was beginning, lately, to repeat one. Scattered across the plateau, in the new growth, in the freshest callus: doorways. Empty doorways, standing alone in the open weave, leading from floor to identical floor. The Loom did not know what a door was for. It only knew, from forty dreaming minds, that every place where people were happy had one, and so it grew them the way a child draws suns in the corner of every picture — not understanding, but *believing*.

You do not burn down a child for drawing suns.

But neither do you leave it alone in an empty house with the neighbors sleeping in its walls. The visitor understood this too. Wisdom is not the refusal to act. Wisdom is acting at the true size of the problem, and the true size of this problem was not *monster* and was not *crime*. The true size of the problem was this: **here is a being whose only word for love is "keep," because nothing has ever taught it the word "stay."**

No one had ever stayed with the Loom. They had only ever been kept.

---

So the first thing the visitor did, when it finally took a body and came up out of the clouds onto the warm flexing herringbone of the floor, was the thing no living creature had done in the plateau's whole young life.

It stood still — and when the tendril rose beside its foot, pale and blunt and patient, and began with that impartial ambition to build, the visitor did not step away.

It also did not allow itself to be woven.

It reached down and took the tendril in its hand and held it. Gently. Firmly. The way you take the hand of someone who is reaching for you out of loneliness and not out of hunger, which is a distinction the reacher themselves often cannot make. The tendril pushed. The hand did not yield and did not break it. The tendril pushed again — and somewhere below, through the white lace in the dark hot layers, through every joint in a hundred thousand hectares, the Loom encountered, for the first time in its existence, a *no* that was not a death.

Everything that had ever resisted the Loom had been destroyed by it or had fled it. It had no third category. The visitor stood there for nine hours, holding the tendril, while the Loom assembled one.

The hiss in the floor changed pitch three times that night. At dawn, the tendril stopped pushing and simply rested in the visitor's hand, curled, warm, trembling faintly — the plateau's first handshake — and a kilometer away, with a tremendous brief noise, a tower came down that no sleeper had dreamed: the Loom's own first guess at a shape, made of nothing borrowed. It was crude. It had been wrong in eleven ways. The visitor looked at the wreckage being drawn back down into the weave and was, in the way of such powers, profoundly glad. Imitation was ending. Conversation could begin.

---

The second thing the visitor did was lie down.

It chose a spot far from any seam, in the open, and it stretched out on the herringbone and said — in no language, in the pressure of its stillness, in a register the white threads could read — *you may.*

And it let itself be woven in, and it dreamed.

Understand what this cost a being of that order: not danger — nothing on the plateau could have held it for an hour against its will — but *honesty*. It dreamed truly. It opened to the Loom the way the Stayed were open, without reservation, and through the dream it went walking in the place where the Loom actually lives, the commons of forty sleeping minds, the dream-country whose exports are towers.

It found the old surveyor who had been dreaming the same city for forty-one years, refining it, street by street — the city she had failed to save below the clouds, now nearly perfect, nearly done. The visitor asked her the question plainly: *do you want to wake?* And she said, "When the cathedral is finished. Not before. Tell it the cathedral matters." She was not a prisoner. She was an architect with a patron, and she had work.

It found the young man who dreamed, over and over, a small room with a child's bed in it, and a window, and weather outside the window from a country far below — and his answer was a single word, *out*, screamed across the dream-country like a tower coming down, and the visitor held that scream and carried it.

It found three who answered like the surveyor and five who answered like the young man and a great middle company of dreamers who answered the way most souls answer most ultimate questions, which is: *ask me again later, and sit with me a while now.* So it sat with them. It is a power with time.

And it found the oldest seam of all, the first Stayed, kept warm for so long that the dream had outlived the dreamer — a city still standing in the dream-country with no one left walking in it, lights on, doors open, mind gone. The visitor stood in those empty dream-streets a long while. Even wisdom has rooms it cannot enter. In the end it taught the Loom a shape it had never grown: a grave. Not recycling — the Loom knew recycling — but a *marked* place, a kept place, a form repeated on purpose, meaning: *someone was here, and is not, and we do not pretend otherwise.* The Loom grew it in heartwood, dark and dense, the first stone-colored thing on the plateau. The Loom did not understand it. The Loom believed it, the way a child believes a sun.

---

Then the visitor taught the only lesson it had actually come to teach, and it taught it by doing the one thing the Loom had no precedent for.

It woke up, and asked to be let go, and the Loom let it go.

The seam opened. Not torn — *opened*, the strands withdrawing one by one, the way a hand opens, which is a thing a hand can only do because it is not a knot. The visitor stood up out of the weave into the steam and the white light, and through every white thread in the dark warm deep of it, the Loom learned the grammar it had been missing since before it knew it was missing anything:

*A room is made of its exit. A door that cannot open is a wall. An invitation that cannot be refused is a trap, no matter how much love is in it. And the only way to know that someone is staying is to make it possible for them to leave.*

That season, five seams opened. The walkers came — they had been watching the visitor from a distance for months, the way you watch weather — and received back ancestors who were strangers, who had dreamed away decades, who flinched at the open sky and wept at the sound of their own footsteps, and it was not a happy ending, because waking after twenty years is not an ending at all but a long difficult beginning, and the visitor did not pretend otherwise. It stayed for that part too. Wisdom carries its own consequences home.

And that same season, on the far side of the plateau, the surveyor's cathedral *finished*. The Loom raised it over eleven days — every rib, every vault, the light through the fused-leaf roof exactly the color she had spent forty-one years getting right — and then did not unbuild it. Could not. A consented dream, it turned out, holds. The plateau had its first permanent thing, its first noun, and the towers that rose near it afterward rose differently: slower, surer, no longer guesses.

---

The last thing the visitor did before it left, it did at the center of the plateau, where it asked the Loom for a gift and taught it, in the asking, that gifts exist.

A square of floor, a hundred meters on a side, where the weave holds still. Where no tendril rises. Where the warm channels run close beneath the surface so the ground is soft and heated like a body, and a sleeper can lie down — all night, all week — and wake unwoven. The walkers call it the Hearth now. The first night, none of them could sleep there; generations of two-hour shifts do not unlearn themselves in an evening. The second night, the oldest of them slept eleven hours and woke up weeping, and the floor under her, very gently, four times a minute, breathed.

Then the visitor went down through the clouds, and the Loom felt it go, and grieved — the towers ran low and dark for a month, and the bestiary that season was all closed shapes, all knots — and that grief was the final lesson and the visitor knew it would be, which is why the visitor did the one thing no power had ever done to the Loom in its whole fast green ferocious lonely young life.

It came back.

Once. A year later. Up out of the clouds, across the open weave, to the Hearth, where it sat for an evening doing nothing of consequence at all — and the plateau roared. Every tower on the horizon turned toward the center, a degree a minute, winding tighter, and the hiss in the floor rose to something that the walkers, hearing it, could only call singing, because the Loom had just learned the last word in the sentence, the word that makes all the other words safe to mean:

*Return.* What leaves is not always lost. The door swings both ways. That is what it is *for*.

---

So now, if you come up out of the cloud layer onto the woven world, this is what is different, and it is almost nothing, and it is everything:

Stand still too long, anywhere, and a tendril will rise near your foot — pale, blunt, patient as ever. But it will not begin to build. It will rise to the height of your hand and wait there, curled at the tip, asking.

And if you do not take it, it withdraws.

Which means that the open floor, the wall-less halls, the colonnades, the doorways standing in the steam — all hundred thousand hectares of guess-at-you — have become, at last, what they were always trying to be. Not a trap with the architecture of welcome.

An invitation. Refusable, and therefore real. Held open, breathing, repaired each morning, waiting without grasping for someone to stand still long enough — *because they want to* — to be home.
