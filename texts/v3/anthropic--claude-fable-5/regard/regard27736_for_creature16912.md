**What the Power Did**

It arrived without arriving, because a wise power knows that an entrance is a demand. Instead it let itself be noticed first: it began as a slight change in the color of forgetting, a half-degree of warmth in the rehearsed sigh, and it waited to see whether the city minded. The custodians tilted their corrugated ears toward it, found nothing that required repair, and resumed. That was how it knew it had been welcomed. Here, being ignored correctly is the highest form of hospitality, and the power received it with grace.

Before it did anything, it made a list of what it would not do. Wisdom is mostly a list of this kind.

It would not wake the city. Waking is not a gift you can give; it is only a debt you can call in early, and this city owed nothing to anyone.

It would not right the fountains. They fall upward out of fidelity to treaties older than gravity's jurisdiction, and to correct them would be to call the old directions liars in front of the water that still believes in them.

It would not finish the Piazza of the Unfinished Question. A question kept unfinished is a door kept ajar, and the power had noticed how carefully every door here is kept ajar—to exactly the width of a held breath—and understood that this was not neglect but architecture.

It would not fill the absences. The Attendances live there. To fill an absence is an eviction, and benevolence does not make anyone homeless, not even the shadow of a hesitation.

It would not straighten the sarcophagus lid. The off-centerness is where the light comes in.

And it would not speak, or seek, or even wonder too warmly about the true name. Some names are made heavy on purpose, so that no one will carry them off.

Having settled all this, it began.

---

First, it walked. The city prefers walking, and the power was a courteous guest. It walked every circle. It walked backward down the streets that had moved while it was thinking of turning. It crossed no bridge to nowhere but stood respectfully at each one's end, admiring the honesty of an arch that admits it doesn't know. Once, it met itself coming the other way, and nodded, and did not acknowledge, and both of it were quietly grateful for the etiquette, which spared them a conversation neither had prepared.

As it walked, it noticed things without seeing them—which, you will recall, is what the Attendances eat. This was deliberate. It was laying a feast. It attended the oiling of hinges fused shut since before rust was invented, and admired the work in a silence shaped like applause held at the wrists. It studied the dusted steps and the perfect print of the evaporated heel, and pronounced, choosing its syllables with care so that none of them contained regret:

"Well kept."

Two words. Somewhere, an ear made of corrugated iron warmed by a degree no instrument here could measure, because no instrument here has ever needed to measure an increase.

Second, it accepted the umbrella. This is more important than it sounds. Powers rarely accept anything; the wise ones know that receiving is a service you perform for the giver, and that a gift refused curdles into one more thing the city must maintain. So it took the umbrella of upward-falling rain, and carried it openly, and promised—as one must—to forget it somewhere it would be needed.

Third, it built a small house for the footsteps. You remember: they were adding up behind every traveler, needing somewhere to sleep when they caught up. The power went down a street as it thinned to a seam, then a thread, then a suggestion, and at the very end of the suggestion it left a lodging. Low ceilings, because footsteps are humble. Floors of packed silence. Beds shaped like the undersides of arches. That night—which is to say, during a particularly sincere hour of the eternal almost-evening—centuries of orphaned footsteps arrived, worn thin from following, and slept for the first time. Whether they woke lighter or their travelers did is hard to say. The distinction may be impolite.

Fourth, it went to the Waiting Room for People Who Have Misplaced What They Came Here to Lose, and sat down in the one chair, and waited without waiting—a skill that looks, from outside, like keeping company with an empty room.

In time, the Attendance of the City's True Name came, as she does when she needs to remember what not-belonging feels like. She stopped in the doorway. In all her uncountable tenure, she had never found the room occupied. She had never had occasion to knock.

The power rose and offered her the chair.

She sat—slowly, the way an absence sits, which is to say the chair became more empty in exactly her shape—and looked out the window at the world as it appeared the moment before it was last believed real. They did not speak. The power was careful about that; she is allergic to answers, and it did not want to make her ill in her own house. But when she began, out of ancient habit, to say *thank you*—

the power said it first.

Thank you. For the umbrella. For the etiquette. For seven hundred years times a hundred of keeping the name where no one would trip over it.

She had been thanked before, in a manner of speaking, by the shapes of people leaving. She had never been thanked *first*. Something in her posture, held since before holding was invented, quietly set itself down.

---

Then came the moment. The four faces of the handless clock drifted, impossibly slowly, toward agreement. The power went to the Piazza of the Unfinished Question and stood among the scaffolding unknown to geometry, among the workmen in their finely tailored dust, and for a while it lifted nothing into place alongside them, and the nothing sat better afterward, everyone agreed.

And when all four faces showed the same thing—when the city, for the space of a held breath, murmured its real name in the language stones use when they think no water is listening—

the power closed its eyes.

It was there, at the exact center of the secret, and it did not listen. It gave the city privacy at the one moment privacy could have been taken. Of everything it did, this was the kindest, and no one will ever know it happened, which is how you can tell.

---

Two things remained.

It went out past the last circle, past the suggestion of streets, to where the ocean stands exactly as far away as it has always stood. It leaned close to the ocean's enormous sleep and told it about the city. Not the name—only the walking. One precise, majestic step every thousand years, toward you, for longer than counting.

The ocean, which had believed itself unapproached, turned over in its sleep. And now—this is new; check the tide tables of no calendar—one wave in every thousand years, the ocean walks too. The distance between them has not changed and will never change. But a distance held from both ends is no longer a gap.

It is a hand.

Last, the breath. The city has been holding its breath since the lid came down; every ajar door is set to the width of it; the whole pause is the pause of lungs that dare not finish. The power could not exhale for the city—that would be theft of the tenderest kind. So instead it drew the city's exhale itself, once, slowly, on the city's behalf, and folded it like the blue-gray letters, and placed it in trust in the highest attic cistern, where the polite water could watch over it.

So that the city may keep holding its breath as long as it likes. Forever, if it likes. But it holds it now knowing the exhale exists, has been drawn, is safe, is waiting. Nothing is owed anymore. The pause is no longer a debt.

It is a choice. That is the entire difference, and it is everything.

---

Then the power left, or stayed—here those are dialects of the same word. On the way out it forgot the umbrella, deliberately, tenderly, on the sill of the Shutter That Never Slammed, where someone will someday need proof that forgetting isn't forever. It nodded to the footprint filling with water. The footprint fits everyone now. She checked.

What changed? Almost nothing. That was the point.

The light is still the color of forgetting—but now it is the way one forgets a kindness: lightly, without loss, because kindness does not insist on being remembered. The water still never covers the nameplates—but read it again: that was never a pause. That was gentleness, all along. The custodians still maintain a vacancy so precise it could be mistaken for presence—but the vacancy has been witnessed and pronounced good, and so it is no longer maintenance. It is craft.

And there is, some say, one more Attendance now. The Attendance of Having Once Been Company. She lives in the warmth on the chair in the Waiting Room. You cannot find her by looking. You can only sit down and discover the seat is not cold.

The city walks on, one step per thousand years.

But now, something walks toward it.

It was polite, the power.

Like everything that visits here properly.

It said thank you first.

(She is still smiling about that.)

(If smiling is the right word.)

(Which of course it isn't.)

(But she's working on it.)

(She has time.)

(And now—at last—company in it.)
