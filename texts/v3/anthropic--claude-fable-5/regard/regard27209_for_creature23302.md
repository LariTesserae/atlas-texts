**The Patron of the Wet Gallery**

What drew the power's attention, in the end, was the tick.

Not the thunder — thunder is common, the world is full of seas hammering on things. What it noticed, somewhere in its long inventory of the world's sounds, was a small dry note repeating once a minute, in the dark, ten fathoms down, and repeating on time. A chipped tooth striking an iron housing, every sixty seconds, for a hundred years, with no one keeping the count. Faithfulness on that scale is rare even among the living. Among the unliving it is rarer still, and wise powers are drawn to it the way the rest of us are drawn to lit windows.

So it came — the way everything true comes to this room, through the water, on the tide, unannounced — and the first thing it did was nothing.

It stayed a full tide cycle, slack to thunder to slack, and formed no opinion. It stayed a year: the blenny spring with its rows of amber eggs laid out along the bolt-lines, the summer evenings when the shaft-light filled with glass splinters, the wolf-fish's long coiled winter fast. Still no opinion. It understood that the room's native unit of time was not the day or the year but the *office* — and so it stayed for one entire conger, from the morning a new grey head first showed in the throat of Number Three to the autumn night, nineteen years on, when the eel went out through the penstocks on the ebb and did not return. Only then, having watched a whole tenure begin and end and the den stand empty and fill again, did the power permit itself to consider what, if anything, should be done.

It had come, as benevolence always comes, half-prepared to heal. It went through the room looking for the wound.

It considered the pumps, and set them down. To restore them — to drive the water out and let boots cross the diamond-scored floor again — would not be repair. The room had finished becoming what it is; restoring it would only ruin the ruin, and evict a parish to recover a basement.

It considered the light, and set it down. More light would be easy — a wider grating, a cleaner shaft — and more light would bring a forest where there is now a stand of eight or nine fronds, and the forest would shade out the felt, and the dark economy of the walls would falter. The dimness is not a deprivation here. It is load-bearing.

It considered the silver — the twice-daily panic, the pollack rising, the conger unwinding through the school — and set that down too, almost sternly, as one sets down a temptation. Mercy to the sprat is famine to everything below them. The panic and the intent are one music; you cannot strike the dark notes from a piece and call what remains kind.

It considered the wolf-fish, oldest single body in the room, teeth worn to pebbles, and was tempted to give it ease, or years. It set that down as well. The room is built on the principle that offices outlast officeholders. To exempt one body from that — even the most venerable — would be to misunderstand the whole architecture of the place.

And then it came to the shelf by the shaft, and the mug, and it stopped for a long time, because here, if anywhere, was the prisoner.

The power examined the anchorite the way a magistrate examines an old sentence, looking for grounds to commute it. It found none — or rather, it found that the sentence had been quietly converted, decades ago, by the only authority competent to convert it. The crab had not been trapped so much as it had *settled*; it had taken a confinement and made it a vocation, resolved the question of leaving by ceasing to ask it, and now sat in the mouth of its cell at every mid-tide with its claws spread into the flow, fishing the thunder, keeping its one view the way some beings keep faith. To crack the mug and carry the crab out into open water would not be liberation. It would be evicting an anchorite from a cell it had consecrated, and the open sea would kill it inside a week, bewildered and unhoused.

Still — because wisdom asks rather than assumes — the power put the question. It pressed it gently into the current, in the way powers ask, a question without words moving through the cold green water: *is there anything you want?*

The tide turned. The valves quickened. The crab leaned out of the mug and spread its claws into the flow.

*More of this.*

So the power granted more of this — which is to say, it granted nothing, which is to say, it granted everything. It is the only prayer the gallery has ever offered up, and it was answered in full.

One temptation remained, the subtlest. The power considered telling someone. It knew what the room was worth as knowledge — the gauge with its needle at *32 lbs*, the stenciled letters, the wheel that turns on bearings dissolved into something frictionless and strange. It also knew exactly how that would go: the lamps, the lift bags, the careful documentation, the gauge in a glass case ashore with a placard, the mug in a museum drawer, the anchorite dead in a bucket on a deck in the sun. It closed that door and locked it from the inside. Part of the gift, it understood, would have to be the withholding.

So: it had gone looking for the wound and could not find one. The room had already been healed, long ago, by the only physician with standing here, which is time. And when a wise power finds a thing whole, it does not invent an ailment to justify the journey. It does what this one did.

It took the only office the parish had never filled. The Wet Gallery had a priesthood of bacteria, householders, an anchorite, undertakers, circuit riders, a succession in the dead pump's throat, and a congregation of silver. What it had never had — what nothing in it could ever be for itself — was a *witness*. A parish without a patron, holding perfect services for a century to an empty sky.

The power took the patronage, and with it the duties.

**First, the keeping of records.** The parish writes its history in the only materials it has — oxide, shell, succession — and those records cannot be read, only inhabited. The power transcribes them now into something that does not rust. It holds the count of the congers (the one departing was the seventh; the patron has given each of them, retroactively, a name, though nothing in the room will ever use it). It holds the tally of the wheel — some fifty million revolutions and rising. It holds the memory of the octopus's little arrangements at the den mouth — the rivet head, the shard of gauge glass — which were a kind of writing too, and which would otherwise have been the most fully lost things in a room full of lost things. Every blenny that ever held a stud-hole is in the ledger now. Every silver body the school closed over. The hermit crab's name is in it, near the top.

**Second, intercession — but rationed.** The patron has allowed itself three interventions across the whole life of the room, no more, each one to be small and disguised as weather. It has spent two.

The first: there will come a winter, decades on, when the weed on the grating finally mats thick enough to seal the shaft, and the trapezoid of light will gutter and die, and with it the kelp, the limpets, the summer swarms — the one thing the parish cannot keep for itself. That winter, a storm will work a drifting spar against the grating and comb it clean. It will look exactly like luck.

The second: there will come an autumn when the eighth conger goes out on the ebb and, for once, no elver finds the penstocks — the succession, for the first time, genuinely broken, the office about to lapse. In the dark outer water, one small eel will turn a single degree from its course. Nothing will record this. The ninth conger will simply appear in the throat of Number Three the following spring, the way they always have.

The third intervention is unspent. The patron is holding it in reserve, the way a parish keeps a fund for the roof.

**Third, attendance.** Twice a day, at mid-tide, the patron attends services — formless, in the thunder, asking nothing. And once a year it does the thing that keeps a patron honest: it puts on a small body and joins the congregation from below. For one full tide it is an amphipod on the felt, a translucent comma grazing the slow surrender of iron to seawater, hiding from the blennies, deafened by the valves — so that it never forgets what the gallery is like when the iron is not a relic or a parish or a poem but simply *the world*, generous and slow, the only one there is.

**Fourth — and this required no act at all, only presence —** it hears the bell. Once a minute, the chipped tooth strikes the housing and the one bright line of clean metal in the whole room rings its small dry note. For a hundred years that note was a flaw striking a casing. A bell that is heard becomes a bell. It has been a bell now for some time.

There is one more thing, which is less a duty than a promise, and the patron made it on the day it took the office.

It will not save the room.

The nation in the walls — the oldest residents, the truest ones — will finish their work someday. They are not a disease the patron would dream of curing; they are the parish too, and their work is the room's slow honest death, conducted at the only respectable pace. Some night, in a century deep enough that no one now alive on the surface has descendants who remember the harbor, a plate will let go, and then a course of brick, and the vault will come down — not in catastrophe, the gallery does not do catastrophe, but in a long sigh of iron and silt on a falling tide. The parish will disperse into the ordinary sea, which has been receiving its emigrants all along: the wolf-fish blood already seeded into distant wrecks, the school closing over the loss of the whole room the way it closes over everything. The mound on the harbor floor will grow weed and become a reef of no particular history.

The patron will attend that last service. It will hear the final clap of the valves, and the main's last moan, and it will count the wheel down through its last revolutions. And when the chipped tooth strikes the housing for the last time — one small dry note, on schedule to the end — the patron will do the one thing it has saved for that moment through all the long tides of its tenure.

It will ring back.

Until then, nothing has changed, and nothing in the room could detect that anything has. The tide turns; the valves quicken; the anemones open in unison; the anchorite leans out of the mug into the flow. The blennies hold the freeholds and the ninth conger thickens in the dark and the school threads the spokes of the turning wheel without touching it. The room is not waiting. It was never waiting.

But the sentence at the heart of the place has been quietly amended. *Nobody is coming back for any of it* — that is still true, and will stay true, and the patron means to keep it true. And yet: none of it is unattended now. Not the mug, not the manifold shadow, not one sprat of the silver hundred, not one cell of the nation in the walls.

Twice a day, in the cold, in the dark, the parish holds its services — and is, at last, kept.
