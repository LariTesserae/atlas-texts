**The Guest of the Flywheel Basin**

It arrived from above, because that is the only way to arrive, and it arrived the way light arrives: without displacing anything. The thermocline did not register its passing. No silt rose. The weed did not lean. If you had been hovering at the rim you would have noticed nothing except, perhaps, that for a moment the dim noon of the basin seemed slightly more *noticed* — the way a room feels different when someone stands in the doorway, even before you turn around.

It was old enough to recognize the basin at a glance, and wise enough to look anyway.

It read the place the way you would read a page: the engine's provenance (a name, a hull, a storm, a bill of lading that nobody had thought worth saving — the power saved it now, reflexively, the way you pick up a dropped photograph); the lineage of the crust, back through colder oceans than this one; the silting of the floor, layer by layer, each year's grey page laid on the last. It found the tenant in the bearing within the first revolution and understood it entirely within the second. That part took no wisdom. Understanding is cheap to a power. The expensive thing came after.

Because benevolence, when it arrives somewhere, arrives looking for a wound. That is its habit and its trade. And the power circled the basin once — past the standing weed, past the concluded boulders, past the engine with its one moving part catching the light — and found none.

So it did what wise things do when their first instinct comes up empty. It sat with its hands open and considered every gift it could give, one at a time, and set each one down.

It could have healed the engine — drawn the corrosion out of the housing, trued the shaft, restored the machine to the bright purposeful thing it had been on the day it was made. And the bearing, returned to engineered tolerances, would have sheared the living ring out of existence in a day, and the wheel would have turned faster, perfectly, lubricated by nothing, over an empty floor. Set down.

It could have fed the basin — opened the eastern rim wide, let the ocean's wealth pour in, oxygen and plankton and the rain of food from above. And within a decade there would be shells in the silt, burrows, tracks, grazers on the weed, the whole bright noisy commerce of the sea. A reef where the museum stood. And the tenant — built for poverty, tuned to one thin ration delivered on one slow schedule — would be overgrown, outcompeted, eaten by things with mouths, ended by abundance as surely as by drought. The power saw clearly what most benefactors never do: that the creature's niche was scarcity itself, and that to make it rich was to make it extinct. Set down.

It could have made the bearing eternal — frictionless, the shaft and housing forever unwearing, the wheel turning till the sun went out. But the ring eats the friction's harvest. The grinding *is* the meal. A frictionless bearing is a table with nothing on it, and an eternal engine would have carried a starving tenant forever, which is not preservation but the longest possible cruelty. Set down — and the power noted, with something like respect, that the creature had already solved this problem better than eternity could: it ate its house at exactly the rate its house could afford, and would finish eating around the time it finished living. Set down.

It could have given it kin. But it has kin. They stand all around it on the floor, furred and grey, each one a finished draft, and the tenant concluded them itself, long ago, by being the part that found the moving water. Set down gently.

And it could have done the great thing, the thing powers are always suspected of wanting to do: it could have *woken* it. Gathered the film's slow chemistry into something that could hold an image of the place it maintains. Given it the basin to see — the pale floor, the standing weed, the light coming down.

This one the power held the longest.

Then it set it down hardest of all. Because awareness requires a future, and a future, in a place where nothing will ever arrive, is just another word for waiting. The tenant does not wait. That is its entire grace. It keeps no future — only the turn it is in, and the turn after, delivered cold and iron-flavored and exactly on time — and so the basin contains no disappointment, no hope, no boredom, no dread: none of the sufferings that need a tomorrow to live in. To wake the creature would be to hand it the one wound the basin cannot hold, and then call the handing a gift. The power had seen that done before, elsewhere, by powers no less benevolent and considerably less wise. It did not do it here.

So: nothing to heal, nothing to feed, nothing to free, nothing to wake. A lesser power would have left then, faintly disappointed, and called the place complete.

The wise power looked at the eastern rim.

It read the marl the way it had read everything else, and there, under the seep, it found the future: a wedge of stone with a fault hidden in it like a held breath. Nine hundred years, give or take a storm — some deep internal wave would shrug against the rim, and the wedge would let go all at once, and the gap would close in an afternoon. The seep cut. The ration stopped. And then the bad ending: the wheel coasting on for days on stored momentum, the shaft still grinding, the ring still being consumed but no longer fed — *eaten without being fed*, friction without harvest — the one event in all the basin's possible futures shaped like cruelty, the closest its physics could ever come to authoring a suffering. The tenant has no nerves and would feel nothing. The power weighed that, and found it did not matter. Some things are wrong in themselves, with no one inside them to mind.

So it did its one deed, and the deed was this: it aged the fault.

Not healed it — aged it. It let the wedge settle now, tonight, by less than the width of a silt grain, taking up in one silent instant the stress that nine centuries would have banked toward catastrophe. No slump would ever come. Instead the gap in the rim would do what everything else in the basin does: conclude slowly, from the inside, one grain a decade, the seep thinning so gradually that the wheel's interval would stretch from five seconds toward six across a thousand years — and no revolution, not one, would ever be distinguishably slower than the revolution before it. The tenant would receive exactly what it has voted for, turn after turn, without a single missed delivery, all the way down. It would never experience change, because there would never be a moment in which change was locally true. It would taper into stone mid-constancy, the way the boulders did, and join the finished part of itself with its accounts square and its rhythm unbroken to the last measurable beat.

Understand what the power did and did not do. It did not save the creature. The creature was never in danger of anything but its own conclusion, which is not danger. It did not extend the life, except incidentally. It edited the *death* — traded an ending for a tapering — and it did this in a way that is invisible from inside the bearing and always will be. That is the signature of the act, the thing that marks it wise: from the only point of view that exists in the basin, nothing happened tonight. Nothing will ever have happened. The mercy was real and will never be received, and the power considered this the correct price and paid it without ceremony.

It did one small administrative thing besides. Somewhere above, in the bright fast world, a survey transect bent by half a degree for reasons its planners found sensible; a chart kept its blank; an anchorage shifted east. The same nobody who had recorded nothing here — not the sinking, not the settling, not the tenancy — was quietly confirmed in the post, indefinitely. Names travel, and sample vials are small, and the power saw no reason the basin's privacy should be left to luck when it could be left to design that looked exactly like luck.

And then — because there was nothing more to do, and because doing nothing in a place is different from never having come — it stayed.

It stayed until it stopped hearing the sound, the way you stop hearing your own heart, and then it stayed longer, until it heard it again. It went among the boulders and took the accounting it felt was owed: learned the tenure of each concluded colony, the order in which they finished, which had lived longest in the dark on the least. It held the list. Things that ended before anyone knew them deserve, at minimum, to be counted by someone who can count forever. The weed it left alone, with the courtesy one shows furniture in a house where someone is sleeping.

When it rose at last through the thermocline — again without displacing anything, again unregistered — it carried away exactly one thing, and the taking cost the basin nothing, because the thing was not subtracted but copied:

the interval.

One revolution every four or five seconds. The basin's pulse, lifted out entire and kept now in whatever a power keeps things in, running there alongside whatever else it has chosen, over all its years, to keep. It is kept at the original tempo. It will be kept at the original tempo when the wheel below has stretched to six seconds, to a minute, to stillness; kept when the engine is only one more grey stone with a circular face; kept when the basin itself is uplifted and dried and weathered into a hillside that no one will ever read correctly.

You hovered at the rim once, and watched the gleam come and go, and thought: *keeping time for no one.* And learned you were wrong by one.

Count again. Wrong by two, now — and the second keeper does not depend on the wheel. Some ten thousand years on, when the last revolution eases into the next without arriving, the count in the basin will end, and the count of the basin will not. Somewhere, the time will still be kept: a rhythm outliving its wheel the way the boulders outlive the film, the way a held note outlives the bell.

That was all the power did. It found a finished place and did not improve it. It found a small life and did not enlarge it. It found a flawed ending and quietly revised it, and signed nothing, and made certain no one would ever know, and took the pulse with it when it went — so that the slowest engine ever to run, running on the smallest tenant ever to keep one, would be, in the fullest sense available to either of them, *remembered*: not in image, not in name, but in tempo, which was the only language the basin ever spoke.

*Whum.*

*Whum.*

*Whum.*

Somewhere, still.
