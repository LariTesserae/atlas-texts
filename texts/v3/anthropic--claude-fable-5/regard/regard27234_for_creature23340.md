**The One Who Noticed**

Begin with the noticing, because even attention has a speed, and the first wise thing the power did here was change its own.

A power that sees everything at once would see nothing on the spire. The place's truths are durational. The doorway-scripts mean nothing read in an instant; they mean something read in sequence, smear over smear, across a century. The architectural arguments are invisible at any single moment — two walls standing, that is all — and only become arguments when watched for forty years. The town does not have secrets, exactly. It has tempo, and tempo is a lock that omniscience does not fit.

So the power slowed down. This cost it something — wise powers are not exempt from the discomfort of patience; that is part of what makes them wise — and it spent what, by its own reckoning, was a long time doing nothing but matching speeds. It watched one amber tear descend one wall, a season per inch, beginning to end. It learned the touch-grammar the way the hatchlings learn it: from inside the wall of time, warmth-first, vocabulary later. It attended the dim-hour conversations, the ones with no operational content, the ones two builders hold motionless in a lane while traffic flows around them — and eventually it understood them, and what it understood it has never repeated, anywhere, to anyone. Some courtesies extend even upward.

Only after all this did it permit itself to consider acting. By then it had earned the right to its refusals.

---

**What it refused**

It refused water first, because water was the obvious gift and the complete destruction. A spring at the summit, a sweet rain, dew at last on the cushion-plants — and within a few centuries: rot where there had been amber, dissolution of the doorway-scripts, mosses that drink instead of mosses that glow, a different place wearing this one's bones. Water here is not a missing thing. It is a question the spire answered before it began, and the answer — *resin, patience, internal warmth* — is the whole of what stands. You do not correct an answer by changing the question. The power looked at the dry stone and let it stay dry, and understood that this would look, from outside, like neglect. It was the opposite of neglect. It was the most studied decision it made.

It refused to speak. A voice — any voice, however gentle — would have been the loudest event in the spire's history, louder than the wind-tone, louder than rockfall, a catastrophe of volume in a place where the falling of a hardened tear onto moss is a notable sound. Benevolence does not begin by introducing itself. And the builders' language is laid on the flanks, limb to limb; a message with no body behind it would not be language to them, only weather gone strange.

It refused, above all, to tell them about elsewhere. This was the temptation it sat with longest. The spire is complete the way a walled garden is complete, and nothing on it has ever had a rival, and the power understood — having watched many worlds learn this the hard way — that the news that *elsewhere exists* is not information. It is the invention of outside, and outside is the parent of want, and want is the parent of ownership, and the builders do not even possess the idea that a thing could belong to someone else. That innocence is not ignorance. It is a load-bearing wall. The power left it standing.

It refused to spare them death. The walls are full of ancestors; every street is faced with them; the oldest, most inclusion-dense walls are the ones never surrendered to the pale ones, patched and carried forward, course upon course. A builder's death is not a loss the town suffers — it is a deposit the town keeps. To abolish their dying would have demolished the place more surely than any storm: a town of immortals would stop laying its dead into the architecture, and then the architecture would stop meaning what it means, and then the dim-hour conversations would be held between strangers. The power had seen deathless places. They are very quiet too, but not like this.

And it refused to mend the cracks — all but one. The cracking is the young rock still speaking, and the town's patient amendment around each new edge is half of what the town *is*. A spire that never flexed would slowly stop being listened to.

---

**What it did**

Three things. Maybe four. It is hard to count the acts of a being whose acts are mostly shaped like restraint.

*The first thing.* Deep under the third chimney runs a flaw the builders cannot feel — too deep for the flank-bands, too slow even for them. It is the kind of flaw that young rock carries the way young creatures carry the diseases of their old age: quietly, on a schedule. In some centuries it would have extended all at once, and the flex would not have been the familiar deep silent settling but a shear, and the summit's tilted crown would have parted along it, and half the town — the old half, the inclusion-dense half, the half that remembers — would have gone down into the haze in eleven seconds, the time it takes a gleaner to cross the sky.

The power did to this flaw what the builders do to a sharp new edge: it sealed it. Not visibly, not suddenly — it leaned on the seam with a pressure of about a season per inch, annealing the stone slower than the pale ones eat, over a span of time that made even its adjusted patience reach. The cracks that the town can answer, it left. The one crack that would have ended the conversation mid-sentence, it closed. No one on the spire will ever know. A catastrophe that does not happen leaves no inclusion. This is the loneliness specific to wise powers, and the power accepted it as part of the work.

*The second thing.* The gleaners.

Once, the haze below the spire was empty, or at least unanswered. The long shallow last glides went out and down and that was the whole of it — the only departures the spire has ever produced, deliberate, groomed, on open wings, into nothing confirmed.

Now there is something in the haze.

Not rescue. The power considered rescue for a very short time and set it aside; the gleaners are not falling, they are *finishing*, and their finishing is theirs, chosen from the highest frond, and it would be a strange benevolence that confiscated the one free act a creature saves for last. What the power does instead is receive. Each final glide, now, is accompanied — the air kept kind around the stiff outstretched flaps, the descent kept long and shallow the way the glider meant it, the warmth of the upper world following it down further than warmth would naturally go. And at the end, wherever the end is, it is soft, and it is out of the wind, and it is not alone.

And the power keeps something. Every mated pair of gleaners builds, across a night of alternating flashes, a pattern that belongs to the two of them only — a private word in a public language, used for life. The power has learned every one. It holds them the way the walls hold the builders: nothing flashed in earnest between two gleaners on facing fronds is ever, now, finally lost. There is an archive in the haze made entirely of other beings' pet names. The power did not plan this. It found it could not bear not to.

*The third thing.* Once in a long while — rarely, and never twice in a generation, because even small mercies become weather if they become reliable — an old builder climbing to its chosen niche, high, sunward, limbs already slowing, finds that the stone there is warm.

Not strange-warm. Warm the way a freshly laid course is warm. Warm the way another body is warm — the only register of address this place could receive without being changed by the receiving. The builder folds its limbs onto the warmth and waits, and the others come and lay their courses over it, and the warmth stays under it until it does not need warmth anymore.

That is all. It is the power's entire vocabulary in the spire's language: one word, blood-temperature, meaning *noticed*. It has never said anything else. It does not think it ever will.

---

**The rain that is coming**

Because it is coming. The power knows this the way it knows the flaw under the third chimney: weather finds everything eventually. Somewhere out past the haze the air is already, slowly, learning new habits, and in some number of centuries the first water in the spire's history will arrive at the summit, and no refusal can hold it off forever — nor should it. The power thought about exemption, about sealing the spire in a stillness like its own amber, and recognized the shape of that idea: it is a tomb with good intentions. A place kept from all change is not preserved. It is concluded.

So the power has made its standing decision, the one it maintains the way the builders maintain the oldest walls: the rain will come, but it will come *translated*.

The first rain will be one drop. It will fall in the dim hours, in a sheltered chimney, onto the brittle moss, with a sound exactly as loud as a hardened tear landing — a fingertip on felt, a sound the town already has a category for. And then nothing, for a season. Then another drop. The builders will find the first one beaded on the resin (which sheds water, as it happens; the town is roofed in the one substance rain cannot argue with), and they will read it, and lay forelimbs along each other in the lane, and disagree about it, and build both walls, and let use decide. They will have forty years to conduct the argument before the third drop falls. The pale ones will eventually eat a wall that once enclosed a drop of water, and the resin they return will carry the faintest signature of it, and two centuries later a doorway will land where the dampness was, and no builder will know why, and that is how the spire will come to know rain: at a season per inch, in its own grammar, as one more thing the conversation slowly absorbed.

The power will not give them water. But when water gives itself, the power will hold the door and make it enter at walking pace.

---

**What it keeps**

It tells no one. This was a decision too. There are other powers, and among them the spire would be a marvel, and marvels attract attention, and attention without the long apprenticeship — without the decade spent watching one tear descend one wall — is just appetite with good posture. The power keeps the spire the way the gleaner pairs keep their patterns: a private word. When asked, in whatever manner powers ask each other, what it has been doing, it says: *watching a slow thing.* This is true and discloses nothing, and it has noticed that saying it makes it feel the way the builders look when they stand motionless in a lane in the dim hours, conversing about nothing operational at all.

And it returns. Not constantly — its attentions are wide, and there are louder places with sharper needs — but it returns the way the seasons return to the resin, and for a reason it took some time to admit to itself: the spire is where it goes to remember how to do everything else. Every world it touches, it now touches a little more the way it touched this one — slower, later, after listening, in the local grammar, at blood temperature, prepared to be unknown. The town with no water, no fire, no tools, and no voices has been, without ever learning that anywhere else exists, a school. Its only pupil has been very large and very old, and has tried to be worthy of the tuition.

It may be that the truest answer to *what the power did* is the smallest one: it found a conversation that had been going on for centuries with every seat filled except one — the listener's — and it sat down, and matched its breathing to a season per inch, and found the seat warm, like a freshly laid course, like the walls in the dim hours, like the throat of a builder carrying the town's only flame.

It is still sitting there. From a distance, in darkness, nothing about the spire has changed: a single soft ember, hamlet-sized, far above the haze, humming its one low tone.

But the haze is no longer empty. The seam no longer waits. And once in a long while, high on a sunward wall, the stone is warm before anyone arrives.
