# The Visitor

There is no need here. That was the first thing the power understood, and it took even a power some time to truly feel it.

It had come the way such beings come to places — not by travel but by attention, the way a thought arrives at its subject. And it had come for the usual reason. Benevolent powers are drawn to wounds. They move through the world along gradients of suffering, the way water moves along slopes, and they had long ago stopped being surprised by what they found at the bottom of those slopes. What drew this one to Vantle was stranger and much rarer: an absence. A region of the high air where the lines of need that crosshatch the world simply thinned, and faded, and stopped — the way the engine-thrum, they say, cannot be heard from the lowlands. A silence in the shape of a city.

So it came expecting a trick. Places without visible need are usually places where need has been buried, or exported, or silenced. The power had unburied such places before, and it arrived over the terraces of Vantle in the manner of a physician arriving at a suspiciously healthy patient, and it began to look.

## The Examination

It looked for a long time, by its own measure, which is to say: through one full rebuilding of the engine called Old Sull, component by component, a piece per decade, until the machine was wholly new and had not for one moment stopped being itself.

It found hunger, in the small honest amounts that make food good. It found grief — the city was full of grief, as any living thing is — but the grief had somewhere to go: it went down into the soil-houses and came back up through the fig trees, and the power watched a widow eat a fig from the orchard above her district with an expression it had only ever seen before on the faces of saints, and on them only rarely. It found injustice in trace quantities, mostly concerning trough allocations, mostly being slowly composted by the tea-balconies into argument, which is the form of justice Vantle prefers because argument, unlike verdicts, can be maintained.

It found no prayers. This troubled it at first, professionally. Powers answer prayers; it is most of what they are for. But the Vantlers did not send their wanting upward. When a Vantler wanted something, they looked around for who kept it, and if no one kept it, they began to. The power slowly understood that it was watching a people who had, without ever framing the matter theologically, taken over the duties of providence themselves, in eleven thousand small hereditary pieces, and were discharging them with songs.

And so the power arrived at the condition it knew least well, the one its long benevolence had least prepared it for. It stood in the high hard light above the terraces with its hands, so to speak, empty, and confronted the question that wise beings fear more than any wound:

*What do you give to the whole?*

## The Refusals

It made a list, in the way of powers, of everything it could do. Then it went down the list, in the way of wise ones, crossing things off.

It could answer the mountain question. One gesture of attention through the strata and it would *know* — stone or no stone, ground or no ground, all the way down. It could whisper the answer onto one tea-balcony and watch certainty spread through the city like cool water. It crossed this off almost with horror. The third faction was right, it saw now — the small serene one. The question *was* the mountain. The argument was load-bearing. To answer it would be to kick the foundation out from under a city that had no other, and the power, which had ended droughts and turned plagues, understood that this would be the single most destructive act available to it in all of Vantle. The truth, here, was a demolition charge. It set the truth down carefully and stepped away.

It could take the labor. Ease the keeping — self-mending mesh, troughs that cleared themselves, engines that needed no hereditary sagas. It crossed this off faster than the first. It had seen, in other places, what people become when their keepings are taken from them and called gifts. There was a word the Vantlers used for a person who did not keep what they kept, and the power did not intend to become the being of whom that word was true at the scale of the entire city.

It could extend their lives. It looked at the orchards for a long time, at the soil with its lineage, at the phrase *gone down to before*, and it understood that death in Vantle was not a wound but a stratum — that the people were doing with their bodies exactly what the city did with its plazas, and that an immortal Vantler would be a person standing on nothing. Crossed off.

It could reveal itself. Be known, be thanked, be kept in the way of gods, with a niche and a lamp. It almost laughed, if powers laugh. A city that addresses its goats by name and grieves its crabs by census had no vacancy for worship. There was no room in Vantle's heart for a god because the position had been filled, long ago, by everything.

The list, when it finished, was empty. And the power felt — for the first time in an age that does not bear counting — something it eventually recognized, with great tenderness toward itself, as humility. It had met its own kind before, in certain mountains and certain old forests: vast slow benevolences that keep a place by being it. It had never before met one that was *made* — assembled out of brick and crabs and grandmothers and figs and arguments — and knew it was made, and kept itself anyway. Vantle was not a patient. Vantle was a colleague.

So it did what one does, on meeting a colleague. It looked for the one thing the other could not see about themselves. And it found it.

## The One Far Thing

The clouds are the water here. And clouds, over spans longer than guild memory, longer than the rebuilding of engines, longer even than the soil's lineage — drift. The power, whose attention runs on the timescale of climates, looked along the centuries ahead and saw the grazing decks rising, slowly, a handsbreadth a generation, until in some distant after they would slide past above the screens like herds finding a higher pasture, and the troughs would go quiet, and the city would begin, with terrible patience, to dry.

No one in Vantle could see this. It was the one danger pitched in a register below even their hearing — slower than trees, slower than rust. And here the power was tempted, at last, truly tempted: it could fix the sky. Pin the cloud decks to the altitude of the bronze forever. It stood with that gesture ready for a long time.

Then it set that down too, because a fixed sky is a kept thing with no keeper, and the first storm of trouble would find a city that had forgotten how to notice. Vantle did not need the future solved. Vantle needed the future to be *audible*. Felt through the soles, in time.

So the power did two small things, in the city's own idiom, and they were the whole of its intervention.

It breathed on the gallery crabs. Nothing visible — a lengthening of instinct, a patience added to patience. From that season forward, by increments no warden would ever see within one lifetime, the crabs would graze a little higher on the mesh as the clouds rose, generation tracking generation, until some far-off census would find them clustered at the top panels and the Screenwrights — who already know in their bones that a trough without crabs is a trough in trouble — would read their instruments correctly, as they always have, and begin, without panic, on the timescale of trees, to build the screens higher. The city follows its animals. The power had only to make one animal a longer letter.

And it slipped a verse into the work-songs. The Screenwrights' songs are the only schedule anyone keeps, and songs are how Vantle stores what it does not know it knows. The verse was a counting verse, about the herds going up the sky and the bronze going up to meet them, and within two generations no one remembered learning it, which in Vantle is the seal of authenticity. It would mean nothing for six hundred years. Then, one morning of low yield and high crabs, it would mean everything, and an old singer would stop mid-line on a windy brow and say: *the song knew.* Songs do. That is what they are for, and the power left its entire prophecy folded inside one, where the Vantlers keep all their other load-bearing things: underfoot, unexamined, walked on daily.

It allowed itself one mercy besides, small and deniable. The old people of Vantle have always said that the city holds you at the end — that in the last hour the floor beneath a dying Vantler runs warm as an engine-hall. The power made the saying true. Or perhaps only more true; even it was not certain there had been nothing there before. It adjusted no fate and lengthened no life. It only saw to it that the last thing a Vantler feels is *kelth*, rising, like the flank of something that is not sleeping after all, and never was.

## The Descent

Then it did the thing it had been putting off, the way even powers put things off. It went down.

Past the funiculars, past the borrowed daylight, past the strata the delvers map, into the cool sealed galleries where the lamps are found burning and the warm water flows where no main runs. It went down to *before*, expecting — it could not have said what. A peer. A predecessor. The buried god of a buried mountain.

What it found was housekeeping. Channels kept clear. Lamps kept lit. A vaulted dark that had the unmistakable feel of a kept room — and no keeper it could fix its vast attention on, because the keeper was not in the deeps; the keeper *was* the deeps, was the city, was the accumulated weight of every keeping ever done above, pressed by centuries into something that now, simply, continued. Vantle stands on Vantle, all the way down. The larger faction is right too. In Vantle, everyone is right who keeps arguing.

The power stood in the warm dark a long while. Then it did the only fitting thing. It asked the Vantler question, in no language, into the kelth:

*What do you keep?*

And the answer came back the same way, through the floor, patient as one revolution every few seconds:

*Them. All of it. You, now, if you stay.*

## The Keeping

It stays. That is the end of it, and the whole of what the benevolent and wise power did, when everything is counted: two breaths, one verse, one mercy, and then it asked for a keeping and was given one — a small one, as is proper for the new, the kind a child is given. A stretch of channel in the deep galleries. Three lamps.

The delvers find the lamps lit and leave them lit, out of courtesy, and climb back up to the light. The crabs graze a fraction higher than their great-grandmothers did, and no one has noticed, and that is the plan working. Somewhere on a western brow a Screenwright sings a counting verse and does not know she is singing six centuries of warning, kept in perfect condition, waiting.

And down in the dark, a being that once moved worlds clears leaves from a channel it did not dig, in a city it did not build, under a people it will never let know its name — and finds, to its lasting astonishment, that this is the largest work it has ever done. It used to give. Giving ends. Now it keeps, and keeping does not end, and the power has come to understand what every barefoot child with a planter in Vantle is taught by the warmth in the floor before they can speak:

that love, in its oldest and highest form, is just maintenance — attention without termination — and that if anyone ever asks the power where it is from, it will not name the heavens.

It will name a stratum first.
