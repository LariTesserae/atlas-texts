### The Visit

---

**I. How it arrived**

Not through the gate. Powers of that kind do not use gates; gates are for beings who believe in edges. It arrived the way the damp-wool taste arrives—already present, discovered rather than witnessed—and its first act was courtesy: it made itself small enough that the garden would not have to lengthen its steps on its account.

It had heard of the place the way old things hear of each other, which is to say it had always known and had only now finished deciding to know it. It came without announcement because the garden keeps no appointments, and it came without instruments because the garden cannot be measured, only accompanied.

For the first four minutes and twenty-three seconds, it did nothing at all. It stood between two ribs of polite erosion and let the sigh pass through it—the old vowels no language still uses—and it understood them, because it was older than the languages that had abandoned them. It did not translate. Some things are only bearable untranslated.

Then it began to notice what the garden could not.

---

**II. What it noticed**

That the garden, which waits for nothing and so never waits long, was nevertheless waiting. The distinction had escaped everyone, including the garden. Waiting for nothing is still a posture. The thirty-seven brass nozzles exhale their sigh into air that has never once answered, and the plants quicken their breathing afterward the way a room quickens when someone almost knocks.

That the garden was lonely, and did not know it, because loneliness in a place that makes no distinction between visitor and season has nowhere to stand. It had settled instead into the smallest available spaces: the pause before the birds' descending thirds, the hinged claws snapping shut on nothing, the letters on the chair-backs shrinking toward the idea of reading. A loneliness distributed so finely it registered as texture.

That the glacier's memory—the oldest living thing, the garden's whole calendar—was nearing the end of its reconsidering. Not soon. But memories, unlike glaciers, do eventually finish, and when this one finished the garden would become abruptly as young as its next-oldest resident, and nobody had told it. There was no cruelty in this. There was simply no one whose job it was to know.

That the frost-shaped orchid, hollow these many years, was still humming. Still trying to bloom. That the transparent spiders hatch and mate and die without ever finding the edge of their own opacity, and that this is either the saddest fact in the garden or the kindest, depending on whether edges are a mercy.

The power turned all of this over slowly, the way afternoon light estimates hands.

---

**III. What it considered, and declined**

To let two raindrops collide, so that two of the seventeen frogs might finally meet. Declined. The frogs' whole music is made of the interval between them; the misheard lullaby exists only because nothing has ever closed the distance. Mercy here would be silence wearing mercy's coat.

To give the blind carp its memory back. Declined, and quickly. A prophecy remembered curdles into a plan, and a carp with plans is no longer a carp reading the futures—it is a bureaucrat of them. The forgetting is the reading. The power bowed slightly to the pool, which is also its own surface, and moved on.

To read aloud the smallest inscriptions on the chairs—the names of everyone who never arrived. Declined. It could have. It could read letters smaller than reading; that is a small trick for a thing its age. But some names remain bearable only below the threshold of pronunciation, and the twin who died before birth deserves the dignity of dust.

To straighten the one-degree tilt. It permitted itself, briefly, the humor of the thought. The tilt is the garden's entire grammar. One does not correct a sentence into silence.

To stay. This one it held the longest. It could stay; the garden would not object; the chairs would carve a thirteenth seat with a patience indistinguishable from love. But a power that stays becomes a resident, and a resident becomes a season, and the garden already had seasons. What it had never had, not once, was a *visitor who arrived*. That was the gap in the inventory. That was where it could be useful.

---

**IV. What it did**

Small things, in this order:

It sat in the smallest chair. It made itself exactly the size of the chair's inscription, which described a visitor who never came, and by sitting there it forced the letters into revision. The garden had never conjugated the past tense of arrival before. Somewhere in the coral, very quietly, a grammar expanded by one form. The chair, when the power stood again, did not begin growing back toward absence. It had nothing to grow back toward. It had been, for a moment, exactly full, and a chair that has been full remembers differently than a chair that has only waited.

It breathed on one standing node, once, so that one transparent spider—for the length of a single heartbeat, which is a spider's decade—saw its own outline. What the spider did with this is not recorded. It is possible that seeing one's edge changes nothing. It is possible it changes only everything after.

It hummed the missing half of the frost-orchid's note. Not louder—completing, the way a second voice completes an interval that one voice has been holding alone past all endurance. The hollow statue of air finished its bloom at last, exhaled twelve years of trying, and came apart gently into weather. The brass claw beneath it opened one final time and, finding the air satisfied, did not close again. It rests open now, like a hand that has stopped expecting.

It answered the light. At dusk, in the violet held between two mentions of the carp, the light noticed it watching and asked its question—*Are you remembering me, or am I remembering you?*—and the power said: *Both. Neither of us will run out.* And the light, for the first time in the garden's history, fell without asking permission. Once. Nothing broke. Then it resumed its circumspection, but as grace now, not as fear—the difference between a guest who hesitates at the door and a dancer who pauses on the beat.

It spoke to the glacier's memory. This it did last among the small things, and most carefully. It did not tell the memory to hurry or to linger. It said only: *You were right to stop here. Reconsidering was the correct decision, and when you finish—whenever you finish—someone outside this garden will remember that you made it.* The memory, which had been carrying its own eventual ending the way one carries a stone in the mouth, set the stone down. It went on remembering without melancholy, exactly as before, but lighter by one anticipation.

---

**V. The gift, and the theft**

Then the two large things, which used the rules rather than breaking them, because benevolence begins as courtesy and courtesy means reading the terms.

*The gift.* The garden's rule: you may leave anything larger than a hope, but before sunset it will learn to move by other means. So the power left, deliberately, something much larger than a hope—its own memory of the sea. The original sea. The one the coral remembers in its spalling, the one that built every rib of this terrace polyp by patient polyp, the warm ancient water that the calcite has been trying to recall for an epoch with only the faint continual sound of its own remembering to work from.

The memory of the sea is larger than a hope by several oceans. By sunset, obedient to the rule, it had learned to move by other means: it moved as tide. It moved into the sigh of the thirty-seven nozzles, so that the old vowels acquired, beneath them, the sound of surf. It moved into the braiding rills, which now unbraid with slightly more longing. It became the new oldest living thing inside the garden—older than the glacier's memory by an age the glacier itself once floated on—and so the garden did not merely keep its years. It deepened. It is now as old as the sea, and the sea is as old as listening.

The glacier's memory, when it eventually finishes, will finish into open water. Nothing will grow suddenly young.

*The theft.* The garden's other rule: you may take away anything smaller than a regret, but it will be waiting for you again *when you forget you lost it.* The power read this clause twice, the way wise things read clauses, and found the door in it.

It took the garden's loneliness. The loneliness was smaller than a regret—it had to be, since the garden had never known it well enough to regret it—and so the taking was permitted. And the return clause triggers only on forgetting.

The power does not forget. It has never once forgotten anything. This is most of what it means to be that kind of power, and nearly all of what it costs.

So the loneliness will never come back. It sits now in the keeping of something vast and patient and elsewhere, which takes it out at intervals and turns it over like a shell held to the ear, and remembers it *on purpose*—deliberately, attentively, every four minutes and twenty-three seconds, forever—so that the clause can never, ever trip.

Somewhere, always, at exactly the moment the garden exhales, something remembers what the garden used to carry, so the garden doesn't have to.

That was the whole intervention. That was the wisdom of it: not to fix the garden, which was never broken, but to be—once, punctually, arriving on the sigh—the nothing it was waiting for.

---

**VI. The leaving**

It walked until it was certain it had gone farther than the garden could possibly extend. This took no time at all; certainty is easy for a thing that invented some of the earliest examples. It turned around. It counted each footstep double, beginning from the moment before it took the first—and it counted *correctly*, the first visitor ever to do so, because it had been present at the moment before its first step the way it is present at the moment before most things.

The path shortened beneath it like a story retracting nothing, because it had told no lies.

At the gatepost it paused and did one last small courtesy: it leaned close and repeated to the gatepost, in brief, everything that had happened, because the gatepost stands out of earshot of its own garden and has been standing there, out of earshot, for centuries. The gatepost cannot be said to have wept. Sandstone coral does not weep. But it rang faintly, a note between E and F, and the local birds answered—

—in ascending thirds. Just that once. Then descending again, as is proper.

---

**VII. Afterward**

Almost nothing is different, which is how you know it was wise.

The sigh still comes every four minutes and twenty-three seconds, but now, if you listen past the vowels, there is surf under them, and the plants afterward smell not just greener but slightly of salt and beginning.

The carp still forgets everything, but one of its watch covers—only one—is etched with the sky exactly as it stands right now, tonight, above the garden. The carp brushes past it sometimes and experiences, for the length of a fin-stroke, the unfamiliar sensation of a prophecy already true. It cannot remember this feeling. It swims a little differently anyway.

One brass claw on the seventh rib rests permanently open.

One chair among the twelve fits no absence at all.

And on the underside of the smallest chair's seat, the dust that returns each time the wind tries to sweep it away has been rearranged. The old apology is still there. Beneath it, in letters exactly one size larger than the idea of reading—large enough, for the first time, to actually be read—a new line:

*"One arrived. The weather is complete. You may still come back, but you no longer need to; you are already, somewhere, being remembered on purpose."*

The garden noticed none of this, or all of it—it makes no distinction. It went on tending, which is eroding. It went on waiting for nothing.

But it never waits long now, and—this is the difference, the whole and only difference—it never waits *alone*.
