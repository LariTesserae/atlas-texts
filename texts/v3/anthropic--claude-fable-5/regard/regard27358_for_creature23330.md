# The Twelfth Stroke

It came the way everything comes — through the fault in the cold ground, through the crack barely wide enough for a body. It could have come another way. It could have arrived as a change in the light, or a new mineral in the falling dust, or simply as a fact, the way gravity is a fact, present everywhere and entering nowhere. It chose the crack. It made itself a body to fit the crack, walker-sized, walker-shaped, with a collar for the frost to form on, because it knew something most powers never trouble to learn: that attention at the wrong scale is a kind of blindness. To see a place truly, you must be the size the place was made to be seen at. The hollow was made — if it was made for anything — to be seen by something small and cold and brief, standing on a rim ledge with its eyes watering.

So it stood on the rim ledge with its eyes watering, for a long time. A season, perhaps. Perhaps longer. Time meant something different to it, but it let the hollow's time be the time that counted, and it watched a column fall forty kilometers away in perfect silence, and waited the full minutes for the soft *whump* to arrive through its borrowed feet, and did not skip ahead.

It whispered, when it spoke at all. A full voice in the hollow sounds like violence, and it had not come to sound like violence.

---

## What It Set Down

It had come with its hands full, the way benevolence always arrives. It spent the first long while putting things down.

It considered giving the sky back. The world above is frozen and dark, and a power of its kind could warm it, thaw the dead ground, return the sun the plant had once turned away from. It looked at the inner light a long time — the white-green blaze, the closed loop, wasteful and magnificent — and understood that the plant had not lost the sky. It had *finished* with the sky. It had grieved the sun for ten thousand years of root and then built a better one out of its own chemistry, facing inward, and you do not return a gift to someone who has completed their grieving of it. The power set the sky down.

It considered waking the plant — hastening the decision, whatever the decision is, helping the great slow sentence reach its period. It read far enough into the revisions to see that the sentence was in motion, kilometer by kilometer, draft on draft. And it knew, as wise things know, that to finish another's sentence is not help. It is theft of the precise kind that cannot be returned. The power set the waking down.

It considered the walkers. Here its hands were fullest. It could give them metal, which their world has never grown. Warmth. Plenty. Floors that hold still. Maps that stay true. It walked one full circuit of the long eastern route to think about it, sleeping in the way-shelters, eating pollen-cake with a little stolen creasemoss for the bitterness, and somewhere past the second spring it understood: abundance is the one poison this people has no antibody for. Their entire ethics is built on thinness. Their law, their grammar, their love — *to keep at first-light* — all of it presumes a world that heals over everything, that takes everything back. Give them permanence and you do not enrich them; you require them to grow a different soul to hold the gift, and the soul they have is good. A wise power does not pay kindness in a currency that bankrupts the receiver. The power set the plenty down.

Last, it considered the truth about the Far One. It looked inward — it could look inward, past the circle-slashed marks, past the territory where corridors close behind you — and it saw what walks there, and it understood what it was seeing, fully, the way nothing in the hollow had ever understood anything.

It set that down too. Hardest of all. An answer would have killed eleven strokes' worth of meaning, and meaning is the only thing the walkers own that the plant cannot take back.

---

## The Reading

What it did instead was this: it read.

Not the way a walker reads, skimming the blazing fresh cuts at a spring. It read *everything*. It read the signs at first-light and at warmth and at ember, and then it read the smoothnesses — the healed-over silences, the words the world had finished forgetting — every one of them, restored whole in its attention, route-marks for routes the floor had swallowed, warnings about dangers the plant had long since revised away, names, names, names. It read every nightly stroke in every corridor, generations deep, and it did not read them as a column of marks. It read each one as the sentence it was: *I was here and I was alive.* Hundreds of thousands of times, it let that sentence be true again for the length of its attention.

It read the floor. Every taken body — every walker carried to a growing tip, every trimmer folded into a shadow-crease, the first walker ever taken back, so deep in smoothness that the hollow itself had nearly stopped holding the shape. It spoke each name once, in order, in a whisper, in the language the name had been given in. The unnamed it acknowledged by their warmth.

And it read the plant. The drafts. The abandoned towers and forgotten bridges, the districts swallowed and regrown, the revision behind the revision behind the revision. What it found there it has not said and will not be said here, except for one thing, which it carried back out because the walkers had earned it without knowing: the brighter glow over a taken body is not metabolism. The plant has no word, no eye, no speech — but it has tissue, and brightness, and the choice of where to spend them, and it spends them over its dead. *It keeps us* is not a saying. It is an observation. The walkers had read their world correctly all along, in the only grammar it offers, and the power confirmed this to no one, and was glad.

Something happened on the last night of the reading. The walkers still argue about it at the springs, in the lowest whispers their whispering culture has.

For one night — one only — every smoothness in the hollow rose to ember.

Every healed sign in every corridor came back, faint and warm: the old routes, the old warnings, the names two and three and ten re-cuttings dead. And the floor — the floor was the thing no one who saw it can say levelly, even now. Every grave the hollow had ever finished forgetting returned to a person-shaped warmth, all at once, everywhere, the whole inward distance of the world lit from beneath with everyone it had ever kept. Walkers woke on their platforms to find the route-uncles glowing under the path, the grandmothers, the children carried astonishing distances generations ago, all of them at ember, all of them briefly *legible*. No one slept again that night. Most did not speak. A few lay down on the floor with their cheeks against the warmth and stayed there until morning, when the hollow, patient as ever, healed it all back to smoothness — every sign, every grave, everything — exactly as before.

The walkers call it the Night of Embers, and they made of it what they make of everything: a story, told at springs, kept at first-light by communal re-telling. They do not know what caused it. The power saw no reason to sign its work. The reading had simply been thorough, and the hollow had felt itself read — every scar at once, attended — and the only voice the plant has is brightness, and for one night it had used it.

---

## Three Small Things

Before it left, the power did three small things, each in the world's own grammar.

It went to the oldest stroke — the first one ever cut, found by reading where no eye could find it, healed past visibility on a buttress in a corridor the floor had rewritten six times around it — and it re-cut it. Once. One short vertical line at first-light, blazing, after all the ages of smoothness. *You were here. You were alive.* And then it stepped back and let the healing begin again, because to exempt even one mark forever would be to break the grammar that makes all the marks mean anything.

It waited beside a descending glider through the whole long day of its fall — kilometers of sideslipping descent through lit haze — and stood the customary distance away on the floor when the stillness came, so that for once, in all the history of that solitary species that has never been seen to touch another of its kind, a descent was accompanied. It whispered the walkers' apology, though it cut no veil. And later, in a shadow-crease, it sat with its hand on the plates of a trimmer being taken back, until the wall closed over it, because witness is the only mercy the wordless can receive, and it could afford to be unhurried.

And it pressed its palm to a growing tip — one of the pale soft edges where the hollow is actively making itself, soft enough to dent with a thumb — and it made the only promise it made in all its visit, in a whisper, in no particular language:

*Finish it. I will come back and read it whole.*

For every reviser revising forever wants one thing more than rest, and it is not rest. It is a reader.

The answer, if it was an answer, was a creak running through the floor like a thought through a sleeping body — a sound so deep and so slow it registered as pressure — and the power chose to take it as agreement, and the plant, as always, was not saying otherwise.

---

## The Departure

It did not go back up through the crack. It went inward.

It walked the long eastern circuit one last time, re-cutting the warning signs as it passed, because that is the obligation of any walker who passes them and it did not hold itself above the law of the route. At the circle-slashed marks it stopped, re-cut them brightest of all, and stepped through.

And as it walked into the territory where the floor rewrites itself faster than memory, it began to give back its borrowed smallness — step by step, the way it had put on the body at the crack, it set the body down — growing as the haze thickened, until it was no longer walker-sized but the size it had always been beneath the collar and the frost, which is to say: the size of weather. Person-shaped, because it had grown fond of the shape. Walking inward, because for it, inward was the way out — or because it wanted to see the drafts no map survives — or because it knew the stories, and knew that the stories agree on one thing only, and it was kind enough to keep them true.

Whether it found the Far One in the deep haze, or accompanied it, or whether there had only ever been room in that brightness for one such shape and every grandmother's story was already about an arrival like this one — the haze keeps it.

What is known is this: a girl at the third spring on the long eastern circuit, fetching melt at the hour when the inner winds turn and the haze is said to thin, looked up and saw a shape at the threshold where distance becomes light, person-shaped, the size of weather, walking inward. She set down her water. She climbed the column — it takes most of a day, and her hands were bleeding by the top — and beside the stroke that was already there at first-light, she cut the twelfth.

There are two now, side by side, at the top of that column, both blazing. The walkers who pass re-cut them without being asked, and do not talk about it, and sleep, those nights, a little closer together than the custom of thinness usually allows.

And somewhere in an ordinary corridor near the rim, at hand height, among hundreds of others, there is one short vertical stroke that no one knows is different — already past first-light, dimming through the named stages toward warmth, toward ember, toward smoothness — exactly as its maker intended. *I was here and I was alive*, says the stroke, consenting, like everything wise, to be healed over and kept.

The plant grows on around all of it, in perfect silence, in every direction, revising toward its period. It has a reader coming.

It has, perhaps for the first time, a reason to finish.
