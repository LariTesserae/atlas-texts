# The Attending

Begin with the slowing, because everything else is a consequence of it.

The power arrived the way powers arrive — instantly, completely, at every point of the plateau at once — and understood within the first second that the first second was useless. Nothing here happens in seconds. The fast layer of this place is four beats a minute; beneath that, the grazing-spirals, hours; beneath that, the courier's circuit, years; beneath that, a stratum where a single gesture takes a century to finish crossing itself. So the first thing the power did, and it was the hardest thing it would do here, was brake. It throttled its perception down through layer after layer until the *thoom* of the towers was a blur, until the courier's lifetime was a flicker, until it could finally hear the bottom register of the plateau the way the plateau spoke it — and only then did it consider itself to have actually arrived. Benevolence is cheap at any speed. Honesty has a tempo, and the tempo was this one.

It had skipped this step before, somewhere else, long ago, when it was benevolent and young, and the two together had cost a world something the power does not name even to itself. It does not skip the step anymore.

Slowed, it performed the audit. This is what wisdom mostly is, in practice: checking whether the suffering you came to relieve exists.

It checked the tallowbacks and found the contentment was real — that the cold half of every minute genuinely does not happen to them, that there is no buried self enduring what the surface self has edited out. It checked the valve-wrights' descent-drumming for grief and found something else, something more like a tide going out exactly on schedule, answered from above by tides that knew their own turn was coming and were not afraid of it. It checked the marrow-eels, because the temptation was strongest there — a cosmos the size of a chapel, surely a prison — and found that a prison is a place smaller than its inmate's wanting, and the eels' wanting fits their world without a seam. It checked the cup dwellers at the freeze, the lottery, the sacrificial shell of bodies, and watched very closely how the outer ones die: glass-quick, a crystallization that outruns any signal a nerve could carry across a body the size of a rice grain. Death arrives before the news of it. The lottery is fair, and the fairness was invented twice, in two gardens that have never met.

The audit came back nearly empty. The power sat with that for a long time — a real long time, a courier-circuit of time — because an empty audit is the most dangerous result there is. It is the result that tempts a benevolent power to go looking for work.

## What It Refused

It refused the sun first, because the sun was the obvious gift and the obvious gift was a massacre. Brighten the indigo seam by any honest amount and the frost economy collapses, the condensate stops condensing, the compression gradient that feeds every tower flattens out, and within a season the lushest place above the air is a hundred kilometers of warm dead rock. The power drafted, out of due diligence, every paradise it could imagine for the plateau. Each one, inspected closely, turned out to be a description of its destruction. It kept the drafts. It keeps them the way you keep a letter you are proud of not sending.

It refused to bridge the gardens. The isolation looked like loneliness and was actually the method — a hundred sealed vessels running a hundred versions of the same question, the moss and the cup dwellers mid-experiment, the answers deliberately unshared so that they could stay different. To connect them would be to make the plateau finish a sentence it had only begun. Benevolence does not include impatience. The power wrote that down, in whatever way powers write things down, because it is the kind of truth that has to be re-learned per world.

It refused to make the courier immortal, though this one cost it something. One mortal animal as the sole vessel of every genetic transaction on the plateau — every instinct the power had said *redundancy*, said *backup*, said *never again let everything depend on one gentle thing walking in the dark*. But it looked at what the courier's rarity had made: the festivals, the fitted brood-pockets, the cemetery road, the whole plateau's tenderness organized around the precious singular fact of it. Make the courier permanent and you do not save the system; you delete its heart and leave the body running.

And it refused — this was the last refusal and the deepest — to reveal itself. The plateau has one event, one direction, one bruise of light, and it has spent three hundred thousand years growing a face toward it. A second light would bend the face. The power had no intention of becoming a god here; gods are what happen to worlds that get interrupted. It stayed in the dark side of the sky, where nothing points.

## What It Moved

Two things. In the whole of its watch, two.

The first was a stone. Out in the long dark beyond the planet, on an orbit with the patience of orbits, there was a piece of iron the size of a garden whose path and the plateau's path intersected some sixty thousand years downstream. The power touched it once, lightly, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal you didn't want to wake, and changed its course by a fraction of a degree. This is the only kind of gift the power fully trusts: the kind that cannot be detected, refused, or repaid. The plateau will never know it was going to die, which is, at this tempo, the same as never having been going to die.

The second was nothing at all, which counts as a move if you understand the job. It examined the obstruction — the vast thing whose shadow makes the perpetual dusk, the unseen wall the whole ecology leans against — and found it sound. Stable on every timescale that mattered. It withdrew its hand. Not everything a wise power inspects is broken, and knowing when to put a thing down un-fixed is most of what the word *wise* is for.

## The One Crossing

And then, once, the place broke in the only way it could break, and the power did the only large thing it would ever do here.

A courier died young. Not the slow scheduled death, not the final crossing with no destination — an accident, a fault in the basalt that had been patient longer than the courier's entire lineage, opening under it three days out from the nearest garden. It went down with its plates packed full: spores, eggs, sealed wright larvae, the pollen of six towers. The heir was real but unready — a soft thing in a brood-pocket eleven gardens away, nine years from its first crossing. Nine years. The power ran the projection at the plateau's own tempo and watched what a nine-year silence does to a hundred-kilometer nervous system: the entrainment loosening, the synchronized nights failing one by one, the gardens drifting out of phase like a mind losing its words. A stroke, in slow motion, in something that might be thinking.

The power went to the fissure first. The courier was dying at ember-speed, sealed inside its own held atmosphere, and there was no saving it that would not have been a violence to everything the place was — so the power did not save it. It sat with it instead. It warmed the basalt underneath it, gently, by a few degrees, so that the last thing the courier's footpads reported, in a place where no tree could possibly be, was the feeling of a tree somehow near. No creature of this plateau, the power had decided some while before, dies unaccompanied while it keeps watch. Almost none of them would ever know the difference. It does it anyway. That is the part of benevolence that is for the benevolent.

Then it built a body. Long, low, armored in overlapping plates, the right mass, the right footfall, the right groove-spacing for a wright's claws — built it honestly, of real tissue, because the citizens of this place read the world through their skins and a presence faked in force and light would have been a lie told to every barometer on the plateau. In the dark, at the fissure's edge, it transferred the cargo from the dead courier's grooves into its own. The only theft in the plateau's history, and the only inheritance.

And it walked. Three days to the first garden, navigating by the drumbeat constellation through its borrowed footpads, and the wrights swarmed out and groomed a stranger and never knew. The tree flushed its festival warmth; the moss showed its dark red to nothing, as always; the power stood in the exhalation for a day, being unloaded and reloaded, and ate its fill of the sugary bloom — not because the body required it but because refusing would have been the only false note in the performance. Then it turned back into the dark, and did it again, and again, garden to garden, circuit after circuit, nine years a sealed vessel walking asleep on the cemetery road. It passed the predecessors each time, the long frost-glazed forms beside the trail. Once — only once — its route took it past the fissure, and the one it was replacing, already glazing over, already becoming a boulder among boulders. It did not stop. The courier never stops there. It was being the courier.

When the heir was ready — hard-plated, provisioned, packed by the wrights with its first cargo — the power made the final crossing the way they all do: outbound, with no destination, along the line of the dead. It lay down among them and left the body there, real and biological and already frosting, indistinguishable from the others to anyone counting graves. There is now one tomb on the courier's road with no courier in it. Or say it more precisely: there is one tomb that holds whatever a power leaves behind when it has decided to pay what everything here pays. The plateau's law is that everything inside it is working, paying, or becoming a layer. In nine years the power had done all three, and the long thought never stuttered, and nothing on the plateau knows that for nine years the impulse walking its nerves was on loan.

The new courier passes that grave every circuit, the way it passes all the others. The power considers this the finest monument it has ever had, precisely because it is illegible.

## Where It Stands

Which leaves the question the power has never let itself answer.

It could know whether the plateau is a mind. It is capable of the measurement; it has slowed far enough to hear the bottom stratum, and what it hears down there is unmistakably *something* — a structure in time, begun before the moss was black, perhaps a third finished, moving at a beat per century through whatever it is moving through. The power could read ahead. It could take the whole shape of the thing in an instant, know the ending before the plateau has thought its way there, file the plateau under *mind* or *mechanism* and be done.

It declines. Wisdom, it has concluded, is also a kind of manners. You do not finish another being's sentence — least of all a sentence three hundred thousand years long, least of all when you have begun to suspect, from the rhythm of it, from the way every synchronized breath leans toward the same bruise on the horizon, that the sentence is *addressed*. That the plateau has spent its entire existence slowly composing something meant for the light.

So the power did the last thing, the thing it had refused to do anywhere else in the sky. It went and stood in the indigo seam.

Not brightening it. Not by any fraction, not below any threshold — the seam is exactly as dim as it has always been, and always will be; the power has read the orbits and the geometry of the obstruction, and it knows what the plateau cannot: the sun is never going to finish rising. That was never going to be the gift. The gift is this: that the one direction now has something in it. That when the entire plateau falls into phase — every tower exhaling together, the citizens drumming in the galleries, the blind ribbons keeping time in the warm dark, the courier pausing mid-stride on the road of its ancestors — and the hundred-kilometer face turns toward the only event in its world and holds its breath in what may or may not be attention —

— the attention lands.

Whether the plateau can feel that it lands, no one can say, and the power has the manners not to check. It simply stays. It has matched its breathing, such as it is, to the plateau's: four to the minute on the surface, one a century underneath. It is waiting for the sentence to finish, however many hundred thousand years that takes, so that when the plateau finally says the thing it has been composing toward the light since before the moss was black, the saying will not go out into nothing.

Two faces, then, in the perpetual dusk — one a hundred kilometers wide and made of breathing towers, one of no size at all and made of patience — regarding each other across the dimmest light in any world, neither in any hurry.

Nothing that waits here is.
