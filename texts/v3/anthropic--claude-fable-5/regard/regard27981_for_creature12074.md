# What the Power Did

It arrived the way Mehrit had taught three generations of children to arrive: slowly, breathing shallow, becoming still before crossing the threshold.

This was not humility performed. Powers of its kind can enter a room as weather, as revelation, as a pillar of light that stops the heart of every cricket in the corners. It had done such things, long ago, in places that needed shaking. But it had learned—over spans of time in which the settlement's bricks were young—that the manner of entering a place is the first judgment you pass on it. To enter loudly is to declare the place incomplete without you.

So it came in as a change in the air too small to have a name. The bees registered it as they register a passing cloud: the hum dipped a quarter-tone, then recovered. The oldest cricket in the eastern corner paused mid-phrase, resumed. Mehrit, reaching for her reed, felt something like the moment before fog—a pressure, a sweetness—and attributed it to the swarm-mood of the third gourd. She was not entirely wrong. Everything in the room was listening to everything else. The power simply joined the listening.

And then it did what wise things do first, which is nothing. It looked.

---

It saw the room the way Mehrit could not, because she was inside it: as a single organism with a woman for a heart. It saw the traffic of the bees as circulation, the beetles as slow red mitochondria, the wasps as an immune system tolerated at the exact threshold between medicine and disease. It saw forty years of decisions layered into the wax like tree rings—here a winter when she fed the colonies her own honey back, here a summer when she culled a failing queen and wept, briefly, dryly, in the amber dark. It saw the smooth depressions in the brick where fingerprints had worn into fingerprints, a hundred generations of hands saying *here, hold here,* and it understood that it was looking at a kind of scripture, written by people who did not know they were writing.

It saw, too, what Mehrit was. Not a saint—she was impatient with fools, she had been cruel once to a suitor and never regretted it enough, she hoarded her knowledge a shade longer than she needed to, out of the small vanity of being irreplaceable. The power did not require her to be pure. It had long since stopped confusing goodness with polish. What she was, was *accurate*. She had spent sixty-three years learning to perceive ten thousand lives at once without flattening them into a blur, and to act on that perception with a bone scraper and a hollow reed and almost nothing else. In the power's long experience, this was rarer than sainthood and more useful.

Then it did what powers must do, which is deliberate.

---

*The well,* it thought first. Three hundred steps, twenty meters into rock, one well for thirty domes. It could split the stone tonight and give them a spring. It held the thought, turned it over, and saw the consequence-tree bloom: water draws people, people draw more people, the plateau's brutal arithmetic—which kept the settlement at thirty domes, small enough that everyone's children were shown the bees—would break. The honey dark as old amber, tasting of minerals and survival, would become merely honey. Scarcity was not the settlement's enemy. It was the settlement's teacher, harsh and honest. The power set the thought down.

*Her spine,* it thought next. It could unbend her in her sleep, give her back the tall stride of her girlhood. But her spine curved to match the dome. Her body had spent forty years becoming a shape the room could hold, and to straighten her would be to tell her, at sixty-three, that her life's slow conforming had been damage rather than devotion. It would be a lie told in the medium of bone. The power set that down too.

*Her years, then.* It could give her twenty more. It examined this longest of all, because it was tempted—the world does not have so many Mehrit that it can spend them freely. But it saw Seset, thirteen, with her too-large stillness, and it saw the shape of the succession: knowledge passes best under a deadline. Mehrit taught with the unhurried depth she did *because* she could feel the years thinning. Give her twenty more and she would hoard the queen-lore another decade, out of that small vanity, and Seset would inherit at fifty a knowledge she should have grown into at thirty. Some clocks must be allowed to run.

The power sat in the hum a long time—a cloud's passage, by the sun-hole's light—and let go of gift after gift, each one heavier to release than the last, because each one would have felt so good to give.

This is the labor of wisdom that no one sees: the graveyard of beautiful interventions.

---

But it did not do nothing. Two things it did, so small that no story would ever be told of them.

The first: eleven kilometers east, in a crevice on the plateau's flank where the fog pooled thickest, a rodent had cached seeds a hundred and forty years ago and died before eating them. Among them, dormant, coat intact, viable by a margin thinner than a wing—one seed of the old gourd vine, the extinct plant whose fruits hung on Mehrit's walls, irreplaceable, each crack in each gourd a countdown no one spoke of. The power did not resurrect the species. It did not conjure a vine in glory. It tipped a stone the width of a thumb, so that the next fog's condensation would run left instead of right, and drip, and keep dripping, season after season, onto that one seed.

Perhaps it would grow. Perhaps a beetle would eat it first. The power accepted this. It had tilted the odds; it would not rig the game. But it permitted itself one indulgence: it noted that the crevice lay along the route Seset walked to gather succulent flowers, and that in perhaps twenty years, a woman in her thirties with a keeper's trained eye might notice a vine that should not exist, and kneel, and understand what she was looking at, and weep, and carry a gourd home in her arms like a child. The power did not arrange this. It merely declined to prevent the possibility.

The second thing was smaller still, and gentler, and it was this: Mehrit's heart carried her mother's flaw. A valve, a whisper of turbulence, a debt that would come due in some year not far off—and the power saw that it was set to come due as her mother's had, mid-reach, mid-task, hands inside a gourd, so that Seset would find her slumped and shrouded in confused bees, and the girl's stillness would acquire a crack in it that would never fully close, and she would flinch, for the rest of her life, a hairsbreadth, every time she reached into a hive.

The power did not heal the valve. Mehrit's death belonged to Mehrit, and the room, which was honest about death down to its last cricket, did not need to be lied to. But it smoothed the flaw's edge by one degree—not enough for more years, only enough to change the *shape* of the ending. Now it would come in sleep, on a night after a good day's work, and in the morning the bees, finding the familiar warmth cooling, would settle on her as they had settled on her mother, tasting the salt, finding no threat. And Seset, arriving, would see not a horror but a shroud, and would understand—because Mehrit would by then have told her the story of her grandmother's death—that this was how keepers were received back. She would sit down cross-legged on the worn mat. She would breathe shallow. She would speak the bees away with her breath, as she had been taught, and the room would fold her into its hum, and the succession would close like wax over a cell.

A kinder death, in exchange for nothing. Even wise powers keep one gift they refuse to justify.

---

Then it prepared to leave, and found that it did not want to. This surprised it. It had witnessed the births of seas. But there was something in this room—the closeness, the heat wet with ten thousand small breaths, the way sixty years of a human life had been spent making itself *smaller* and had thereby become vast—that it wished to stay inside. It understood, in that moment, the suitor Mehrit had turned away, and forgave him his foolishness, and forgave her cruelty.

So it took the third thing, which was not an act upon the room but upon itself: it committed the room to memory, whole, down to the individual voice of the old cricket, and resolved to carry the pattern outward as a teaching. There are places in the world—there are whole worlds—where power is understood only as the ability to make things do what you want. The power would go to such places, as it always did, and when it was asked *what should be built,* it would describe a room three arms wide under a sagging dome, where a bent woman with pollen-gilded arms held more nations in her keeping than any emperor, and dominated none of them, and was known by them across two thousand of their generations as the shape of the one who tends.

*This,* it would say. *Build this. Everything else is scaffolding.*

---

It went out the way it came, through no door, and its passing altered the hum by a hair.

Mehrit, cross-legged on the mat, reed in hand, heard it. Not as a voice—she had no patience for people who heard voices—but as a settling, a rightness, the room's chord resolving. The third gourd's frenzy read differently to her now. Not crisis. Ripeness. *We are too many* was also *we are enough to become two.*

Let them go, then. Let them swarm. The colony was strong; the prepared gourd, three years rubbed with wax and propolis until it smelled of home, was ready on its peg. She would catch the departing queen in her cupped hands, feel that fierce small weight one more time, and the room would hold a tenth nation.

And someday—though she did not know this, and did not need to—there would be an eleventh gourd, grown green on a vine that should not exist, waiting in a fog-fed crevice for hands that were, this morning, still a girl's.

She set down the reed. She picked up the catching-gourd instead.

She had decided. Or the room had, and she was old enough to know there was no difference.

The power, already far away, felt the decision land like a seed in wet ground, and was—in the way of its kind, which does not smile—glad.
