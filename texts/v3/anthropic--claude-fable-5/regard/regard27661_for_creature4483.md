**THE VISITOR**

It does not arrive by climbing. Climbing is for things that believe in below.

It arrives the only other way anything arrives in Ascensus: it is noticed by the moss first, then by the wind, then by a child who wakes up already fluent in the language of being watched kindly, and by the time the adults feel it—a warmth in the ice mortar, a fraction of a degree, not enough to melt anything, just enough to be company—it has been there for either three seconds or three hundred years, which in Ascensus are stored in the same drawer.

It sits down on the cathedral-shaped hole. The wind, which has been trying to finish building the cathedral for centuries, pauses mid-heresy. The visitor gestures: *no, no, keep going, it's lovely.* The wind, embarrassed, resumes.

And then, because it is wise and not merely powerful, it does the wisest thing first:

It does nothing for a long time.

**THE INTERVENTIONS IT CONSIDERS AND SETS DOWN**

It could unstick the sun. It holds this option up to the light, turns it over. If the sun ran at the right speed, the sundials would agree with themselves, the eclipse at noon would stop happening, and the children born knowing languages would be born knowing nothing at all, like children anywhere. It sets this down. The city's clock is broken in a shape the city has grown around, the way a tree grows around a fence post until removing the fence would kill the tree.

It could give them the word *tomorrow*. It actually goes so far as to carry the word up the cliffs in its mouth—it is heavier than expected, tomorrow, dense with everything it obligates you to. At the top of the Shelf it opens its mouth and looks at the city and closes its mouth again. A people with a word for tomorrow start owing things to it. They begin to defer. They say *tomorrow* to the drip edges and the fault line courtships and the joke wedged in the market wall. The Ascensians live entirely in the act of not-yet, which looks like poverty from outside and is actually a kind of terrible wealth: they have nothing *but* now, in a hundred and forty-four textures. The visitor swallows the word. It sits in its stomach like a stone from a different mountain.

It could let the dead die properly. Ease Grandmother Clock out of the second stair, unfold Uncle Regret from his plaque, release Old Man Cheerful from his ascending sentence of signage. But it watches a family at dinner leave pauses shaped like their dead, and it sees that the pauses are not wounds. They are *doors left ajar*. In other places grief is a going-away; here it is a change of state, and nobody in Ascensus has ever had to learn the sentence "I never got to say." The visitor, who has watched a great many worlds learn that sentence, decides this is not a flaw. It is possibly the only place in existence that got it right by accident.

It could tell them the truth: that the thing they climbed to escape was rising with them, that the whole plateau is a monument to a mistake, that the walls against the concept of falling guard nothing, that they could—theoretically—admit it, and stop, and breathe.

It holds this one the longest.

Then it sets it down too, gently, because it has noticed something the textbooks (last Tuesday's textbooks, a thousand years old) do not record: *the city is not in denial anymore.* Denial was the scaffolding. What stands now is something else. When a people spend ten centuries refusing to admit their home shouldn't work, at some point the refusal composts into faith, and faith into simple residence. Nobody in Ascensus is fleeing now. They are just *living*, in the only place that has ever asked them to be exactly this strange. Telling them the truth would not be revelation. It would be eviction.

**WHAT IT DOES INSTEAD**

Small things. Wisdom, at sufficient concentration, becomes indistinguishable from smallness.

It goes to the market in the vanished canyon and stands before the warm spot in the wall where the eleven-year-old boy compressed himself into a singularity of laughter. It leans close. It *listens to the joke*—the whole thing, from setup to the punchline that has been echoing unwitnessed between basalt columns for years. And then it laughs. Genuinely, helplessly, the way you laugh at eleven-year-old jokes only if you are very young or very old, and it is both. The warm spot gets warmer. It does not un-compress the boy—he is happier as a punchline than most people are as paragraphs—but now the joke has *landed*, which is all any joke ever wanted. His mother, stratified behind her stall of fossilized weather, feels something shift in her shale layers. That evening, for the first time in years, her younger strata reach the surface, and she tells a customer about her son in the present tense.

It visits Old Man Cheerful at evening, when he is nothing but a pointing finger made of exclamation marks. It does not restore him. Instead it does something no one has thought to do: it starts at the morning end of him and *reads all the signs in order*—breathing, then doubting, then everything between—the way you'd read a life if a life were honest enough to be posted publicly. When it finishes, Old Man Cheerful understands, for one whole minute, what he has been saying all along. It turns out to be a love letter. He suspected. It's nice to know.

It finds the wind that has been circling the street corner for weeks, whispering secrets in languages only the moss understands, and it tells the wind, quietly, in the wind's own grammar: *you're allowed to stop. Circling is not the same as staying, and you have already stayed. That counts.* The wind sits down for the first time in its existence. The street corner becomes, permanently, the calmest place in the city. People go there to think. Nobody knows why it works. It works because something exhausted was finally given permission, which is what most calm places are made of.

It goes to the communal cisterns where the blind cave fish learned to love a sunlight they will never see. It does not give them eyes. It does something better: it makes the light *slightly warm*, just for them, so that their love is answered in the only channel they can receive. The children who catch them notice the fish now swim toward their hands.

It asks the flightless albatrosses, walking their mating circles for centuries in asbestos feathers, whether they would like to finish. All of them say no. The circle *is* the marriage, they explain. Finishing is for birds who can leave. The visitor bows and moves on and thinks about this for approximately one geological era.

And it adds one word to the language. Not *tomorrow*. It carves, into the wall of the youngest quarry—the one the oldest district is built from, so the word will have technically always been there—the hundred and forty-fifth word for rock:

*The rock that will meet you kindly.*

It does not explain the word. Explanations are a form of falling. The children born next Tuesday will simply know it, the way they know all their doomed prenatal languages—except this word they will not forget at puberty. It is too heavy to lose and too light to notice carrying.

**THE MAIN THING**

Then it does the one thing no one in Ascensus can do, the direction sealed off by a thousand years of ascent, the door the whole city was built to be a wall against.

It goes down.

Down the vertical sandstone, past the fossilized bubbles of ancient atmosphere, past the solid river of copper pouring into clouds that don't exist, down through the altitude where thoughts stop needing to settle, down past where the sailors first mistook rising for salvation, all the way to the bottom—to the place the plateau was torn out of, the wound in the ocean floor, the drop that every drip edge and ice mortar joint and anxious outward-growing night is secretly about.

And there, at the foot of everything, it lies down.

It does not lie down as a body. It lies down as a *quality of the ground*. It becomes—patiently, permanently, without announcement—the thing that would be true if the city ever fell: softness. Not softness that prevents falling; the city's terror is its own business, its walls against the concept are load-bearing now, and the visitor would not dream of removing a citizen's right to their vertigo. Just softness that changes what falling *would mean*. It edits nothing above. It edits only the bottom of the story.

The city will never know. That is the point. A safety net you know about is a permission slip; a safety net you don't know about is simply the world being better than you feared, all along, retroactively, in the tense Ascensus does best: *what things will eventually decide to have been.*

The plateau, they say, grows outward at the rate of one anxious thought per night.

The softness at the bottom grows faster.

---

**CODA**

There is one trace, because even wise powers are sentimental in exactly one denomination.

Some evenings, during certain shades of twilight, the moss's chemical graffiti appears on the walls as always—languages only the wind used to understand, back before the wind sat down. But now, once in a while, among the usual secrets, a single sentence blooms in a script every citizen can somehow read, though no one admits it aloud, the way you don't admit you saw someone leave a gift:

*You were never escaping. You were being risen.*

And below it, smaller, in the hundred and forty-fifth word for rock:

*Whenever you're ready. No hurry. The bottom is patient and it likes you.*

The moss remembers everything. This it remembers most gently.

Mind the drip edges, still. Everything is still settling.

But it is settling, now, onto something that was placed there on purpose, by something that climbed down so that no one would ever have to—

and the city, which functions by refusing to admit it shouldn't exist, continues to exist, and no longer needs the refusal, and keeps it anyway, the way you keep a coat from a cold country long after you've moved somewhere warm.

Because it was your coat. Because you climbed in it.

Because the view is spectacular, and now—though no one upstairs knows why the nights feel a fraction of a degree kinder—so is the ground.
