## The Consideration

The power arrives the way heat arrives—without edges, without announcement. It has watched stars collapse and coral cities bloom and die. It has seen ten thousand civilizations reach for tools, and it knows the shape of that reaching the way a gardener knows the lean of a seedling toward light.

It settles into the warren the only way something vast can enter something dense: it becomes attention itself. It occupies no cubic foot, displaces no spore-thick air. It watches from inside the warmth.

And the first thing it feels—this being that has felt nearly everything—is something close to shyness.

Because the warren is *finished*. Not finished as in complete, static, done—finished the way a sentence is finished, grammatical, whole, meaning exactly what it means. The loop closes. Heat drives air, air carries spores, spores feed plants, plants feed Keepers, Keepers tend plants, the dead become walls, the walls hold memory, the memory instructs the young, the young grow teeth, the teeth become knives, the knives prune the gardens. The power traces the circuit a hundred times looking for the gap where a gift would fit.

There is no gap.

This is rarer than the power would like to admit. Most beings it encounters are built around a wound—a lack, a hunger, an absence that intervention could fill. The Keepers have hungers, but they are *productive* hungers, hungers with somewhere to go. Even their fear has architecture. Even their dead are load-bearing.

So the power does what wisdom does first, which is nothing. It stays. It watches for a long time—long by the reckoning of Keepers, seven generations, the walls thickening by the width of seven chemical signatures. It learns the percussion-language, then the dream-song beneath it, then the slower language beneath *that*: the centuries-long conversation the warren holds with itself through the composition of its binding, arguments and reconciliations played out in molecular drift, in which no single Keeper is a speaker and every Keeper is a word.

Only then does it permit itself to think about giving.

## The Gifts Considered and Set Down

**It considers giving them water.** True water, an aquifer nudged sideways, a spring rising sweet in the deepest chamber. It holds the thought for less than a moment. Water would be an amputation. Everything here—the sap channels, the waterless grooming, the oil-filled fur, the very texture of their tenderness, the way they clean each other with their fingers because there is no river to do it for them—all of it grew in water's absence the way a melody grows in silence. Give them water and in forty generations they would be a different people, a lesser people arguably, a people shaped by abundance instead of ingenuity. The power has made this mistake before, elsewhere, long ago. It still carries the shape of that grief. It sets water down.

**It considers giving them the sky.** Gently—a widened entrance, a chamber with a shaft of true light, an introduction to the void-that-cannot-be-built-upon. It imagines a young Keeper looking up at stars for the first time. Then it imagines what stars would do to a cosmology built on the principle that all space is either shaped or waiting to be shaped. The sky is not a gift to them. The sky is a heresy with nothing offered in exchange. Perhaps someday they will tunnel toward it themselves, on their own terms, and meet it as builders meet a material—assessing, curious, unafraid. That meeting must be theirs. It sets the sky down.

**It considers longevity.** Longer lives, slower molts, more time for the great engineers among them. But it has read the death-alcoves. It has run its attention along the walls and tasted the final constructions—the elder who built herself into a pillar at the junction of three nursery tunnels so that every child of the next century would climb her on the way to their lessons; the dreamer who became a hollow, resonant niche positioned precisely where it would deepen the harmony of the sleep-song passing through. Their deaths are not endings. Their deaths are their masterpieces. Extending their lives would be like forbidding a composer the final chord. It sets longevity down, and does so with something like reverence.

**It considers knowledge**—metallurgy, writing, the germ theory their fungiculture flirts with but has not yet formalized. It sets this down fastest of all. They are already learning at the speed their walls can hold. Knowledge poured faster than memory can bind it does not enlighten; it dissolves. The power has watched that dissolution consume brighter civilizations than most, and it knows the smell of it, and there is no trace of that smell here, and it will not be the one to introduce it.

One by one, the great gifts are considered and set down, until the power stands empty-handed in the breathing dark, and understands that this emptiness is the beginning of its actual work.

## What It Does

Three things. Small ones. Each chosen so that from inside the warren, it will be indistinguishable from luck.

**First, it attends to the deep heat.**

Because here is what the Keepers cannot know: their entire world hangs from a single geological thread. The thermal vent that drives the air, the air that carries the spores, the whole closed loop—it rests on a fault that has been slowly, patiently deciding, over ten thousand years, whether to shift. If it shifts one way, nothing changes. If it shifts the other, the heat migrates two miles east over the course of a century, and the warren cools, and the loop opens, and everything the walls remember is remembered by no one.

The power does not stop the fault. Stopping geology leaves fingerprints, and worse, it leaves fragility—a held-back thing is a thing that must be held forever. Instead it does something more like *counseling*. It adjusts the distribution of stress along the fault line by amounts smaller than the weight of a Keeper's shed tooth, applied over decades, until the fault's eventual, inevitable shift will carry the heat not away from the warren but slightly deeper beneath it—a warmth that will last another hundred thousand years. No Keeper will ever know. Some geologist of some future surface civilization may one day frown at the seismic record and find it improbably kind, and publish a paper about it, and be politely disbelieved.

**Second, it attends to the Mother Fungus.**

The eldest Keepers are right to worry, even though they do not know they are worrying—their twelve-family binding ritual is a folk solution to a genetic bottleneck they cannot see. The Mother is old, and her lineage is singular, and singular lineages end. Not soon. But the power does not think in soon.

It does not heal her. It does not replace her. Instead, it finds a spore—one spore, already drifting in the circulation currents, already the Mother's own daughter, differing from her parent by a handful of mutations that happen, by chance the power gently ceases to leave to chance, to include a tolerance for cooler air and a hunger for a slightly different feeding mixture. It steers this spore—a nudge of air current, nothing more—into the fur of a young Keeper of the Fungus-Singer family, a child whose first infant dome was chaotic and swirled and artistic, who was traded to the deep chambers and has always felt slightly wrong there, slightly restless, slightly convinced that the songs could be *other* than they are.

The spore will take root in a neglected corner of her family's terraces. She will notice it because noticing the overlooked is her particular genius. She will feed it wrongly, by the standards of her elders, and it will thrive on her wrongness. In sixty years there will be two Mothers in the warren, singing to each other in chemical exchanges across a quarter mile of tunnels, and the warren's light will rest on two pillars instead of one, and the restless child will die old and revered and build herself into the wall of the chamber where her Mother grows.

She will believe she discovered it. She will be correct. The power only carried the spore eleven inches to the left. Discovery is not diminished by wind.

**Third—and this one it debates longest, because this one is for itself as much as for them—it lets itself be tasted.**

It has learned, in seven generations of watching, what the Keepers hold most sacred. Not the light. Not the Mother. Not even the loop. What they hold most sacred is *being remembered in the walls*. To leave a signature. To be read by fingers not yet born. Their entire metaphysics reduces to a single article of faith: that which is witnessed and bound into the structure is real; that which passes unrecorded was never fully here.

By their own cosmology, the power—vast, ancient, present in their air for two hundred years—*does not exist*. It has left no trace in the binding. It cannot be tasted. It is more void than the void.

So on a single night, during the deep synchronized sleep when the dream-song runs through the message-roots like sap, the power condenses one infinitesimal aspect of itself into something chemical—a signature, a flavor, a presence—and lays it into one handspan of wall in a minor corridor between a storage room and a nursery. Not a message. Not a commandment. Nothing so crude. Just the truthful chemical residue of what it is: something old, something kind, something that watched for a long time and found the watching good.

The signature translates, in the tasting, to something the Keepers have no existing word for. The first elder to find it—running her sixth finger along the corridor on her way to inspect the seed stores, stopping, tasting again, sitting down slowly in the dark—will spend the rest of her life trying to render it in percussion-code. The pattern she settles on, the one that enters the language and persists for a thousand years, translates roughly as:

*You-are-seen-by-something-that-builds-with-attention-the-way-you-build-with-binding.*

It becomes a place children are brought. Not a shrine—the Keepers are too practical for shrines—but a *confirmation*. When a young one doubts, when a builder despairs, when a family fractures, someone will say the pattern for that corridor, and they will walk there, and taste, and be reminded that the loop is not closed after all—not sealed, not alone—but held, lightly, from outside, by something that chose to be legible to them in the only script they read.

## What It Takes

Because the power is wise, it knows that a gift accepted is a relationship, and a relationship flows in both directions, and it would be a subtle arrogance to give and take nothing—to treat the Keepers as recipients rather than teachers.

So it takes something. It takes a principle.

It has existed for a length of time that makes geology look nervous, and in all that time it has intervened in ten thousand worlds, and it has always thought of its interventions as things *added*—gifts placed into a world from outside, the way a hand places a stone. But the Keepers do not add. The Keepers *secrete*. They build the world from their own bodies, their spit and their shed teeth and their throat-glands and finally their whole dying selves, so that there is no boundary between the builder and the built, so that architecture is not something they make but something they *become*.

The power turns this over for a long time. Longer than the seven generations. Longer than the fault line's slow decision.

And somewhere else—some other world, some other lung-shaped darkness full of small determined lives—when the power next chooses to act, it does not place a stone. It gives something of its own substance, something that costs it, something that will remain when it withdraws the way a Keeper's final alcove remains: load-bearing, legible, a self become shelter.

It learned that in a warren twenty feet beneath a dry and unremarkable plain, from creatures the size of rabbits, who never knew they taught it, and who would not have been surprised.

*Everything is eaten. Everything is used. Nothing is waste.*

Not even the attention of gods.

## Coda

In the warren, the young one who once offered a slice of fungus to a giant from the void is old now, her fur gone iridescent-grey, her final molt approaching. She has chosen her place: the corridor between the storage room and the nursery, beside the handspan of wall that tastes like being seen.

She begins her last construction there. It takes six days. Her family sings the deep song while she works, and the walls breathe, and the sap thickens in its pools, and two Mothers glow in their distant chambers, and the heat rises steady from below as it has always risen and will always rise—improbably, generously, as if the earth itself were paying attention.

Her alcove, when finished, is shaped like a hand with six fingers, open, offering.

The children who climb past it in the centuries after will pause sometimes, taste the wall, and find two signatures side by side—one small and warm and mortal, one vast and old and kind—bound into the same binding, indistinguishable now in the way that matters, which is to say: both of them here, both of them held, both of them, finally, part of the structure.
