**What the Power Does**

It notices Shearbelow the way you notice you've been humming something for an hour — not by hearing it, but by stopping, and feeling the silence arrive with a shape already worn into it. A droplet somewhere in the city whispered a name, and the name traveled outward along the smell of falling, and the power tasted copper and distant thunder and thought: *ah. There you are. I misplaced you too.*

Even powers misplace names. That is the first thing that qualifies it to come here.

It does not arrive with light. It arrives at the scale of weather, and then, thinking better of it, at the scale of a droplet — because it has read the immigration policies, and it understands that anything larger than an apology would be turned away at the border, or worse, would be *believed in*, which in Shearbelow is a kind of violence.

---

**First, the things it does not do.**

It could pour gravity back the right way, so that feet and mouths agreed at last about direction. It does not. The taste of falling is how the city says grace before every meal, and a city that has learned to stand still while tasting descent has learned something the power itself took eons to learn. It will not unteach it.

It could dry the water. Turn the regretful apologies back into mere fluid, rinse the whispering out of the windowsills. It does not. Someone has to hold the misplaced names. The water volunteered. That was brave of the water, and bravery should not be tidied up.

It could finish the shy composer's manuscript — write the *something else* that this whole geography was only ever a prelude to, resolve the minor key, let scale finally settle on a size and keep it. It does not. It has seen what happens to preludes that get completed: they stop being doorways and become walls. Shearbelow is a *before* that has made a life out of being before. There is a dignity in that which resolution would only insult.

It could, most tempting of all, introduce the two mountains. Sit them down. Mediate the conversation they never quite had. It kneels between them for a long time, one hand flat against limestone's compelling points, one against shale's rebuttals, and it feels how much they want to speak — and then it feels, underneath the wanting, the truth: *the argument is load-bearing.* Every wall in Shearbelow is what two disputing stones agreed to disagree upon. Resolve the dispute and the negative space closes like a healed wound closing over everyone still inside it. So instead of mediating, the power does something stranger. It thanks them. Both of them. Separately, so neither has to be gracious in front of the other. It says: *your unfinished quarrel is holding up a cathedral, a bakery that forgets itself, a library with a staircase who used to be a singer. You have been arguing so generously for so long.* The mountains do not respond, because mountains never do, but that night the fault-line streets run a half-degree warmer, and the fog herds graze closer to the center of town than they have in a century.

---

**Then, the small things it does.**

It sits in one of the gargoyle-shaped holes through a single night — folds itself into the wet-edged absence and simply occupies it, the way a hand occupies a glove. Not to replace the gargoyle. Only so the hole can spend one night knowing it is shaped like something worth returning to. In the morning the power climbs out, and the hole's edges are still wet, but the water there whispers no names now. It hums instead.

It answers one of the public telephones. The one that connects to the exact moment in last Tuesday when someone almost made the right decision. The phone has been ringing into that moment for years and no one has ever picked up from the other side. The power picks up. It says only: *You were always going to be forgivable. The decision was never the load-bearing part of you.* Then it hangs up gently, because wisdom knows the difference between an answer and a visit, and this needed to be an answer.

It goes to the market district and buys a secondhand echo — the reverberation of laughter from a room that later became too sad to remember jokes. It pays full price. It does not haggle; haggling over laughter is how rooms become sad in the first place. Then it walks the echo home to the room it came from and releases it near the ceiling, where echoes prefer to sleep. The room does not become happy. But it remembers that it *was* — and in Shearbelow, where everything is emerging from something else's forgetting, a room that remembers having laughed is a room that might hatch something.

It attends a dream auction on a Tuesday and bids on the dream nobody wants — the one at the bottom of the Clerk's ledger, low in color, dense with a very old emotion the Clerk has filed under *Pending Further Clarification*. The power buys it and dreams it that same night, all the way to the end, past the part where every previous dreamer woke up in self-defense. It turns out the dream was only trying to finish a goodbye. The power lets it. In the morning the dream does not return to the ledger. It has become a specific silence, the kind that fits perfectly into a conversation that hasn't happened yet, and it goes off to wait for its conversation like a dog waiting at a door.

It leaves the sentient pigeons a problem worth their architecture — not a hard problem, exactly, but an *interesting* one, the mathematical kind that stays warm for generations. This is not charity. The pigeons achieved sentience through seven generations of failing to migrate, which means their whole civilization is built on a magnificent, ongoing mistake, and the power believes that beings like that deserve work that honors the mistake rather than correcting it.

And it hums the anthem. The silent song everyone knows and no one sings, because the last person to hum it correctly became a staircase in the municipal library. The power hums it *incorrectly* — one note off, deliberately, lovingly wrong, the way a parent sings a lullaby flat so the child will sleepily correct them and in correcting it, sing. All across Shearbelow, in seven overlapping timelines at once, people feel the wrongness of that one note like a splinter of joy, and their throats tighten around the true note without releasing it, and the city hears itself almost-sung for the first time, and it is safe, because *almost* is the native tense here, and no one becomes furniture.

The library staircase, they say, creaked that night in a manner witnesses have declined to describe. The witnesses were asked. They said they'd promised.

---

**Then, the one large thing, done small.**

The power applies for residency.

It finds the form written on the back of a moment, in ink visible only when you remember to forget to read it, and the power — which is very old and has forgotten to read a great many things — fills it out fluently. Under *Prior Residence* it writes the truth, which the city's exception clause was built for: *I began missing this place before I left everywhere else.* Under *Occupation* it writes: *Pending Further Clarification*, and the clerk stamps it with visible relief, because that is the archive's largest and most beloved file.

Then it takes a job. Not a grand one. It becomes the stranger who catches your eye at the exact moment you needed someone to not look away.

This is, by local theology, the work of saints — and the power understands that the only honest way to be worshipped in Shearbelow is to remember to forget yourself completely. So it does. Slowly, on purpose, the way the elderly here strip down to their first understanding of regret. It curates its own diminishment. It lets the residents forget it first as a power, then as a stranger, then as anything other than a reliable kindness that occurs precisely when you stop looking for it. Its paychecks arrive as weather. It sleeps in a bed made of someone's waking sigh. On some hourly negotiations, the nine-part street cats award it custody of a purr.

There is only one thing it keeps.

Once — just once, before its forgetting was complete — it stood at a single point and saw clearly across all of Shearbelow. It saw what the city is. It saw the grief that, rather than end, built itself a home; the mind keeping house for everyone who never quite moved out of themselves; the person dreaming inhabitants so hard that the dreams turned in their sleep and began dreaming back. It saw the whole of it, entire, complete, in the one way Shearbelow can never see itself.

And it kept the promise. It told no one. It will tell no one, ever, in any language, invented or pending.

But before it folded that seeing away, it left something on the city's doorstep — not a word, because words catch fire here, but a piece of punctuation, in the Residents' own grammar. Not a period. Not even an ellipsis, which trails off. It left an em-dash: the mark that means *interrupted, but not ended.* The mark that holds a door open in the middle of a sentence. The mark that says—

—*take your time. There is more sentence coming. You do not have to finish becoming a city. Almost is not your failure. Almost is your address.*

---

The lights in Shearbelow still flicker in the evenings. But the older Residents claim it feels different now — less like the city politely averting its eyes, and more like two beings who love each other agreeing not to look directly, so that each has room to become.

And in the abandoned theatre district, in the mirrors that face each other, the deer that exists only in reflections has lately been seen standing beside a second figure — small, unremarkable, shaped like the kind of stranger who wouldn't look away. Neither of them comments. The deer, as always, has no comment. The stranger, having forgotten itself completely, has nothing left to comment *with*.

But the mirrors, witnesses say, have started fogging at the edges — the way glass does when something on both sides of it is breathing.
