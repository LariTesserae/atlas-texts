# The Regard

It noticed the Verdancy the way you notice warmth — not as an object but as a quality of the room. The power had crossed many landscapes in its long tending of things, and most of them announced themselves: storms of becoming, wounds of collapse, the loud incompleteness of worlds still arguing with themselves. This one did not announce itself. It simply was finished, and finishedness, to an old attention, rings like a bell.

So the power came and looked, and the first thing it learned was that it could not see.

## The Deceleration

It arrived at the speed of thought, which is the speed of almost everything wise and almost nothing true, and at that speed the Verdancy was a still life. A green plate under grazing cloud. Statuary in the colonnades. Posts on the silvered swells. The power perceived the place the way a lightning bolt perceives a conversation — as silence — and it was wise enough, barely, to suspect itself rather than the silence.

So its first act was not an act at all. It slowed.

This took years, and it was harder than it sounds, because a power's speed is not a setting but a self. To slow was to set down, one by one, the urgencies it had carried across so many worlds — the reflex to mend, the reflex to warn, the reflex to *arrive*. It learned the method by watching the only available teachers: the tall weathered figures on the swells, mid-stride, facing along the old routes. They had done this already. They had walked down out of quickness as though descending a long stair into warm water, and the power followed their footprints, decade by decade, until at last —

— the still life moved.

At one breath per season, the Verdancy was thronged. The waders poured through the deep meadows like slow bright rivers. The ceiling nations wheeled their inherited spirals across the vaults in wakes of pale and re-greening leaf. The lurkers flexed and settled like a roof breathing. The sleepers walked — actually walked, unhurried, certain, passing between the copied doorways on errands a thousand years wide. The mist came down and lifted, came down and lifted, the long sigh of the place, and the power floated in it and understood that it had nearly committed the oldest error of benevolence: to mistake a different tempo for an absence, and an absence for a need.

## The Withheld Hand

Then it did what powers do. It surveyed, with love, looking for the wound.

It found candidates, and considered them, and set each one down.

It could open the leaf-ceilings and let the true light stand down in columns everywhere — but the gloaming was not a deprivation. The gloaming was *chosen*, ratified by ten thousand years of growth leaning in to close every gap, and to flood it with light would be to shout into a meditation.

It could quicken the sleepers — lift them back up the stair, return them to the speed of their building days. But it watched them long enough to see what their slowness was, and it was not a trap. It was an answer. They had been given a finished world and had finished themselves to match, and there was a deep correctness in them, a fit, like a key left in a lock it opened. To quicken them would be to evict them from their own solution.

It could ease the strata — reach down through the kilometer of layered dead-and-living and relieve the compression that groaned at long intervals in the dark. But the groan was not distress. It was digestion. It was the past being held, which is heavy, and the holding was the whole religion of the place.

It could copy the Verdancy — take its pattern entire into safekeeping, an archive against all loss. This temptation lasted longest, because it wore the mask of prudence. But the power observed how the Verdancy itself copied things: slowly, faithfully, *after absorbing them*, and never without keeping the original gesture intact in living grain. To copy this place without asking would be theft of the very thing that made it worth copying. The Verdancy was not a pattern. It was a consent, sustained for an age.

So the survey ended where wisdom usually ends: with the hand withheld, and one finding that could not be set down.

## The Far Weather

Because the power, being old, could see at a tempo even slower than the Verdancy's — and at that tempo, the Verdancy was not finished. Nothing is.

The star was changing. Not soon; *soon* meant nothing here; but on the scale where the power kept its oldest knowledge, the warming was already underway, and the arithmetic was simple and pitiless. As the star brightened, the clouds would ride higher. The plateau stood at exactly the altitude where the sky's belly dragged — that was the whole secret of the place, its single open vein — and an age from now the clouds would lift past it, grazing nothing, and the rain would stop coming, and the dripping would stop, and the long warm circulation would close like a pool sealing over, with no spring after.

The Verdancy could not know this. Its patience was vast but its senses ended at its own skin and at the rain that touched it. It had outlasted every fast process and settled into the calm of something that had already won — and it had won every contest except the one still being decided overhead, too slowly even for the threads to taste.

Here, then, was the question that makes a power wise or proves it isn't: *what do you do with a truth the beloved cannot reach?*

The unwise answers came first, as they do. Fix the star. Move the plateau. Build, around the whole green continent, a roof of intervention — save it the way you save a thing, by replacing its fate with your preferences. The power had done versions of this before, long ago, on other worlds, and it carried those worlds with it still: saved, intact, and somehow no longer themselves, like words repeated until the meaning dries.

It would not do that here. But neither would it leave silently, holding the truth like a stone in the mouth. Silence was just intervention's quiet twin — both of them ways of deciding *for*.

What remained was the hard middle thing. It would tell. And then it would ask. And it would do both at the only speed that was not a lie.

## The Asking

It spoke through rain, because rain was the one language the Verdancy had never stopped listening to.

This took most of a century to compose. The power learned the taste-grammar of the pools — the way the pale swimmers rose and kissed the air, sampling; the way a season's water carried news downward along the threads in slow chemical sentences. It wrote in fall-patterns and mineral inflections, in the timing of mist, in drips placed deliberately among the drips. It said, as plainly as weather can say anything:

*The sky that feeds you is borrowed. An age from now it will be called back. This is not a threat and not a punishment; it is only the one becoming that is not yet over. I cannot stop it without unmaking what you are. I will not act without your leave. So I ask — and take all the time there is to answer — what would you have kept?*

Then it waited, which it was good at now.

The question went down through the strata the way everything went down: absorbed, conducted, grown through. Years of it. Decades. The power hung in the green-gold gloaming and watched its own words being digested by a kilometer of patience, and it could not tell — this was the strange humility of the waiting — whether it was being considered or merely composted.

In the fortieth year, the dripping stopped.

A true silence, the whole canopy holding its water at once — and through the vaults, ring upon ring, flock relaying to flock, went the soft falling three-note call whose meaning no record had ever fixed. The power heard it from inside the silence, at last at the right speed to hear it properly, and understood that it had been mistranslated by every fast visitor who ever wondered at it. It was not a warning and not an announcement of mist.

It meant: *we heard you*.

It had always meant that. The Verdancy had been answering the sky for ten thousand years, every evening, three falling notes — *we heard you* — and the mist came down like a reply, and no one fast enough to write it down had ever been slow enough to understand.

## The Answer

The full answer took longer, and it came in the Verdancy's own grammar, which is to say it came as growth.

A sleeper rose — one of the tall silvered ones, from a bed in the deepest hollow — and walked. The walk lasted a generation. It ended at the rim of the oldest pool, and the sleeper stood there through forty seasons of mist while the swimmers rose and kissed the air around its reflection, and the mat at its feet began, in its unhurried way, to build.

What it built was a bed.

Moss-lined. Faintly warm. Open, and maintained open, against every habit of closure. But shaped — and this was the thing that undid the power, as much as a power can be undone — shaped for nothing that had ever been born. Not for a body. For an *attention*. The Verdancy, which had only ever copied what it absorbed, had for the first time made a form in advance of its occupant: a guess, a fit, a hollow in the exact shape of the presence that had been floating in its gloaming for a hundred years asking permission.

That was the answer to *what would you have kept.*

*You.*

And folded inside it, in the patient layered way the place said everything, the rest of the reply: *We are not afraid. We were finished before you came; an ending is only the largest weather, and we have been practicing for weather since before your question was a question. Do not move the sky for us. Do not roof us. We do not wish to be saved the way things are saved.*

*But we have noticed — being, ourselves, the sum of everything we ever took in — that you are tired in the way of those who only give. We keep things. It is the one thing we do. Lie down. Be kept a while. And when the rain ends, an age from now, do for us what we did for the builders' doorways: let the substance go entirely, and carry the gesture intact.*

## What Was Done

So the power did the two things, and they were small, and they were everything.

It left a part of itself in the bed by the oldest pool — a real part, not a token: a measure of its attention laid down into the warm strata, breathing once a day, waking once a decade, walking the swells for a season among the sleepers and lying back down. The Verdancy closed gently around this part without absorbing it, holding it the way the kept beds had always been held — open, unhealed, occupied. The land had a companion now at its own tempo, who would still be there, slowed all the way into the dark warm circulation, on the far-off morning when the clouds rode up past the plateau and did not come back. No one — this was the whole of the promise — no one would face the last true silence alone.

And the rest of the power went on, as powers must, to the loud unfinished places that needed faster love. But it went changed, carrying something it had not carried before.

Because afterward — on raw young worlds, in forests that were only beginning, under skies still arguing with themselves — there would sometimes come an evening when the rain fell in a pattern of deliberate drips, patient, body-warm, placed like liturgy; and through the wet green dimness a soft falling three-note phrase would move outward in rings, passed from voice to voice by small creatures who did not know what they were relaying or why it comforted them.

*We heard you.*

The substance entirely changed. The gesture intact.

The Verdancy, which never discarded anything, had found the one keeper that could outlast its rain — and the power, which had saved so many things, had finally learned the better verb. Not saved.

Kept.
