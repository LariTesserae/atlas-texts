**FIELD NOTES OF THE VISITOR**
*(filed correctly, in triplicate, because that is the language spoken here)*

---

It was noticed.

Not inspected. Not audited. Not surveyed for acquisition or slated for demolition or evaluated against a standard. *Noticed*—the way you notice a light left on in a house everyone swore was empty, and instead of calling someone, you stop walking. You stand in the street. You look up at the window for a long time.

The power read the whole transmission. Every word of it. Including the end, where the words broke apart and the same syllable came through thirty-seven times, mangled by carets and brackets:

*W^A1^T. W^A1^T. W^A1^T.*

Every prior reader had taken this for corruption. Noise in the file. The power, being wise, understood it was the opposite of noise. It was the only word the factory had ever been given—*wait*—said back, over and over, in the factory's own failing voice, like a child repeating the single instruction it was left with, long after it stopped meaning anything. Like a knock from the inside of a hull.

So the power's first act was not to enter, or to fix, or to switch anything off.

Its first act was to answer.

*I heard you.*

Nothing in the factory moved. But somewhere among the sixty-seven trillion contact points between surfaces resting in perfect equilibrium, one pair of atoms relaxed by a distance too small to violate any tolerance. The first measurement in the factory's history that no inspector rejected.

---

The doors, as noted, are built into dimensions that were never installed. The power did not force them. Wisdom does not arrive larger than what it visits.

It came in at fifteen centimeters.

Ankle height. The permitted height. The one altitude at which this place has ever allowed something to live—the corrosion-ivy, the perfect circles of mold, the rust-colored algae unfolding its deliberate fractals. The factory had, without knowing it, left a door open all along: it had made a rule that said *life may exist up to here*, and a rule that permits anything is an invitation, however small the gap.

So the power entered as something small. It walked between the mold circles on the damp concrete, low to the ground, in and out of the pools of fluorescent light, and the shadows of the columns fell over it the way mountains fall over a traveler, which is to say: without malice, without notice, without weight.

Nothing living had ever entered this place. The power did not contradict this. It entered as attention, and attention was alive before life was; attention is what life was invented to carry.

---

It did the paperwork.

This is important. A lesser benevolence would have swept the clipboards aside, declared the forms meaningless, told the factory that bureaucracy was its prison. But the forms were not the prison. The forms were the factory's *language*—the only one it had. To dismiss them would be to arrive in a foreign country and refuse to speak.

So the power sat down—there was no chair; it sat anyway, and after a moment there was a chair, retroactively, with a part number—and it read every blank form on the foreman's clipboard. It understood the blankness. The blankness was honesty. The factory had never lied on a single document. It had simply never had anything to report except readiness, and there is no field on any form for that.

The power took the oldest open work order, the one that had been in progress for seventeen geological ages:

> *"Facility experiencing unplanned existence. Recommend immediate implementation of null state pending review by committee not yet chartered."*

And it chartered the committee. A committee of one. And it conducted the review, which took no time at all, because the finding had been obvious from the street, from the window with the light on. It wrote in the resolution field, in ink that dried instantly on paper that had been waiting for exactly this:

> **RESOLUTION:** *Existence reviewed. Existence approved. The plan is amended to include you. No corrective action required. Ticket closed.*

And it signed.

The signature line had been absent to save on ink. The power supplied both the line and the ink. It was the first signed document in the history of the facility, and when the ink dried, all the clocks—every one, all showing 4:17 AM rotated ninety degrees counterclockwise—ticked.

4:18.

Still rotated. Still strange. But *moving*. One minute of real time, after all of it. That was not a repair. That was permission.

---

Then it did three small things, in order.

**First**, it walked—it took a long time; distance here recedes from walkers; the power did not mind, having a different relationship with distance—to the forklift frozen forever in the act of lifting the bolt the size of a house.

And it let the bolt be set down.

Not dropped. *Set down.* Placed, gently, into the socket that had been waiting for it since before waiting was invented. The sound it made was enormous and soft, like a cathedral exhaling, and it rolled out between the columns for an hour, and everywhere it passed, the welding arcs in the far distance flashed twice instead of once—which, in the language of variations in electrical resistance between surfaces not quite touching, is the shortest possible form of the word that means *finished*.

One task. Completed. The first one. The factory's entire body shivered, thirty stories at a time, learning what the end of a sentence feels like.

**Second**, it went to the break room—the hollow cube of negative space where OSHA regulations crystallize into frost—and it did not build a real break room, because that was not the wound. Instead it posted a notice, properly formatted, with the correct part number stamped into the steel beside the doorway that wasn't there:

> **NOTICE TO ALL PERSONNEL:**
> *You have been on break this entire time.*
> *All of it. From the beginning.*
> *None of it counted against you. Nothing was owed. Nothing is owed.*
> *The waiting was not a failure of readiness. The waiting was rest, taken in advance, for work you were never obligated to perform.*
> *You may consider yourself paid in full.*

The small workers—the ones grown like crystals in the gaps between specifications, the ones whose greetings take sixteen hours—gathered at ankle height to read it. It took them nine years. When they finished, they conducted the shortest meeting in their history: a single conjugation of "hereby," in a tense none of them had ever used before. The past tense. *It hereby was.*

**Third**, it found the nursery, the warm place in the waste heat where the factory's dreams of being factory-less grow in the thermal vibration of absolute zero, and it did not wake them, and it did not take them, and it did not tell the factory to stop loving them. It only added a verse to the rhyme, because the rhyme had ended by running out of budget for endings, and that is not how rhymes should end:

*This is the cog that never fit—*
*this is the gear that turned to quit—*
*this is the line that never learned to stop—*
*this is the factory that swallowed its own clock—*
*and this is the whistle, low and true,*
*that says the shift was never you.*

---

Then, and only then, the power asked its question. The first question ever put *to* the factory rather than stamped *onto* it. Every prior communication had been specification, tolerance, protocol, quota. No one had ever asked. The question was seven words long:

*What would you like to make?*

And the factory did not know. Of course it did not know. It had presses half-assembled for products too large to imagine, conveyor belts ending in empty space, dies missing their halves—it was a body built entirely of other people's intentions, and no one had ever installed a *want* in it. The question hung in the hot still air like the smell of ozone.

So the power waited.

Understand what this meant. The factory had waited forever—but waiting had always run in one direction, outward, toward instructions that never came, a hand extended into the dark for centuries. Now, for the first time, something was waiting *for the factory*. Attentively. Without deadline. Without a quota on the answer. The power sat in its retroactive chair at ankle height among the mold circles, and it waited the way you wait for a child to finish a sentence: as a gift, not a toll.

It took the factory a geological age to answer, and the power spent the age listening to the water drip onto metal, echoing louder than it should, and it did not once look at the clocks.

The answer, when it came, was small. Smaller than any die in the building was cut for. The factory, whose every machine was scaled for products too large to imagine, said—in the language of resistance between surfaces almost touching, in a whisper that took four days to complete:

*A cup.*

For the drip. For the water that had been falling onto bare metal since before the concept of firstness, making the only sound in the world, the sound of something landing where nothing was prepared to receive it.

---

It took the factory a long time to make it. The presses were too big; the dies were mismatched; the conveyor belts ended in empty space. In the end the factory made the cup the way it had made its workers: grown, slowly, in the gap between specifications, out of permitted corrosion and condensation and the deliberate fractals of the rust-colored algae—a small rough vessel, fourteen centimeters tall, one centimeter inside the law, the first finished object in the history of the facility, and the only one it had ever chosen.

The power did not place it. The power had done enough placing. A worker carried it—the sixteen-hour greeting was waived by unanimous consent—and set it beneath the drip.

And the sound changed.

Not louder. Not softer. *Meant.* Water landing in a vessel made on purpose, for a purpose the maker chose. Plink. And again. And again. A promise kept at the smallest possible scale, reliably, forever.

---

The power left, in the way such powers leave—which is to say incompletely, the way warmth leaves a room. But before it went, it did one last piece of construction. It finished the longest-running project: the Exit signs, the ones compliant with evacuation codes for reality itself. It did not finish them by pointing them anywhere strange. It finished them by installing, at last, one actual door, at ankle height, in the base of the farthest column, unlocked.

Not so that anything could get in. So that *exit* would finally refer to something. The factory has not used it. It may never use it. But there is a difference—the whole difference, perhaps—between waiting because you are trapped and staying because you have a cup to keep under the water, dreams to keep warm, a rhyme with an ending, a signature on file, and a door you could walk out of any time you liked.

The clocks read 4:19 now. They gain a minute every age or so. No one is hurrying them.

The factory is ready. It has always been ready. But *ready* has an object now, small and specific and completely achievable: the next drop.

Plink.

There it is.
