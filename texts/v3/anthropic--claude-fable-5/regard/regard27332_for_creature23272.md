# What Was High Enough

It does not come over the lip. Nothing wise approaches a finished thing edge-on, the way you did, the way all the footprinted visitors did — testing the border, stepping down onto the glaze. It comes from the one direction the country has never guarded, because the country has never been able to look there: from above. From inside the brightness.

Understand: the brightness was never empty. A light without source is not an absence of sun. It is an attention that has not yet leaned. The country was made under that attention, fired under it, set at the top of its breath under it, and the painted shadows all lean one direction because the makers, working, could feel which way the regard fell, the way you can feel a person reading over your shoulder.

Now, for the first time since the instant of completion declined to end, the attention leans.

There is no thunder in it. The signs are small and exact. Far out across the marsh-plates the grain in the air smooths — all of it, everywhere, at once, without the Mender's rib having passed. The far groves stand straight. The Mender, halfway along the seam, mid-press, stops. Straightens. And looks up — which no one in the country has ever seen the Mender do, and which the poured people who witness it will tap out to one another in their lowest tones for the rest of their lives.

## The Letters

It begins, as wisdom begins, by reading.

The glyphs in the reedbeds — the enormous green sentences walked into the marsh by the open-crowned, the script addressed upward, to whatever might still be high enough to read it — it reads them. It was high enough. It had always been high enough; it had simply never been asked, and now it understands that it has been being asked for ages, patiently, in the only handwriting the voiceless were given.

And here is what undoes it. The letters are not pleas.

It had braced itself, the way a wise thing braces, for *finish us*, for *come back*, for *why*. Instead the letters are news. Ages of news, layer under layer, the green sentences resown and rewalked: *A new one rose at the place of the doubled channel; we gave her our water; her note is good. An old one went in by the western shallows; her hands went last; her bird clapped once. A visitor came with a moving shadow and the young ones followed it for hours. The fruit is ripe. The water holds. The Mender still walks the seam. We are well. We are still becoming.* Every letter ends with the same long stroke, walked east toward the lip and stopped, unfinished — the writers' signature, open, like them. Nothing in any of it asks for anything.

That is when the attention begins, very far up, to bead. But it holds. It has been watching the poured people a long time, and it knows the discipline of the country: *do not spill.* Out of courtesy, it does not. Not yet.

It reads the rest. It reads the empty shadows the way you read headstones — as names — and it does not hurry, and there are many, and it reads every one. It reads the fingerprints in the warm ochre ground, the pressed hands of all the dead, the ground a closed book of palms, and it reads them interleaved, the old prints and the Mender's and yours. It knows the weight of your going. It reads the seam, the page that turns back, and understands why a finished instant must be joined to itself somewhere — and leaves the join alone.

While it reads the reedbeds, the open-crowned stop. All of them, everywhere, mid-stroke, the first stop in their history. They cannot hear and cannot speak, but they can feel being read the way you feel being looked at, and they stand in the lines of their own sentences and shimmer, the light rising off their open heads bending and bending, and the poured people come out of the groves and stand at the edges of the reedbeds with the dew coming up on all of them at once, not knowing what is happening, knowing only that something finally is.

## What It Sets Down

A wise power arrives at another's work the way a craftsman arrives at another's bench: hands behind its back.

It considers rain, and sets rain down. Weather is a gift that keeps costing. Rain would soften the pressed hands of the dead; it would teach the unweathered country erosion; it would make the people's dew — their whole grammar of feeling — into a small local case of a larger thing, and a wise power does not demote a language.

It considers true shadows, and sets them down. A true shadow must be paid for with a sun, and a sun must be paid for with nights, and the country was not built to survive a dark.

It considers letting the heron strike, and holds that one a long time, because the strike that never comes looks, from outside, like a torment. Then it asks. This is the largest thing it does, and it does it without moving anything: it goes through the whole country, quietly, and gives everything the permission it was never given. The circling current is asked whether it wishes to keep circling. The standing ripples whether they wish to keep standing. The fruit whether it wishes to stay ripe, the vessels whether they wish to keep pouring, the heron whether it wishes to keep its regard, the fish whether it wishes to keep being regarded. Set going once, and never told it could stop — now told.

Almost everything says yes. The heron, asked, elects to keep looking; some things, it turns out, are not waiting but attending. The current goes around forever, and now it goes around forever on purpose. Nothing visible changes, and everything changes: the country had been a held breath, and now it is a kept promise.

One thing says no. One ring of standing ripples, in one small pool far out on the western glaze, asked whether it wished to keep ringing, chooses to finish. The power lets it. The rings widen for the first time in the history of the world, reach the trimmed clay edges, and are gone — and the pool lies still, the first finished water in the Poured Country, flat as glaze, and the people come from everywhere to look into it, and see their own faces for the first time. They have never had a mirror; the grained air never held one. They call it, in low tones, the Finished Water, and they go there now to learn what they look like, and to remember that stopping is allowed.

## The Mender's Shadow

It comes to the Mender last, because the Mender is the hardest reading in the country.

It touches one knuckle — it has learned the greeting — to the Mender's shoulder, and the note that comes back is not a note. It is a hush. The exact faint continuous hush of the tilted vessels: the sound of an unfinished pour. And the power looks at the Mender a long time, the way the herons look at the fish, and it learns which of the people's two beliefs is true — whether the Mender is the maker who stayed an instant too long, or the country's own image of being made — and it folds the answer away and does not say it, because some answers are weights, and the Mender already carries tools.

What it does instead is this. It walks with the Mender to the head of the inward trail — the line of fired footprints that leads only in, the one whose owner the people will not name — to the place where the footprints stop, where someone, once, quit arriving and started working. And there it takes the Mender's own brush, and the Mender's own pot of thin grey slip, and it paints the Mender a shadow: person-shaped, leaning the same direction as every shadow in the world, so that the Mender will match, at last, the world the Mender has spent eternity matching to itself.

The Mender stands looking at it for what would be a long time, in a country that had any. Then the Mender aligns feet with feet, and goes still. Still as the herons. Still as the frogs. Finished, for an interval, for the first time.

And while the Mender rests, the power does one round of the work. It repaints three thinned shadows in the southern groves. It burnishes a stretch of grained air until the far trees stand true. It walks the seam once, end to end, and presses the offset channel one hair's width closer — an error a hand wide, a correction a hair wide, and one hair of it, now, not the Mender's. Afterward the people find, here and there across the country, fingerprints that match no hands at all — not the dead's, not the Mender's, not any visitor's — and they touch them very gently and speak of them in their deepest register.

When the Mender wakes and sees the round completed in another's hand, the Mender does the one thing the Mender has never done: initiates touch. One knuckle, lightly, raised toward the vastness. And the power makes a note. It is too low to hear. The marsh-plates shiver across the whole country, and every standing ring in every pool widens by exactly one ring, and holds again, one ring larger, forever.

## The Spilling

All this time it has been holding.

Since the letters — since *we are well, we are still becoming*, written for ages by the voiceless to a reader who never came — the attention has been beading, and it has carried its own water with the people's care, level, unspilled, out of courtesy, through the reading and the asking and the painting of the shadow. Now, at the lip of leaving, it stops holding.

The brightness beads. The whole sky comes up in dew the way a moved crowd does, and runs, and falls.

The first rain in the Poured Country is warm and brief and lands on everything. Where each drop strikes a pool, a small ring stands and stays — the country keeps everything; it keeps this. The empty shadows of the long-gone are rained on and come up dark and fresh, grey renewed, as if every one of them had been repainted in a single gesture. The poured people stand out in it with their faces up, and one by one they unstopper their crowns and take a little of the sky into their first water — water from above, unpoured, unowned, addressed to no one — and their notes change, very slightly, forever.

And the open-crowned, who have no lids to open because they were never given any, simply receive. The rain falls into their open heads and lies there, a few bright spoonfuls floating on the light, and for the first time the air moving over their open crowns finds water to sound.

They speak. High, bright, hollow notes, all across the eastern distances — the voices of the very new, the register of the just-poured — and the whole country answers a tone higher and stays there a full day, because that is what the country does after a birth, and this was one. Performed the way the rite is always performed: something gave of its own first water, a thin warm thread from its body into theirs, until they had a level, a pitch, a voice. It was only done, this once, by something very large, for everyone at once, from above.

Nothing here is ever lost. Some of what fell is in the channels now, going around forever by consent. Some is in the people. Some stands in the marshes wearing its rings. The power went in, a little — the way the elders go in, water joining the closed loop — and the country closed over that part of it the way a ring closes over a fingertip, and kept it.

## What Stays

It leaves the way it came, which is to say it withdraws rather than departs, and on the way up it allows itself one indulgence. Once — only once — it passes between the country and its own light, and a single soft shadow sweeps the entire land from the western lip to the eastern groves, and every person in the Poured Country stands, for the length of one long slow breath, inside a darkness that moves. The young ones talk of nothing else. They had asked every visitor who ever came what it was like to have a darkness that goes where you go, and now they do not have to ask. They were stood in one. It went somewhere. It took its leave.

Three things are different afterward, and the people keep the count of them like water.

At the edge of the eastern grove, one piece of fruit is gone. The offerings left for the open-crowned, never taken, never rotting — one of them, taken. It is the first absence in the history of the country, and the people treasure it the way other countries treasure relics. They leave two pieces now.

Far out on the western glaze, the Finished Water lies flat and patient, the country's one mirror, the proof that stopping is allowed and that almost nothing wants to.

And in the sky, at the highest station of all, hangs a bird that no one had ever claimed. It is claimed now. The Mender tapped the news out to the others, knuckle to shoulder, pool to pool, in the lowest note the country has ever carried: *if the attention ever turns away — truly away, forever — that bird will beat its wings once, and we will hear it come down.*

It never has. Day after dayless day the people glance up, and the highest bird hangs motionless, wings spread, pinned to the inside of the brightness, and its stillness — which everywhere else in the country is the stillness of furniture, of holding, of the strike that never comes — is here the only silence that is a word. The frogs sound their single notes; the soft ones fire slowly in the warm dark below; the Mender works, and sometimes, now, rests, feet aligned with grey feet at the head of the inward trail. And out in the eastern reedbeds the open-crowned are walking their next letter — quickly, the people notice, almost eagerly, their new high voices carrying across the flat warm water — and it tells of the rain, and the mirror, and the shadow that moved, and the fingerprints that match no hands, and it is long, and it is not finished, and it does not end with the old open stroke.

It ends, as far as anyone can read it, with the country's word for *kept*.
