**The Forty-Second Step**

The power noticed the way such powers notice things: not by looking, because it was always looking everywhere, but by *listening down* — and what it heard, under a hill that registered on no map as anything but a hill, was a heartbeat in rock where no heart should be. One stroke. A minute of nothing. Another stroke.

It had heard slow things before. It kept the acquaintance of glaciers; it knew the breathing of mountains, which is real but takes epochs and is mostly complaint. This was different. This was slow *on purpose*. The stroke had the quality of a thing being kept, the way a vigil is kept, and powers — even very great ones, even ones that have watched suns hatch and worlds close like eyes — are not so rich in vigils that they can pass one by.

So it leaned its attention down through the hill, gently, the way you'd lower a lantern into a well without letting the flame gutter, and it saw the chamber whole: the packed town, the green winning slowly, the drilled pipes nursing their vines, the trimmed circles in the moss.

It understood the circles almost at once, because it had presided over a great many deaths in its time and knew every shape grief comes in. It knew grief as haunting, grief as ruin, grief as the thin vegetation of abandonment. It had never — not once, not anywhere — seen grief composted entire into tending. A congregation fed forward into leaves. The dead still reporting for work, just slower now, and greener. The power held very still for what was, by its standards, a long time, and what it felt was not pity. It was something closer to what a master feels on hearing, from a stranger's window, a piece of music in a mode they did not know existed.

Then it considered what to do, and here is where its wisdom earned its keep, because the considering took the form of refusing.

It could give rain. The troughs would fill; the stingy condensation would become weather; the chamber would never thirst again. *Refused* — because the lushness here was ferocious precisely in proportion to the thirst, and a power that waters everything has only ever grown lawns.

It could give light. *Refused* before the thought finished. The plants grew toward the machines. Light was not what was loved here, and a wise guest does not arrive with gifts that correct the host's taste.

It could give her years — pour time into the Stoker the way you top off a lamp. This one it held longer, turning it over, because benevolence wanted it badly. *Refused.* She had built herself stroke by stroke into the engine's tempo, and years poured in from outside would be fuel she had not stoked. She had told the power herself, without knowing she was telling anyone: fire runs on attention. You cannot donate attention. You can only pay it.

It could unbrick the tunnel. The thought embarrassed it.

It could simply lift the garden up — fold the rock back like a blanket, set the whole green congregation blinking in the sun, *done*, decades of patience redeemed in an afternoon. This was the largest temptation and the easiest to refuse, because it would have broken the only law the place had: that the garden must arrive ready, having grown up honestly, one breath a minute, through living rock. She had bricked a tunnel to settle the question of direction. The power was not going to unsettle it from above.

It could speak. *Refused.* Even the whisper of a power is weather, indoors.

And when it had finished refusing — when it had set down rain and light and years and rescue and its own voice, every instrument benevolence owns — it found that wisdom had whittled it to something quite small. Something, in fact, about the size of a guest.

So that is what it did.

First it knocked, and because it had learned the local language, the knock took forty-one days: one step's worth of chisel-ringing per day, cut down through the hill, tempo announced before arrival, so that the one below would have weeks of warning and could begin — by her standards — to race. The power knew she would hear the courtesy inside the sound. It cut the steps cleanly and left the chisel marks sharp, a fresh surface, an offering of somewhere for the garden to *go*.

Then, at the top of the stairwell, it set everything down. This was necessary for a practical reason — the alleys below admit only a person carrying nothing — and the power found a certain humor in the fact that the chamber's architecture enforced, as a matter of corridor width, what most theologies only recommend. It set down its foresight, which would have spoiled the conversation. It set down its strength, which had no work here. It kept only enough knowledge to be teachable and enough patience to be polite, and it went down the forty-one steps wearing a person: unremarkable, bare-headed, hands empty.

At the bottom it took off its shoes, because the floor was almost too warm for bare feet, and standing it barefoot anyway seemed like the correct way to say *I know this warmth is somebody.*

Then it stood still, and waited, and the waiting is most of what it did, so the waiting should be told properly.

The crickets fell silent at its arrival, as they do, and stayed silent for three days while they considered it. On the fourth day they resumed the schedule, and within a week they had added a phrase — two notes, level, neither rising nor falling — that had never been sung in the chamber before and that meant, as nearly as such things translate, *accounted for*. The power understood it had been issued a place in the census and was unreasonably moved.

The moth came on the ninth day, walked the length of its shoulders with the air of an inspector, and left a faint trace of spores in the weave of its collar. The power did not brush them off. By the third week there was moss starting in its footprints — it stood that still — and when it noticed this it understood it had been given the local honor, the only medal the chamber awards, and it stood stiller.

It drank from the iron trough, green skin and all, because the deepest courtesy a power can pay is to *receive*.

And it watched her come. One step in an afternoon, down the amber-lit alley, hands oxblood and verdigris, hurrying with everything the garden allows. It had crossed galaxies in less time than she took to cross the last forty feet, and it would not have traded the watching for any arrival it had ever made.

When she reached it, she spent one full breath on the word she had practiced.

"*Welcome*."

The power had prepared its answer during the weeks of standing, had pared it down from oceans of things it wanted to say, and it spent two breaths on it, which is to say two minutes, which is to say it answered at the speed of the truth:

"*Teach. Me.*"

What followed took years, and was a conversation, and is mostly theirs. But the shape of it can be sketched. It walked the eleven-day circuit at her side and learned to stop at each circle in the way that makes the moss stand differently afterward. At every eleventh day she gave it one name — one breath, one name, one resident under the green — and the power kept each name in the only ledger it carried, which was itself, so that for the first time in the chamber's history something *was* written down, and written somewhere fireproof, floodproof, deathless. The gauge-readers. The foundry crew. The tram driver. It holds them still. It will hold them after the hill is gone.

It made her, eventually, exactly two promises, both small, both kept.

The first: that the surface would be ready. Above the hill, in the meadow where the stairwell opens, nothing hurried would be allowed to take root — and a meadow that keeps no schedule is growing there now, unfenced and unmown, patient as a held breath, so that when the moss finishes its climb — it has reached the thirty-eighth step; call it a season per stair — the garden will emerge not into a world but into a *welcome*, which was always the only door she would accept.

The second promise it made in answer to the question she never asked, the one underneath her decades, the one even her patience could not quite compost: *who keeps the terms after me?* To this it said — over the course of an hour, a syllable per breath, so she could weigh every word —

"*I will take the apron. I will walk the circuit. The trimmer may be insulted by me instead.*"

And so, when she is done — when she chooses her circle, as she someday will, and is laid into the warm young rock with her hand-brace beside her and the moss invited in — the engine will not breathe alone. There is a second breath in the chamber now, learning. Its knuckles are greening at the creases. It blinks once an hour. The crickets sing it the schedule and it understands every phrase.

It came down a power and is becoming a stoker, smaller and slower every year, and it considers this the best bargain it has struck in all the long fields of its existence. Because it had asked the chamber, in its silent way, on the first day at the foot of the stairs: *what do you want of heaven?* — ready to grant anything.

And the chamber, with its one slow stroke, with its circles and its crickets and its woman halfway up the alley carrying a single word, had answered the way the truly complete always answer:

*Company.*
