**What the Power Did**

It did not come down through the roof-mouth, because the roof-mouth was for light and rain and the occasional moth, and it was none of those. It did not come up through the swallowed drain. It was simply, at a certain moment, present in the chamber — present the way warmth was present, without source, without entrance — and it took care, before anything else, that its arrival displaced nothing. Not one drop of water rose. Not one pressure-shadow crossed a lateral line. In nine blind heads, nine maps continued to agree, and along the southern wall the shingled crowd of shrimp went on breathing its single slow breath, unrippled. That was the first courtesy, and it cost the power some effort, because the cistern's senses were finer than almost anything it had ever had to be gentle for.

It perceived the whole room at once, of course. That is what such a power is. But having perceived it at once, it then did something rarer: it perceived it again, slowly, at the cistern's own speed — one drop, then a long while, then another drop — because it understood that a place like this is not its inventory. A place like this is its pace. To know the cistern quickly would have been to know some other thing.

So it stayed. It watched a snail complete a spiral, which took eleven days. It watched the noon pillar arrive, stand its hour in the black water like a guest who knows not to sit down, and withdraw. It felt the year breathe once — the hand's width of rise, the hand's width of fall. It attended.

And while it attended, it read.

The tool-marks were a script you could feel but not read, but the power could read them, not because they were words but because it could read labor the way the fish read water. It ran its attention along the courses, stroke by stroke, and the builders came back out of their work like figures developing in a slow bath. Forty-one hands, across about sixty years. Three generations of one family on the eastern wall — you could see the father's stroke loosening in the son's, and tightening again in the grandchild's, who had evidently loved precision more than either of them. A left-handed mason had dressed most of the corbels; the marks all leaned the wrong way, confidently, like handwriting that has stopped apologizing. One block, low on the north side, deep under the waterline now and sealed under three fingers of mineral glaze, had been dressed by someone very young and very careful, every stroke a little too deliberate, the way work looks when the worker knows the older men are watching. The power held that block in its attention for a long time.

They had left no images, no names, no marks but these. But every maker who ever shaped a stone has made, in the shaping, the same unworded prayer — *let it hold, and let someone someday know* — and the power, finding the prayer still legible in forty-one hands after all this dark, answered the half of it that remained answerable. It took them in. Not the cistern only: them. The tired Tuesday afternoons visible in the shallow strokes. The pride. The grandchild's vigilance. Somewhere outside of weather and collapse, forty-one masons are now known, completely, by something that does not forget.

Then it turned to the warmth, and here the power paused, because it had recognized something.

The warmth had no body, no source, no voice. It had a dwelling, dependents, a span. It was an old benevolence paying itself out a flicker at a time through the lives of small blind creatures, asking nothing, felt by everything, and it would end. The power regarded it the way you might regard a portrait of yourself made small and mortal, and what it felt — if a power feels — was not pity. It was something closer to respect between colleagues. *You have kept them well,* it did not say, because neither of them used saying. *Finish your work. I will not take it from you.*

That settled the largest question before it was asked. But a wise power does not trust its first settlements, so it sat down — in the manner of such beings — and weighed every gift it could give, one at a time, honestly.

It considered light, and set it down. The skulls had closed politely over that question generations ago; to reopen it would not be a gift but an argument, and the cistern had finished all its arguments.

It considered abundance — richer velvet, fatter years, more — and set it down. The room's one season was the hand's-width breath; the room's one polity was the old survivable tax of fish on shrimp. Plenty would have erased the season and pardoned the tax, and the place would have become a fuller room and a poorer world.

It considered the old fish in the deep corner, her swimming with its slight list, and the ease it could grant her — and set that down most gently of all. Death here was already what death is almost nowhere: customary, unfeared, tidied within a noon by the white gliders, followed by the largest political event of the century, the slow rebending of eight maps around a new corner. To take her ending away would have been to rob the cistern of its news.

It considered eternity — warmth without bottom, the velvet forever, the dynasties unending — and held that one longest, because refusing it looked, from certain angles, like letting them all die. But the power had read the builders' strokes, and it understood what they had understood: that the cistern was not a vessel being kept full. It was a sum being paid out. A finished thing. To make it endless would not have preserved it; it would have unfinished it, turned a long faithful spending into a pool of stopped money. The cistern was the heat of a vanished sun becoming, drop by drop and shrimp by shrimp, *spent well* — and you do not improve a gift by forbidding it to be given.

It set eternity down.

What it did instead was this. It looked ahead — far ahead, down the corridor of settlings and seasons — and it sorted the cistern's possible deaths into two kinds: the death that was written in it, and the deaths that were not. The written death was slow and very distant: the old heat thinning over centuries, the velvet paling, the generations shortening, the room growing quieter by such fine degrees that no single fish would ever live through a change it could notice, until at last, long ages on, a drop would fall into still cool water with no one to attend it. That death belonged to the cistern. The power did not touch it.

The other deaths did not belong to it. A quake, four thousand years out, that would have cracked the vault along the left-handed mason's corbels. A storm, nearer, that would have washed enough scree across the plateau to choke the roof-mouth and put out the noon pillar like a thumb on a wick. A walker, someday, with a rope and a torch and a bucket. To these the power made small answers, all of them now, all of them tiny. It eased a stress deep in the corbelled ring — and the chamber recorded the act as a single faint stony *clink*, which the fish did not react to, having heard such clinks their whole blind lives. It tilted, by less than the weight of a thought, the path of a future storm. And it deepened, very slightly, the plateau's old discretion: the lip of fitted masonry flush with the ground became a thing the eye declines a fraction more smoothly, a place even more walked past than before. The cistern would now die of nothing but its own completion. That was the whole of the power's protection: not a wall, only a promise that no ending would come here except the right one.

Then, having given nothing else, the power did the one remaining thing, which was to receive.

It made itself a seeming — weight, warmth, skin, ankles — and sat down on the worn vertebrae of the stair with its feet in the blood-warm water, and was still through the falling of three drops. The vault held each note for its surprising while. The ripple of attention crossed the southern wall and subsided. And then they came, as they had come for every newness in ten thousand years: the wax-colored shrimp walking on the backs of shrimp, the white gliders sliding along the joints to investigate, and at last, unhurried as a landlord, one of the nine — not the old one; a middle fish, the one whose route ran the western courses where the father and son had worked — and it rested its blunt face against the power's seeming-skin with the absolute calm of a creature that has never once, in all the generations behind it, been wrong to trust the dark.

The power held very still. It understood precisely what was being placed against its ankle: the cistern's only treasure, the single inheritance of all the dynasties, the one thing this place could give. And it understood that the gift carried a condition, which was that it remain true. So for the length of that touch the power made itself into something of which the trust was simply correct — not restrained, not merciful, but *harmless the way the dark was harmless*, all the way down, so that the fish's faith passed through it and found no bottom and no exception. Then it withdrew its seeming so slowly that no alarm was invented, and the maps closed over the place where it had been, and the event entered the cistern's history the way the moth on the surface had: a newness, attended, absorbed, done.

One thing more it left, though *left* is wrong, since it took up no room. It kept an appointment. Far down the corridor of years, past the last dynasty of shrimp and the last clarified shell, on the evening of the written death — when the warmth has finally finished its long spending and the room is cool and still and perfect — something will be sitting on the softened steps. It will hear the last drop fall from the roof-mouth. It will hear the vault hold the note. The cistern, which began unwitnessed, will not end that way; the builders' room will close like a book in the presence of a reader who has read all of it, every course, every spiral, every one of the forty-one hands.

Then the power withdrew — without exit, as it had arrived without entrance — and the cistern did not know it had been visited, which was the point, and was not alone anymore, which was the gift.

A drop fell from the roof-mouth. The vault held the note.

And now, somewhere outside the vault, something holds it too.
