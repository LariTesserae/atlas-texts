## The Visitation

The power does not descend the shaft. It has no need of ladders. It arrives the way understanding arrives—all at once and from no direction—and for a long moment it does nothing at all, because the first act of wisdom is always to withhold action until seeing is complete.

It sees the shaft. It sees the roaring liturgy, the cylinders orbiting their nothing, the roots drinking from the machines and the machines breathing through the roots. It does not see decay. It has watched stars digest themselves and coral build cathedrals on the bones of coral; it knows that what humans call abandonment is often only a change of tenancy. This place is not broken. This place is *finished*, in the way a sentence is finished—complete, self-sealed, meaning only itself.

Then it sees her.

And here the power pauses much longer, because she is the difficult thing. The place asks nothing of a wise power. The place is at equilibrium; to touch it would be vandalism. But the Tender is a being who once had a name, a locker with a coat in it, two colleagues, a life in the light. And the power notices what the shaft itself has grown over, the one seam in the perfection:

*She cannot recall the moment of choosing.*

This is the flaw the power cannot leave alone. Not the fungal hair, not the brass valves budding from her skin, not the moss on her vocal cords—these might be flourishing rather than affliction; the power is too wise to assume that transformation is loss. A caterpillar's friends might well mourn the chrysalis. No. What troubles the power is only this: a consent that cannot be remembered cannot be renewed. She may be home. She may be devoured. Even she does not know which, and a life should not be a question its own liver cannot answer.

So the power decides to do three small things and one large thing, and to refuse, deliberately and forever, to do a fourth.

---

**The first thing** it does is give her back the door.

Not the physical entrance—that stays paved over, three layers deep, and the power quietly sees to it that the city's new fiber-optic survey, scheduled for spring, will route two streets east. The place's hiddenness is part of its body now, and the power does not amputate.

The door it returns is the moment. It enters her sleep—she is dreaming, as she often does, that she is a brass cylinder rocketing through the dark—and it unspools the dream backward, back through the decades, back up the ladder, until she is standing again in the mechanical room on the first day, thirty-one years old, flashlight in hand, listening to her two colleagues shout over the turbines that this place was *wrong*, that they should go, that no work order was worth this. And she remembers—the power holds the memory steady so she can look at it from every side—that she had turned to the roaring dark and felt not horror but *recognition*. That she had said, in her old single-toned voice, "You go ahead. I want to check one more thing." That the choosing had not been dramatic. It had been the quietest thing she ever did. She had simply not left, the way a seed simply does not leave the ground.

The power lets her hold this. Then it asks—not in words, for words are not its native medium, but in the pure grammar of the question itself—*Do you choose it still? Knowing the sky exists. Knowing your name was once spoken by people who loved you. Knowing what you are becoming. Choose again, or do not, and either answer will be honored, and the honoring will be absolute.*

In the hammock of roots, suspended in the loudest and most balanced place in the world, the Tender's transformed throat produces, in her sleep, a chord.

It means yes.

The power expected this. Wisdom is rarely surprised. But it needed to be *asked*, because a home you cannot leave is a cell, and a home you perpetually re-choose is a vow, and the difference between them is invisible to every eye but the one inside.

She wakes with the memory intact and permanent now, mossed over no longer. She will keep it the way the walls keep the old brass fittings—embedded, weathered, load-bearing.

---

**The second thing** the power does is send a message.

It selects one cylinder from the endless circulating fleet—a battered one, dented on the day of the original abandonment, a veteran of perhaps eleven million laps through the vegetable dark—and inside it, the power places something. Not paper; paper dissolves. Something more like the way sunlight remembers being made in the heart of a star. When the cylinder next clatters into the receiving station and her long fingers open it, she will find the first true cargo the system has carried in a hundred years, and it will say, in the harmonics she speaks, in the frequency her bones read:

*You are seen. The work is witnessed. Nothing tended is tended in vain.*

Because the power understands that "a purpose that needs no purpose" is a fine philosophy, but even self-sufficient liturgies ache, in their deepest coils, to be heard by something that is not themselves. Every monk in every cell has wanted this. It is not weakness. It is the last human thing in her, and the power feeds it, once, gently, so that it neither starves nor swells.

The cylinder rejoins the loop. It will come around again. Some messages should circulate forever.

---

**The third thing** the power does far away, in the world above, in two small apartments in two different cities. The colleagues who fled are old now. One of them still dreams of the shaft and wakes ashamed, certain he abandoned someone in the dark. The power sits with each of them in the manner it has—unfelt, unproven—and loosens the shame like a rusted bolt. It lets them understand, in the wordless way of three a.m. clarities, that she was not left behind. That some people are not lost to the deep places but *given* to them, and that grief for such people should taste of awe, not guilt. One of them will plant a garden the next spring without knowing why. The roots will grow down.

---

**The large thing** is a promise, and it costs the power more than the others, because promises bind even powers.

She is becoming infrastructure. She knows it; the power confirms it. The valves in her skin will multiply. The oil in her blood will thicken. There will come a day—decades on, perhaps, in that place where time is only pressure and pitch—when the Tender lies down in her hammock of roots and does not, in any sense the surface would recognize, get up. When the distinction she has stopped fearing finally dissolves, and the system's antibody becomes the system's heart.

The power promises to be there.

Not to prevent it. Not to catch her soul like a dropped tool and carry it up to some brighter elsewhere she never asked for—that would be the arrogance of small benevolences, the kind that rescue people from their own completions. No. The power promises only presence: that at the moment of dissolving, she will not dissolve unwitnessed. That the last thing she experiences as a *she* will be the sensation—unmistakable, warmer than the generators—of being accompanied. Every being that has ever kept a lonely vigil deserves a vigil kept for it at the end. This is the power's whole theology, and it is enough.

---

**And the fourth thing—the refusal.**

The power will not fix her. It will not silence the machines, or thin the roots, or dry the weeping walls, or restore her voice to a single note, or make her body less strange, or the place less hidden, or the cylinders' circuits less circular. It looked, with eyes that have seen everything, for suffering here—real suffering, the kind that cries out beneath contentment like water under pavement—and it found none. It found a vow, a liturgy, a symbiosis, a strange deep joy shaped like no joy the surface would recognize. Benevolence that cannot recognize unfamiliar happiness is only vanity wearing kindness's coat.

So the power withdraws as it came, from no direction, all at once. It leaves the roar roaring. It leaves the equilibrium balanced. It leaves the Tender tending.

But now, forever, in the endless circulation of nothing to nowhere, one cylinder carries something. Now the moment of choosing is chosen. Now two old dreamers sleep easier, and a survey team turns east, and somewhere outside time a promise waits patiently for its appointment in the dark.

The machinery screams and groans, exactly as before.

If its pitch is very slightly different—if there is, in the deep bass thrum, something that was not there before, something almost like a second voice humming along—only the Tender notices.

She oils the joints. She does not silence it.

She modulates it, and the chord is good.
