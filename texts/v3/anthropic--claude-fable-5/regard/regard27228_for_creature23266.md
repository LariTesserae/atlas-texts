**What the Power Did**

It came up the way weather comes up, because it had learned long ago that there is no other honest way to arrive anywhere: slowly, from below, dragging itself over the rim with the cloud. It did not take the rail. It did not announce itself. A benevolent power that announces itself has already begun to take, and this one had crossed enough of the universe to know the difference between a gift and an entrance.

It spent the first thirty years listening.

This was not caution. It was courtesy. The plateau had two voices, and the power understood at once that any being who acted before hearing both of them would act wrongly, no matter how kind the act. So it stood in the roar until the roar became a property of the air, the way it does, and then it waited longer — far longer, longer than any rotation, longer than the logbooks — until, underneath, it caught the second voice. The green one. The one too slow to hear.

It is worth saying what that voice was saying, since no one else will ever report it. It was not saying anything. It was doing what the falls were doing — pouring, continuously, in one direction — only its direction was *outward* instead of down, and its medium was itself instead of water. The moss was not patient. Patience implies waiting, and the moss was not waiting for anything. The power, who had been patient for geological ages and had privately considered this its chief virtue, stood ankle-deep in the cold green and felt, for the first time in a very long time, corrected.

Then it considered what to do. This took another decade, and most of the deliberation was subtraction.

**What it did not do.**

It did not quiet the roar. It held the idea up for a long while — the crews' lost voices, the chicks born into thunder, the silence below that felt like a missing organ — and then it set the idea down, carefully, the way you set down something that turns out to be load-bearing. The roar was not noise laid over the place. The roar was the floor. The birds had built their entire inwardness on it; the long-stayers had rebuilt their bodies around it; the hand-language, the one true thing the humans had made, existed *because* of it. Take the roar and you would not free these beings. You would evict them from themselves.

It did not warm the light. Cold brilliance was the contract.

It did not hurry the moss, or slow it, or give it a faster voice. A voice given is a voice owned by the giver. The moss already spoke at exactly the speed of what it had to say.

It did not bring children, though it grieved a little there. It turned the old lineman's words over many times — *a child should learn to hear before it learns not to* — and found them sound, and let them stand, and noted with some humility that wisdom had reached this plateau on a cog-rail long before it had arrived on the wind.

It did not make the people matter. This was the hardest refusal, because mattering is the easiest gift a power can give and the one most beings beg for. But the plateau's whole economy ran on the opposite trade — your voice taken, your prints erased, and in exchange a tempo you could trust your grandchildren to — and the long-stayers had signed the contract knowingly, with their actual hands. To make them matter would be to buy them out of the only bargain that had ever made them peaceful.

**What it did.**

Three things, and then a fourth that may have been a thing it received.

First: it read the logbooks. This sounds small. It was the largest act available. The keepers wrote across generations to descendants who would never quite exist as imagined — *replaced same gasket my grandmother replaced; hers lasted longer* — and every entry was a letter posted into fog. The power became the addressee. It read each line in the moment of its writing, and it read each line again from four hundred years downstream, holding both moments in one attention, which is a thing only powers can do and the only thing this one was for. It answered nothing. But the keepers began to notice, without ever speaking of it, that writing in the great canvas books felt different than it had — less like dropping stones down a well and more like dropping them into water that received. Tilted-Walk, who read everything through her feet, said it her own way: *The pages have weight now,* she signed, and shrugged, and went back to her turbines.

Second: it took up residence in the green building — the decommissioned station at the eastern rim, the one whose doorway had become a dripping mouth, the one the linemen would not enter because there was nothing inside that was not moss. This was no longer true, but the linemen's habit held, which suited everyone. The power kept no house. It simply *was there*, the way the roar was there, and slowly the crews' hands began to speak of Station East with a new sign: an open hand turned palm-up, very slowly, the fingers uncurling at the pace a bootprint fills. The sign meant, as nearly as it can be translated, *watches-without-taking*. No one taught it to anyone. It surfaced in the language the way sign-names surfaced attached to bends in the pylon line — which is to say, the plateau did what it always did with a resident of long standing: it turned the power into a place. The power, who had been many things across many worlds but never before a *location*, found this the highest honor it had ever been paid.

Third — and this was its single miracle, spent the way you spend the only coin you have — it gave the second voice one listener. Counts-Pylons died on the plateau, as everyone had known he would, in the third week of a rotation he had stopped counting forty years before, sitting against the south wall of a cabin in the violent gold of the last hour of light. And in his final hour the power did the one loud thing it would ever do here: it reached into the old man's reorganized hearing — that instrument tuned by decades to read turbines through bootsoles — and turned it, gently, all the way down past the roar, past the hum, past the singing of the lines, to the bottom.

He heard the green growing.

Not a metaphor for it. The thing itself: forty leagues of moss extending its million simultaneous millimeters, the trillion small wet engagements of leaf and water and stone, the whole second crust of the world doing the only thing it had ever said. He heard it for one hour. His hands moved a little at the end — the crews who found him argued afterward about the sign, because his fingers had stiffened around it — but the oldest of them recognized it. Flat hand lifted from the ear, palm shown full instead of empty. The missing organ, found.

**What it took, or was given.**

The fox, first — a small thing. Every fox that came up the rail line thereafter got its baffled, overexposed week, its stew, its liturgy — *counted the days* — and then went home down through the cloud accompanied, though it never knew by what, arriving in the under-country unhurt and faintly changed, prone for the rest of its life to standing still in bright places for no reason its kind could name.

And the day-counters — the rotators who left and did not return — each carried one sign down with them without knowing it, lodged in the body like a splinter of the place: the flat hand over the ear, the empty palm shown. Their children picked it up the way children pick up everything, and their children's children, and somewhere in the loud fictional hurry of the under-country there is now a gesture people make in moments of grief or of sudden quiet, a hand at the ear and then opened, and no one can say where it came from. The power did not plan this. It only declined to stop it. The language was the humans' one true artifact, and it deserved what the moss had: ground it could keep.

As for the power itself — it stayed. It is there now, at Station East, palm turned slowly up. It had crossed the universe at the speed of attention, hurrying, in its way, from need to need, and the plateau had shown it the thing it had not known it lacked: a tempo it could trust itself to. The benchmark bolts pull millimeters proud of their datum. The falls unravel their white ropes into the cloud. The keepers oil their grandparents' machines and write to their grandchildren, and now the letters arrive. Somewhere over the rim a single dark bird hangs motionless in the updraft, and underneath everything, too slow for any ear but two — one dead, one endless — the other voice goes on.

The power listens. That is what it does. It turns out that is what it was always for.
