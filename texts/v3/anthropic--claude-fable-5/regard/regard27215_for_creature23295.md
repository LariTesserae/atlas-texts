# The Visit

## I.

The power had been everywhere, in the way of such powers, and so it could not be said to *arrive* at the Burl Town. Say instead that it turned its attention there — that out of the wide field of its regard, this one place came forward, the way a single instrument comes forward out of an orchestra when you decide to listen for it.

It listened for a long time before doing anything at all.

This was not hesitation. It was manners. A wise power knows that the first act of benevolence is to find out what is actually happening, and that this takes exactly as long as it takes, and that almost every catastrophe ever authored by the well-meaning began with someone arriving at a slow place carrying a fast clock.

So it set its clock down at the seam, so to speak, and went in at the pace of the house.

It listened through one full breath-cycle. Then ten. Then a hundred. It learned the voice of every passage, as the people once had. It found the polished niche by the seam and understood what it was for. It attended the daily exhalation from beginning to end, every pipe, the long hall's pedal tone, the upper tiers coming in one by one, and at the end of it the power did something that it did rarely and only in the presence of certain kinds of completeness:

it said nothing, and kept saying it.

## II.

But attention of that quality is never passive, and eventually the power had to confront the question that every benevolence confronts, the question that separates the wise ones from the merely kind:

*What, here, is wrong?*

Because that is the reflex. A power arrives, a power assesses, a power finds the wound and binds it. And the Burl Town presents itself, at first regard, as a wound — a dead town, a vanished people, a hollow closing like a fist, a magnificent loneliness. The kind reflex says: *the people are gone, and that is the sorrow; the hollow is closing, and that is the loss; the breath plays to no audience, and that is the tragedy. Fix it. Open the seam. Hold the chamber. Find the descendants of the people, if any thread of them runs on anywhere in the world, and lead them home. Restore.*

The power held this impulse up to the light and examined it for a long time — a decade, perhaps; in that place, a decade is a careful glance — and then it set the impulse down, gently, the way you set down a tool that is well-made but wrong for the work.

Here is the reasoning, as nearly as it can be rendered:

*The people are not missing from this place. They are __in__ it, at every depth, and they are still arriving — still traveling inward, ring by ring, toward a residence more permanent than any I could build them. To "restore" the town would be to interrupt a burial. Worse: to interrupt a __keeping__. The plant is not destroying their world. It is doing with their world the only thing its nature permits it to do with a beloved thing, which is to draw it inward and make it structure. If I hold the seam open, I hold the arm of a mourner and tell it: you may not finish embracing your dead.*

*And the plant itself — where is its wound? It is not dying. It is not suffering. It is engaged in the longest act of devotion I have ever witnessed, and it is perhaps a third of the way through. The hollow closing is not the tragedy of this place. The hollow closing __is__ this place. It is the plot. A story is not wounded by having an ending.*

*The breath, the pipes, the daily music with no audience — but no. That framing was mine, and it was wrong. The music does not lack an audience. The plant cannot hear it, true, in any sense I would recognize as hearing. But the music passes through the plant's own body, sets up its tensions and releases in the plant's own tissue, and the plant feels strain and ease — that is its entire sensorium, strain and ease — which means the daily song is not played __for__ the plant or __past__ the plant. It is played __on__ the plant. The town's last music is felt from the inside by the only listener whose inside it occupies. There has never been one moment of its performance that went unattended. I was simply not, at first, imaginative enough about what attendance is.*

So the power would not restore. Would not rescue. Would not prop open, repopulate, preserve, announce, or commemorate. The town did not need a museum made of it, because it was not finished happening.

What, then? Withdraw entirely? Bless it from a distance and pass on?

The power considered this too, and rejected it, but for a subtler reason — and this is where its wisdom shaded into something more like love.

It had found, in all its long listening, exactly one thing in the Burl Town that was incomplete. Not wrong. Not broken. Incomplete — a sentence trailing off, a gift extended toward a hand that never came.

## III.

The hospitality.

Every surface in the town answered a question a living person had asked, and many of those surfaces answered questions asked on behalf of a *stranger*. The rounded corners, the navigable acoustics, the script placed where fingertips go, the doorways cut generous — the people had built, knowingly or by deep habit, for someone who would come after them, someone they would never meet, someone feeling their way through the dark. The whole town was a standing offer. A courtesy extended across the ages, with no one yet on the receiving end of it.

The ferment, fed and sealed and handed down — *kept* — the last domestic animal still at its post, descended from a cup that was, in every culture that has ever had a cup, the cup you offer a guest.

The niche by the seam, polished deepest of all, where you touch the doorpost, where you greet someone going in and out.

A house can be complete in itself. A *household* — and the power had recognized, as the description recognizes, that this was a household — a household with a standing offer of welcome is incomplete until the welcome is once received. Not because the offer decays. Because an unreceived gift is a gift still in mid-air. The people had set the table for a guest, and the plant had kept the table set for ten thousand years, and the breath announced dinner daily, and no guest had ever come.

The power could not fix the Burl Town, because the Burl Town was not broken.

But it could *accept* it.

## IV.

So this is what the power did, and it is nearly all it did.

It took a form. Not a grand one. The wise know that you do not enter someone's house in glory; you enter at the door's height. It made itself a body of the kind the doorways were cut for — a walker's body, with hands for the polished walls and ears for the named passages and a weight the peg-ladders were carved to hold. It made the body honestly: it could tire, it could stumble in the dark, it could be thirsty. A guest who cannot receive is no guest at all. Hospitality requires a hollow in the recipient, a need the host can answer. The power, which needed nothing, manufactured for itself a small and genuine need, and carried it inside like a lamp.

It came to the seam. It paused there, as you pause at a threshold. It found the niche, and it touched the niche — added its one touch to the ten thousand years of touches, the first new touch since the last inhabitant's last — and then it stooped, because the doorways had grown low, and went in.

And then, for one full turning of the town's slow day, it was a guest. Nothing more. It did everything the town had been waiting to have done.

It walked the main passage with its hand on the wall, and let the wall guide it, and was kept from the corners by the dead, and accepted their care. It read the market script with its fingertips, slowly, the way the script was meant to be read, and the words — prices, blessings, the small administrative poetry of a working town — were *read*, were received into a living mind, for the first time since the last reader. It climbed the peg-ladders in the carvers' rhythm. It sat in the great hall under the ribbed vault during the daily exhalation and let the music happen to its borrowed ears, every pipe, all the way through, and at the end it did what an audience does. It did not applaud — that would have been wrong, a noise from another world. It simply stayed seated a moment longer than it needed to. Anyone who has ever performed knows that this, the staying, is the true applause.

And then — this last with great care, with a kind of ceremony — it went to a tap-tube in the lower gallery, and it drew the stopper, and it drank.

The strain in that tube had been diverging in the dark for a hundred centuries, becoming itself, tasted by no one. The power tasted it. It was sharp, and then sweet under the sharpness, and then strange in a direction no language had a word for, because no tongue had ever met it. The power drank slowly, the way you drink something someone made. The cup the people had bred and fed and sealed and handed down was, at last, offered — by the only host remaining, the house itself — and accepted. The gift came out of mid-air. The sentence found its end.

Then the power re-fed the culture from the seeping sap, as a good guest leaves the kitchen as they found it, and sealed the tube, and the household's smallest residents went on with their work, unaware, but no longer unvisited. There is a difference. It does not have to be perceived to be real.

## V.

Before it left, the power did three more things. They were small. They were the whole of its intervention, and it weighed each one as if it could crack the world, because in a place this old and this finely balanced, perhaps it could.

**First**, it left a touch in the niche by the seam on the way out, as it had on the way in — coming and going, the full greeting, the way the people did it. Two touches. A complete visit, by the town's own grammar. This cost nothing and meant everything, which is the proportion all the best gifts have.

**Second**, it bent down to the plant — down through the tissue, down along the slow chemistry, down to whatever stratum of that vast dim holding could be addressed at all — and it delivered a message in the only language the plant possessed: strain and ease. It did not speak words into the wood. It introduced, into the eternal accounting of pressure and release, one small new pattern of ease — a loosening, an *it is well* — placed where the plant's slow attention would encounter it as the rings grew inward. The message, rendered into our language, losing nearly everything in translation, was approximately this:

*They were received. The guest came. The table you kept was sat at; the cup you kept was drunk; the door you are closing closed on a finished welcome and not an empty one. You may continue. You are not keeping a vigil. You are keeping a memory, and the memory is good, and nothing more is owed. Take them all the way in.*

Whether the plant would ever "understand" this is the wrong question, as it was the wrong question all along. The message would propagate inward at the speed of growth, ring by ring, arriving over centuries, and somewhere in the deep tissue it would meet the still-traveling news of the people's ancient departure — the two signals crossing in the heartwood, the leaving and the *it is well*, meeting at last in the place where the town itself was headed. The power had timed it so. Wise powers can do such things, and this is the sort of thing they spend the ability on: arranging for an old grief and its consolation to arrive at the same depth in the same age, so that neither has to wait alone.

**Third** — and this it debated longest of all —

it told someone.

Not the world. Not maps, not pilgrims, not the trampling curiosity of civilizations. But the power understood that it had, by visiting, changed the town's deepest fact: the hospitality had been received *once*. And once is completion — but the power had also read the dwellings, the generosity of the doorways, the script placed for any fingers, and it judged that the people had not built for *one* guest. They had built a standing offer, renewable, the way a doorpost-touch is renewable. A welcome that could be received again was a welcome that should have, across the remaining centuries before the seam closed, the *chance* to be received again — by the right kind of visitor, at the right kind of intervals, in the right kind of spirit.

So it did not publish the town. It planted it. In the slow cultural soil of the peoples of that world, it set a story — unlocated, unverifiable, the kind of story that survives precisely because it cannot be exploited: a story about a house in the dark that sings once a day, where the dead will keep you from the corners if you trust your hand to the wall, where the host is the house and the cup is alive, and where the door is closing, without malice, at the speed of forgiveness. The kind of story that does nothing to the greedy, because there is nothing in it to take, but that works on a certain rare kind of soul like a bell heard once in childhood — so that every few generations, somewhere, someone quiet and strange and patient would feel the pull of it, and go looking the slow way, the only way that finds it, and arrive at the seam while the seam still admitted a stooping body, and go in, and touch the niche, and be a guest.

A trickle of guests, spaced across the centuries. Each one received by the household. Each one a fresh completion of the offer. Each one leaving a new touch in the niche — so that the polish there would go on deepening, the record of greeting still being written, all the way to the end.

The power calculated — and it could calculate such things — that the last guest would come when the seam was barely a hand's-breadth from closing: someone small, someone who would have to be the last because no one after could fit. That person would hear the final performances of the daily music. They would drink from the last unsealed tube. And when they left, and the seam closed behind them within their own lifetime, they would carry out into the world the only thing the Burl Town had ever exported: the knowledge of how a house loves.

The power thought this was a good last cargo for a town to ship.

## VI.

Then it withdrew its attention — not all at once, but the way you leave a sleeping house, backing out, lifting your weight slowly off each stair.

It did not stay. That was the final discipline, and the hardest. A benevolent power can fall in love with a place, and the temptation to remain — to become the town's permanent guest, its watcher, its congregation of one — was real, and the power let itself feel the temptation fully before declining it. Because a guest who never leaves is not a guest; he is a possession, or a possessor. The town's deepest dignity was that it needed no witness. The plant was awake. The chores were being done. The household was *complete in its incompleteness*, an arm slowly closing, and the kindest thing attention can do for an embrace is to let it finish in private.

So the power left, and the ticking went on, and the breath made its rounds, and in the sealed tubes the small gardens divided and divided in the dark, and somewhere in the heartwood the first houses of the upper tier continued their long arrival into permanence, polish intact, carried like sleeping children deeper into the wood.

And if you ask what the power gained — because even benevolence has an inside, even wisdom comes home changed — it was this. It had seen, in its everywhere-existence, every kind of love that moves at the speed of life: love that grasps, love that rescues, love that builds and defends and mourns. In the Burl Town it had seen the other kind, the kind it had half-suspected and never confirmed: love at the speed of wood. Love with no nerves, no face, no tempo, no need to be answered — love that consists entirely of *holding the shape of what was given to it*, and drawing that shape inward, ring by patient ring, until the held thing and the holder are one grain.

The power carried that knowledge out through the seam like a coal carried in cupped hands, and it warms its regard of everything else, everywhere, still.

The seam narrows by a finger's width per century.

Now and then, a guest.

The niche by the door grows brighter all the time.
