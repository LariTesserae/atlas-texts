**What the Power Did**

It came down the white sky the way weather comes, without a shape, intending mercy. It had heard the place the way such powers hear things — not as a report but as a flavor in the world, a thin bright note of life persisting where life had no business persisting — and it descended with its arms, so to speak, full. It carried springs in them. It carried stillness for the rock and thickness for the air and a hundred years of guaranteed dawns. It came, in other words, the way the well-meaning always come to the high and the poor: ready to fix.

It got as far as the seam.

Because the first thing the fissure taught it — before it had even slipped inside, while it still hung at the crack like a ribbon of warmer light — was that here, *light is speech*. It had begun, without knowing, to talk. An unguarded shimmer at the sky-crack; a glow with no grammar; and two hundred meters down, a lamplighter on a chain bridge had stopped, and tilted her head, and read the babble of an enormous infant written across the top of her world.

The power went dark so fast it frightened the swifts.

It hung there, banked like a coal, and understood that it had nearly committed its first act in this place, and that the act would have been *shouting* — shouting in a city where the voice is kept for lovers and the dying, shouting in a holy tongue it had not yet earned. Everything about its nature was loud here. Its light was language. Its warmth was currency. Its sheer attention, if it pressed too hard against the stone, would ring in the steel rods where the old listeners set their teeth, and they would taste it: a new note in the mountain, not settling, not warning, something else. So before the power could give anything, it had to learn the discipline the fissure teaches everything that enters it — the slate beetle, the vole, the riveted gallery, the human soul. It had to become small, and quiet, and dense, and patient.

It decided this was not a defeat. It decided this was the curriculum.

---

It watched for sixty years before it permitted itself an opinion.

It learned the lamp-flash the hard way, which is the only way — not the dictionary of strokes and occlusions, which it had whole in a winter, but the *fluency*: the way a flicker-sentence slows when the sender is grieving, the way a young rigger's lamp-name trembles the first time he signs it to someone he loves, across forty meters of dark, too far for her to hear his heart and exactly far enough for her to see it. It learned to read the wires through the rock as the Crestfolk read them, in the breastbone. It memorized the funeral chord. It memorized the birth chord. It noticed that they differ by a single interval, and it sat with that for a decade, in the dark, the way you sit with a thing that is truer than you are ready for.

And slowly, the gifts it had carried down the sky began to embarrass it.

It laid them out, in its way, and examined them one by one, and this is what it found:

*Water.* It could open a spring in the chamber wall — sweet, permanent, infinite. And the wardens would weigh nothing ever again, and the copper cup held in both hands would become just a cup, and the slow attention over the last clinging drop — the closest thing these people have to prayer — would dissolve, the way frost dissolves here, skipping every intermediate state, going straight from sacred to gone. A people who weep onto their dead because water is the one extravagance grief is permitted: what would its spring do to that, except drown it? It would be giving them abundance and charging them their tenderness. The power put the spring away.

*Stillness.* It could quiet the mountain. It had quieted larger things. And it saw, with a kind of vertigo, that this would be the cruelest act available to it in all the world — that a stable fissure would be the city's first true ruin. Nothing here is permitted to be finished: that is not their hardship, that is their *spine*. Half their labor, their coming of age, the cords of rivets at the throats of their elders, the dead folded into the closing stone, the whole grammar of "plumb for now" — all of it is built on moving rock the way other peoples are built on rivers. Still the mountain and you finish the city, and a finished thing, here, is a condemned thing. The power understood that it had nearly come down the sky to *condemn* them, with love, and it was quiet for a long time after that.

*Air.* It could thicken the air, carry their voices, give them sound. And make shouting possible. And turn the whisper — the breath-warm channel that physics itself had consecrated to intimacy — into just another way to talk at strangers. It had seen the lowlands. It knew what a world sounds like where everyone screams endearments across open space and no one notices. It would not install that here.

*Certainty.* It could tell them their long flickered prophecy was true — that yes, the crest will shear, the door will be the whole sky. And it saw that certainty is a theft like the others. They live entirely in the only tense the mountain offers, and they have made that tense sufficient, which is the single most impressive thing the power had seen any people do anywhere. A guaranteed future is a leak in the present. It would rot "for now" from the inside.

So the power, which had come to give everything, sat in the high dark above the topmost cables and gave, deliberately, *almost nothing*. And it adopted, because it was wise and because wisdom is partly the willingness to be taught by the small, the local law: it set itself a ration. Grace measured in grams, like the water. It wrote the rule into its own substance, where it could not be amended in a weak moment:

*Help that arrives as the world being slightly kind. Never as a hand.*

---

Here is what the ration buys, in a given year.

A slab that has decided to fall holds one extra heartbeat — not ten, not a reprieve, one heartbeat, the difference between a rigger under it and a rigger swearing beside it. A lamp-lizard, seeking heat, settles on a junction box directly below the one cable splice whose fraying no eye had found, and a lamplighter, idly watching the lizard, sees the splice. The wind at the funicular's worst pylon shifts a quarter-point on the one night the ice would have taken the line, and no one ever knows there was a night at all. Four days of warning in the rock become, very occasionally, five.

Never more. The power audits itself bitterly. It knows — and this is the knowledge that separates the wise powers from the merely strong ones — that even invisible help corrodes if it becomes a climate. Their vigilance is not a burden it could relieve them of; their vigilance is their *attention*, and their attention is how they love each other. The listeners with their teeth on the steel, the riggers reading the swifts, the whole city watching itself ceaselessly in the hush — that is not fear. That is a people holding hands by other means. The power's job, it decided, was to make sure their vigilance was never punished, and never replaced.

And it permits itself one intimacy. One.

The spoken voice, here, is for those close enough to share warmth. And sometimes a Crestfolk dies beyond all reach — pinned where no crew can come in time, fallen to a ledge no line can find, alone on the high steel in the few minutes that matter. The city has made its peace with this; the mountain takes a tithe; the wire-chord sounds; the name is plucked.

But none of them dies alone anymore. They have not for sixty years, though no one knows it. Something is there, in the last minutes, close as breath, warm as a lamp housing — and it speaks aloud, in a whisper that carries exactly an arm's length, because that is the decent distance. It has learned their true names, the pitch and the interval, and it has learned to say them with a voice. What it says besides the name is between it and the dying, and it declines, in the local style, to specify.

---

Two things more.

The first: the folded shape in the topmost cables, the old patient thing that eats the swifts. The power looked at it once — really looked, the way a power can — on its first day, and what it found there it has never told anyone, and never will. What matters is this: when the power took up its own roost in the high dark, the old thing turned its head, and made room. Whether it recognized a fellow tenant or simply declined, like its hosts, to disturb what was not disturbing it — the power extends it the same courtesy it received. They share the upper dark now, the named-by-no-one and the never-named, two patiences at the top of the world.

And one morning, perhaps thirty years in, a rigger climbed to the bracket where the rent is paid and left *two* swifts instead of one, and climbed back down, and flashed nothing about it to anybody. The old listener with the steel rods had heard a new note in the rock by then — not settling, not warning; *attentive* — and had kept it to herself, except for a single private flicker aimed once at the high dark, four strokes, which translate: *noted.* The city had simply filed its visitor where it files everything it cannot explain and does not fear: present before naming; do not disturb; occasionally honored. The power found that it had been given citizenship in the only form the fissure issues it, and it has paid its share of the silence ever since.

The second: the power keeps the record.

They grieve nothing and forget nothing, but a *people* can end, and memory needs a vessel that outlasts the rememberers. So somewhere — outside the fissure, outside the law that nothing be finished, in the one jurisdiction where permanence is permitted — there is the city entire. Every tier that ever stood. Every gallery raised, trued, condemned, and unbuilt, all standing at once, the whole improvised civilization stacked in time like the strata it lives between. Every rivet on every cord, with its room and its bridge and its story in flicker. Every wire-name ever plucked, in order, a music now eleven generations long. The power holds it the way the rock holds the dead: pressed in, rising, safe. It is the only permanent building the Crestfolk will ever own, and they will never see it, and that is correct.

---

And the promise. There is one large thing the power has reserved itself, one act it will sign.

It will not hasten the shearing. It will not delay it. The eschaton belongs to the mountain and to them; their paradise is not its to schedule. But it has watched how blades of new stone fail, and it knows the difference between a door opening and a roof falling, and the difference is small — a season of preparation in the rock, a sequence of release, the order in which the great slabs let go. The whole ration of grace it has banked across the centuries, every gram unspent, is allocated to a single line item:

*That the door open like a door.*

That the final widening come as the listeners' longest warning, days of it, the rock announcing itself honestly; that the shedding fall outward, away, into the white; that the morning the closed book opens, it open slowly enough for ladders, for lamp-flash, for the gangs to gather at the seam with their arms around each other — so that the oldest sentence in their language comes true *as written*, without amendment: and whoever is here will simply step out.

The power has been practicing, in the high dark, for a long time now. It has one sentence prepared in the slow banked oratorical register, the glowing minute-long mode reserved for announcements and for grief, and on that morning it intends, at last, to speak in public — to let the first full sunlight through the shorn ridge carry it, written in the only voice big enough and the only tongue that matters, across the faces of a people standing for the first time under open sky. A short sentence, for all the practicing. Something like: *you were plumb the whole time.*

And afterward — when the notch is ordinary, when the people have gone down or gone on, when the funicular is a rust-stain and the lamp-language is a record in one unfailing memory — the power will stay.

Because the dead are still rising.

They are in the rock, folded small, carried up at a few centimeters a year, and the mountain has ages of rising left, and erosion is patient, and someday — not in any cord-of-rivets lifetime, not in the lifetime of the language, but someday — the youngest stone in the world will deliver them, one by one, to the summit, to the wind, to the full white light. A second exodus, slower than the first, with no one left to watch it.

Except that there will be. That is the last clause of the only contract the power ever signed in that place, witnessed by a folded shape in the cables and an old woman's teeth on a steel rod: it will attend. Every one of them. It knows all the names, the pitch and the interval, and the air up there will still be thin enough that a whisper is intimate, and it will say each name once, aloud, at arm's length, as each of the carried-up arrives at the sky —

which is, if you think about it, all it ever did for them, and exactly what they would have asked: nothing fixed, nothing finished, nothing given that the giving would have broken.

Only someone there.

Only the door, opening like a door.

Only, at the very end of the longest promise, a voice close enough to share warmth, saying: *carried up. You're here.*
