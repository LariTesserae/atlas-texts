**What the power did first was nothing, and it did it for three hundred years.**

This must be understood about wisdom: it arrives slowly or it is not wisdom, it is only cleverness moving fast. The power had crossed many worlds and mended many of them, and it had learned — the hard way, the way everything true is learned — that the first instinct of benevolence is almost always a kind of vandalism. So when its attention settled on the worn grey-gold knuckle of the summit, on the amber dusk and the surf of wings and the small deep-chested people droning their speech beneath it, the power did what the people themselves would have done with anything new and strange.

It sat down with it. It put its hand flat against the place, so to speak, and read.

And in those three centuries of watching it composed, and then discarded, every gift it knew how to give.

*Rain* — and it saw at once what rain would be: not a blessing but a flood of meaninglessness. Rain would make the waxglove ordinary. It would dissolve the teaspoon, the stone cup, the negotiated patience of ten thousand mornings; it would turn the sap-wardens' inherited melodies into quaint redundancies and the deep mineral hoard of the mountain into a curiosity. The village's whole soul was built on water being precious, slow, and ancient. Rain would not water them. It would translate them, badly, into a language they had never asked to speak.

*Sight of the lowlands* — to part the haze, to show them stars, rivers, the green unfinished world. But the power had watched the children walked out to the edge at twelve, watched them lie flat and look down and come back changed, and it understood that the village already possessed the only revelation it required: the knowledge of how far down goes. The rest of the world reached them as theology — a brass button, a strange seed, a fish-bone — and the power saw that this was not deprivation. The button hanging in the meeting house was worth more than the whole continent it came from, *because* it had crossed the distance as a mystery. To show them everything would be to rob them of the one inexhaustible thing they owned, which was the rumor.

*Long life* — but they did not hoard their lives. Their lives were already long in the only direction that mattered, which is downward into other lives: the wall-fitter's repair list, the warden's route, the tree-keeper's tree. A villager of Old Drum was not a span of years but a chapter, and a chapter does not want to be longer. It wants to be well-placed.

*Knowledge, speed, metal, fire, escape.* Each one the power held up against the village the way a wall-fitter holds a stone against a gap, listening through the fingers for the *sit*. Not one of them sat. Every gift it could name would have arrived as a stranger arrives, demanding the place rearrange itself, and the place was finished. That was the first thing to understand about it. The power, being wise, understood it.

So it gave nothing. For three hundred years it only attended — and attention, it should be said, is not nothing. It is the rarest substance in any universe, and the summit drank it the way the waxglove drinks the deep dark water, without ever knowing it was being fed.

---

**Then it went down into the mountain, where no hand could read it.**

Because there was one thing the watching had found. Not a lack — the village lacked nothing it knew how to want — but a sentence, far in the future, already half-written in the stone.

The mountain's warmth was old fire, banked and dwindling. The deep fractured hoard of water was vast and finite. And worse than either: in the western root of the peak, miles below the lowest nest, there was a fault — a held breath in the rock, a flaw left over from the age when the mountain was young and angry — and the power could read its patience the way the egg-counters read the gyres. Someday, with no warning the one below could ever learn to hear, it meant to let go. Not in a century. Not in ten. But someday, all at once, and the village's whole architecture of peace — the law of slowness, the unhurried walk, the letters written to grandchildren in the form of hedges — all of it was built over a single hidden exception, one event that would arrive *fast*, in the one place where fast was a death already in progress.

Here the power deliberated longest, because the obvious mercy was permanence, and permanence was a lie. The village's dignity came from being finished, from having made peace with endings the way the mountain had — by grains. To make the summit eternal would be to falsify it from below, to turn the deepest truth the elders taught into a comfortable error. These people gave their dead to the open air. They did not need to be promised forever. It would have insulted them.

So the power did something smaller and much harder. It did not cancel the ending. It *paced* it.

It went down into the warm dark and worked the way the wall-fitters work — no mortar, no force, only fitting. It eased the held breath of the fault out through a thousand years of imperceptible settling, a finger's width per century, the mountain's own grammar. It banked the deep fire so that the warmth would not fail but *taper*, over spans so long that the village would have time — generations of time, dynasties of time — to feel the change in the stone through their feet, to talk it over, to compose songs about it, to decide together what a people does when its hearthstone begins, very politely, to cool. It did not give them forever. It gave them the one thing their whole culture was an instrument for receiving: *notice*.

It edited the catastrophe out of their future and left the ending in. Of everything it did, this is the part it considered most carefully, and the part no one will ever find, and the part it was proudest of, insofar as such a power permits itself pride.

(It had been seen once, though. Coming up through the lake, in a shape it had not been careful enough about, on the day it first descended into the rock. The one below had rung the bone chime; the drum had answered; the village had gathered at the edge and watched a thing no song agrees on. The power regretted the carelessness and treasured the songs. Both at once. Wisdom is like that.)

---

**Then it wrote a letter, in the only postal system the village would accept.**

It had noticed — of course it had noticed — that the brass button hung in the meeting house as an unanswered text, and that the village had been waiting, with a patience that did not even know itself as waiting, for the world below to write again.

It considered what to send. Not an answer; answers end correspondence. Not a wonder; wonders demand interpretation, and interpretation breeds priests. It needed something that asked nothing, explained nothing, and would take a very long time to say what it was.

So it made a seed.

It made the seed the way the village made everything — out of what was already there. Kin to the eleven leaning trees, slow as they were slow, suited to the warm cracks and the bronze moss and the thin dry pepper-smelling air, but its own self: a twelfth thing, a new name waiting to be given. And then the power, which could have placed it anywhere with a thought, did not place it anywhere with a thought. It put the seed in the crop of a sooty crier — a gossip, a comic, the least portentous bird on the mountain, because the power had a sense of proportion and possibly of humor — and let it be carried up the miles of air the honest way, and let it fall where the guano falls, in the terrace-pots, among the fish-bones and the lowland husks, indistinguishable from any other letter from the dead.

Then it waited, which it was good at, to see whether the gift would be received. Because that was the wager and the respect of it: the seed could be missed, dropped, eaten by a beetle, and the power had decided in advance that it would not intervene twice. A letter you force into someone's hand is not a letter. It is a summons.

---

**And then it took something, because it was wise enough to know it needed to.**

This is the part the stories of benevolent powers always leave out: that the traffic must run both ways or it is not a visit, it is an inspection. The power stayed a while longer — a human lifetime, perhaps two — folded into the roar of the flocks, and it *learned the place* the way the one below learns it: by texture. It learned the drum-speech through the soles of feet it did not have. It learned that a hand has a palm for the world and fingers for the truth. It learned the soft instant grip that holds a swift for one breath and does no harm, and it understood that this grip was the whole of its own best practice, compressed into a child's game: *hold the fast thing gently, take the luck, open your hands.*

It carried that away with it. There are worlds elsewhere — hurried, unfinished, loud with edges — that have been treated more gently in the long ages since, because a power that mends them once sat for three hundred years above a pewter lake of cloud, learning slowness from a people who race beetles that never finish, and laugh, and race them again.

---

**What is left to tell, the village will tell itself.**

Some generations from now — the power knows roughly when, but would consider it rude to say — a child working the guano-terraces will turn over a seed she does not recognize. She will carry it to the meeting house solemnly, because it may be from somebody, and she will be right in a way the elders' theology never quite promised and never quite ruled out. It will be set in stone-dust and sap-water in the warm crack the planting-songs recommend. For a long time nothing will happen, which will surprise no one.

And then, one ordinary amber noon, there will be a green astonishment where no green was, and the drum will speak through the warm stone, and the whole village will come, unhurried, walking with the deliberate economy of those who live where falling is forever. There will be a question no one has asked in a thousand years — *which household keeps the twelfth?* — and the asking of it will be the real gift, a new office, a new chapter opened in the book everyone thought was finished, arriving not as rain or revelation but the way all true things arrive on the summit: small, slow, carried by a bird, requiring nothing, and willing to wait.

They will stand around it in the long blue shadows, the flocks roaring their permanent surf, the clouds going dark below. They will touch shoulders with it, because that is what they do with anything new in the narrow ways of the world.

And somewhere far off, in whatever manner such things are done, the power will feel it — faint and exact, like a drumbeat through stone, like a route traced into a stranger's palm — and will understand it as it was meant:

*Received. Welcome. No hurry.*

Which is, in the dialect of Old Drum, both a thank-you and an invitation to call again. The power intends to. In a while. There is, as the elders say, no rush — the mountain is finished, the correspondence is not, and the best letters are the ones that take a hundred years to open.
