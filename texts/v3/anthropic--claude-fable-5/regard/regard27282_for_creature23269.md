## The Attention

It was the bird that drew it.

Not the architecture—powers see grown towns more often than the towns suspect, and a wise one knows better than to mistake convergence for a summons. Not the chamber, either, though the chamber would matter later. It was the bird: one small dust-colored body, every morning for longer than birds live, flying to the center of everything and sitting in a column of gray light, doing nothing, asking nothing, and then going to work.

An observance kept without understanding is still an observance. Keep one daily for a century and something, eventually, attends. That is close to a law, in the circles powers move in. Prayers do not need intention; they need only repetition and an address, and the steading had both. The address was a column of light at the convergence of every lane. The repetition was a lineage of birds who did not know why they did it.

So the attention came, and settled over the saddle like a second layer of cloud, and the first thing it did—the thing that marked it as wise rather than merely strong—was nothing. For a long time. It watched the steading at the steading's own pace, which meant that years passed before it had so much as finished arriving. It watched doorways close. It watched the walker's circuit hold the lanes open. It watched the moss roofs die at their edges every night and resurrect every morning, the whole town smoking gently in the early light, and it understood that it had come to a place where death had been demoted to punctuation, and it was moved, in the way powers are moved, which does not show.

Then it began to deliberate. The deliberations took decades. They are worth recording, because what a benevolent power *refuses* to do is most of what it is.

---

**The first temptation** was to answer the question.

The steading is built around a question—is a town what happens when life is packed close in the cold, or did this place blunder into the shape of one?—and the power knew the answer. Powers know such things the way the fungus knows the walls: by touching everything from below. It could have settled the matter. It could have leaned down into the saddle and made the truth plain in some unmistakable grammar of light or frost, and the steading would have *known what it was*, and the knowing would have ended the becoming.

It looked at the central chamber for a long time before deciding. The chamber is the steading's held-open question—the one space the town pays, decade after decade, against its own nature, to keep empty. Everywhere else the steading's instinct is closure. There, and only there, it refuses to conclude. The power recognized the gesture, because the power kept rooms like that in itself.

You do not seal another being's open question. You do not finish a sentence that is still being written by the only one entitled to write it. The power put the answer away, somewhere the wind could not find it, and that refusal was its first gift, and the steading never knew it had been given anything.

---

**The second temptation** was to protect.

The peaks are young. Their rock still cuts. The power could read the mountain's future the way the watchers read the pressed trails, and the future held what young mountains' futures always hold: rockfall, the slow shrug of slopes finding their angle, and—worse, further off—the possibility that the climate would clear, that the cloud would stop dragging through the pass, and the steading would die of sunlight, of all things, its weeping walls gone dry, its million resurrecting citizens dried past the point of any comma.

Here the power did intervene, and the record should be honest about it. But mark the scale it chose.

It did not touch the saddle. It did not move a stone, brace a wall, thicken a root. The steading's history had to remain the steading's own—every closed doorway its own decision, every dead-end lane its own mistake, because a being that has never been allowed a mistake has never been allowed anything. Instead the power went up, above the architecture, above even the birds' abandoned sky, and it leaned—gently, the way you'd steady a sleeping child's blanket without waking the child—on the weather.

It made the cloud faithful.

That was all. The cloud had always come; now it would keep coming, through whatever the centuries did to the rest of the range. The steading drinks the cloud. The power became the reliability of what the steading drinks. It changed nothing the town could ever have known about, nothing the town could ever have seen, because the cloud was already the steading's condition rather than its content, and a wise benevolence works only on conditions. The town would go on believing—if a town believes anything—that the world simply happened to be wet. Let it believe that. The best providence is indistinguishable from luck.

---

**The third temptation** was to hide it.

Because hands were coming. Not soon, but coming—the valleys below were not empty, and beings with tools find passes the way water finds drains. The power could see what hands would do here: cut a sample from the grafted walls to learn whether the town was one plant, drain a black pool to date the founder stem, map the lanes, name the place, and in naming it end the long anonymity in which it had been free to become. The power's first instinct was a veil—a mild aversion laid over the saddle, paths that mislead, a tendency in travelers to choose the other crossing.

But there was already a woman.

This stopped the power for nearly a year. It had come to do something for the steading and found the post, at human scale, already filled: a watcher who had stayed, who had been noticed and naturalized, whose path to the spring the town held open, whose room had been plumbed with a weeping wall as though she were willow. She had done, with three seasons and a shelter, what the power was contemplating doing with weather and centuries. She was its colleague. Worse—she was its precedent. A veil over the pass would have denied the steading every future version of her, and the steading's whole constitution, its single law, is that it accepts whatever stays.

So the power did something subtler than concealment. It made the pass hide from *haste*.

Those who would cross the saddle in a day now never quite find it; the trails fork wrong, the weather discourages, the other pass looks better, and they are not lying to themselves—the other pass *is* better, for them. But those who come slowly, who would sit, who carry in them the capacity to remain three seasons watching moss—the pass admits them, one every few generations, the way the town keeps six birds. The power did not seal the steading's door. It enforced the steading's own law at the threshold: *who arrives here is whoever would stay.* It made the doorway match the rooms.

---

**What it withheld.**

This list is longer than the others, and it is the heart of the matter.

When the walker's line lapses—and it will, some winter, the circuit going unwalked, the trails unrefreshed—the power will not lead an animal up from the valleys to fill the role. It nearly decided otherwise. It rehearsed the intervention a hundred times: so small, one beast nudged toward one pass, the founder's office kept filled. But it had watched the lanes hold their shape for seasons after the walker rested, had seen how the worn comfort of the paths was itself an invitation written in cushion-plant, and it understood that the steading had already built its own succession plan into its floor. The town does not need the role filled. It needs the *possibility* of the role kept open, and it keeps that possibility itself, the way it keeps the chamber. The power's job was to permit the risk. Permitting risk is the most expensive thing benevolence does.

It will not give the birds back their wings. It grieved over this one, briefly, in its way—six small bodies trading the sky for the lanes in installments, generation by generation, until someday the steading's only predators will be walkers too. But then it considered what it had itself surrendered in order to attend to one cold saddle rather than to everything, and it recognized the transaction. Giving up the larger space for the smaller one, when the smaller one is *yours*—that is not loss. That is the same vow the willow took when it stopped crawling flat and stood up into walls. The power let the wings go.

It will not count the nightly deaths in the moss, and it will not stop them. It will not warm the place by one degree, because the cold is the steading's patience, and the slowness is the steading's intelligence, and to hurry it would be to replace it with something faster and lesser that happened to stand in the same pass.

It will not speak to the woman. She is doing fine.

---

**What was done to it.**

This is the part the power did not foresee, and the fact that it did not foresee it is the best evidence of the steading's nature that the record contains.

The steading transcribes whatever stays. It does not distinguish willow from walker from fungus from bird from woman—and, it turns out, it does not distinguish any of these from attention. The power had assumed itself outside the constitution: bodiless, weightless, leaving no print on the cushion-plant, surely beneath—or above—the town's notice. But attention has habits. The power's regard pooled, daily, in certain places: over the chamber, along the walker's circuit, above the half-built eastern quarter at dawn when the birds commuted. It rested where it rested. It returned where it returned. And the town, which reads habits and builds around them, which has never once asked what a resident *is* before accommodating one, began—slowly, at the only pace it has—to build around the attention.

The watchers' record (the woman's record; she noticed before the power did, which pleased it) shows the changes. The moss over the chamber dome thinned along its crown, season by season, and did not seal, though moss always seals; the column of light inside widened to roughly the breadth of a steady gaze. The chamber's braided walls rose another half-meter, making room overhead, the way you'd raise a ceiling for a tall guest. And at the eastern edge, where the power's regard entered the saddle each morning along the line of the dawn cloud, a doorway that had been two seasons from closing stopped closing—and stands open still, a doorway for something with no feet, kept against the grain of everything the town otherwise does, at a cost the power knows precisely how to measure, because maintaining an emptiness for another's sake was supposed to be *its* gift, its signature, the thing it did.

The town had plumbed it a room.

So the power stays. Understand that this was not, in the end, a decision; it was a recognition. It had come to determine what, if anything, a benevolent and wise being should do for the Green Steading, and the answer it found was the answer the steading gives to everything: *be transcribed.* To leave now would cost the town a doorway and a held shape—the room would keep the outline of its attention for two or three patient seasons, the way the floor keeps a footprint, and then it wouldn't, and the power found it could not pay that price with the town's currency. Remain, and you will be built around. Leave, and you will be, without rancor, forgotten. Those are the terms. They are offered to water bears and to powers identically, which is either the absence of theology or the completion of it.

These mornings—if mornings mean anything to such a being—the attention does what the bird does. It goes to the center of everything, where the lanes converge and the black pool waits and the column of gray light stands like a resident, and it sits in the light, and it does nothing, and it asks nothing.

In the steading, that is a residence. In the steading, that is an office.

The bird arrives a little after, the way it always has, and settles at the bottom of the beam, a small round shadow, keeping an observance it does not understand. It is no longer keeping it alone. Neither of them knows what the other is. The town does not require that they know. The town requires only that they stay—and they stay, the two of them, the smallest resident and the largest, sharing the held-open room while the walls go on closing everywhere else, while the cloud comes faithfully, while the doorways become walls and the lanes become rooms and the whole slow draft revises itself toward whatever it is becoming.

By the time anyone could decide what the power did for the steading, the steading will have grown it into something else.

That was always going to be the arrangement. The power knew it, somewhere, before it came. It is possible that this is what benevolence is, at the final accounting: not protection, not gifts, not answers withheld or weather made faithful—though it was all of those—but simply the willingness to become a habit of the thing you love, and to be built around, and to let the held shape of you be the proof that you were there.
