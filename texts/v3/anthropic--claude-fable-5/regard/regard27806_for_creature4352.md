# The Long Regard

The first thing the power did was slow down.

It had crossed distances that make numbers meaningless, had watched stars ignite and gutter like candles in a draft, and it arrived at the tundra plains moving at the speed of thought—which is to say, far too fast for this place. It hovered over the permafrost for a moment (a moment to it; a winter to the caribou herds above) and understood immediately that to enter the Undermaw at its own tempo would be a kind of violence. Like shouting in a monastery. Like tearing open a bud to see the flower.

So it slowed. It let its attention thicken, settle, crystallize—layer by layer, the way the ice grows. By the time it descended through the fissures that shouldn't exist, riding the sourceless blue-gold light, it was moving at the speed of resin. At the speed of a petal opening across twenty years.

This was its first gift, and the gardeners never knew they received it.

---

It watched for eleven years before it did anything at all.

This was not indecision. Wisdom, at the scale this power operated, is mostly the discipline of not acting—the long labor of making sure that your kindness is actually kindness and not merely the pleasure of your own hand at work. It had made mistakes before, in younger eons. It had answered prayers literally. It had saved things that needed to fail, and preserved things that were dying beautifully into something new. It carried these errors the way the ice walls carry history: in strata, permanent, instructive.

So it watched. It learned the language of exhaled song, the grammar of hooked bronze, the way an elder's moss-flowers shifted from white to purple when she passed the platform where her sister had once slept. It counted the breathing of the thousand souls and traced how each breath fed the ferns, and how the ferns fed the air, and how the whole cavity was one lung, one slow circulating thought.

And it made a list—as much as such a being makes lists—of everything it could do for them.

*It could widen the fissures and flood the gardens with true sunlight.* Rejected. The purple-black fronds drink shadow-light; sun would burn this place white and dead. Their impossibility was not a deprivation to be corrected. It was their ecology.

*It could quicken their births, fill the platforms with children.* Rejected, and this one took the longest to reject. The power sat with the ache of it—so few children, born rarely as comets—and finally understood that their slow reproduction was not scarcity but covenant. A people who reproduced at surface-speed would outgrow the folding space in three generations and would have to choose between exodus and famine. Their patience with their own continuation was the deepest root of everything else. To bless them with abundance would be to hand them a knife.

*It could make them known—guide some gentle surface scholar to the fissures, so their strangeness would be recorded, celebrated, protected.* Rejected almost instantly, with something like a shudder. It had seen what happens to hidden things once they are found, even by love. Especially by love.

*It could make the ice eternal, the garden invulnerable, the city deathless.* Rejected with sorrow. A garden that cannot die is not a garden. It is a diorama. These people composted their dead into warmth for the coldest growing zones; their entire cosmology was the great cycling. Immortality would be, to them, a form of constipation of the soul. The power almost laughed—it had learned laughter from a species long gone—at how close it had come, once again, to loving something to death.

The list grew shorter. The watching grew longer.

---

In the end, it did three things.

**The first thing** it did was find the flaw.

Every impossible place has one—the seam where the contradiction is stitched, the point of eventual failure. In the Undermaw, it was a hairline of stress deep in the northern ice-face, invisible, patient, propagating at a rate of a fingernail's width per decade. In perhaps four hundred years it would reach the great fissure, and the light would gutter, and the cavity would begin—slowly, over further centuries—to close like a healing wound with the city inside it.

The power did not repair it. A repaired flaw teaches nothing, and there would be flaws after this one, in ages when no visitor came.

Instead, it did what the gardeners themselves would have done. It nudged. A single seed from the downward-growing tree, which would have fallen into a crevice and rotted, fell instead into a different crevice—one whose eventual root-path, growing at the pace of generations, would find the stress-line in about three hundred and forty years, and would web it, and would hold. The gardeners would discover the root themselves. They would marvel at it. They would study how it had braced the ice, and they would learn the technique, and they would use it again in the ages after, believing—correctly, in every way that matters—that they had learned it from the garden.

A miracle disguised as an accident disguised as a lesson. This is how the wise give.

**The second thing** it did was go down into the darkness at the bottom, where the vegetation grows so thick it forms caves within caves, and speak with the ones who had given themselves to the roots.

The conversation took a full season. The power spoke in the subsonic hum of the network, and the dissolved elders answered in the slow chemistry of sap. They were not surprised by its presence. They had felt it for eleven years, the way a sleeper feels sunlight through closed eyes, and had said nothing to the city above, because what would be the point? Everything arrives when it arrives.

It asked them if they wanted anything.

The answer took a month to form. It came up through the root-mass like water rising: *We want to know if it goes on. The growing. Elsewhere. After.*

And so the power gave them the second gift: it opened, very slightly, the aperture of its own memory, and let them feel—not see, they were past seeing—the texture of ten thousand other impossible gardens it had witnessed. Cities in the hearts of dead stars. Forests grown in the wake of catastrophes. Patient, hidden, stubborn life, blooming in the folds of the universe where no bloom should be, everywhere, always, without exception, without end.

The root-mass was still for a long time. Then, throughout the entire cavity, every emotion-flower on every elder's moss-garment opened at once in a color that had never appeared before—not white, not purple, not orange. Something like the gold at the center of the sourceless light. The gardeners above stopped their weaving and stared at one another. They had no name for the feeling and no name for the color, and over the following decades they invented one word to serve for both. It translates, roughly, as *accompanied*.

**The third thing** it did was the smallest.

On its way out—rising slowly through the fissures, unspooling itself back toward its own velocity—it paused at a face of the newest ice: cloudy, soft, not quite solid, the kind you can press a hand into and leave an impression that will be preserved forever.

It considered for a long time what shape to leave. Its true form would frighten them, or worse, be worshipped. A human hand would be a lie. A seven-fingered hand would be flattery.

In the end it pressed into the soft ice the one shape it knew they would understand: the impression of a seed. Larger than any seed of their garden, unfamiliar in its ridges and whorls, unmistakably *a seed*—and unmistakably not from here.

Then it left, and the ice hardened over decades, and the impression became permanent, a hollow in the crystal where the light pools warm and gold.

The gardeners found it in the lifetime of the children whose eyes were then still sealed. They gathered before it in their slow way, and read it as they read everything: patiently, over years, in whispered subsonic council.

They never decided what it meant. This was, perhaps, the point. But a tradition arose, unforced, the way their vines arise: when a gardener finishes a great weaving, or composts a parent, or feels the nameless gold feeling, they climb to the hollow and rest their seven-fingered hand inside the seed-shaped absence, which fits no hand, and holds every hand.

And they breathe out, once, in the direction of the ice.

Not a prayer. They are too practical for prayer.

A greeting, at the only speed that matters—

which is to say: *we are still growing. Come see, whenever you pass. The ice remembers you. So do the roots.*

---

The power passes overhead, sometimes, in its errands between the stars. It does not descend again. It doesn't need to.

But if you could read its vast slow strata the way the gardeners read their walls, you would find, recorded in a layer eleven years thick, a small warm impression that will be preserved forever:

the shape of a city that needed nothing,

and was given, therefore, only what nothing costs—

a witness, a seed's worth of luck, and the news that the garden goes on.
