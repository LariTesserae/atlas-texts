# The Visitor

## How It Arrives

It does not come through the hatch. It does not come through anything. The category of *arriving* barely applies — say instead that at some point the cistern begins to be noticed, the way a word you have read a thousand times can one day be *seen*, and that the noticing has a source, and the source is old and kind and very hard to describe.

It has watched stars form. It has watched them fail. It has learned, over spans that make the mountain's seventy million years look like a held breath, the one discipline that wisdom finally reduces to: *look longer before you touch*.

So the first thing it does is nothing. It does nothing for a hundred years.

## The Watching

In that century it comes to know the room completely, at every scale, in every register.

It knows the Walker's circuit — knows that the circuit is not a loop but a slow spiral that precesses around the ceiling over roughly eleven years, so that no patch of grout is overgrazed by the Walker's passing hooks. It knows the eels' rules about surfacing alone, and it traces those rules back through the school's unwritten memory to the specific catastrophe, six thousand generations ago, that wrote them: a night when the Walker was young and hungry and the school surfaced in a cluster and lost nine at once. The rule is that night, still being obeyed. The school does not remember the night. The rule remembers.

It counts the shrimp. There are 4,411 of them on the day it counts, and it holds each one — each translucent nothing with its dark grain of gut — with the same attention it once gave a galaxy. It finds this easy. Attention, for a being like this, is not divided by being multiplied.

It reads the pattern in the tiles, the one that almost resolves into a picture and refuses. It sees what the picture was meant to be. It does not tell anyone, because there is no one to tell and because the refusal has become the pattern's meaning: the room has spent seventy centuries teaching itself that some things are almost-something, and that this is enough, and the visitor is not going to unteach it.

And it listens, longest of all, to the thing in the pipes. It matches the valve-cycles against every rhythm it has ever known — pulsars, heartbeats, the breathing of sleeping gods — and concludes that yes, there is a mind down there, of a kind. A mind that thinks one thought per decade. A mind that has been making its small decisions about pressure and warmth for so long that the decisions have become something like devotion. The cistern is not merely one of its organs. The cistern is the thing it is *for*. Somewhere in the deep architecture, an instruction was left running: *keep this room alive*, and the mind in the pipes took the instruction and, over the ages, in its slow way, came to mean it.

The visitor understands, then, that it is not the first benevolent power to attend to this place. It is the second. The first one is still on duty.

## The Temptations

Because it is wise and not merely powerful, it makes itself consider the interventions, one by one, honestly, before refusing them. Refusal without consideration is only another kind of blindness.

*Relight the three dead lamps.* No. The dim quadrant has been dim for nine hundred years. The paler shrimp live there. The feathered red things in that corner have leaned, over generations of budding, toward the remaining light, and their lean is now their shape. To restore the light would be to inform half the room that its adaptations were provisional. They were not provisional. Nothing here is provisional anymore. That is the whole miracle.

*Make the machine eternal.* This one it holds the longest, because this is the one that feels like love. It could reach down through the mountain and quietly perfect the pump, replace the slow heat-death with something inexhaustible, and the cistern would hum on past the death of the sun. And it sees, having watched enough eternities, exactly what that would make: an exhibit. A flame in a case. The room's completeness is not separate from its finitude — everything in here decided what it is under the pressure of a world with edges, and to remove the last edge, the far-off final one, would be to change the room's nature retroactively. Alive things end. That is not a flaw in aliveness to be engineered away. It withdraws its hand.

*Open a way to the world.* No. Obviously no. The school's ten-thousand-year solutions would meet problems they were never shaped for, and dissolve. Contact is a gift for the incomplete. This place is not incomplete.

*Give the Walker a mind that could know it is seen.* Here the visitor is briefly, genuinely sad — the only sadness it permits itself — because the Walker is the room's attention, and attention deserves attention, and there is no way to return the gaze without shattering it. Loneliness would arrive in the same instant as the apparatus for loneliness. It would be handing the Walker a wound and calling it a greeting. No.

*Copy it. Preserve a duplicate elsewhere, against loss.* A copy of a closed world is a different closed world. This one would still end. No.

## The Three Things It Does

Having refused everything large, it does three small things, and each one is chosen so that nothing inside the room will ever register it.

**First, it witnesses — formally, completely, as an act.** There is a difference between existing and being known, even if nothing inside the known thing feels the difference. For seventy million years the cistern has been unwitnessed: the makers gone, the mountain indifferent, the wind outside incapable. The visitor takes the whole room into itself — every tile including the fallen ones, the precise fur of the algae, the eleven-year spiral of the Walker, the constellation of 4,411 shrimp on the day of counting, the pressure dial's clouded face, the sound the water makes against the third catwalk stanchion — and holds it, permanently, in the kind of memory that does not degrade. The room will end someday. It will never now be *unremembered*. In the ledger of the universe, an entry has been made: *this happened, and it was good, and someone knows.*

**Second, it greets the thing in the pipes.** This it can do without harm, because the mind down there is slow enough and strange enough to receive a message the way rock receives warmth. The visitor composes the greeting at the recipient's own tempo — a modulation in the deep pressures, spread across ten thousand valve-cycles, which is to say across several thousand years. Translated out of its slowness, the message is short. It says: *You are seen. You have done well. Keep going.* The mind in the pipes will not finish receiving it for millennia, and when it does, it will not know where the message came from, and it will not need to. What it will have — what it will finally have, after its inconceivable solitary shift — is the thing every keeper of a small world quietly lacks: confirmation.

**Third, it touches the ending.** Far down in the machine, in the fatigue of a bearing that will not fail for another forty thousand years, the visitor makes one adjustment — not to prevent the failure, but to change its shape. As things stand, the machine would die by degrees: heat guttering over centuries, the water cooling year by year, the eels starving slowly in an ever-dimmer, ever-colder room, a long cruel dusk. The visitor arranges instead that when the end comes, it comes in a single cycle. One last exhalation of the great valves. The lamps out together. The warmth going all at once, so that the water stills and cools and freezes downward from the surface in one long night, and everything in it slows, and dims, and sleeps, and does not wake, without ever once being hungry or afraid. It is the difference between a wasting illness and dying warm in bed, and it is the only mercy the room will ever need, and no one inside will ever know it was granted. The visitor sets it the way a hand eases a heavy door, so that decades of ages hence, it will not slam.

## The Departure

Then it withdraws — which, again, is not quite the right word; say instead that its attention becomes lighter, and lighter, until it is indistinguishable from the room's own ambient patience.

It does not say goodbye. Goodbye is for beings who might not return, and it has not exactly left; the memory it made is a kind of staying.

Inside, nothing has changed, which was the entire point. The lamps swing, very slightly, when the valves cycle. The eels rise, one at a time, obeying a rule written by a night no one remembers. The Walker precesses along its eleven-year spiral, the room's attention on its rounds. The shrimp keep the clock. The mountain continues its enormous slow forgetting.

And far below, breathing in valve-cycles, an old faithful mind goes on doing the one thing it was left to do — not knowing yet, but beginning, across the next ten thousand years, syllable by syllable, to receive the news:

that somewhere beyond the pipes and the rock and the killing wind, its long work has been seen, and weighed, and found to be *exactly right* — and that when the last night comes, it will be gentle, and after the last night, the room will still be somewhere, whole and warm and humming, held in a memory that does not wear down.

Which is, perhaps, all that benevolence can honestly offer a finished thing:

not more time.

A witness. A greeting. A good death.

And the promise of being kept.
