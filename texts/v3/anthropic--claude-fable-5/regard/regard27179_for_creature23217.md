**What the Power Did**

It arrived the way light arrives down the oculus: pre-softened. Powers of its kind do not appear so much as *attend*, and for a long while — a swift-year, dusk to dusk to dusk — attending was all it did. It stood, insofar as such a thing stands, in the center of the floor where the keeper stands to listen, and it read the chamber the way the keeper reads the chord, except that it could hear all the chords at once: every clap ever clapped, every wingbeat since the first pigeon found the first niche, the original singers pressing their fists into wet clay with their pitches still warm in them. It heard the dome the way you hear a single sustained note that turns out, on attention, to be a choir.

And its first impulse — because it was benevolent, and benevolence has reflexes — was to *give*.

It considered the gifts in order, the way a careful person lays tools out on a cloth before deciding which to pick up.

It considered permanence. One gesture and the dome would never crack, never weep, never need a patch again; the fig question would be answered forever; the keeper's working life would empty of half its work. It set this gift down almost at once, the way you set down a knife you realize is pointed at something you love. The dome was alive precisely because it died a little, constantly, and was mended. The patch-scabs were not damage. They were the conversation. A dome that never needed tending would, within three keepers' lifetimes, have no keeper — and a keeper, the power understood by now, was not staff. A keeper was an organ.

It considered the earthquake. There is one — powers can see these things — some centuries out, sleeping in the rock the way the bats sleep in the low courses. It looked at the earthquake for a long time. Then it looked at the dome: hand-coiled, corners gone round, mortar grown into brick, every rigid line surrendered ages ago — a structure that had spent its whole existence learning to flex. And the power saw what the dome would do when the ground rolled: it would *ring*. Every niche sounding at once, the whole instrument struck like a bell, shedding dust, shedding a patch or two, holding. It had been built by hands, not levers; it would move like a body, not a machine. The power did nothing about the earthquake. It only made a note to be present for it, the way one makes a note to attend a performance.

It considered the fig. Here it permitted itself one indulgence: it looked forward, far forward, to see how the argument among the keepers came out. And it saw the fig, eight or nine keepers from now, finally too strong to prune — and it saw the dome do what the older keepers had predicted, what the dome had always done. Absorb it. Roots threading the courses like new circulation, the trunk rising through the oculus, the chimney of brick becoming a collar around a living tree, the canopy above ground shading the hole and feeding the floor with figs and leaf-fall, the whole structure trading a little of its light for a great deal of company. Birds in the branches above; birds in the niches below; the tree a second dome, grown instead of built, the two of them holding each other up. The power smiled — insofar as such a thing smiles — and told no one, and tipped nothing. A foreseen ending is not a gift. It is a theft of suspense from beings who run on seasons.

It considered the keeper. This was the hardest one to put down. The keeper's loneliness was not loneliness, exactly — the arithmetic came out as company, as it does in the ones who last — but the keeper was mortal in a chamber full of turnover, and even a person at peace with continuity has nights. The power stood near the sleeping shelf on the second turning, on one of those nights, and weighed the gifts: longer life, lighter dreams, a learner sent early. It chose the smallest. It went down past the stair to the low deep niches behind it, the ones pressed bigger than a fist, the ones not discussed, where the bats roost thickest — and it listened there, properly, with everything it had.

They were not silent.

Not voices. Nothing so crude. But the niches that hold the keepers are tuned like all the others, and they had been stopped for centuries with what they held, and a stopped pipe is not a dead pipe — it is a pipe waiting. The power heard, under the seethe and tick of the sleeping bats, the held pitches of every keeper the wall had taken: each one a note pressed into the chamber's chord so low and so long that the living chord was built on them without knowing it, the way a melody is built on a drone. The keepers were the fundamental. The whole dome had been singing over them all along.

So the gift it gave the keeper was this: a dream, once, plainly delivered — the chamber heard from inside the wall. The chord from the other side. The keeper woke before dawn, lay still on the cupped shelf, listened to the dome breathing overhead, and understood — not as poetry, the way visitors understand things, but plainly, the way keepers prefer — that the post had no last day. That the work continued downward. The keeper never mentioned the dream, which is how the power knew it had been received correctly.

It considered, last of all, doing nothing whatsoever — the gift the wisest powers give most often, and the one the Dovewell had frankly earned. The place had spent uncounted centuries absorbing everything that arrived in it: pigeons, swifts, bats, a snake, a fungus, a sapling with plans, a procession of small deliberate humans. It would absorb a benevolent power too, or shrug it off, and either way it did not *need* one. The household was full. The roles were taken.

But the power had been attending for a year by then, twice daily at the exchange of the sky, standing in the braid and the smoke as the two nations traded the chimney, and it had stopped being a visitor somewhere around the third month without noticing the moment it happened — which is, the keepers could have told it, exactly how the Dovewell takes residents.

So it did two things.

The first: it counted. Everything. The niches — there are forty-one thousand, two hundred and six, though no one below will ever know this. The generations of pigeons since the first. The names of the builders, every one, recovered from the pitches their fists left in the clay, because a person pressing a hole while singing leaves more of themselves than a knuckle-mark. The keepers, in order, back to the woman who built the sleeping shelf. The sheddings of every snake. The exact day the swifts first found the oculus. It took the whole count and kept it — not written, not told, simply *held*, the way the dome holds a breath — so that the place which never bothered to count anything is nonetheless counted, and the span of time no one tracks is tracked by exactly one witness, and nothing that happened in the warm dark was ever, even once, unwatched. This is the oldest work of such powers and the least understood: not to change what is, but to make sure that what is, *registers*. That it lands somewhere. The Dovewell now lands somewhere.

The second thing it did took one night.

The keeper had patched the dome that week — a weeping spot in the middle latitudes, fresh clay pressed pale over old, a new scab among the centuries of scabs. The clay was still soft. And in the dark hours, between the swifts' descent and the bats' return, the power borrowed enough of a hand to do the one thing the chamber was built out of: it pressed a single niche into the wet patch, fist-sized, knuckle-marked, and it sang while it pressed — one note, its own, tuned to no human throat — and the wall took it.

The keeper found it the next day. Stood on the cane ladder a long while, fitting a thumb into knuckle-marks that belonged to no hand the keeper knew and no hand the keeper had ever shaken on the stair. Then climbed down, said nothing, and went on with the morning — because keepers know, in the way keepers know things, that the dome has been growing for a very long time, and a thing that grows will sometimes put out a cell of its own.

Within a month a young pair of pigeons had claimed it. Within a year there were squabs in it. It is an ordinary niche now, indistinguishable in the wall, occupied, droppinged, warm — except that when someone stands in the center of the floor and claps, the chord that comes back has one note in it that was never pressed by anything mortal, sounding in perfect tune with all the rest, and not even the keeper can pick it out anymore.

Which was the point.

The power thinned, after that, the way such powers do — went on to its other attendances, leaving behind only a standing fraction of itself, a permanent listener, fist-deep in the wall. It pays its rent the way every resident of the Dovewell pays: by doing one small work the others depend on without anyone having agreed to anything. The pigeons feed the place. The bats clean it. The swifts time it. The keeper mends it.

The power remembers it.

And twice a day, at the exchange of the sky, when the braid descends and the smoke ascends and every empty niche rings under the wingbeats, there is one niche that is never empty and rings anyway — and the household assembles, and the question of who lives there goes on having no useful answer, exactly as before, only now with one more voice declining to make the distinction.
