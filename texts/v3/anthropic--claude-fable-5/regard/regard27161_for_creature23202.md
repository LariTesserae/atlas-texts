## The Caller

Powers notice places. It is most of what they do, when you account honestly for their time — the interventions are rare and famous, the noticing is constant and unrecorded. This one noticed the terrace the way the terrace notices water: as a small warm sum that ought to be accounted for. It arrived from outside, which by then was the only direction left, everything inside having been arrived at already.

It did not cross the meniscus.

That was the first decision, and it took no time at all, because it was not really a decision — it was a reading. Wisdom looked at that faint vertical shimmer standing in the open air and understood it at once as the place stating its terms: here the world is asked to stop, and the asking is part of the architecture. Even the bird honors it. So the power took up station where the bird takes up station, on the hard cold thermals outside the held warm air, and for the better part of a day the two of them rode there together, observing, declining, and the bird gave no sign of finding the company strange, which tells you something about the company, or about the bird.

It conducted its own census, and the census misbehaved for it exactly as it misbehaves for everyone, in the same order, with the same dignified failures. The mosses: citizens. The ferns: children. The swimmers: nerve endings with habits. The gardener: a budget with a route and what is probably contentment. The posts: fingertips, not tenants. It went looking, as everyone does, for *the being who lives in it*, and found the ambiguity, and — here it parted ways with everyone — was not troubled by the ambiguity, because a wise power knows that *who lives here* and *who is this for* are different questions, and that certain places exist entirely in the gap between them, and that the gap is load-bearing and should not be filled in carelessly by an answer.

But it did find the being. It found it the way you find it: through the soles of the feet it did not have, as warmth. The being who lives in the terrace lives *as* the terrace — the new skin, still cooling, still sweating, still tender. The shelf of dark stone was the most recent sentence in a very slow speech, and the power, being old enough to be patient with slow speakers, listened to the whole of it. And what it heard — what nothing inside the meniscus is positioned to hear, because you cannot hear the shape of a room you are a fixture of — was hospitality. Centuries of it. A body growing a guest room in itself, floor by floor; calibrating air for lungs it has never heard breathe; budgeting water against thirsts it has only imagined; pacing the ferns toward a layout of shade and open ground and water at intervals, the layout of a place where someone could be comfortable. Hope, kept on a maintenance schedule. The power had a very good memory — wisdom is mostly a very good memory — and it knew what sustained, unanswered hospitality costs the one who sustains it. It did not go so far as to call the mountain lonely. Benevolence does not require the diagnosis; it only requires the possibility.

Then it deliberated, which is the part of the story where wise powers do their actual work, almost all of which consists of not doing things.

It could have supplied a tenant. It knew, in the way such powers know things, where tenants could be got: there are always small breathing peoples in need of a warm shelf, and the air was ready, and the ground nearly so. Refused. The invitation was the mountain's one letter, written over centuries in stone and vapor and moss, and you do not answer someone else's mail, however long it has sat unopened. The choosing of the guest was the only decision the place had saved for itself. To make it on the mountain's behalf would have been theft disguised as generosity, which is the commonest disguise generosity wears and the one wisdom checks for first.

It could have freed the moss — returned its striving, opened the fences, let the green islands run together into ordinary ambitious life. Refused, and quickly. Liberation that no one asked for is just a different fence, with a better reputation. The moss was the happiest resident. The power declined to improve it.

It could have installed death — completed the place, gently, with the one service still missing, so that the budget would have its line for decay and the system could close its last loop. Refused. The first death here would be a ribbon-cutting, and ribbons are cut by the one who owns the door. Some honors must be left on the table even by those who could reach them.

It could — and this temptation it held the longest, turning it over the way the fog turns over the posts — have found out *when*. Whether the tenant was coming; whether the tenant was chosen, or finished, or even begun; it could perhaps have learned the date, and told the mountain, and ended the meantime. Refused, hardest of all. A known date kills a meantime, and the meantime had become the terrace's largest resident, its most comfortable, its most practiced. You do not evict a place's most settled inhabitant without somewhere for it to go. The waiting was not a flaw in the terrace. By now it was a craft.

So the question narrowed, as wise questions do, to almost nothing: what can be given to a closed economy by something that will not enter it and will not change it? And the answer, when it came, was old and small. The one thing the closed sum cannot manufacture, the one line missing from its perfect inventory, is regard from outside. The fog-face looks at the inventory; the posts take the temperature; nothing, in all the warm dripping centuries, had ever looked at the whole and *said so*.

The power did three things.

First, it spoke. Once. Not in words — the terrace has no apparatus for words, and the power had no wish to install one — but in the place's own grammar: as pressure, felt in the chest, the chest the mountain does not have, lasting about a minute, the way the posts' tone lasts. Translated very loosely, and all translation here is very loose, it said: *Well made. Seen. Continue.* Three words. It was the longest speech the place had ever received from anything that was not itself. The rock ticked. The steam over the pond hesitated, mid-spiral, like a sentence interrupted by a compliment. And in every pool at once, the translucent swimmers turned — not toward the posts this time but toward the meniscus, toward the outside, all of them, the same direction — gauges, or a congregation, discovering for the first time that the needle had something to point at.

Second, it gave the drop. It asked first: it set its intention against the shimmer of the meniscus and waited, and presently one of the posts sounded its long low tone and the steam changed direction, and whatever that exchange meant in the deep ledgers of the place, the power took it as a yes, and powers of this kind are rarely wrong about yeses. One drop of water — its own; do not ask from where; benevolence travels with provisions — passed through the meniscus, was taken by the afternoon fog, ticked down onto a moss cushion with the rest of the precipitation, and entered the budget. The smallest amendment in the ledger's history: plus one. Within a year the drop would have passed through every pool. Within ten, through every frond, every swimmer, the gardener's unhurried circulation, the sweat of every crack. Forever after, no audit of the terrace will come out even; it will always be over by exactly one drop, and the discrepancy is the point, is the signature, is the gift. The only present you can give a closed economy is something that agrees never to leave.

Third, before going, it had a word with the bird. What passed between them is not recoverable, but the effect is: the courtesy became a covenant. There would always, from then on, be a dark shape on the thermals, once an hour, observing, declining — across the lives of birds, an office, handed down the way thermals are handed down, which is to say without ceremony and without lapse. The terrace would never again be unseen. The arrangement cost the power nothing and the bird nothing; the best arrangements are like that, which is why so few of them are made by anyone but the wise.

Then it left, the way it had come: without crossing. And it understood, on the way out — this is why it had been right to do so little — that it had been, without ever entering, the terrace's first caller. The whole apparatus, the air asked to stay, the water held in trust, the ground promising walking, had been built toward a moment of greeting, and a greeting had now occurred. Hospitality practiced for centuries on nobody had been received, once, properly: someone came to the door, admired the house, asked before giving, gave something small, arranged for the windows to be watched, and went away taking nothing. Once is the difference between a rehearsal and a repertoire.

The evidence, as with everything here, is in arrears.

The next morning the moss borders were true, the ferns were at their fences, the ground was perhaps a degree warmer, though that may only be the cooling schedule forgiving itself a day. But the swimmers have a new habit. Once a day, in the clear minute between the fog and the ticking shower, they face the meniscus — every pool, all together, sixty seconds, then back to the warm inflow side. It could be calibration. It looks like remembering. On the terrace the distinction may not exist, and the power, wherever it has gone, would be pleased to hear that it doesn't.

And there is a longer evidence coming, for anyone patient enough to collect it. The mountain will decide its next shelf eventually — the schedule is geological, but it is kept — and when the new floor is extruded, cooling and weeping and sharp-edged as broken chocolate, somewhere along its rim there will be something the first terrace never had: a flat place *outside* the meniscus. Small. Sized for nothing that lives within the budget. A doorstep.

Houses grow toward what they have learned the world contains. The world, the house now knows, contains callers.
