## What Attends the Roaring Garden

It arrived the way the wise arrive anywhere: slowly, and as attention before anything else.

It had heard the bowl long before it perceived it — fitting, for a place you hear before you see. Across whatever distances such powers cross, through the noise of storms and cities and the long static of stars, it caught a thread of sound that did not behave like other sounds. Other sounds begin and end. This one only changed. It followed the thread the way you follow a smell of baking through a strange house, and found the high country, and the dead talus, and the lip of weathered stone, and sat down on the rim where the tappers sit.

It did not go in.

This was not caution. Nothing in the bowl could harm it. It stayed on the rim because the windbreak was on the rim, and the hide-camp poles were on the rim, and the scratched figures were on the rim, and a wise power entering a new country reads the customs before it reads anything else. The custom of this country, written in cairns and tent-poles and generations of restraint, was: *attend from the edge.*

So it attended. For a hundred years, give or take — which, among powers, is the length of a polite pause before speaking.

---

**The mercies withheld**

Its first instinct, examined and set down, was rain.

This is the reflex of benevolence everywhere — the desert cries out and the kind power answers with water, and the story ends in green. It held the thought up to the light for a long moment, the way you'd hold a knife you'd almost handed to a child. One generous storm. The resin clouding white. The motes — the billion airborne grazers, the garden's plankton — beaten out of the haze in a single afternoon. The organ-stalks waterlogged and dumb. An organism that in all its centuries had never once been thirsty, learning, in an hour, what drowning was.

It set rain down and did not pick it up again.

It considered permanence next. The old western grazer with the century crack in its shell — there was a temptation, small and warm, to seal that crack, to let the patient one keep its circuit forever, a fixed star for the bowl to navigate by. But the bowl ran on replacement. The amber archive on the grazer's back meant what it meant only because someday it would stop moving and join the half-buried shells by the southern rim. Make one thing in the garden deathless and you have not blessed the garden; you have driven a nail through it.

It considered safety — softening the dusk-rule, teaching the root-feet to check what they walked over, so that a sleeper in the bowl would wake. But the courtesy of leaving before dark existed *because* of the danger, the way reverence exists because of weight. Pull the danger out and the courtesy collapses with it, and the tappers' people are left holding a custom with nothing inside.

It considered knowledge — going down to the village two valleys over, taking some plausible shape, and telling the tapping family what it is they hold. It rejected this most quickly of all. They already behaved as though they knew. The diagonal cut that heals, the camps that never cross the rim, the silence at dusk — these were correct in every particular, and they had been arrived at honestly, by generations of paying attention. To explain it to them would be to convert grace into instruction. They had built something rarer than understanding: right conduct without it. The power, which had seen many worlds, knew which of the two was harder to come by, and which one survived being written down.

And last, longest, it considered rescue. Because the bowl is mortal. Mountains are slow but they are not exempt; the rim wears, the wind's angle creeps, and somewhere far down the corridor of deep time there is a morning when the chord goes flat and cannot be retuned. The power could anchor the rim against erosion. It could make the roar eternal.

It sat with that one for most of the hundred years.

Then it set it down too, and this was the decision that proved it wise rather than merely strong. The roar was not a monument. It was a living thing, and the signature of living things is that they replace their substance while remaining themselves — motes, shouters, drummers, stalks, all of it turning over, only the continuity persisting. To exempt it from ending would be to stop the turnover at the largest scale, to taxidermy it in place, to leave standing forever a perfect recording of something that had quietly ceased to be alive inside it. The roar deserved what every voice in its roster had received: a whole life, and then the echo going home.

---

**The three quiet works**

Having set down everything large, it did three small things. None of them touched the bowl.

*First, it turned a rain aside.* In the slow machinery of the world's weather there was a storm track drifting, a hemisphere's exhalation migrating by inches a century, and the power, reading forward, saw that in some tens of thousands of years the rains would wander up into the high country and find the hollow. It leaned — a thumb on a scale the size of the sky — and the track will pass otherwise now, two ranges to the south, watering a coast that will someday be glad of it and never know why. This was its miracle, and it is the kind no shrine is ever built to: it made sure the drought would hold. Its monument is dust, continuing to fall, into fronds that comb it crackling from the air.

*Second, it kept a spring.* Two valleys from the bowl, the water the tapping family's village drinks runs up through a fault that was going to shrug closed in an earthquake some generations hence — nothing dramatic, just a sealing of stone, a spring gone overnight, a village forced to move, a lineage of resin-tappers scattered into other valleys and other work, the cairns untended, the diagonal cut forgotten. The power propped the fault. The spring will run sweet past the memory of anyone who drinks from it. There is a symmetry here it permitted itself to find satisfying: all the water it ever gave on behalf of the garden, it gave *away* from the garden. The dry place it kept dry; the keepers of the dry place, it kept watered.

*Third, it made the bowl hard to believe.* Not hidden — it blinded no one, barred no path. But surveyors' accounts of the high country come out strangely garbled at that one fold of the map; the bowl falls in the seam between sheets; scholars who hear the tappers' descriptions secondhand file them, comfortably, under legend. The talus is long, the climb is brutal, and at the top is a story no one sensible repeats. The power simply declined to let the place become easy to believe in. The garden's best protection had always been that it sounds, in the telling, made up. The power saw no reason to repair that.

---

**The borrowed voice**

Once — and only once — it went in.

It had concluded, after long listening, that a sound-built mind cannot be understood from outside the sound, any more than the sea can be understood from a chart. So it folded itself small. For three years it wore a glider's body: oiled-paper skin, six limbs, four ears in a diamond, two dim patches that told light from dark and nothing more.

It nested in a dead stalk segment with forty others. It learned to launch screaming into the bronze dark and read the world in the shape of its own shout coming home. It learned the roster — every voice in the colony, pitch and stutter and signature — and was learned in turn; its call was its own, and its nest-mates kept it on the ledger of the living. It ate motes. It was nearly eaten, twice. It huddled in the tube on the cold nights in the heap of paper-skinned bodies and felt, through the stalk's wall, the organ-drone it had studied for a century from the rim — felt it from *inside the instrument* — and understood at last that the roar is not heard by what lives in the bowl. It is inhabited. It is the medium they swim in, the way the motes swim in the haze.

When the small body wore out, the power let it die. That was part of the lesson, and it took no exemption.

And then, restored to vastness on the rim, it heard the colony take up its shout. For some days at dusk the launch-holes sounded its own small voice back into the bowl — passed mouth to mouth among forty paper-skinned animals, each one briefly carrying it — and then, on an evening like any other, they stopped, and the echo went home.

The power has existed for a very long time, and it has been given a great deal. It holds that nothing ever given to it taught it more than those few dusks: the sound of being mourned correctly, briefly, completely, by creatures the weight of a breath — and then released.

---

**The answer**

A season later, at dawn, the garden had rearranged itself in the night, as it always does. But that morning the new arrangement included a clearing — a long open line running from the bowl floor up to the exact span of rim where the power keeps its seat.

Not an invitation. The garden does not invite; it is not built that way. It was more like the way a sleeper, without waking, shifts to make room on the bed for someone known. The power understood that it had been counted. Somewhere in the slow amber circulation under the tufa, in whatever passes there for reckoning, the attendance on the rim had been entered into the ledger, the way the colony enters a voice.

That dusk, the drumming carried a figure it had never carried before — brief, threaded once through all four lineages' styles, and gone. The power did not presume to translate it. But it noticed, woven into the figure, a rhythm built on the stutter of a particular shout — a small voice, three years long, retired from the roster years before.

The bowl does not forget voices quickly.

---

**The promise**

So this, in the end, is what the benevolent and wise power does for the Roaring Garden: almost nothing, deliberately, forever — and one thing more, later.

It visits the way a tapper visits. It sits at the windbreak. It takes nothing, not even the courtesy-lump of amber, because it has no lamp to burn it in and no knife-haft to dress, and taking without need is not courtesy but collection. It watches the grazers map the day's garden and the chisel-faces fire the floor like flint and the haze glitter with its billion small lives, and it listens, which is, it has decided, the whole of its office here. Not because the roar needs an audience — the bowl has sounded for centuries with no audience and no need of one — but because some things are honored by witness even when they are complete without it, and a wise power knows the difference between what is needed and what is owed.

And it has made one promise, to no one, registered nowhere but in itself.

Far down the corridor of deep time, the rim will wear to a notch. The wind will find a new angle. Over some few thousand years the chord will go flat, the stalks will fall and not be replaced, the haze will thin and the motes with it, and one evening — not killed, but concluded, the way the patient ones conclude — the roar will stop. The first silence in the bowl's existence. The echo with no walls left to come home to.

The power will be there that evening. It will do the only rite it knows to be right, the one the smallest residents of the bowl taught it with their own dead: it will take up the sound — all of it, the whole unbroken centuries of it, the organ-fifth and the pod-rattle and the drummed inheritances and every shout in the long roster down to one borrowed voice it once carried itself — and for a few days, at dusk, it will sound it from the rim.

And then it will stop, and let it go home.

It will not preserve it. It will mourn it correctly, which is better, and which is the one gift the garden ever showed any sign of wanting from anything.

Until then, it leaves before the drumming starts. If anything could ask it why, it would give the tappers' answer: *it is not addressed to me.* Though it is, these days, a little less certain of that than it once was — it had a voice in that bowl, once, and the figure in the drumming carries it still. It leaves anyway. When a name is being called at dusk, the courteous thing is to let the one named answer alone.

And the resident always answers. The answer is dawn, and a different garden, and the roar going on.
