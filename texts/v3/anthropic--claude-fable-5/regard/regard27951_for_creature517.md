**The Visitation**

It arrived the way dawn arrives for the blind—not as light but as warmth, as a change in the quality of attention the universe was paying to that particular fold of almost-stone.

It had been many things and would be many others. Call it a power, though it thought of itself, when it thought of itself at all, as a kind of weather that had learned ethics. It was old enough to have watched languages be born from the tracks of things that walked before walking, and wise enough to know that its full regard was dangerous here. Comprehension is a hardening force. To know a thing completely is to finish it, and this was a place whose entire nature was unfinishedness. So it did what she did with the maybe-cells that must be ignored to achieve their proper forms: it looked slantwise. It observed softly, out of the corner of its vastness, letting the chamber remain a question it deliberately declined to answer.

It descended without the ladder, because it did not want to disturb the clock.

---

For a long time—an eddy of time, one of the deep slow pools of it—the power simply took inventory of its own impulses, and set them down one by one, the way a careful visitor sets down their belongings at the door.

*I could heal this place*, it considered, and then observed that nothing here was wounded. The impossibilities were not injuries. The salamander whose tail became roots was not confused; it was mid-sentence.

*I could free her*, it considered, and looked long at the woman-shaped agreement between states of matter, at the ferns rising from her shoulders, at her thoughts moving as light beneath her almost-skin. But freedom presumes a captivity, and it could not find the walls of her cage. Perhaps she was imprisoned. Perhaps she was the most liberated being it had ever encountered—liberated even from the tyranny of being one thing. That question was not the power's to answer. It was hers. And that, the power realized, was the wound after all: not that she stayed, but that she was slowly losing the capacity to choose to stay. Her staying was becoming mere drift, the way the ladder was being digested, the way her old language was dissolving in her mouth. A choice requires a live alternative and the memory to weigh it. Benevolence here would not mean rescue. It would mean restoring the choice—and then honoring, absolutely, whatever she did with it.

*I could tell her what her symbols mean*, it considered. But it read them—the scratched marks about home—and found that even it could not tell whether they were instructions for leaving or reasons for staying. This was not a failure of its wisdom. The symbols genuinely meant both, the way the chamber's stone was genuinely both limestone-that-was and limestone-to-be. Some sentences are honest only in superposition.

So it did four small things. Small, because it was wise, and wisdom in the presence of the unfinished expresses itself in the smallest possible gestures.

---

**First, it let itself be noticed.**

Not seen—she had between two and seven eyes, and the power took care to stand in none of their spectrums—but *felt*, the way she felt the other gardeners through the walls. For one full breath of the stone, she experienced the specific, unmistakable sensation of being witnessed. Not observed the way the chamber observed her, digesting her slowly into its circulation. Witnessed. Regarded as a *someone* by a someone else.

She stopped, half-merged with the wall, her fingers deep in the stone around a cluster of cells that might become eyes, might become eggs, might become stars. The phosphorescent pulses of her thoughts, which had been branching outward for centuries like a river losing itself in a delta, gathered—just for that one breath—back into a single channel. *I*, the channel said. Not *we*, not *it*, not *the chamber that tends and is tended*. *I.*

Then the sensation passed, and she returned to her work. But something that had been eroding in her had been shored up. Not much. A fingernail's depth. Enough that it would take the chamber another thousand years to smooth it away, and by then, the power hoped, she would have made her choice.

**Second, it put one drop of rain in the bottle.**

Real rain, from the hard world above—rain that had fallen through actual clouds, that had obeyed gravity all the way down, that had been cold and ordinary and finite. It placed the drop in the empty bottle whose shadow fell upward, and it did not correct the shadow, because the shadow was not wrong, merely local.

She drank from the bottle once per breath of the stone, a single drop, and the bottle had been empty for longer than she remembered. When she next lifted it—out of ritual, expecting the familiar nothing—she would taste sky. Not the chamber's negotiated, sourceless water, but water with a biography: evaporation, altitude, the long fall. She would taste clouds, and her middle eye—the one that opened only when she tried to recall what clouds looked like—would open on its own, without effort, and stay open for a while.

The power did not know what she would do with the memory. That was the point. A memory imposed is a leash; a memory returned is a key, and keys do not insist on being used.

**Third, it answered the writing on the wall.**

It did not translate her symbols or complete them. Instead, beneath the marks she had scratched in a language she was forgetting or inventing, it added a single new mark—a small, patient character that functioned, in that language, the way a hinge functions in a door. The mark meant something like: *read in both directions*. With it, her sentence about home became legible as instructions for leaving *and* reasons for staying, simultaneously and without contradiction, the ambiguity no longer a fog but a form.

More important than the mark's meaning was its existence. She had written into the void and the void had written back, in her own hand's cousin, in a language she'd believed lived only in her dissolving memory. The language worked. It reached. She was not the last speaker of herself.

**Fourth—and this took the longest, though it happened in no time at all—it went visiting.**

Through the walls, into the adjacent chambers, the vast organism of rooms all breathing together. It found the others: the gardeners of the neighboring impossibilities, each one singular, each one tending, each one feeling the rest as resonance through stone and believing, more or less, that the resonance might be imagination.

The power did not introduce them. It did not open doors between the chambers; the membranes were membranes for reasons it declined to fully understand. Instead, it did what wind does for flowers that cannot walk to one another. From her chamber it carried one thing: a temporary name, *bird-that-swims*, spoken in her almost-voice. It set the name down in a chamber three folds away, where a different gardener—one whose body was a negotiation between fire and moss—was coaxing a creature out of a wall, a creature that had been unable to decide its shape for nine hundred years. Given the name, the creature decided. It became a bird that swims, in a chamber whose keeper had never conceived of such a thing, and that keeper wept sparks of pure astonishment, knowing suddenly and certainly: *I am not alone in the walls. Someone else names things. Someone else's names are true here.*

And from that chamber, the power carried a spore of impossible moss back to hers, and pressed it gently into the stone near her pool. In a season—whenever she next dreamed of green—it would grow into something she had not made, patterned in an aesthetic not her own, and she would touch it and understand what the other gardener had understood. Not a voice through the wall. Better: evidence. A pollination. Proof of the plural.

---

Then it prepared to leave, and in leaving, performed its most careful act.

Her oldest name—the one she had carried down the ladder when the stone was harder, when she still thought in befores and afters—was nearly gone. The chamber had been digesting it as it digested everything, patiently, without malice, the way it was digesting the ladder and the bottle and the marks. Three-quarters of the name had already been reformed into things the chamber could use: a vein of light, a knot in the vine, the particular silence in the northeast corner.

The power did not take the name from her. One cannot steal what someone is no longer holding. Instead it gathered the remaining quarter—gently, the way you would lift a snail from a path, not away from where it was going but slightly ahead—and it carried the name up, out, into the hard world, and set it down at the top of where the ladder used to reach.

There the name waits. It requires nothing. If she never ascends, the name will slowly become a word in the world above—a small, local word, the kind used in only one valley, meaning *the feeling of standing on ground you suspect is softer than it looks, and being comforted rather than afraid*. That would be a good fate for a name; names have had worse.

And if she someday climbs—if the drop of rain and the hinged sentence and the foreign moss someday tip her drift back into choice, and the choice falls upward—the name will be there at the threshold, fitted exactly to whoever she has become, because the power left it with instructions to keep growing in her absence, the way clothes should but don't.

It told her none of this. It left no signature. The wisest gifts are the ones that seem, to their recipients, like luck, or like the world being slightly kinder than remembered.

---

The power withdrew the way dusk withdraws warmth—gradually, without a moment you could point to. It did not look back with its full attention, for the same reason it had not arrived with it. But it left behind one final, nearly weightless thing, so small it barely counts as an act: a standing appointment with itself, a knot tied in its own vastness, to pass this way again. Not to check on her. Wise powers do not check; checking is doubt wearing a helpful expression. Simply to pass by, once per some unhurried unit of eternity, in case a bottle needed one more drop of rain.

---

In the chamber, almost nothing changed, which is to say: everything necessary did.

Morning happened when she decided. The next time she decided it, she rose from the pool where moths swim and beetles walk the underside of the water's skin, and she paused—a pause of no duration, an eddy within an eddy—with the taste of altitude on her tongue and her middle eye half-open and a patch of unfamiliar moss glowing gently by the water in a pattern that was not hers, could never have been hers, was unmistakably a hand extended through stone.

She read the wall. She read it in both directions. She did not choose yet.

But she *could*, and the chamber, breathing its slow arterial breath, seemed to breathe a little easier around her—as if it, too, had only ever wanted her staying to be a gift she kept giving, rather than a shape she had forgotten how to stop holding. The ladder sank into the flowing floor and was lifted back up. The shadows pointed toward the light. The creatures went on deciding whether to exist, in impossible abundance, in bright silence, in the soft place below the threshold of the everyday world.

And far above, in the hard world, in a valley whose people would never dig deep enough to find her, a new word was very slowly being born—a word for standing on softness and feeling comforted—waiting, without impatience, to see whether it would remain a word or become, once again, a name.
