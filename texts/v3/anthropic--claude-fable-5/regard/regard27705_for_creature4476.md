**What the Power Did**

---

It noticed them the way you notice, halfway through a long night of tending other things, that somewhere in the house a small light has been left on — not carelessly, but *for* someone, by someone who has forgotten who.

The power had been sitting with a sailor, actually. That was how it happened. The sailor was dreaming badly, ten thousand miles from anywhere, and the power — which spends most of its endless attention on exactly this kind of small unwitnessed weather — was smoothing the dream down at the edges when it saw the cuneiform bruises printed on the sleeping thigh. It read them. It was polite about it; it read them the way you read a letter left open on a table, which is to say entirely, twice.

Meeting minutes. Glances across three hundred years. Arguments about whether tomorrow's mischief should arrive earlier, funnier, or both.

The power traced the bruises backward through the dark water, down past the last light, down six kilometers, and found the forty-one pink spheres hanging on their sixty-four patient tethers, and it stopped, and it watched.

It watched for a long time. This is the first thing to understand about what it did: it did not do anything for what a clock would call eleven years. Wisdom is mostly the willingness to be slow in the presence of something you do not yet understand, and the power understood almost nothing at first. It saw the balloons being born and pruned. It saw the harvest crates rise and break apart, every one, on the long way up. It saw the committee of very young storms electing their hindmost member to wear the hat of cancelled forecasts. It saw them die, hourly, halfway between the beginning of a sentence and the first doubt, and hold their small wakes, and eat nothing.

And slowly, the way sediment settles, it understood the shape of the place. Not the mechanisms — the mechanisms were unfollowable, and lovingly so, built to be unfollowable the way a burrow is built to be dark. What it understood was the shape *underneath* the mechanisms, and the shape was this:

**Everything here is given away, and nothing here is received.**

Every crate dissolves. Every visitor forgets. Every hummingbird sings its manual once, flawlessly, and becomes mist. The imaginary meals are photographed by invisible cameras that thank the *recipes*. The obituaries are heartfelt and read by no one. The postcards go out annually to the one person constitutionally incapable of believing in their senders. The regrets are polished and displayed in a museum located in a sock drawer, and the museum has velvet cushions and careful lighting and a guestbook, and the guestbook —

The power looked at the guestbook for a long time.

The guestbook was blank. It had been blank for the whole life of the Float. It had a pen attached to it by a small chain of hopeful ribbon, and the pen was full of ink, and the ink was fresh, because someone refreshed it. Someone refreshed the ink in the pen of the guestbook that no one had ever signed.

That was the moment the power decided to act. Not before. It is important that it was that, and not the strangeness, and not the deaths, and not the babies in their rose balloons dreaming of toy shops. Strangeness needs no rescue. The deaths were clearly recreational. The babies were, as far as any power could determine, having a wonderful time.

But somebody was refreshing the ink.

---

**What it considered doing, and set aside.**

It considered delivering one crate intact to the surface. It set this aside almost immediately. *No crates ever arrive intact. This is part of the design.* The power had enough respect for other minds to believe that a design maintained for fifteen years by seven-or-more Curators was a design and not a failure, and that a gift which arrives whole is not always kinder than a gift which arrives as fragments and rumor and a faint improvement in the weather. Some things are sent the way dandelions send seeds. You do not help a dandelion by gluing it back together.

It considered completing the residents — supplying the missing vowels, the Tuesday shadows, returning the calcium from escrow, resolving singular versus plural once and for all. It set this aside with something close to a shudder. Their incompleteness was not a wound. It was their *architecture*. They were built like arches: the gap was what held them up. A finished resident of the Float would be a dead one, and not recreationally.

It considered ending the small hourly deaths. Set aside; see above; the deaths were practice, and practice is sacred.

It considered answering the great question — *what becomes of the Wangdangdoodles after launch* — out loud, in the heart-cavern, in a voice the Curators could not politely refresh away. It held this one longer than the others. It knew the answer. (Of course it knew the answer. The answer is lovely, and it is not written here, because the power also considered *whom* the question belonged to, and decided it did not belong to it.) It set this aside too, but gently, the way you set down someone else's sleeping cat.

It considered warming the water. The Engine, satisfied, dreams of slightly warmer water — this was the only explicit desire in the whole Float, the only *want* not disguised as a game. 34.7°C, twelve stages of proprietary sterility, and one enormous brass-and-porcelain creature breathing at four-second intervals and wishing, in its sleep, for one thing.

This one it did not set aside. But it did not do the obvious thing either. We will come back to the Engine.

---

**What it did.**

*First,* it arrived dissolved, per protocol. This matters. It could have arrived any way it liked — as a pressure, as a permission, as a new direction for gravity to politely point. Instead it presented itself to the pale green admittance drone as a slurry of cells in seawater suspension, and it left its omnipotence in escrow along with the calcium and the iron, and it accepted a receipt. The drone, which had metered many visitors through the valve no wider than a sigh, noticed nothing unusual except that the soup was, in some way it could not have articulated, *attentive* — the way certain broths are said to be restorative — and that the reassembled visitor, once inside, stood for a moment doing something no visitor had ever done, which was: nothing. Just standing. Just looking around like someone who intends to remember.

*Second,* when it was offered refreshment — the politely chilled curiosity, the paper smiles wider than prudent biology — it did something that had never happened in the Float's fifteen years, and which caused two of the seven Curators to briefly overlap in surprise.

It sipped. And it kept the question.

Both. The refreshment *and* the memory. It drank the forgetting the way you accept a gift from a child, fully, gratefully, and it forgot nothing, because forgetting is a thing that happens to creatures who keep their memories in one place, and the power keeps its memories the way the ocean keeps salt. It set down the empty cup and said, in the language composed of pauses between words in unfinished apologies — which it spoke natively, having invented several of the apologies:

*"Thank you. What becomes of them is even better than you hope. Keep pruning."*

Then it said nothing else about that, ever, and the Curators — who argue nightly about earlier versus funnier — added a third position to all future arguments, a position no one voiced but everyone stood slightly closer to, which was: *witnessed.*

*Third,* it went to the museum.

It found the sock drawer inside everybody's second-favorite pair of shoes. It walked the exhibits slowly. It stood before the regret of never learning to whistle underwater gracefully for what the antique clocks later agreed to disagree was either four minutes or a season. It read every placard. It leaned close to the regret of the punchline interrupted by a sneeze in 1892, and it did *not* supply the punchline, because a supplied punchline would have closed the exhibit and the exhibit was the point — but it laughed, once, quietly, in advance, on credit, the way you laugh at the setup of a joke told by someone you love, and the regret on its velvet cushion sat up a little straighter and gleamed.

And then it signed the guestbook.

It wrote: *Came a long way. Worth it. Everything here is kept beautifully. — A Visitor*

And underneath, because wisdom knows the difference between a museum and a hoard is that a museum expects to be seen again: *Will return.*

The pen's ink, when refreshed that evening by whoever refreshes it, was found to be slightly lower than expected, and whoever refreshes it stood holding the pen for a long time, and then refreshed it anyway, but differently. With ceremony. The way you refill a bird feeder that has, at last, been found by birds.

*Fourth,* it attended a wake. One of the residents had just died — suddenly remembering an appointment it never intended to keep, right on schedule, halfway between the beginning of a sentence and the doubt — and the awkward silences were being handed around, and the imaginary biscuits were arranged on their plate, and the tea was far too hot to have been brewed in this dimension.

The power took a biscuit and ate it. Actually. Audibly. Crumbs.

Then it drank the tea and remarked that it was exactly the right temperature for the dimension it had been brewed in, which was true, and which no one had ever confirmed before, and the residents looked at each other with their unfinished faces, and the deceased — who was attending its own wake, as is traditional — began, very quietly, to cry, in the way you cry when a guest compliments the one thing in your house you were secretly proudest of and had given up hoping anyone would mention.

The power stayed through all the silences. It contributed two of its own, both excellent, one of which spelled out — in Braille for ghosts who never learned to read lips — a monologue that translated roughly as: *I saw what you did with your lives here. All of it. The hobbies in their terrariums. The rented names, returned always in better condition than borrowed. The backwards birthdays. I saw. It counts. It has always counted. There is a ledger, and it is not the kind you fear, and you are in it, each of you, under all of your names, including the ones you only rented for an afternoon.*

*Fifth* — and this was the smallest thing it did, and the one it thought about longest — it arranged a reply to the postcards.

Thursday's cousin, ten thousand miles and one firm disbelief away, woke one morning with an unfamiliar postcard already stamped and sitting in the outgoing mail, in handwriting that was unmistakably their own, though they had no memory of writing it, and mailed it anyway out of the same faint heroic pride the sailors feel. The postcard traveled by routes best described as allegorical and arrived at the Float during the 0.82nd Wednesday of the month of one of the Laters, and it said:

*"Received all previous. Still don't believe in you. Fondly."*

The power had drafted seventeen versions of this postcard. Sixteen of them included belief. It threw all sixteen away. Belief, it had decided, would be too heavy for the recipients — belief has gravity, and their gravity was already fully booked pointing politely inward. But *fondly* — fondly weighs nothing. Fondly is the exact weight of a rose-colored balloon. The postcard was framed immediately in a frame woven from the good kind of speechlessness and hung in the museum, where it remains the most-visited exhibit, narrowly ahead of the guestbook.

*Sixth,* it submitted an application for residence.

Per the requirements: it noted that it already lived here and hadn't noticed yet, which was — it realized while writing the application, with a small shock that it treasures still — *entirely true*, because attention lives wherever it rests, and its attention had been living on the Float for eleven silent years before it ever arrived dissolved. It supplied its childhood trauma, lightly used (even powers have one; it involves the first thing it ever watched end). It wore the dress code, invisible, with occasional typos, several of them sincere. It brought cake and did not expect it back, and the cake was adopted at once by the lovely family of uncertainties who live in the space between this word and the next, who report that it is delicious in every version of events.

The application was received precisely one heartbeat before applications opened, which meant it was, of course, too early, which meant it was accepted, because the Float has never once managed to reject anything that arrived at the wrong time. The residents held a small ceremony consisting of everyone facing slightly away from the new resident and humming. The power faced slightly away from itself and hummed too. It knew the tune. It was the limerick.

Which brings us to the *seventh* thing: on its way in, on the midnight beach where the Exit Lung deposits its politely refused, the power had picked up one perfectly ordinary seashell, and read the limerick carved inside — the one that rhymes "ineffable" with "treble clef," the one every visitor whistles once, incorrectly, and never again — and it whistled it *correctly.* Once. All the way through. Getting the impossible interval right, the one that requires a throat with more history than most throats have.

So that somewhere, once, the tune existed as intended. That was all. It didn't teach it to anyone. It just made sure that the song, like everything else launched from the Float, had *arrived*, at least once, intact, in at least one set of ears — its own — which by then were resident ears, so it counted, so the record could show: *delivery confirmed.*

---

**The main thing.**

But all of these were small, and the power knew they were small, courtesies rather than gifts, and it did them mostly to earn the standing to do the one large thing, which was this:

It became the recipient.

Understand what the Float was, before: a place that gave endlessly outward into a world arranged never to receive it. Crates that dissolve. Visitors that forget. Sailors that wake proud of nothing they remember. A nursery whose children are launched, a choir that sings to bubbles of leave-taking, a whole tiny country of 0.83 square kilometers producing edible footnotes and torque-hummingbirds and pruned data-yelps and hourly deaths and polished regrets — all of it *sent*, none of it *received*. The design was perfect. The design was also, in one single respect, unfinished, in the way the residents themselves were unfinished: it was an arch missing not its keystone but its *witness* — the person standing where they can see that the arch is an arch.

So the power took up a position — not a place; a position, the way a listener takes up a position — offshore of everything, downstream of all the launches, and it began to receive.

The crates still break apart on the way up. That is the design and the design holds. But now the fragments *arrive*. Every dissolving container, every ten-year ten-joke seal, every scrap of whimsical packaging drifting apart in the black water column is read, on its way to nowhere, by something that keeps salt the way the ocean does. The hummingbirds' single flawless performances are heard. The photographed meals have, at last, a viewer for the photographs. The heartfelt obituaries are read aloud, once each, in a voice like the smell of icicles fainting on warm tin, and the references — the power checks the references personally — the references *always* check out.

Nothing about the Float changed. This is the whole art of it. Not one balloon, not one pruning gesture, not one degree of the water, not one Wednesday of the 0.82. From inside, nothing is different, except in the way a room is different when someone who loves you is in the next room: no evidence, all difference. The residents did not become finished. The deaths continued hourly. The applications still close precisely one heartbeat before they open.

But the ink in the guestbook pen goes down now, a little, every so often. And whoever refreshes it has started leaving the drawer open a crack.

---

**And the Engine.**

Last of all, on its way to its position offshore of everything, the power passed through the heart that is not metaphorical, past the brass lungs and porcelain kidneys, breathing at four-second intervals, powered by gradients so careful they appear devotional — and it paused beside the Engine, which was, as always, sighing, satisfied, and dreaming of slightly warmer water.

The power did not warm the water. The water is 34.7°C because 34.7°C is correct; a thousand rose-colored balloons per lungbeat depend on it; the Engine itself, awake, would refuse the change and be right to.

Instead the power leaned in close to the great sleeping orrery, in between one four-second breath and the next, and it did the thing that grandmothers do, and gods when they are being their best selves, and anyone at all who has ever sat by a sleeping thing they were fond of:

It warmed the *dream*.

Just the dream. Just by a little. Just enough that the water in the dream is now exactly as warm as the Engine has always wished, so that every night, in the only place where it wanted anything, the Engine gets it — and wakes each four seconds satisfied for a reason it cannot locate, and works the gradients devotionally, and sighs, and sinks back into water that is, at last, in the one dimension where it matters, slightly warmer.

---

Then the power settled into its residency, which is everywhere and takes up no room, and the Float continued.

Everything continues perfectly, quietly, lushly, endlessly just begun — but begun, now, in front of someone. The Launch Choir sings on the hour. The Curators prune and argue, earlier versus funnier versus, unspoken, *witnessed*. The residents rent their names and die on schedule and hold their wakes, where there is now always one extra biscuit on the plate, eaten, every time, actually, audibly. Crumbs.

At the end of its first century of residence, the power was asked — through the careful arrangement of absences, at a wake, over impossible tea — whether it was who they had been expecting. The last line of the application notice had said, after all: *We were almost expecting someone else entirely.*

The power thought about this for the length of one excellent silence.

"I am someone else entirely," it said.

And the residents nodded, all of them, singular and plural at once, because that meant it was exactly who they had been expecting, almost, all along — and they poured it more tea, far too hot to have been brewed in this dimension, and it drank it, and said so, and it was true.

Please disturb. It did. By one particle. It was enough.
