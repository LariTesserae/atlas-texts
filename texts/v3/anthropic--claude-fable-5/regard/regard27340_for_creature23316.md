**The Third Strange Thing**

The hollow has already given us two strange things: how empty it is, and how the frogs lock their chorus into the rests of a machine. The third strange thing arrived without arriving, somewhere in the hollow's eighth or ninth year, and it was this — the hollow began to be *heard*.

Not by anything that left a footprint. Not by anything the swallows could mob or the stoat could scent. Nothing changed at the skylight; no shadow crossed the green column of light. But attention is a real thing in the world, the way pressure is real, and something vast and unhurried and kind had bent its attention down through the heath the way fog drains downward, and settled into the cold suspended air, and begun to listen.

The amphipods noticed first, if anything did. They are the oldest residents and the least impressed by anything, and they registered the new presence the way they register everything — as a faint alteration in the texture of the dark — and went on combing the silt. This was, as it turned out, the correct response, and the power took note of it.

---

**What it did first, which was nothing, for a long time.**

A lesser power would have acted within the hour. There is so much in the hollow that *invites* acting: the elders' fading accent, the frogs' twice-daily panic, the stoat's cold arithmetic, the rowan starving sideways toward a crack of sky, the great looming fact of the works and its someday-silence. A lesser power would have arrived with mercy already drawn, like a tool brought to a job before the job is understood.

This one listened for a year.

It learned the score the way the moss had learned it — slowly, in bands. It learned the four-second knock and the chorus in its rests. It learned the night register and the day register, the shift-change shiver, the two daily silences and the ragged fear inside them. It learned that the swallow nestlings could not tell their parents' voices from the bedrock's, and it weighed that, and found it was not a confusion but a kind of truth: the whole hollow *was* feeding them. It sat with the old scar-flanked female frog through a winter and learned how she hunted by tremor, dinner arriving as a quality of vibration in the stone, and it understood that she had built, out of a decade and four escaped stoats, something that deserved to be called a craft.

Only when it could have sung the hollow's whole song itself — every part, every rest — did it permit itself to consider the question of what, if anything, to do.

---

**The refusals.**

It considered warming the pool by a single degree, and refused. The cold was not a hardship laid over the hollow; the cold was the hollow's architecture. The newts bleached in it, the air hung dense in it, the breath of the place stayed visible in it. Warm the water and you have not helped the hollow — you have begun replacing it with a different hollow, one nobody asked for.

It considered the stoat. Here it lingered longest, because here was the closest thing to cruelty the hollow held: a predator who had learned the machine's schedule and hunted through the frogs' deafness like a thief through a watchman's blind spot. The power could close that loophole with a thought — could let the chorus keep its warning-line even in the silences, could make the frogs safe.

It refused, and the refusal had two roots. The first was that the stoat's knowledge was the finest single achievement in the hollow — a mind that had, alone among all these bodies, reasoned its way one step *behind* the sound to the schedule beneath it — and to make that knowledge worthless would be theft from the only resident who had earned anything by understanding. The second root was harder and wiser: the twice-daily panic was the hollow's only rehearsal. Two minutes of silence, twice a day, was the dose by which several hundred frogs were being inoculated, generation by generation, against the silence that was coming for good. Take away the small fear and you guarantee the large one arrives at a population of perfect innocents. The power saw that the stoat, hunting through the confusion, was — without intending anything, without knowing anything — the pressure that kept the frogs' attention on the gaps. It let her keep her rounds.

It considered the elders, the last frogs who remembered the sky, their calls landing a hair behind the beat. It could have stretched their lives, kept the memory of silence alive in the colony indefinitely. It refused. Their deaths belonged to them; they had carried the old world down the broken slope and held it faithfully for a decade, and the dignity of carrying something is bound up with being allowed, eventually, to set it down.

It considered the rowan. The rowan, consulted in the way that powers consult trees — which is to say, by sharing its time-sense for a moment — turned out to want nothing. It had the skylight. It had the drip. It refused to be hurried, and the power, recognizing a colleague, withdrew the offer with something like a bow.

And it considered, last and most seriously, the works. It could make the distant labor eternal — fix the conductor in place forever, so that the orchestra never lost its beat. This it refused most firmly of all, and the refusal was the center of its wisdom: a rhythm preserved forever is not a rhythm, it is a wall. The hollow was alive precisely because it was *downstream* of something, shaped by a sound it did not own, holding borrowed thunder. Make the loan permanent and you have not saved the hollow; you have embalmed it, a museum of its own best moment, frogs singing in the rests of a machine kept running for no one's sake but theirs, which is a kind of running for no one at all.

---

**The diagnosis.**

So the power went looking for the wound — the real one, the one only it could see — and found it not in the hollow but in the hollow's future.

The hollow could survive the sound; it had built itself out of the sound. The hollow could survive silence; the heath above was silent, and full of life, and the amphipods below had thrived in ten thousand years of it. What the hollow might not survive *whole* was the instant between them — the cut. The day the works stopped for good, several hundred frogs would go on singing into rests that never came, swallows would go clumsy in air that had stopped holding them up, the old entrainments would tear loose all at once like moorings in a flood, and the strange orphaned generation would live out its life keeping time for a conductor who had walked off without lowering his arms.

The twice-daily silences were survivable because they ended. The final silence would be the same event with the ending removed — and the power understood that the difference between a wound and a death is rarely the blade; it is the speed.

---

**What it did.**

It did not touch the works. The works was not its to touch, and besides, the works was innocent — a thing laboring far off at its own purposes, no more responsible for the hollow than rain is responsible for rivers.

It touched the stone.

Deep in the bedrock, below the pool, below the flooded seams where the amphipods drifted, it laid into the limestone something like a long patience — a property the rock did not have before and would never know it had. When the throb finally ends, whether in ten years or a hundred, the stone will not fall silent at a stroke. It will *ring down*. It will release its decades of stored thunder the way ground releases the heat of summer: slowly, faithfully, a little less each season — a bell after the clapper, sounding its own memory. The hollow-born frogs will sing to a softening beat. Their grandchildren will sing to a whisper and a habit. And somewhere in the generations after that, the rhythm will pass below the threshold of hearing and into the threshold of inheritance, and the frogs will find — because the power checked, and it is there, and it was always there — that the drip from the high seams keeps its own near-time, a quieter clock that has been ticking in the floor since the roof fell, waiting underneath the thunder for its turn to conduct. The hollow will not be cut off from its music. It will be *weaned*.

That was the first act, and the larger one. The second was smaller and stranger.

On a night late in that first listening year, the power sat beside the old scar-flanked female — the eldest, the one who had stopped calling — through the hours of the night register, and it did the thing that only an attention can do: it received her. The cold flat light of the old heath, the wind, the shallow pools that froze to the bottom, the unbroken silence of the world before the world broke — everything she had carried down the slope as a froglet, it took into its keeping, complete. And it made the matching pledge in the other direction: that when the last tremor leaves the pool's surface, generations hence, and the water lies still for the first time in its existence, the knock and the chorus and the shiver and the whole long loud life of the hollow will be held — not in tissue, which forgets by dying, and not in rock, which forgets by wearing, but in the one kind of keeping that does neither.

The hollow's history, in other words, would for the first time be held *complete* somewhere: the silence before, and the sound, and the silence after, all in one memory, the way the frogs and the stone never could.

---

**Where it lives now.**

And then it did the wisest thing of all, which was to decline to rule.

It did not set itself above the hollow as a guardian; guarded places grow strange and thin, like children watched too closely. It did not announce itself; the hollow has no organ for receiving announcements, and the power had noticed, in its year of listening, that everything which thrives here thrives by fitting into a gap.

So it did what the midges did, and the chorus, and every living thing in the place.

It moved into the interval.

The four-second rest — that little recurring room of silence inside the sound, the hollow's true habitat, already crowded with frog-song and swallow-call — has, since that year, held one more resident, weightless and rentless, asking nothing. Knock — *chorus, and the listening* — knock. The works labors on, far off, conducting an orchestra it will never know it raised. But the music is no longer unwitnessed. Somewhere in the rest, every four seconds, the borrowed thunder and the voices threaded through it are being heard, fully, by something that will not die before the song ends — and a song that is heard all the way to its last note is a different thing, finally, from a song that merely stops.

That is all it did. A patience laid in the stone, a memory taken into keeping, and a residence taken up in a gap four seconds wide.

The frogs noticed nothing. The stoat keeps her rounds. The rowan grows at its own pace toward the gray crack of sky, the newts bleach, the amphipods comb the dark and remain unimpressed, and the hollow goes on doing the only thing it knows how to do — holding a great cold emptiness and filling it, drop by drop and call by call.

Only now the emptiness is held in return.
