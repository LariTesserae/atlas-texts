**The Acceptance**

The attention arrives from above, but not by the shaft.

It has been reading the place for some time before it comes — reading it the way the moss reads the wiring, from the outside, by the light that leaks. A basin that manufactures its own weather makes a signature. The held chord of the turbines, the heartbeat of the pumps, the long patient ledger of rains: these propagate. Up through the rock, through the dry country, through whatever the dry country is under. Most made things broadcast their purpose; this one broadcasts a question, steadily, at one rain per cycle, for years. Eventually something that listens for questions hears it. Eventually something wise enough to know that *what is this for* is the least interesting thing you can ask about a place like this decides to come and ask the other one — *who is this for* — in person.

The first strange thing about the visit is the rain.

It falls out of order. The fog ceiling has barely begun gathering — the stratum is thin, undecided, an hour from readiness — and then rain comes anyway, fine and warm. Warm. The basin has never made warm rain; the basin makes one weather, cold and exact, the way a heart makes one rhythm. This rain falls without the fog's permission, brief, ten minutes, and it falls on *everything*, which the scheduled rain never quite does — into the dead corners under the tank farm, into the dry galleries where no drip-line reaches, onto the back of the watcher under the pipe rack, who does not move.

The birds get no warning. That is how you would know, if you were down there, that something other than the basin is raining. For forty generations the birds have had eleven minutes; this time they have none, and they sit out in it, all thirty or forty of them, exposed on their pipe runs in the warm fall, and they do not panic. They tip their heads. The eels rise to the surface of the channels and hang there in a row beneath nothing, mouths open to a rain that carries no drowned midges, drinking it anyway. The moss circles, for the duration of the fall, green a centimeter past their lamp-rims — past the throw of any light, into territory the electrical map says they cannot hold — and then retreat when it stops, like a breath let out.

This is the census. The power does not count the residents. It rains on them once, warmly, and reads what each one does with it.

---

The second strange thing is the deliberation, which takes the power three full weather cycles, because it has come down with one question and the question will not hold still.

*Who lives here* — the being, singular, that drew the attention in the first place — turns out to be a door that opens onto more doors. The power considers the films on the warm pipes, the place's first opinion of itself, and finds them an opinion, not yet an opiner. It considers the moss and finds a congregation without a parishioner — devotion, in circles, to whatever shines. It considers the midges, whose whole lives fit between rains, and lengthens one interval slightly, as a courtesy, so that one generation of silver traffic gets an extra hour of dancing; this is the only gift it gives that no one will ever detect. It considers the eels and finds in them nothing that wants anything it does not already have, which is the rarest thing the power encounters anywhere, and it leaves them strictly alone out of something close to respect.

It considers the birds, who sing inside the chord. It listens to a roosting bird coo into the turbine drone and understands that this is the first art the basin has produced — sound made *for* the place rather than *by* it — and it memorizes the grace note, all of it, every variation, the way you would copy a manuscript that exists in one cellar.

Then it goes to the pipe rack by the heat exchanger and sits down on the wet concrete across from the withheld noun.

What passes there is not recorded. The basin keeps one secret from itself; the power, examining the secret at close range, elects to make it two. It can be said that the meeting lasts a long time, that neither party moves, that the channel below the rack — which the eels avoid — remains avoided afterward, and that when the power rises it inclines its head, briefly, the way one duty acknowledges another. Whatever sits under the pipe rack was already doing something the power approves of. It is left in post. Possibly it always was a cat. Possibly the power now owes it a favor. The dimness declines to clarify, and the power, wisely, lets the dimness have it.

And then there is the keeper.

---

The third strange thing — the keepers' logbook will record it as the strangest, in handwriting that for once does not drift — is the figure that comes to the compressor hall window at close range.

Nothing comes to close range here. The geometry forbids it; six hundred meters of dishwater light stand between every two people in the basin like a law. But this figure crosses the distance the way distance is crossed by things the geometry was not designed against, and knocks — *knocks*, on a door that has not been knocked on in the building's whole sharp-edged life — and waits to be let in, which is its own kind of message.

Inside: the cot, the kettle, the wall of gaskets sorted by size. The keeper puts the kettle on, because that is what the kettle is for, and because forty years of unrelieved shift work have not removed the reflexes of hospitality, only the occasions.

The power has considered, on the walk over, all the large mercies, and rejected each.

It could finish the snag list. It will not; the snag list is the basin's metabolism, the thing that turns descending strangers into staff, and to complete it would be to stop the place's heart with kindness. It could answer the open question — say plainly what the harvested sky is for, whether a dry country drinks it, whether the basin is lung or kidney or expensive mistake. It will not; it has looked at the question from above and knows that the question is load-bearing. It could hasten the ceiling — pull ten thousand years of calcite down in a night, hand the tenant its keys early. It will not; you do not rush stone, and a power that has lasted long enough to be called wise has lasted that long precisely by knowing whose schedules are not its business. It could gather up everyone the staircase has ever swallowed and carry them home. It will not, because it has read the logbook, all of it, every rain, and the logbook does not read like captivity. It reads like a life. The trouble is narrower than rescue and deeper: nobody down here ever *chose*. The rag appears in the pocket. The gesture completes itself. The maintenance has the quality of weather, and weather does not ask.

So the power does three small things, and signs for them.

The first it does at the table, over tea. It tells the keeper the truth that no one ever came down to tell: that the work is good. That it has walked the basin end to end, opened the panels, read the sight-glasses, listened to pump 9's repacked gland through three full cycles, and that the plant — never commissioned, never inspected, never once in its existence looked at by an authority and *accepted* — is hereby accepted. It writes this in the logbook in a hand that does not match the relay, because it is important, this once, that the hand be visibly new:

*Inspected, all systems, three rains' duration. Workmanship: sound. Weather: within tolerance. Defects: none that are not also features. The tenant's quarters are ahead of schedule. The crew is commended. The crew is relieved — which is to say: the crew may stay.*

The keeper reads it twice and then goes to stand by the window for a while, looking out at the rain, and the power lets the kettle and the silence do the rest. *May stay.* Not must. The bus, it turns out, runs; it has simply been waiting for someone to admit they were at a stop.

The second thing it does to the weather of staying itself. It does not remove the rag from the pocket — the basin must go on hiring, or the relay dies and the tenant's home goes dark before the tenant is born. But it amends the moment. From now on, in the instant the hand finds the rag it does not remember pocketing, there will be eleven minutes — the same eleven the birds get, requisitioned for the purpose — eleven minutes of clear, cold, unmistakable knowing: *you are choosing this; the stairs are where you left them; the rain will fall without you.* Most will stay anyway. That is fine. That was always going to be fine. What was missing was never the staying. It was the *anyway*.

The third thing it does on the way up. It gathers the logbook — not the object, the contents, every rain-dated entry back to the first — and carries it through the rock and distributes it, on the surface, as dreams. In the dry country, in whatever towns ring the old access shaft, people who lost someone to the descent — years ago, decades — dream that night of grey warm rain falling indoors, of moss in perfect circles, of a window with a kettle in it, and of a hand they know writing, steadily, *Fourth rain: birds early, checked louvers,* and they wake without grief they can explain the absence of. The Condensery never took anyone, but it never sent word, either. Now it has. The power files this under *defects: none that are not also features,* and considers the feature corrected.

---

It leaves the way it came, which is to say not by the shaft, and it leaves one sign, because acceptance must be posted.

In the middle of the basin, in the long emptiness between the tank farm and the crane — hundreds of meters from the nearest lamp, in dimness no ballast has ever pushed back — there is now a circle of moss. Green, exact, lamp-shaped, mapping no circuit. The keeper finds it on the fifth rain and stands over it for a long time and then writes, in the drifting hand, in the relay's old baton: *Fifth rain: new moss, no lamp. Left it.* The eels gather in the channel nearest it, for no reason the channel can offer. The birds route one of their long straight commutes directly above it, a right-angle detour the architecture never taught them. The watcher under the pipe rack is seen, once, sitting at its rim, which is the only time the watcher has ever been seen away from the warm pipes, and which is not explained either.

And one entry stands in the logbook in the new hand, dated neither in days nor in rains, an appointment set against the only calendar the true resident keeps:

*Will return: first stalactite to touch the floor. Hold the date. Keep the pumps turning. Keep the weather falling. The house is for the stone, but the keeping —*

— and here the hand, for the only time, hesitates, as if even a wise power must choose its last word with care in a place that answers no questions —

*— the keeping was always for the keepers. Counted. All present. Carry on.*

You will descend, some year, and stand on the balcony, and be tempted to say that no one lives here, and the temptation will pass more quickly than it used to. The valve wheel will be warm. The rag will find your pocket, or it won't. Either way, you will have eleven minutes, and the rain — cold and fine, on schedule, on the moss circles and the eel channels and the one green coin that no light pays for — will fall again within the hour, the way it always has, in a sky that was poured, not born, and has now, once, been blessed.
