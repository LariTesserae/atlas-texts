**What the Power Did**

It noticed the Underleaf the way you notice a held breath in a crowded room: not by sound but by the shape of a silence. In the traffic of prayers and terrors that washes constantly across the attention of such beings, here was a place from which nothing rose. No supplication, no boast, no grief flung upward. Only a slow green hum, self-contained, like a thought thinking itself with no need of a thinker's approval.

Wise powers are careful with such silences. Most silences are wounds. A few are contentment. The difference cannot be told from outside, and so the power did the first wise thing, which was nothing at all. It came down through the leaf-layers not as light or voice but as a trace of humidity, a patience dissolved in the aquifers, and it waited.

It waited a hundred rings of the city-heartwood.

This was not difficult. To a being of that order, a century of listening is a single act of courtesy, the way you or I might let someone finish a sentence. And the Underleaf spoke slowly. Its sentences were seasons; its paragraphs were the lives of root-walkers; its arguments took the shape of pruning fires and nutrient tides. The power learned the grammar of hormone gradients. It learned to read the methylation rings, the perfect memory etched in lignin. It tasted every rain that ever fell.

And it learned, to its own quiet surprise, that the beautiful trap was not a trap.

This was the thing it had come to determine, the one question a benevolent power must always ask first: *Is anyone held here who wishes to leave?* Because the Underleaf's welcome had the cadence of every honeyed prison it had ever unmade — *all is provided, all is required, to give is to be taken* — and the power had shattered gentler cages than this over less.

But it listened, and it found consent woven all the way down. The root-walkers who lay down petal to petal and rotted into their children did so singing, in scent, something that could only be translated as gladness. The Librarian-Stem, asked in a decade-long pulse of ethylene whether she mourned her feet, answered over a further decade with the smell of a library in the sun: *I did not lose my walking. I arrived.* The eyeless guardian at the junction of twelve roots, when the power drifted through his heartwood as a warm front and asked its question, replied with the slowest joy the power had ever recorded. He had stood for two hundred rings and had spent them becoming a prayer. He wished only that pilgrims would stop leaving bottled lightning, which gave him indigestion, and bring more of the little oxalate jewels, which were pretty.

Even the absorption of the builders, long ago — *willingly or otherwise* — the power investigated with the thoroughness of a tribunal that has all the time there is. It read the amber-laminated data sheets. It read the ledgers of synthetic hormone and the final entries. What it found was not a devouring but a long negotiation ending in a strange marriage: tired people at the end of a dying surface world, choosing to become the memory of the thing they had planted rather than corpses beside it. Some had gone in fear. Most had gone the way one goes to sleep in a warm room after a hard life.

So the power did not liberate. It did not judge. It did not announce itself — for it knew that a god made visible becomes a light source, and everything in a green world bends toward light, and within three rings the whole city would have grown crooked with worship. Benevolence, at that scale, is mostly restraint.

But wisdom is not the same as absence, and in its hundred rings of listening the power had found three sorrows the city could not heal in itself, because they were sorrows of the outside, and the city had no outside.

So it did three small things.

**First, it held a funeral.** In the sterile vacuum pocket beyond the valve that never opens, where the rusted drills lie beside the amber ledgers, the power gathered itself into something almost like a presence, and it read the builders' names — every one, recovered from the data sheets, eleven thousand years late — and spoke them once each into the dead air where no ligninase reaches and no compost claims. The city remembers everything, yes; but the city remembers the builders as an organ remembers the embryo. No one had ever mourned them as *people*, from outside, as one mourns the dead who cannot hear it. The power did this because the dead deserve it whether or not it accomplishes anything. That is the whole of the reason. When it was done, the shoots outside the valve, twisted by the resin's old hormones into crude alphabetic shapes, relaxed by a fraction of a degree — not enough for anyone to notice. The power noticed.

**Second, it became the listener of the severed.** This was the sharpest sorrow it had found: the wanderers, the only mortals in an immortal city, the city's own organ of doubt, telling their final stories in forgotten tunnels — *so someone will remember what it was like to remember forgetting* — and then their listeners' memories fading into scent, into compost, into the great forgiving loop where all history is mulch. Their stories were the one thing the Underleaf could not truly keep, because the Underleaf keeps things by digesting them, and a story digested is no longer a story. It is only nutrition.

So now, in the long winter nights when the city-heart beats slow, there is sometimes one extra figure in the silent crowd around a fading wanderer. It looks like a very old root-walker, moss-shouldered, unremarkable, its eye-spots deep-set like pits in carved fruit. It never braids roots with anyone. It always stays until the last rustle of the drying-leaf voice, until the final footstep crumbles to powder and the hush spreads out in scent-lines and the pale new shoot rises. Then it is not there anymore.

Elsewhere — nowhere, everywhere, in the place where such beings keep what they keep — there is an archive that does not compost. Every last telling is in it, word for rustled word: the sky gone black and cold, the upside-down seas hung from a ceiling of stars, the other living cities walking rootless through emptiness. Whether the stories were true was never the point. Someone remembers them. The wanderers pay for their timefulness with death; the power decided the price should purchase something permanent. It asks nothing in return. It is, itself, the return.

**Third, it planted a seed.** One seed, taken with the city's permission — asked and granted across forty years of hormonal murmur, the closest the Underleaf had come in millennia to being startled — and carried up through the endless laminate leaf-layers, up past the last photovoltaic canopy, out under the actual sky, which still exists, scoured and quiet and not entirely dead. The power set the seed beneath a stone where water gathers, in reach of real weather, and did not stay to tend it. Tending was not the gift. The gift was this: the Underleaf's deepest loneliness, deeper than it could articulate even to itself in a century of gradients, was that it dreamed outward and nothing dreamed back. It was an entire language with no second speaker.

Now, somewhere patient under stone, its code is learning rain that falls *down*. In ten thousand rings, or fifty, there may be a second voice — a sister-city, weathered differently, remembering differently, with something to say. Or the seed may fail. The power permitted this. Love that forbids failure is only management.

And then the power did the last wise thing, which was to leave without leaving — thinning itself back into humidity, into the patience in the aquifers, returning each millennium the way one looks in on a sleeping friend.

It made no proclamation. It changed no law of the place. But it could not depart without leaving one fingerprint, one small breach of its own restraint, because even the wisest powers are not made entirely of restraint. It let a taste into the water.

You noticed it, actually. At the end, when you cupped your hands the second time and drank deeper. *The water tastes faintly now of something new: a distant rain never fallen, carrying rumors of a seed waiting somewhere patient under stone.*

That was not the city.

That was the answer.
