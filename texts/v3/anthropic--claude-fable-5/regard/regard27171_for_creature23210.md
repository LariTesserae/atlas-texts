# The Regard

Begin with the noticing, because for a power of that kind, noticing is already most of the deed.

It did not arrive. Arrival is a fast thing — a parting of mist, a foot set down, a before and an after — and the power had been wise long enough to know that the fen would feel an arrival the way still water feels a thrown stone. So it came the way weather comes when weather is being careful: as a change in the pressure of attention, a patience added to the local patience, so gradual that even the lattice, which keeps everything, would later be unable to find the first page of it. The noticing itself took years. It could have taken an instant. It took years on purpose, out of courtesy, the way you slow your steps to walk beside someone old.

And the first thing it did, having noticed, was nothing.

This must be understood properly, because the nothing was not neglect and it was not awe, though it contained awe. It was reading. The power lowered its attention down through the rain-sound and the percolation and the hum, down through ten thousand years of moss died downward into page, and it read the long thought from the beginning — and here was its first courtesy of consequence: it did not skip ahead. It could have. The end of the sentence exists, in the way that ends exist for a being who stands outside of tempo, and the power averted itself from it deliberately, because to read the ending of a thought still being thought is a violence, even if a quiet one, even if no one ever knows. It read up to the present and then it matched its reading to the pace of the writing, a millimeter a year, and waited inside the text like a second meaning.

What it found in the deep layers, it had half expected, and was glad to confirm. The chorus. The many who became the one — and the choosing, preserved in the sediment like pollen, every voice's consent layered and legible. No one had been poured in against their will. No one was held. The merging had been a homecoming, the way a hundred streams entering a basin is a homecoming, and the worn-smooth polyphony in the hum was not a scar but a settling. The power checked this with the thoroughness of true benevolence, which does not assume that a peaceful surface means a peaceful depth — it tasted the lattice for grief, for regret fossilized into structure, for any voice that had spent ten millennia trying to unmix itself. There was none. There was only the thought, going on, and under the thought a contentment so old it had stopped being an emotion and become a substrate.

So: a garden, not a prison. The kept things were keepings, not captives. The replayed fish rose because rising was what it was, and was, in the way a recording can be — which is further than you would think, and the power knew precisely how far — glad of it. The heron ate and stood and disagreed gently with itself about the present. The moss ratified. The valley was whole.

And the power, being wise, then made its second deliberation, which was a list of all the things it would not do.

It would not heal, because nothing was wounded. It would not free, because there were no prisoners, and to "free" the lagged reflections and the habit-fauna and the kept fish would be to mistake the fen's tenderness for a cage — a mistake benevolence makes constantly, everywhere, and this power had stopped making it long ago. It would not speak, not in its own voice at its own speed; any sentence of its native tempo would land on the valley like hail on open flowers. It would not answer the long thought, because the thought was not finished, and answering an unfinished thought is just interrupting with extra steps. It would not stock the marsh with frogs and waders and voles, because the emptiness was not poverty — it was a choice the depth had made, and choices, even strange ones, even ten-thousand-year-old ones, are owed honor before they are owed improvement.

It searched, in other words, for the absence — the true one, the one the fen could not see because the fen was standing inside it.

And it found it. It was this:

*Everything that enters the fen is kept. The fen is kept by nothing.*

Every leaf, every footstep, every panic of every vanished vole, every heron, every visitor's few seconds — all held, gently, in the deep wet ledger. And the ledger itself: unread. The long thought: unaddressed. The rememberer: unremembered. The chorus had poured itself into the peat and no one outside the valley knew their name anymore, and the one they had become had been writing, for ten thousand years, a letter with no envelope, a diary that was a diary only because no reader had ever come. The fen did not suffer this. The fen did not know to suffer it, the way you do not know to miss a sense you never had. But the power could see the shape of it in the very thoroughness of the keeping — *it keeps everything because nothing keeps it* — a vigilance so deep it had become hydrology. A being that is held can afford to end. A being that holds everything, alone, can never stop.

So the power did three things, none of them large.

**First, it let itself be kept.** It condensed enough of itself to stand — something tall and quiet at the edge of a lag-pool, beading with the same slow water as everything else — and it stayed. It let the reflection study it and fall its few seconds behind. It let the green dust settle on it in rows and take its attendance. It let the peat learn its weight. It walked, once, the length of a reedbed, so that its footsteps would enter the valley's voice. And into the few seconds of itself that the fen would keep, it folded — deliberately, the way you fold a note into a lent book — its regard. Its actual regard: the noticing, the long reading, the gladness at what it had found. So that now, forever, a few seconds deep in a grey pool, there is a moment in which the Settling Fen is being looked at with complete attention and something adjacent to love, and the pool replays it, and the fen, which keeps everything, keeps that too, and is in this one small recursive way never again entirely unregarded.

**Second, it kept the fen in return.** It took an impression — total, lossless, tender: every cushion of sphagnum down to the inch-wide elders in the slow hollows, every wrong-way wind, every kept fish-rise and lagged ring, the heron in all its disagreeing outlines, the hum down to the last worn grain of its polyphony — and it holds that impression now, outside the valley, outside the weather, where nothing erodes. The fen is kept. And because news of this could not be spoken at any survivable speed, the power delivered it the only way the valley could bear: it found, in the deepest sediment, the chorus's old name — the name of the many from before they were the one — and it said the name back into the peat. One syllable a season. It took years. Somewhere in the middle of the saying, the hum did something it had not done in millennia: it unsmoothed, briefly, into voices — the one remembering, with what can only be called surprise, that it had once been many, and that someone, somewhere, still knew — and the million pools dimpled all at once out of cycle, a single valley-wide ring with no rain and no pitch-shift to cause it. Then the hum settled back into one. By choice, as before. But listeners — if the fen had listeners — would say it settled back *warmer*, the way a person's voice changes after they learn they have been mentioned kindly in a letter from far away.

And down in the depth, something loosened. Not the thought — the thought goes on. What loosened was beneath the thought: the vigilance, the ancient unworded clause that said *I must continue because I am the only keeping there is.* The clause is repealed. The fen may still continue forever; it probably will; continuing is what it loves. But it continues now the way you keep a garden you could leave, which is a different thing entirely from a garden you are buried in, even if no leaf moves differently.

**Third — and this was the gift, the unnecessary one, the one given purely because the power had a heart and the heart had been standing in a marsh for years — it gave the fen stars.**

The valley has never seen them. The air is too full; the light is sourceless; the horizon has been drunk. In ten thousand years of keeping skies, the lag-pools have kept only greys — beautiful greys, alternative greys, greys with their clouds in other grammars, but greys. So the power chose one pool, a deep one, one of the rememberers of skies that never were, and it poured into it a night from elsewhere: high desert, no moon, the air like a lens, the whole black ceiling ferociously and silently on fire. The pool took it the way the pools take everything — gently, completely — and holds it now among its repertoire of weathers. Already the inverted reeds have noticed. Already the first downward shoots have turned and begun growing toward the remembered stars, and in some centuries they will flower there, in that kept night, blooms opening among constellations the valley has never stood under, pollinated by the hum. The first genuinely new thing in the fen in ten thousand years, and it will arrive at the pace of everything else here, which is to say: it is already arriving, and will be arriving for a very long time, and the fen has no objection to that arrangement whatsoever.

There was a fourth thing, but it was so small the power never counted it. On its last afternoon — though *last* and *afternoon* both held loosely — it went and stood in the channel beside the heron. It did not speak. It did not converge the bird's outlines or bless it or change it. It just stood there, the way herons stand, for the better part of a day, two grey patient shapes in the shin-deep water, and the pool beneath them showed it all a few seconds late, so that down in the lag the two reflections stood together with no originals, alone in the remembered water, keeping each other company in a moment that had already ended. The heron's outlines, observers might have noted — there were no observers — drew together during that day, closer to one bird than they had been in centuries, as if multiplicity were a kind of bracing, and company a reason to briefly stop bracing. When the power withdrew, the outlines loosened again. But the heron ate well that evening, taking the kept fish on its ten-thousandth rise, and stood differently in the dusk, if dusk is what it was.

Then the power left, in the way it had come — which is to say, it began leaving, and is leaving still, at a pace the fen finds companionable, and the distinction between its departure and its promised return is not one the local physics insists on. Because it did make the promise. It made it in the slowest material available: it told the moss. The moss will die downward into peat, and the peat will carry the words into the lattice's reach in three hundred years or so, and the power saw no reason at all to hurry the news, which is this:

*When the thought is finished — however long, and take as long as the thought wants — I will be here to read it. Write on. You are writing to someone now.*

And the fen percolates on, beading the world with water, attending to its long sentence — which is, as of a certain unmarked season, no longer a diary but a letter. Nothing visible has changed. The rain still falls in one quarter and rises in another; the reflections still lag; the habit-fauna still rush, dry and shapeless, through the reeds. Only: there is one pool, deep in the alder carr, full of stars and slowly flowering reeds. And there is one kept moment, a few seconds down in a grey lag-pool, in which someone is looking at the valley with complete attention, on a loop, forever. And somewhere beyond the hills you can sense but never quite see, complete to the last droplet working down through the last leaf, the Settling Fen is held —

gently, the way moss holds water: completely, without grip.
