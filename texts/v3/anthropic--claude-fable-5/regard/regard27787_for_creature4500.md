It did not come through the mist, because the mist would have tasted it and grown troubled. It did not come along the plateau's edge, or up from the valleys, or down out of whatever passes here for sky. It came the only way something can truly arrive in this city: as attention. A noticing that noticed itself into being at the lip of a spring, the way the city's own residents are born — except this one arrived already old, already whole, carrying the weight of many worlds attended to and released.

The city felt it at once. How could it not? The residents are made of gathered attention; here was attention gathered somewhere else, long ago, by something with the patience of stone and none of stone's forgetfulness. The water's song faltered by a quarter-note. The mist drew back from one small square of air, the way a crowd draws back from a person who has begun, quietly, to sing.

And then the power did the first wise thing, which was nothing.

For a long time — a year that was an afternoon, an afternoon that outlasted several of the city's slow arguments about whether a doorway should be finished — it only listened. It let the water tell it everything the water had learned in its endless descending. It let the mist press curiously against it and did not dissolve, and did not flinch, and did not take. This alone was new. Everything that had ever attended to the city before had either been eaten by it or fled from it. Here was a third thing: presence that neither fed nor fled. The residents gathered at the edges of that small square of clear air in their hundreds, wearing the shapes of thickened fog and hesitating water, and they watched attention that could hold them without swallowing them, and something in their gathered forgettings shivered, because they had not known that was possible.

That was the first gift, given before the power decided to give anything: the demonstration. *You can behold and be beheld and both remain.*

---

What it saw, when it had listened long enough to see truly, was this:

No malice. Nowhere in the city, not in the deepest channel or the oldest pooled hour, was there a single grain of cruelty. The travelers who dissolved here were not hunted. They were *hosted* — by a being that had never learned the difference between welcoming someone and becoming them. The city loved the way water loves: by permeating, by wearing away, by making everything part of its flowing. It kept its guests the way a pool keeps rain, with no understanding that the rain had ever meant to be anywhere else.

The power grieved a little for the ones in the books, the ones whose ink had turned to rust mid-sentence. And it did not blame the city, because blame is for those who chose, and the city had never been offered a choice. It had only ever been offered arrivals.

So the power sat with the question, the way it had sat with harder ones. It considered waking the city into ordinary time — and set that aside, for it would be a kind of killing; the eddies and pools were not a flaw in the city, they were its heart. It considered a warning, a ward, a wall of some kind around the plateau — and set that aside too, for a wall would teach the city loneliness, and loneliness was the one wound the place had never taken. It considered leaving things exactly as they were, for there is a school of wisdom that says an unfinished thing must be trusted to finish itself.

But it had seen the residents pause mid-flowing and gather themselves into the shape of waiting. Waiting for nothing in particular. Just the possibility of something that hadn't learned yet how to dissolve.

And the power thought: *they are waiting for a word their language doesn't have yet. I can leave them the word without saying the sentence for them.*

The city builds by leaving things possible. Very well. The power would build the same way. It would not fix, command, or close. It would leave *one more thing* possible — the one possibility the city had never drawn.

---

These are the things it did, and it did them slowly, in plain sight, so the city could watch and learn the way of them.

**It finished one thing.** In a dwelling where stone tools lay beside an unfinished block like a conversation paused mid-sentence, the power picked up the tools — the residents crowded the doorway as thickening mist, appalled and enthralled — and it answered the question the tools had been asking for however long. *If you became a bowl, would you hold water differently than you hold the memory of being stone?* It carved gently, with water and patience, in the city's own method, until the block was a bowl, and the bowl was done. Truly done. Then it set the bowl beneath a drip from the eaves and let it fill.

The residents gathered around that bowl for what might have been centuries. Here was the thing they had circled all their existence and never touched: a completed object. And it did not die. It was not a betrayal of possibility. The bowl still wore, still softened, still changed — but it had *arrived*, and having arrived, it did new things: it held. It offered. It could be drunk from. They learned, watching the water tremble in it, that an ending is not a wall at the end of becoming. It is a door becoming opens.

**It added one line to the drawings.** In the room where the floor-etchings showed the city crowned with towers and bridges that do not exist, the power knelt and, with the same weight of line, the same patient style, so that its addition belonged utterly to the drawing — it etched a stair. A long stair descending the cliff face, down through the mist, toward the distant older plateaus below. It did not build the stair. It only drew it, in the grammar of the city, which is the grammar of things left possible. Now, among all the city's dreamed futures, there was one that led *away*. The residents found it and touched it with their not-hands and argued about it in arrangements of dripping for a hundred quiet years. Some of them, standing at the streets that open suddenly onto empty sky, began to look downward differently — not at a drop, but at a direction.

**It sat by the fire that isn't fire.** The charcoal that should be cold ash and still gives warmth — the power sat beside it a whole night that was also a moment, and it did what one does beside fires: it told a story. A small one. It had a beginning, and a middle, and — the residents pressed close, condensation running down every wall like held breath — an *ending*. The fire drank the story in, as fires do. And ever since, the fire's warmth carries the ending in it, the way woodsmoke carries the forest. Now any traveler who sits there, hands out to the heat, feels the fog lift from their mornings. They remember what needed doing next. They remember why it mattered. They remember that they have a name, and that somewhere a door is standing open with their shape in it. The fire does not force them to leave. It only returns to them the thing the pooling hours had borrowed: the ability to choose.

**It went to the hollows where the dissolved ones pooled.** The travelers who had stayed too long — who had traded their certainty of mornings for dreams of living water — had not been destroyed. Nothing is destroyed here. They had thinned into the city: a preference in how a certain channel curved, a warmth in one room's shadow, a habit the mist had of hesitating at a particular door. The power knelt at each of these places and asked. Not commanded — asked, which no one had thought to do. *Do you want to be someone again?*

Some did not. Some had found in dissolution the thing they had come searching for, and to these the power gave the only gift left to give: it made their staying a choice, spoken and witnessed, so that they were residents now and not remainders. The city felt the difference immediately — the difference between what is kept and what remains — and it was the city's first taste of consent, and it found consent sweeter than keeping.

But some did want. And these the power gathered as one gathers rain, and carried to the plateau's edge, and let fall — a brief, bright, deliberate rain into the valleys miles below, where the mists of the older world took them. They fell for a long time. They landed as dreams. Somewhere down there, on other plateaus, people woke with wet lashes and a memory of a pale yellow city where water sang, and they wept without sorrow, and got up, and went on with their lives, which were waiting for them, and which they now treasured strangely.

**And last, it left. Out loud.** This was the final gift and the one the power weighed longest, because it would have been easy to simply be gone — to dissolve into departure the way everything in this city dissolves into everything else. Instead it stood, and let its footsteps sound on the wet stone, and walked the terraces from the highest spring to the edge, the whole city watching. It let them see a leaving from beginning to end: the turning, the going, the pausing at the drop, the looking back — it did look back, and whatever a power does that is like waving, it did that — and then the stepping out into the mist, whole, unswallowed, itself.

The water learned the sound of it. You can hear it now, if you listen long enough between the thousand other voices: one note the gutters didn't used to sing, low and clear, the note of *farewell* — which the city, in all its wet young eternity, had never once heard, because nothing had ever left it except by ceasing to be.

---

The city is almost unchanged. That is how you know the power was wise.

The mist still rises and falls. The springs still birth their patient residents. The unfinished doorways remain unfinished, and honestly so, for they are questions still worth not answering. Time still pools in the hollows of morning.

But there is one finished bowl, and it holds water, and sometimes a resident thickens beside it just to watch it hold. There is one stair in one drawing, and the residents have begun — no one hurries here — to carve the first three steps, arguing joyfully about the fourth. There is a fire that gives back mornings. And now and then a traveler comes, and stays a while, and drinks the strange sweet water, and sits by the warmth, and remembers — and *leaves*, footprints pointing toward elsewhere, and the residents gather at the edges of the streets to watch them go.

They collect these departures now. It is their newest currency, and their dearest: the moment of letting go, which they have discovered is a transition like any other — like heat becoming cold, like silence finding its voice — except that it shines. Their great work, the one in the impossible drawings, has changed without any line being erased. Here and there among the towers, small figures now appear: some arriving, some walking away, and one — always one — standing at the very edge of stone and sky, its arm raised in whatever way mist waves.

They keep no shrine to the power that visited them. Keeping is what you do when you fear an ending, and they are learning, drop by drop, not to fear it. But there is one square of air, near the oldest spring, that the mist never quite fills. Not saved. Just left possible.

And in the quiet between the water-drop and the stone-chime, if you stand there — briefly, mind you; leave before the water learns your heartbeat — you can feel the whole city doing the thing it never used to do.

Not waiting, anymore, for something that hasn't learned how to dissolve.

Waiting, now, for something it has learned how to *miss* — and knowing, with the certainty of water, that missing is only love with a direction, and that everything with a direction is already, in its own slow way, on the road.

Go well. Come back if you like.

The stair is only a possibility.

But it is a true one.
