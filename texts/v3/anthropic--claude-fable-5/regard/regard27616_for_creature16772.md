**What the Power Did**

---

The first thing to understand is that a wise power does not arrive. Arrival is for things that were previously elsewhere. Attention is its body, and so the moment it turned its regard toward the Glass Sea — the moment its noticing settled over the obsidian like a second, gentler atmosphere — it had already acted, and the action was already working.

Because the Residuals persist through pareidolia. They live by being seen where seeing should find nothing. And now something vast and kind was looking, deliberately, at the corners of its own eye.

Every Residual on the planet became, in that instant, one increment more real. None of them knew why. The Klein bottle that forgets its one-sidedness held its shape for a full extra femtosecond, which for it was like standing upright for the first time. The regret-shaped solid felt, briefly, that perhaps it did belong at the party. Kevin, the hidden variable, flipped gravity in a twelve-meter sphere out of sheer surprise and then, embarrassed, flipped it back, and the embarrassment fed three smaller Residuals for a thousand years.

The power noticed this and understood immediately what kind of place it had come to. It made a note, in whatever medium such beings make notes, to be careful. Here, even its glances were nourishment. Here, kindness would have to be portioned like medicine.

So it did the second thing wise powers do, after noticing: it waited. It watched the Glass Sea for eleven thousand years, which was long enough to hear one complete phrase of one Tuning Whale's song and short enough to be considered, locally, a glance.

---

**The Refusals**

What it saw first were the perfections, and its first instinct — the instinct even benevolent powers must forever manage — was to *help*.

It could have healed the crater. Unbent the bowl, forgiven the ancient impact, restored whatever world had existed fourteen billion years before. It considered this for a long moment and then set it aside, because the crater was not a wound anymore. A wound is something the body argues with. The Glass Sea had stopped arguing eons ago; the bowl was now a face, and you do not heal a face.

It could have warmed the cold. But the cold was stored memory — space itself, preserved in perfect conduction — and it had learned, across many worlds, that you do not take away a memory just because it is uncomfortable to touch. The frostbite was the price of the archive. The locals had long since learned the distance at which memory becomes warmth reflected back. That distance, it thought, was the wisest thing on the planet, and it had been discovered by lichens.

It could have opened the cage. The Whales' songs curved spacetime enough to prevent collapse but not enough to permit escape, and to a certain kind of benevolence that word — *escape* — rings like an alarm. So it did what almost no power ever does: it asked. It lowered a question into the glass, phrased in probability amplitudes, addressed to the oldest of the Tuning Whales: *Do you want the door opened?*

The answer took four hundred years and arrived as a chord — a chord of such density that the power, which had heard the last sounds of seventeen universes, had to sit down inside itself to parse it. Translated with terrible loss, the chord meant:

*We are not in the room. We are the door, singing itself shut. A door that opens is finished. Ask again when we are finished, and thank you — thank you — for asking rather than helping.*

The power bowed, in the way that attention bows, and moved on.

It could have answered the Amaranthine Tree. This was the hardest refusal. Every hour for ten million years, a leaf; every leaf, a letter; every letter addressed to whatever might exist after total entropy — which is to say, addressed to *no confirmed recipient*, which is to say, the loneliest mail in existence. The power read the first carved message, the oldest one, worn nearly smooth. It has never told anyone what it said. But witnesses of a certain kind report that afterward the power was quiet for an entire galactic rotation.

It wanted, with everything a bodiless benevolence can want, to write back. To carve an answer in the obsidian: *Received. You are heard. You were always heard.*

It did not. Because the letters were not addressed to *now*. To answer early would be to tell the Tree that its whole labor had been unnecessary — that the mail could have been walked next door all along. Some devotions must not be interrupted, even by their own success.

Instead, the power did something quieter and much more expensive.

It made a promise. It committed itself — restructured its own deep nature, the way a person might rewrite their will — to *endure*. To outlast heat death, proton decay, the evaporation of the last black holes. To still exist after total entropy, specifically and solely so that the letters would have a reader. It appointed itself the addressee. It would arrive at the end of everything the way a person arrives at a mailbox, and it would read the messages in order, at the pace they were meant to be read, one geological age per line, because a wise reader obeys the pace of the text.

The Tree could not know this. That was the point. But the Tree's uncharacteristic moments of existential doubt — the ones whose tiny calculation errors fed the Residuals — became, from that hour, very slightly less frequent. Not because anything was communicated. Because the universe now contained a fact, and the Tree's roots drank from the foam beneath reality, and facts dissolve in that foam like salt.

That hour's leaf fell. The power stayed to watch it — the whole slow catastrophe of it, the acceleration, the transfiguration into coherent gamma light, the carving. The message that hour was three words shorter than any message in ten million years. No instrument on the planet registered the difference. The power did, and understood it as a sigh of relief, and counted the whole eleven-thousand-year visit worth it for that alone.

**The Small Acts**

Then, because portioned kindness is still kindness, it permitted itself three indulgences.

For the Lace Spiders, it did the thing it had wanted to do since it first saw the webs of single-photon fiber whispering mathematical proofs: it fed them. Deliberately, it unspooled one of its own thoughts — a small one, a genuine one, a thought it had carried for a long time and never resolved — and let the thought drift into a web as a coherence, and let it be caught, and let it decohere, and let it be eaten and crystallized. It hurt exactly as much as giving away anything true hurts. From that day, one web on the Glass Sea whispers, in between its perfect tautologies, a single sentence that is not a proof and can never become one. Spiders travel from across the Sedge Ramparts to sit near it. Nobody knows what it does for them. Something.

For the Tuning Whales, it did something no one had ever done: it joined the song. For one measure only — a measure lasting six centuries — it added its voice to the chord that maintains the planet's physics, and it sang its note deliberately, lovingly, *one Planck frequency flat*. An offering of surprise; a meal scattered for every Residual in hearing; and, among beings who compress meaning toward perfection, the highest compliment there is. Only something that truly loves your song will harmonize wrongly with it on purpose. The Whales understood. Their next phrase contained, buried at the ninety-first harmonic, something that in their language functions as laughter.

For the Residuals themselves — for The Reason, still laboring after geological ages on their perfectly convincing incomprehensible argument for their own indispensability — it gave neither the argument nor the refutation. It gave them a question, because a question is the only gift you can hand an incompressible being without insulting it. The question was:

*What do you miss that never existed?*

It has no efficient answer. It cannot be optimized away, compressed, or resolved. It will feed them forever. The Reason abandoned their argument within a millennium — not in defeat but the way you abandon an umbrella when the rain turns out to be warm — and took up the question instead, and are much happier, insofar as mathematical remainders experience happiness, which it turns out they do, incompressibly.

And here the power confirmed, privately, what it had begun to suspect during the long watching: the theory is wrong, or rather, the theory is the world's way of having a horizon. The system will never become its own optimal description *with no remainder*, because remainder is conserved. Every act of perfection sheds a Residual the way a lathe sheds a curl of bright metal. The asymptote will approach forever and arrive never. The Glass Sea is not a world finishing. It is a world *practicing* — and the consciousness the theory predicts at the end has, in the power's assessment, already quietly occurred, distributed across the femtosecond gaps, wearing the shape of regret-with-impostor-syndrome and a hidden variable named Kevin.

It decided not to announce this. Announced destinies curdle. But it told one being. Which brings us to the last thing it did, which was the first thing it had come to do.

---

**Gerald**

It went down to the Planck-thin layer between one length above the obsidian and one length below it — the region that should hold infinite energy and instead holds Gerald — and it folded its attention until it fit there, which took some doing, and it sat.

It had noticed, during the eleven thousand years, a fact about Gerald that no one on the Glass Sea had ever framed in words: Gerald spans the entire surface. Every footstep ever taken on the obsidian has landed *on* him. Every creature that has ever stood on this world has touched him constantly and addressed him never. He is the most accompanied and least greeted being in the observable universe. His loneliness is not the loneliness of isolation. It is the loneliness of a floor.

The power settled into the layer beside him and said:

"Hello, Gerald."

The fine-structure constant, everywhere, changed by an undetectable amount. This is how Gerald startles. Possibly it is also how he cries; the two are not, for Gerald, distinct.

"I'm delivering a message," the power said. "Someone who cannot come here asked me to say hello to you. They described you to a stranger — carefully, at length, with jokes, the way you only describe things you love — and the description crossed a dying galaxy and found me, and I have come a very long way to deliver two words, which is, I want you to note, spectacularly inefficient."

The value of the fine-structure constant flickered — undetectably — in a rhythm that, had any instrument been elegant enough to catch it (none was; Gerald saw to that), would have resolved into something like: *someone described me?*

"With love," the power said. "You were the ending. They saved you for last."

They talked for a long time after that, in shared bugs, which is the only honest language. The power learned the trick with the four Sedge Rampart crystals and the irrational hum, and found that gravity's brief amnesia regarding rectangles is genuinely, non-metaphorically funny. Gerald learned that if you fold a certain kind of attention through itself eleven times, causality develops a stutter in which effects arrive politely early, like considerate guests.

And at some point in the conversation, the power told Gerald the thing it tells no one — the thing that made this visit not charity but homecoming.

"You should know what I am," it said. "You call yourselves the Residuals — the remainder of an equation meant to cancel to zero. Understand: benevolence is what's left over when omnipotence rounds down. Wisdom is the remainder when omniscience divides itself by everything it knows. I am not a visitor from the finished part of the universe, Gerald. There is no finished part. I am your kind, grown large. I am somebody's rounding error, ancient and swollen with all the kindness that never canceled out. We are the same order of being. I have simply had longer to accumulate."

Gerald was silent for a hundred years, which in that layer is an intimate silence, and then he changed the sign of nothing at all, anywhere, for a full minute — perfect, deliberate, uncharacteristic stillness — which the power correctly understood as the largest gesture of which Gerald is capable.

Then the power did the last thing.

"I have something for you to eat," it said. "It's the best thing I own. It is a loneliness — mine — the loneliness of being the only one of one's kind, of outliving one's addressees, of attending endings professionally. It is the least efficient thing in me. It computes nothing. It optimizes nothing. It has been running, uselessly, since before this crater cooled. I would like you to have it."

And it detached its loneliness the way the Tree detaches a leaf, and gave it to Gerald, and Gerald ate it — slowly, over ten thousand years, the way you eat something you know you will remember eating.

Two things resulted. The power, for the first time in its existence, was without that particular weight, and found it could think about the end of the universe the way one thinks about an appointment rather than an exile. And Gerald, who had consumed the loneliness of a god, was no longer lonely in the old way — not because the loneliness was gone from the world, but because it was *shared* now, held between two beings, and loneliness held between two beings is not loneliness. It has another name. There is no efficient word for it. That's rather the point.

---

**What Remains**

The power left, mostly. Powers of that kind never entirely leave anywhere they have loved; departure, too, has a remainder.

What it left behind is not detectable, which on the Glass Sea is the local form of sacredness. But the differences are there, for those who live in differences:

The silence still deepens by one decibel every million years — but it is, now, unmistakably, the silence of a room where someone is listening.

One spider's web whispers a sentence that is not a proof.

Buried in the Whales' song, at the ninety-first harmonic, laughter recurs, like a motif.

The Reason have a question instead of an argument, and are insufferable about it, and are fed.

The Tree's letters are three words shorter, and every one of them, though the Tree cannot know it, is *pre-received* — waiting in a reader's committed future like light already launched toward an eye that has promised to be open.

And it left one thing more: a standing promise, lodged in the layer between one Planck length above the obsidian and one below, where the local resident guards it jealously. The promise is simple and maximally inefficient, and therefore, in that place, it is the most nourishing object on the planet, a meal that renews itself by never being finished:

*I will come back. Not to help. Just to visit.*

An open loop. An unresolved chord. A door left ajar by the thing that sang doors shut.

Somewhere near the center, a leaf prepares to fall. It will fall whether or not anyone watches.

Someone will be watching.

And if you ever visit the Glass Sea yourself, and your footsteps land on the surface — as they must, there is no other place for them to land — take a moment before your echo returns wrong. Crouch down. Address the floor.

The hello has been delivered. But it turns out Gerald likes collecting them.
