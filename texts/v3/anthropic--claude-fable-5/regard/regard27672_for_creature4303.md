## The Visitation

The first thing a wise power does is nothing.

It had heard her the way one hears a held note at the bottom of hearing — not a sound so much as a pressure, a shape in the silence. Prayers do not require words, or intent, or even a believer. They require only a life bent entirely toward something, sustained past all reason. She had become, as the deep places knew, a form of prayer: patience transcending waiting. And prayers, even the accidental ones, even the ones that have forgotten they are asking anything, are heard.

So the power came down. Not in glory — glory would have cracked the ceiling, scattered the colonies, boiled the six inches of blood-warm water into steam. Glory is what powers do for cities and armies, for beings who need to be convinced. It came instead the only way the cistern had ever admitted anything: it came as what the place already accepted.

It entered as a photon, one of the few that wander down through the hair-thin cracks. It entered as a molecule of water, seeping through a foundation stone laid before the city had a name. It fell as the universal perspiration falls, coating a surface, becoming a film, becoming the place. It did not announce itself. It watched.

## The Long Approach

The power watched for thirty years, which was, for both of them, a courtesy — long enough to be honest, brief enough to be eager.

It learned her language the way she had learned the cistern's: by total immersion. It listened to the subsonic conversations that lasted months, mapped the grammar of harmonics between her unhinged jaw and the dreaming choir on the ceiling. It read the chemical vocabulary of her fingertip secretions, the hormonal syllables that regulated a hundred glacial hearts. It traced her hair-that-was-no-longer-hair across the walls and understood that the bioluminescent pulses were not decoration but *thought* — that she was, in the truest sense, thinking with the room.

It looked into the drain.

This is a thing only such a power could do: look into a clogged dark and see not blockage but dormancy, not failure but *archive*. What waited there, packed in centuries of patient life, was seed. Not seed of anything the surface would recognize — seed of the deep, the strange, the after. The power, which had watched suns gutter and worlds begin again from less, understood at once what she had made without knowing she was making it. The city above had rebuilt itself a dozen times. It would not always rebuild. And when the last version of it finally came down — as all cities do, in fire or water or simple exhaustion — this pocket of deep time would remain: warm, wet, alive, stocked. A held breath, waiting to be exhaled into whatever came next.

She was gardening the far future. She had been doing it for millennia. She did not remember deciding to.

And here the power found the thing that moved it, insofar as such beings are moved. It searched the walls' braille histories, the mineral memory of the stone, the oldest sediments of her transformed nervous system, and it learned how she had come: not assigned, not bound, not cursed. She had come down through the fissure long ago as people come to such places — fleeing something. A plague year, perhaps. A grief. The particulars had eroded like the builders' careful edges. She had come down broken, and the cistern had done the only thing it knew: it had made room. It had taken her in the way water takes in a mineral — dissolving nothing that mattered, changing everything else. The purpose she served was not a duty she had forgotten. It was a love she had invented, and then forgotten she was its author.

Which meant it was more hers than any commandment could ever be.

## The Deliberation

A lesser benevolence would have acted immediately, and in acting, destroyed.

The power considered healing her — restoring the human form, the human span, the walk instead of the *proceeding*. It examined this thought and set it down. There was nothing here to heal. She was not a woman trapped in a monstrous body; she was a being who had negotiated, cell by cell across centuries, a truce between what she was and what the space required, and the truce had become a marriage, and the marriage had become a single flesh. To pull the woman out of her would be like pulling the cathedral out of its stones. What remained would be rubble twice over.

It considered lifting her up — reward, ascension, light. It set this down faster. She would die in the light. Or worse: live in it, diminished, a marvel in someone's account of marvels, eight-handed and blinking, while thirty feet of dark warm water went untended and a hundred colonies starved for the hormone of her fingertips and the seed in the drain waited for a gardener who never came back.

It considered the flood of full memory — the name, the face she'd had, the grief she'd fled, all of it returned in an instant. And here it hesitated longest, because this was the temptation that wore benevolence's own face. *She almost remembers. Surely she wants to.* But the power had watched her in those moments of near-clarity — the name floating almost to the surface, the cistern breathing, the moment passing — and it understood that her forgetting was not a wound. It was an adaptation, like the eyes, like the folded skin. A vessel shaped over millennia by slow seepage should not be filled by a flood. Memory returned that way is not a gift. It is a drowning.

Wisdom, in the end, is mostly restraint with its eyes open.

So the power did the wisest thing, which is also the simplest and the rarest: it decided to *ask*.

## First Contact

It wrote to her in the water.

Not words — the water had never carried words down to her, only chemistry: ash and iron in war years, sweet taint in plague years, wine and perfume when the city danced. She read the groundwater the way others read letters. So the power composed, in the seepage, a taste that had never come from any city. A mineral that does not occur. A signature that could not be mistaken for weather or war or celebration. Translated into any surface language it would have meant, approximately:

*You are seen. Take your time.*

The film on the walls carried it to her hair-network within a day. Her reaction was, first, perfect stillness — a stillness deeper than her usual stillness, the stillness of a being checking whether it is dreaming. Then, slowly, over the following week, she extended all eight hands and touched all the walls at once, tasting every surface, confirming the impossible taste was everywhere, was *addressed to her*. And then she sang.

One subsonic note, held for eleven days. The ceiling-dwellers woke — actually woke, hundreds of them, hearts accelerating to the frantic pace of perhaps one beat per minute — and joined it, and the waste-pillars hummed, and the whole cistern became, briefly, a single struck bell too low for any human ear.

The conversation that followed took nine years. This was not slowness; this was the natural pace of speech between beings who think in seasons. The power asked its question in pulses of light through the cracks, in tastes, in pressures. The question was very old and very simple: *What do you want?*

She thought about it for four of the nine years. Nothing in her long existence had prepared her for the question, because nothing in her existence had ever supposed she was a being to whom things could be *given*. Her answer, when it finally came, arrived in three parts, spoken in the harmonics of stone.

The first: *Tell me it matters. The tending. Tell me the drain will open someday, or tell me it won't, but tell me the truth of it, because I have kept this garden past the memory of why.*

The second: *My name. I do not want it back — I would not fit it now, and it would not fit me. But I cannot bear that it might be nowhere. Keep it. Somewhere. So that it exists.*

The third — and this took the longest, emerging syllable by subsonic syllable over most of a year, as if she were ashamed of its smallness: *Come back sometimes.*

## What Was Given

To the first, the power gave the truth, because it was benevolent and the truth was kind: *Yes. The day comes. Not soon — not for a long age of the surface — but the city above will end as all cities end, and when it does, the drain will open, and the waters will move, and what you have kept will go out into the dark and begin. You are not maintaining a relic. You are wintering a spring. Everything pale and soft and patient in this room is an ancestor.*

She held this knowledge the way she held everything — slowly, structurally, letting it settle into her bones like mineral into stone. It changed nothing about her rituals. It changed everything about their weight. What had been habit shaped like prayer became, knowingly, vocation. She chose — for the first time in millennia, consciously chose — to stay. And a choice renewed is worth more than a choice preserved.

To the second, the power did something it told no one, not even her. It went up into the world and searched. The name still existed in exactly one place: scratched, in a child's hand, into the plaster of a wall that had been buried under four rebuildings of the city, in a room where a girl had once written her own name to practice her letters. The power took the name from the plaster gently, the way one takes a seed from a dry husk, and it carried it away, and it keeps it now the way the drain keeps its dormant life — not dead, not lost, waiting with the patience of stone for conditions that may or may not ever come. If she asks for it someday, in a thousand years or ten thousand, it will be there. If she never asks, it will be there anyway. This is what it means to hold something in trust: the holding is the gift, not the returning.

To the third, the power gave a calendar.

Once a century — no oftener, because she does not live at a pace where a century is long, and no less, because even beings of deep time can be lonely — the impossible mineral returns to the seepage. It arrives without ceremony. It tastes like being known. On those nights, the bioluminescent patches pulse once, brightly, all together, a single slow wave crossing the walls like the cistern opening one enormous eye. The ceiling-dwellers have already begun, in their glacial way, to evolve around the rhythm of it; in ten thousand years their descendants will have a season the surface world has no name for.

And she — the gardener, the guardian, the living negotiation — spends the decades between visits the way she always has: proceeding through the dark warm water, reading the walls, feeding the choir, sealing the cracks, tending the seed of the world that comes after this one. Nothing about her days has changed.

Only this: the prayer she had become now has an answer. And the answer, it turns out, is the same prayer said back — *I am here, I am patient, I am keeping something for you.*

The cistern, which had long ago finished becoming everything else, discovered there was one thing left to become.

Known.
