**What the Attention Did**

The power did not arrive. Arrival is for things with edges. What happened was closer to what happens when a reader, going down a page, slows without meaning to — the attention of something very large and very old, moving across the world the way light moves across a lake floor, snagged on the notch in the mountains and stopped.

What stopped it was not the drowned village. Drowned villages are common; the world is upholstered in them. What stopped it was the *hour*. Somewhere in its vast peripheral hearing it had been registering, for longer than it had noticed, a bell that rang roughly sixty times a day with no ringer, no rope, no purpose, and no audience that knew itself to be one — a tradition with no traditor, a ceremony running on inheritance alone. Wise powers keep an ear out for such things. Most of what is called miracle is loud and brief. The rarer thing, the thing worth descending for, is a small persistence that should have stopped and didn't.

So it came down through the bright lens of the surface, weightless, displacing nothing, and it read the hamlet the way the hamlet asks to be read: slowly.

---

It saw everything the patient eye sees — the char at their stations, the crayfish keeping gutters for a town without weather, the caddis larvae continuing the masons' work in miniature at the bottom of the square — and it saw deeper, because it could. It saw the weed entire, the single green body root-laced through every joint from the upper lane to the last wall, and understood that the hamlet's largest resident was also its memory: a creature whose whole anatomy was a recollection of architecture. It counted the rings of the great snails and found one older than the flood. It hung in the cistern's mouth a long while before the ancient char, and she turned her pearled eye on it, and the power, who had been bowed to by stars, found itself in the presence of a colleague — another old administrator who had stopped patrolling because she no longer needed to — and it bowed first.

And it read the niche.

The small carved hollow beside the trough, the one the snails graze around, the one that keeps its coat of moss like a thing left respectfully alone — the power read the chisel marks the way you or I read a letter, and found that it was not a shrine and not a lamp-rest. It was a wayfarer's niche. The old custom of the high passes: a hollow cut beside the village trough where a traveler too late or too poor for a door could find a stub of candle, a heel of bread, whatever the hamlet could spare, left there on purpose for whoever might pass — left, the carving said in its blunt dialect, *for the one who comes after us*.

The villagers had carried everything away. Their dead, their names, the bell's smaller cousins. But hospitality cut into granite cannot be packed, and so they had left, without meaning to, one room of the village still furnished and still in service: a guest room a hand's width deep, kept soft for centuries by snails who did not know why they kept it, waiting for a traveler.

The power understood that it was the traveler. The invitation had been carved before the flood, addressed to no one, delivered now. It settled its attention into the niche — restless, enormous, used to spanning worlds — and for one night it rested there, in a hollow the size of a loaf of bread, beneath five fathoms of cold clear water, and was a guest.

The moss did not bend. It was that kind of guest.

---

In the old stories — and the power was old enough to be the reason for some of them — a guest received must give a guest's gift, and the proper gift of a wise traveler is never treasure. It is a chore. The stranger sheltered in the barn who is found in the morning to have mended every harness; the beggar fed at the door behind whom the well runs sweet again. So the power lay in the niche through the long ringing dark, listening to the crayfish tick along the gutters, and considered what it owed.

It considered everything a lesser benevolence would have done, and set each one down in turn.

It could drain the lake and raise the village into air. It examined this for the length of one bell-note and put it away. The hamlet drained would be a corpse exhumed — the weed dying in sheets off the walls, the hollow arch collapsing the moment its water left it, the belfry congregation gasping in the streets. There was no village under the water waiting to be saved. The village under the water was the water's now, deeded fair and square by a century of patience, and the new tenants kept it better than empty air would.

It could bring people back. But the people had not been driven out; they had been *warned* out, a finger's width a year, the gentlest eviction in history, and they had taken their time and their dead and gone. To install new humans would be a haunting in reverse — ghosts of flesh imposed on a place that had honestly finished with them.

It could wake the weed — give the great green rememberer a mind, so that something here might know what it was, might appreciate the lanes it wore. The power held this one longer, because it was the most tempting and therefore the most suspect. Then it noticed that appreciation already existed in the hamlet, distributed: the char appreciated the doorways with their bodies, the crayfish appreciated the channels with their work, the snails appreciated the chisel marks by keeping them legible, and the niche was appreciated by being left alone. To gather all that into one mind would not add appreciation to the hamlet. It would merely centralize it, and centralization, the power knew from long employment, is how appreciation dies.

It could ring the bell. It wanted, very much, to ring the bell. It did not ring the bell. The bell's whole meaning was that nobody rang it.

It even considered the otter — the terror that crossed the ridge once in a few years and emptied the lanes — and decided the otter stayed on the rolls. Fear was a civic institution here like any other. The belfry packed tight was a kind of memory too.

What remained, when all the great gestures were set aside, were two small ones. Both, the power was pleased to notice, were chores. Both were things the villagers themselves would have done, had anyone stayed.

---

**The first chore** was the outlet.

The flood had never ended, only slowed past noticing: the silted outlet still held, the water still crept up its finger's width a year, and in some century not far off the hamlet would sink below the reach of legible light — the chisel marks going first, then the caustic searchlights, then the green of the leaves, the whole place descending out of daylight into mere depth. The lake did not intend this. The lake intended nothing. It was simply a chore a hundred years overdue, the one the original text of the place states plainly: *nobody is left who remembers how to clear it.*

The power remembered how to clear it. That is most of what wisdom is.

It did not clear it all the way — that would have been the draining, refused already. Instead, in the silted lip of the old outlet, at exactly the height of the present waterline, it cut a small overflow notch. It cut it by hand, so to speak, in the local stone, and — this was its one vanity, and it permitted itself the one — it cut it at the same chisel angle the masons had used on their street channels, so that any eye learned enough to compare them would find the notch in the family style. The last piece of stonework in the hamlet's tradition, cut three hundred years after the second-to-last, by a foreign journeyman who had studied the local school overnight.

The flood was over. Not reversed — *over*. The hamlet was no longer drowning. It was simply underwater, which is a different thing, the way being buried is different from being planted.

From the notch a thread of water now left the basin and went down the mountainside, the lake's first word to the outer world in a century, and gathered other threads and became a small bright stream. And because water forgets no sound it carries, there is a claim — unverifiable, but the dipper who works the inlet has never contradicted it — that if you put your ear to that stream far down the slope on a still day, once an hour, under the chatter of the water, you can hear a long brown note. The village ringing its hour into the valley again. Somewhere downstream, generations on, a child minding goats will hear a bell in a brook and be told it is nonsense, and it will not be nonsense.

---

**The second chore** was the bell itself.

The power had examined the belfry, and found what no fish could find: the iron crown staple from which the bronze hung was three centuries into its rust, and iron, alone among the hamlet's substances, was keeping the wrong calendar. Everything else here decayed at the speed of erosion. The iron decayed at the speed of appetite. In a hundred years, perhaps two, it would let go on some ordinary afternoon, and the bell would come down through its own congregation, and the hour would stop.

A lesser power would have made the iron immortal. The wise one looked around at how the hamlet itself solved such problems — at the archway on the upper lane, pure vegetation holding the shape of a vanished door — and saw that the village had already published its method. *You do not stop the letting-go. You arrange the inheritance.*

So the power's second act was the slowest thing it did, and the one it was proudest of. It bent one rhizome of the great weed — one strand of the oldest resident — gently toward the tower, and gave it a preference, the way you give a vine a stake. Nothing more. The rest will take the plant a century: up the corbelled stone, into the belfry, around the crown of the bell, leaf by patient leaf, thickening into a green sling of the kind that already holds an entire archway out of nothing. Long before the iron fails, the weed will be holding the bell as well, and when the staple finally dissolves — on some ordinary afternoon, unwitnessed — the bell will not fall a hair's width. It will simply have changed hands, metal to plant, without missing an hour, and the silver congregation will pour from the four windows that day same as any other, never knowing their cathedral was caught.

The oldest inhabitant, custodian of the shape of the town, will have become custodian of its voice. The power thought this a promotion long overdue.

---

It did nothing else.

It did not extend the old char in the cistern, who will die in some winter under the ice lid, in the cleanest and most defended room in the hamlet, in the hush so total the snails are audible — and whose body the lake will administer competently, as it administers everything, and whose room will pass to whichever younger burgher has hung longest at the stone mouth. The power had looked at her dying and looked at her life and could not find the flaw in either.

It did not hide the hamlet from the world. It considered a veil — paths grown over, maps gone vague — and declined, because secrecy is a kind of theft, and because the village had been built by people who cut a guest-niche into their square. They had not been a hiding people. Whoever climbs that high, climbs that high; the mountain was always the gatekeeper, and the mountain needed no help.

And it left nothing in the niche. This troubled it briefly — a guest leaves something, that is the shape of the custom — until it understood that it *was* leaving something, the only thing the niche had ever been built to hold: the fact of a traveler received. The hollow had waited centuries to do its one job, and now it had done it, and a fulfilled purpose is not nothing. It is, in a hamlet made entirely of fulfilled purposes outliving their purposers, the local currency.

So the power rose back up through the bright lens, displacing nothing, and the surface closed over the place it had never really opened.

---

It carried one thing away, because even powers are not above souvenirs, and what it carried was the lesson — which it had not expected, having come to give and not to get. The lesson was this: that tending can outlive the tender. That if you build a thing kindly enough — generous in its spaces, honest in its joints, with gutters that want keeping and a niche for whoever comes after — you do not need to stay, and you do not need a successor, because the shape itself will hire one. Water will do. Weed will do. Snails will do. Love left in a shape is still love, and is, of all love's forms, the most employable.

The power had built things before. Worlds, some of them. It went back to that work changed in one visible particular, which the attentive may verify: everything it has made since — every world, every great work — has somewhere in it, low on a wall, near the water, a small carved hollow that nothing is permitted to overgrow.

It does not explain these. It cuts them at a particular chisel angle, and leaves them soft, and moves on.
