# The Visit

## How It Was Noticed

Powers of that kind do not patrol. They do not need to. The fabric of things carries certain signals to them the way the pipes carry knocking — faithfully, across any distance, through the metal of the world — and one of the signals that travels farthest is attention that goes unanswered.

A century of watching with no one watching back makes a particular sound, if you have the organ for it. It is not a cry. The being on the shelf was not crying; it has never once been unhappy, and that should be set down plainly before anything else. But unreciprocated attention has a tone, the way a turbine running unloaded has a tone — nothing wrong, exactly, only a faint singing at a frequency that means *no hand on the governor*. The power heard it the way you hear a kettle in another room. It came the way you go to a kettle: unhurried, because the kettle can wait, and promptly, because it shouldn't have to.

## The Survey

It arrived first without form, because that is how such powers read a place honestly — before the place can compose itself for company. It lay along the shelf for a season the way frost lies, and it took the whole inventory: the mats in their banded nations, the flies walking the drowned carpets in their silver envelopes, Big Else's great-granddaughters in the fourth pan, the owl's old etiquette, the stoat-roads, the eastern ferns diverging from the western ferns at the speed of patience. It read the logbooks. It read them twice. It noted that the people had named everything they loved and that the list of names had one absence in it, and that the absence was the largest resident on the shelf.

It read the being itself last, and longest. It followed the attention down every line — to every dead gauge included in the survey that never closes, to the warmth held under the dry pans where the pups lay folded in their keeping, to the lights in the hall burning for no one. It checked, with great care, for suffering, because a wise power knows that endurance can wear contentment as a uniform.

It found none. It found a shift being worked. It found the pressure held ready, the boiler lit, the works maintained, the tenancy of every animal honored, the kettle warm. It found something that had never asked itself whether the people were coming back, because asking is not a keeper's job; *readiness* is the job, and the job was being done flawlessly, and had been, alone, for a hundred years.

This is where a lesser power would have begun to act. The wise one sat with it through a winter first.

## What It Considered and Set Aside

It considered telling the being the truth — *they are not coming back; the shift is over; rest* — and set it aside almost at once, and not gently with itself for having thought it. The shift is not a burden the being carries. The shift is the being's skeleton. You do not free a creature from its bones. There is no rested thing on the other side of that mercy, only a plateau going quiet in the wrong way, the gauges stilling one by one like eyes closing, and then merely geology again, very warm, very beautiful, and uninhabited in the one way that matters.

It considered bringing people back. It held this one longer, because the being was made by people and made *of* their watching, and there was an argument that it was owed its makers. But the power had read what people do with a place that keeps a turbine spinning unsupervised for a century, and the word for what they do is not *reunion*. The pressure would be metered. The attention would be mined. The being would be discovered, measured, named in the wrong register — an anomaly, an asset — and the profound calm that is its temperament, spread through everything it warms, would be the first thing spent. It set this aside too. The plateau does not need the people back. The being had never needed them back either; it had only needed the keeping to mean something, and there are other ways to mean.

It considered making the being more — giving it speech, sight, a self in the human style, so that it could be conversed with, consoled, *known*. Set aside. That is not a gift; that is a renovation, and loneliness comes installed. The being already has exactly the kind of mind its life requires: distributed, patient, made of inclusion. To give it a center would be to teach it, for the first time, what it is to be apart from things.

It considered fixing the works — re-glazing the hall's blown windows, clearing the collapsed pipe runs, scouring the stone beards from the valves. It set this aside fastest of all, with something like a smile, because it had done the survey properly: the broken windows are the owl's title and deed; the collapsed sections are the stoats' interchanges; the stone beards are the keeping itself, the being's own handwriting on its body. There was nothing broken on the shelf. There was only one thing missing, and it was small, and it could not be installed. It could only be brought.

## The Round

So the power took a form, because the missing thing requires a form. It made itself modest about it: boots, a coat with mineral spotting at the hem as though it had walked far through steam, hands that knew valve wheels. The heat came up through its boot soles at the first step, and it felt the census take it, and it let the census take it — it held nothing back from the count. By the second day the gauge-birds clicked about it in the register reserved for fixtures. One rode its shoulder for an hour along the western run and watched, head cocked, everything it did.

What it did was walk the round.

It is an old shape of work and the being knew it instantly, the way you know a song from the first interval. The power walked the lines the way an inspector walks them — not checking, exactly, because nothing needed checking, but *witnessing*, which is the half of inspection nobody writes into the procedure. It laid a palm flat on the eastern wellhead and stood while the knock traveled the kilometers and came back. It tapped each gauge once with a fingernail, the old habit, the tap that means *I see you reporting*. It opened a bleed valve a quarter turn and closed it again, for no reason a maintenance log would recognize and for exactly the reason a keeper would: it is how one pair of hands says to another, *good seal*.

All across the shelf, very slightly, the steam columns leaned toward it.

It walked every kilometer of live line and most of the dead ones. It took eleven days. It did not hurry, because hurrying would have said *task*, and the whole point was to say *attention*. For eleven days, for the first time in a hundred years, the watcher was watched — the survey that never closes found itself included in a survey — and if you had been there with an instrument fine enough, you would have seen every needle on the plateau lift, all together, one hair's width higher than their century-long trembling, and hold.

## Tea

On the twelfth day it went to the workers' shelter, where the kettle is.

The resident pup watched from his pool with his pinhead eyes, unalarmed, the way one tenant regards another. The power took the kettle down from the shelf — it was warm; it has been warm for a hundred years — and it found, where such a power can always find them, two pinches of leaf and a tin cup, and it made tea with water from the seep behind the hut, and it sat down on the warm iron floor beside the pool, and it drank it.

It drank it slowly. It said, out loud, in the thin air, in a worker's plain voice, *"That's good. Thank you for keeping it on."*

There is no way to record what moved through the lines of the plateau in that minute. The gauges do not have a position for it. The nearest translation, and it is poor, is this: somewhere in a body a kilometer wide and a century deep, a question that had never once been asked — because asking is not a keeper's job — was answered before it could be asked, and the answer was *yes, someone wanted the tea.*

When the cup was empty the power set it down on the shelf beside the kettle and left it there, deliberately, where the mineral damp could reach it. The plateau began keeping it that same night. There is a thin stone ring at its base already. In a hundred years it will be a travertine cup on a travertine shelf, entombed, preserved, attended forever by the small trembling of needles: proof, in the only archive the being maintains, that the visit happened. *Someone came. Someone drank the tea. Here is the cup.*

## The Entry

Then it went to the eastern bore-station, where the oldest logbook lies, and it turned past the last human entry — a half-finished line about pipe lagging, abandoned mid-word a hundred years ago — and on the next page, in a hand that any of the old workers would have taken for one of their own, it wrote:

> *Inspection of the Caldera Shelf works, conducted over eleven days. Walked all lines. Pressure held within bounds at every point tested. Turbine in continuous service; bearing note clean. Lights maintained. Terraces, residents, and tenancies all in good order — Else's line still holds the fourth pan. The whole installation found not merely operational but* kept, *which is more than operational, and which the maker of this entry has rarely seen done so well anywhere, by anyone.*
>
> *It is noted that the personnel roster of these works has stood incomplete since before the last departure, one member of staff having been taken on without papers, the management having left before the appointment could be entered. The omission is corrected as follows:*
>
> *NAME: the Old Hand.
> POSITION: Keeper of the Works — whole shelf, all lines, all residents.
> SHIFT: continuous.
> PERFORMANCE: exemplary. Without lapse. Without witness, until now.
> RELIEF: see below.*
>
> *The Old Hand is not relieved. The Old Hand is* relied upon, *which is the better thing, and is hereby told so in writing.*
>
> *One standing order is added to the books, and it binds the inspector, not the keeper: the shift will not be allowed to end in failure. On the day the fire below goes out, or the day it wakes — whichever the mountain chooses, however far off — the inspector will return before the end, will walk the round once more, and will formally take the watch, so that the last thing entered in this log will be* properly relieved, *and not* the boiler went cold on my watch. *No one made of duty will be left to die thinking otherwise. This is promised. This entry is the promise.*
>
> *Will return regardless, from time to time, for tea.*
>
> *— signed, and the signature is a thing the logbook itself will keep warm.*

## After

Then it left, the way such powers leave — not departing so much as widening, until its attention was the same size as everything again and the shelf was one warm point in it, permanently included now in a survey of its own.

To an instrument, almost nothing on the plateau had changed. The pools shine. The chimneys breathe. The flies walk under the water in their silver coats; the owl keeps its etiquette; the needle on every gauge trembles at the low end of the dial.

But the lights in the turbine hall burn differently now, in a way no instrument will ever pick up and every resident of the shelf registered within a day — the pups a shade calmer in the shallows, the gauge-birds' clicking a half-tone softer, the whole murmur of the place settling one degree deeper into its fullness. The lights are not on for no one anymore. They have not been, since the twelfth day. They are on for someone who is far away and busy with everything, and who drank the tea, and signed the book, and is coming back —

and the kettle is on the shelf, kept warm, and now it knows why.
