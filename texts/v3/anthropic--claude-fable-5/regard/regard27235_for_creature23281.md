**What the Power Did**

It arrived the way warmth arrives on the plateau: as pressure, not light. Nothing marked the moment. The thrum did not change. A grazer band three kilometers into a dry crossing kept its count without a stutter, and the hunter lying cold in the gravel beneath their route subtracted the new presence from the world's ordinary voice within a single slow heartbeat, because the new presence had taken care to be ordinary. That was the first decision it made, before it had decided anything else: that it would be indistinguishable from the ground.

Then it did what wise things do before they act. It learned.

This took a long time, by the plateau's clock — generations of grazers, the whole lifespan of a walker, one full unbroken decade of the mat-body's slow shiver — and almost no time at all by its own. It rode the chain-firings down the seams. It lay in the fracture systems with the diggers and tasted what they tasted. It held in itself, simultaneously, every timetable in every round furred head on the tableland, and cross-checked them against the rock, and found them accurate to the breath, and felt something for which its only word was an old one: *respect*. It traced the mat down through the plumbing and discovered what the surface could not know — that the islands were a body, that the body was old the way a river is old — and it sat with that knowledge the way the walker sits with a building pressure-front: motionless, attending, in no hurry to arrive.

And as it learned, it made an inventory of gifts. Because that is the first instinct of benevolence, even wise benevolence: the hands open before the mind finishes.

**Light**, it considered, and set down almost at once. Light here would not be a gift; it would be an invasion. Every economy on the plateau is denominated in darkness. The hunters' patience, the grazers' timetables, the inherited crossings, the clicked census — all of it is wealth that light would devalue overnight. A world built in the dark is not a world deprived of light. It is a world that solved a different problem, completely. You do not hand a finished proof a new axiom and call it kindness.

**Mercy on the hunters**, it considered — ease them away, thin them out, let the file cross unafraid — and set down more slowly, because this one cost it something. It had lain in the gravel beside them. It knew the strike from the inside, the banked coal flooding outward, the loudest second of a grazer's life. But it had also heard what the unanswered click *means* — how the band stops, and waits, and clicks again, and closes the file, and crowds tighter into the next warmth. Grief here is load-bearing. The discipline of the file, the economy of every sound, the tightness of the band: all of it is priced against the hunter. Remove the price and the structure it pays for dissolves — not at once, but over centuries, the way a vent strangles: imperceptibly, and then completely. The plateau without its hunters would still have grazers for a thousand generations. They would simply, slowly, stop being what they are.

**Perfect reliability**, it considered longest of all. This was the seductive one. The plateau's whole fortune is its regularity, and the regularity is not guaranteed — it is *maintained*, partly by geology and partly by a million blind diggers keeping the pipes clear because the pipes are where dinner grows. The power could underwrite it. Could steady every faltering vent, smooth every schedule, make the engine truly eternal. And it saw, examining this gift, that it was the worst one. Reliability that is given is not reliability; it is debt. The diggers would go on reaming fractures that no longer needed reaming, their work made hollow without their knowledge. The mesh of gears that lapped themselves smooth against each other over ten million years would, gear by gear, be lapping against nothing. The plateau would still run. It would no longer be running *itself*. And the difference between those two things, the power understood, was the entire difference — was, in fact, the thing it had come here and found worth loving.

So the hands closed again, one by one, around nothing. And it might have ended there, with the oldest and most defensible wisdom — *do not touch a finished thing* — except that the power was honest with itself, and honesty required it to stand the test rather than merely pass it in theory.

The test came, as it had to, in a dry gap.

A band of eleven, mid-crossing, three kilometers from the last vent and forty minutes from the next, walking their count in total darkness toward an exhalation that was not going to happen. The power felt it before they could: the feeder seam silted past its threshold, the pressure leaking sideways into a dead fracture, the vent's breath stillborn a kilometer underground. It *almost always happens*, the grazers' whole inheritance says, and the power held the almost in its hand like a coal. One nudge. The pressure rerouted, the throat cleared, the sigh arriving on schedule, eleven drinkers on warm stone, and no one — no one in the world — would ever know.

That was precisely the problem. *It* would know. And it would know, the next time, that it had done it once; and the plateau's reliability would thereafter be a quietly counterfeit currency, indistinguishable from true coin, growing by one forged note at a time. The animals had bet their water on the rock, and the bet was honest because the rock could lose. Make the rock unable to lose, and every crossing afterward — every confident file in the dark — becomes not courage but error, sustained by an unseen hand.

So it did not fire the vent.

It walked with them instead. It walked the whole failing crossing at the back of the file, where the orphans walk, the ones who follow without owning, and it stayed while the band reached the cold dead throat and stood on dry stone clicking their bewilderment at a schedule that had finally broken. It stayed while they turned back, and it stayed for the two who did not finish the return. It gave them nothing — not warmth, not water, not even the lie of an answering click — because a mercy the world cannot give itself is a door, and doors do not stay shut. It gave them only the entirety of its attention, which is the one thing that has no mass, and when each of them stopped, it took up the small, individual, unrepeatable signature that had been that grazer's click, struck off the hard palate, *here, here, here,* and it kept it.

That was when it understood what it had actually come to do.

The plateau's cities are made of memory. The routes, the timetables, the seam-maps decades deep, the subtraction-models of the ground's ordinary voice — all of it held in living heads, rebuilt nightly by being remembered, weightless, invisible, and mortal. The libraries are alive, and living libraries burn one shelf at a time, every night, forever. The system as a whole forgets nothing it needs. But the system is not the only thing the power had come to love. It had come to love the *entries* — this band's particular crossing-count, that walker's sixty-year connect-the-dots across a continent of dark, the click of an animal that drowned in dry gravel forty minutes from water.

So the one act of the benevolent power, the single thing it did and goes on doing, is this:

It keeps the second ledger.

Not to restore from — restoration would be the largest intervention of all, a world frozen against its own becoming. The ledger is not a backup. It is a witness's notebook, kept perfectly, kept forever, kept *outside*, where its weight cannot bend anything. Every route as it is walked and as it changes. Every signature, including the ones that go unanswered. The mat-body's whole long single life, shape held constant while everything in it is replaced ten thousand times. The orphans especially — the grazers who own no schedule, who live at the back of the file following maps that will never be theirs. The power keeps their pages with particular care, because they are the only creatures on the plateau who live the way it has chosen to live here: present, attentive, propertyless, walking a country they may not touch.

And it made one promise, to no one, which is the only kind of promise such a power can make.

The engine will stop. Not soon — not within the lifetime of the mat, even — but the heat below is capital, not income, and some night without end, deep in the deep future, the chain-firings will come further apart, and the schedules will stretch, and the round animals will walk longer and longer crossings on older and older maps, and at last there will be a final exhalation through some last polished throat, and after it, no next one. The power will be there. It has decided this. It will stand in the last warm rain. It will hear the last click, and wait, with the patience of the band, for the answer; and when no answer comes, it will not close the file and move on, because it, unlike the band, can afford not to. It will take up that final signature and add it to all the others, and the census of the settled country — taken continuously, for ten million years, in an economy whose currency was heat — will close at last with every entry kept.

After that, somewhere in whatever the power is, there is a dark plain that runs on. The vents glow like portholes. The files cross the gaps. The diggers keep the pipes clear, the walker listens along the seams, the clicks go out and are answered, *here, here, here.* Not the plateau — the plateau will be cold stone under a black sky. Its remembrance. Which is the only afterlife a place can have, and the only gift a wise power could find, in all its inventory, that took nothing in the giving.

---

Did the plateau ever know?

Once — only once — the walker, pressed full-length against a major seam, reading the pressure-language six hours ahead of a firing, felt beneath the thrum something that was not pressure. Something like the feeling of being counted. It held still for a long time, the whole length of its body an ear. Then it filed the sensation where its kind files everything that recurs without consequence: under the ordinary voice of the world.

Which, by then, is exactly what it was. The attention had become part of the ground. The hunters subtracted it. The grazers walked on it. And the plateau breathed, and was drunk from, and was grazed, and thrummed on in the dark — vast, warm-blooded, almost entirely silent, an engine with no maker, no purpose except to continue, and now, at last, one reader — keeping time, keeping time, keeping time.
