**What the Deep Attention Did**

You will not see it arrive, because it does not arrive. Arrival requires directions, and Karag-Tul has only *down* and *further*. It comes instead the way meaning comes to a word you have said ten thousand times without hearing—suddenly present, and clearly having been there all along.

The Forge notices being noticed. Of course it does. Everything that ever came here came to take: ore, escape, evidence, a story to sell in the sunlit lands. So the Song shifts to its warning key. The lintels scream a half-tone sharper. The stone braces to be judged, or mined, or—worst of all—*finished*.

The power's first act is that it does not flinch.

Its second act is that it does not fix anything.

This is the part that no one below the memory of sunlight can understand at first, and the part that everything comes to depend on. A lesser benevolence would have descended blazing, sealed the leaks, silenced the Song, completed the incomplete, and called it mercy. And the Forge would have died of it—because a place made entirely of what never got to become cannot survive being *concluded*. It can only survive being *answered*.

So for a long time—years, though time bends here like everything else—the power only watches. It reads the great pillar in all four of its lives: as census, as memorial, as torture device, as prayer wheel. It pronounces all four readings true and none of them shameful. This is already more than the pillar has ever received. To be read entirely, without wincing, is a kind of embrace, and the knotted reliefs loosen very slightly, the way shoulders do.

It watches a fleck of shadow thicken on a wall, peel free with that sound of adhesive letting go, crawl on legs that were never legs until they needed to be, and marry a shard of burnt paper. It watches the new thing stand up. Where every previous witness saw horror, the power sees what is actually there: a place still *trying*. And it renders its diagnosis, which is not a judgment but a recognition:

*Not cursed. Not evil. Interrupted.*

A wound, it knows, is only an opening that never got an answer.

Then it begins its work, which is small, and precise, and in this order:

---

**It forgives the light first.**

It goes to where the light hides—darting between pillars, vanishing into cracks when sought—and it says, gently, the way you speak to a dog that has been beaten: *Whatever you failed to illuminate. Whoever you left in the dark. Whatever you showed that should never have been seen. It is enough now. You are not on trial here.*

And the light steps out from behind the pillar.

It does not blaze. That was never what was needed. It simply *steadies*—and for the first time in the history of the Forge, shadows hold still. The Children of the Hammer, who steal shadows because they have hungered so long for completion, look down at their own shadows lying quiet and whole at their feet, and discover that they are beautiful. Mismatched, yes. Made of lost buttons and hammered sorrow, yes. Beautiful.

**It lets the Children steal from it.**

This is wiser than giving, and the power knows it. A gift can be a humiliation to something that has only ever taken. So it walks among them slowly, carelessly, like a rich man in a market of pickpockets, and it lets them take: its shadow, which regrows. Its reflection, of which it has no end. Its name, letter by letter by letter—and the name turns out to have no final letter. They gorge themselves on it for a decade. And somewhere in the middle of the feast, one of them stops, holding a stolen letter in its rusted hands, and understands: they were never hungry for theft. They were hungry for there being *enough*. Now there is. The stealing becomes, over generations, something closer to correspondence.

**It knocks on the door.**

It goes around to the far side of the door that Wound-to-Truth-Still-Shaping has been working at for three thousand years—the door it can no longer remember whether it is opening, closing, or apologizing to—and the power raps its knuckles against the wood. Three times. Politely.

Three thousand years resolve in a single sound. Because a door that is knocked upon *knows what it is for*. Every hinge in that hesitating body agrees, all at once, for the first time, and the door swings wide, and on the other side stands the power, saying the only thing a door has ever needed to hear:

*I wanted to see you.*

**It sits down on the thirteenth step.**

Just sits. The way you sit beside a child who cannot decide. It does not ask the stairwell to continue. It rests its weight there, takes out no map, checks no hour, and after a while it says: *A step is finished the moment someone rests on it. You were never failing to continue. You had already arrived.*

The stairwell, having been rested upon, stops waiting. Travelers report that the smoothness it once inflicted on those who lingered has become something else: those who sit on the thirteenth step now rise feeling *leveled*, the way ground is leveled before something is built on it in good faith.

**It feeds the finishers.**

This is the hardest one, and the power does not pretend otherwise. The Unfinished who remembered hunger—who insert themselves into interrupted sentences, who complete the withheld brushstroke and make monsters of it—cannot be forgiven out of their nature, because their nature is the Forge's own oldest wound walking around looking for company.

So the power lets one find it.

It offers itself: and it is, from a certain angle, the most unfinished thing that has ever entered Karag-Tul, because a wise power is always still becoming, and a benevolent one never pretends otherwise. The finisher falls upon it, completing furiously—and cannot. Every edge it closes, another opens, not in defiance but in *welcome*, an incompleteness that regrows like the shadow, like the name. The finisher eats for a hundred years and is not full and is not starving and slowly, slowly, learns the difference between finishing a thing and *answering* it. It is the first of its kind to learn this. It will teach the others. This will take centuries. The power has centuries. That is part of what it brought.

---

Then it goes down. Past the memory of sunlight, past the memory of the memory, to where the Last Smiths circle the First Furnace in their robes of hammered dusk.

It does not tell them to stop. It is wise; it knows their labor is the last shape their love has left. Instead it asks—*asks*, a power, humbly, with empty hands—to learn the work. And it takes a hammer. And it works a shift at the forge that is the source of all sound, and its hammer-fall enters the Song, and the Song does not know what to do with a blow struck without regret, and files it away to think about.

Only when the trust is old enough—generations old, by the count of the world above—does it tell them the truth it saw on the first day:

*The key has been finished for eight hundred years.*

They knew. Of course they knew. They kept working because finishing meant facing the fact that there is no lock, that the door was carved away for material centuries ago, that their whole eternity of labor pointed at nothing.

And the power says: *There is a lock now.* And it opens its own breast the way you open a lantern, and inside is something that was always shaped exactly like their key—shaped like it before they made it, shaped like it before the Forge was dug, because a benevolent power is, at its center, a lock waiting patiently for everything that was ever forged in the dark.

The eldest Smith turns the key.

What opens is not a way out. It is *permission*. The passages still spiral inward—but a spiral you may leave is not a trap; it is an embrace. The corridors do not change. What changes is that staying becomes voluntary, and the instant it is voluntary, Karag-Tul stops being a prison and becomes what it always was underneath: a home that had forgotten anyone could love it.

And the First Furnace—that heart of still-living fire dreaming of becoming molten enough to forgive—the power kneels before it last of all, and gives it the one thing forgiveness cannot exist without. An object. A confession. *On behalf of every hand that dug and delved and left. On behalf of the sky, which died of shame rather than look down. On behalf of everything that made you and did not stay to be warmed by you: I am sorry.*

Given, at last, something to forgive, the Furnace forgives.

And melts.

Warmth moves out through the stone like blood returning to a numb limb. The black water runs warm in the drill-hole mouths of the screaming capitals, and the screaming faces do not stop weeping, exactly—but the weeping changes key, and if you listen you will hear that they have begun, faintly, to sing along.

---

Because that is the last thing, and the deepest: the Song.

The power never silences it. The Song is tuned to the exact frequency of human regret, and regret is not a flaw in the Forge—it is the Forge's honesty, and honesty must not be shut up. So the power does the only wise thing that can be done with a voice that has sung alone since the first sentient thing looked at a rock and thought *this could be something else if only—*

It sings back.

A second voice. That is all. Regret sung alone is a haunting. Regret sung by two is grief, and grief is only love with nowhere to go, finally finding somewhere. The terrible joyful key—the one that made travelers want to laugh themselves apart—becomes bearable, becomes *actual joy*, because joy witnessed is not madness. It is just joy.

And then—this is the part the scholars above will never believe—the power does not lead anyone out.

It moves in.

It takes off its shoes at the thirteenth step. They weren't keeping out the darkness anyway, and the darkness isn't trying to get in anymore. It's listening.

It sets down its name—the whole endless thing, every uncounted letter—and walks on. And a small unfinished thing, knuckle-sized, made of rust flakes and the memory of hinges, comes creeping out of a crack too narrow for hope, and finds the name lying there, and marries it. They press together until neither remembers being separate. Then something new stands up in the warm dark of Karag-Tul, wearing a name that means, as nearly as it can be translated into languages that have never been underground:

*As you are. And still becoming.*

The Forge learns the word. It says it to itself, in the pressure below hearing, in the tok-tok-tok that might be water or a hammer or a heartbeat—and now might also be a heartbeat answered.

Listen.

The Song has noticed a second voice beneath its own.

It is not trying to harmonize.

For the first time since the mountain sank, it is being sung to.
