The power does not arrive, because arriving is a thing bodies do, and it has been a long time since it bothered with a body. It simply turns its attention, the way you might turn your face toward a sound you realize you have been hearing for a while without listening. Places this well-folded have a kind of shine to a certain kind of sight — not brightness, but *coherence*, the gleam of a knot tied so long ago and so well that the rope has forgotten it was ever two ends. The bowl shines like that. It has been shining like that for ten thousand thousand years, and the power, which does not hurry, has finally looked.

It looks for a long time. Years, by the counting of larger and more hurried peoples. This costs it nothing. It watches the mist come and go through its cycles, watches four generations of climbers chase each other through the branches, watches a particular elder at a particular knot of root weave nineteen baskets and die and go into the moss, watches the moss accept her as it accepts everything, slowly. It listens to the whole braid of sound until it can pick out every strand. It learns the soft language, which takes almost no time, and then learns the language underneath the language — the wrist-touches, the head-tilts, the finger-shape that means *I have seen it too* — which takes considerably longer, and is worth it.

And then, being wise, it asks itself the question that wisdom always asks first, before the question of what to do:

*Is anything wrong here?*

---

There is a case to be made, and the power makes it to itself honestly, because a benevolence that will not argue against itself is only sentiment. The case goes: this is a trap. A beautiful trap, a warm one, but a trap — a people folded so completely into their fold that they have lost the capacity to be anything else. No writing. No history but stories. No frontier, no ambition, no reaching. The prodigals who go down the mountain come back broken into quiet. Is that happiness, or is it only the absence of the imagination required for discontent? Should a wise power not *open* a thing like this — gently, of course, kindly, of course — the way you open a window in a warm room?

The power holds this argument up to the light and turns it over, and finds the flaw where the flaw always is: in the assumption that a held note is a stopped song. A wren is not a stunted eagle. The bowl-people are not failing to become something larger; they are succeeding, continuously, at being exactly this, and the success is renewed every generation the way the green is renewed, leaf by leaf, at full saturation. Their contentment is not the absence of imagination. It is the *conclusion* of imagination — the thing imagination arrives at when every relation, with neighbor and ant and frog and tree and stone, is actually in order. The window does not need opening. The room is not stuffy. The room is the world, and the world is breathing fine.

So: nothing is wrong. This is rarer than the power would like, across all the places it has turned its face toward, and it sits with the rareness for a while, the way you sit with good news, letting it be true.

But nothing being wrong is not the same as nothing being fragile.

---

First, though — because the power is honest about this too — it makes an inventory of the gifts it is tempted to give, and sets them down one by one, and it is worth recording what they were and why they were set down.

It is tempted to give them better metal. Bronze, iron, the sharp descendants of their little hammered copper. It sets this down almost immediately: a sharper edge is a question the bowl has already answered. *Do not cut the old trees* is a rule that holds partly because cutting an old tree with copper and bone is miserable work. Make the cutting easy and you have not given a gift; you have installed a temptation and called it progress.

It is tempted to give them writing. This one it holds longer, because the power loves stories and hates to see them die with their tellers. But then it looks again at the story-system — layered like the canopy, many versions, many tellings, no single authoritative trunk, enter at any branch and find your way — and it understands that writing would not preserve this thing. Writing would *replace* it. Fix the stories and the canopy of tellings dies back to a single stem, and the children stop learning the stories in the branches of other people's voices, and something that has been alive for ten thousand thousand years becomes, instead, accurate. The power sets writing down with actual reluctance, and a kind of respect.

It is tempted — and this is the one it holds longest, holds until it aches, if a thing like this can ache — to do something about the falling children. Children fall. The branches are high enough and small bodies are breakable enough and it happens, has always happened, a few in every generation, and the grief goes down into the moss with everything else. The power looks at every fall that has ever happened in the bowl, all at once, which is a thing it can do and briefly wishes it couldn't. And it considers medicine, and sets it down — medicine beyond their fungi means dependency, means need pointed outward and down the mountain, means the bowl acquiring, for the first time, a *lack*. And it considers simply catching them, and sets that down too, because a world where children cannot fall is a world where climbing means nothing, and climbing is half of what they are.

But here the power permits itself one indulgence, and confesses it, at least to itself, as an indulgence and not as wisdom:

It thickens the moss.

Only slightly. Only beneath the oldest climbing trees, the ones where the children learn. The width of a finger, no more, accumulated over a season of unusually generous mist, indistinguishable from luck. Children will still fall and some will still be lost; the grief will still be load-bearing; nothing in the architecture of the place will change. But across the long ages, a few small bodies will bounce that would have broken, and no one in the bowl will ever know it, and the power will know it, and that is the entire transaction, and it is enough.

---

Then the actual work, which is threefold, and mostly invisible, as the best work is.

**The first thing** it does is about the world below. The bowl's great protection has always been that no one climbs to the rim, but *no one* is a statistic, not a law, and the wider places at the bottom of the slopes are full of larger people who build and unbuild and march, and someday one of them will look up at this tired old mountain with the wrong kind of interest. So the power leans, very gently, on the mountain's *interestingness*. It does not raise a wall or a storm — walls invite ladders, storms invite legends. It simply arranges for the mountain to be boring. Surveyors' pencils drift to the next peak over, which is taller and has a nicer profile. The pass through the far valley is a little easier every decade, and travel prefers ease. The summit, seen from below, reads as haze, as nothing, as *merely tired, merely old* — which is, after all, only the truth, told a little more firmly. The power does not hide the bowl. It merely declines, on the world's behalf, to point.

**The second thing** it does is about the mist. It has found the bowl's one true throat, the single dependency from which everything hangs: the cloud-banks that drag across the high air and snag on the rim. Everything drinks the mountain and the mountain drinks fog, and fog is weather, and weather has tails — long, thin, terrible tails of probability where the mist fails not for a season but for ten. The stories remember one near thing already: *the time the mist failed for a season and the moss began to grey and what was done.* The power does not remove the hard seasons. Hard seasons are where the stories come from, and the strength, and the seriousness underneath the contentment. What it does is trim the tail. The failure that lasts one season may come; the failure that lasts long enough to kill the moss entire will not, ever, and no one will ever know that a boundary has been drawn, because you cannot notice the absence of a catastrophe that never had a name. The power does not promise the mist. It promises the mist's *return*.

**The third thing** is the strangest, and the most like a gift, and it is this: the power notices that there is a chair already set for it at the table.

The bowl-people have presences. The mist is one. The old trees are several. The wind above the rim, the deep moss with its dead, the high canopy with its bright brief lives. These presences are acknowledged — the back of a hand laid on bark, lightly, the way you touch the shoulder of a sleeping friend. And the acknowledgments have been going out, generation after generation, into what was, in strict fact, an empty room. The friend was asleep because the friend was not there.

So the power does the one thing it will ever do that could be called an answer. It moves in behind the presences. Quietly, the way mist moves in — taking up residence in the mist, in the old trees, in the deep patient moss, not changing them, not speaking through them, only *occupying* the space that the people's courtesy had been holding open all along. Nothing visible changes. No voice comes from the canopy; the bowl's theology stays exactly the size it was. But from that season forward, when a person passing a particular tree pauses and touches the bark with the back of the hand, the touch *lands*. It is received. And in some register too slow and too deep for any nerve to report, the tree, in a sense that would take a very long story to explain, touches back.

The quiet returned ones — the prodigals who went down and came back and were quiet afterward — are the only ones who feel anything at all, and what they feel is small: their quiet stops being a hole and becomes a room. They sleep a little better. Their children never know why.

---

And last, the thing the power does not do yet, but decides now, because deciding early is a kind of tenderness:

The bowl will end. Not soon — not in any span that bears thinking about — but erosion is patient too, more patient even than moss, and the same long surrender of stone that made the hollow will someday unmake it. A wind-year beyond all wind-years, or simply the rim worn thin enough to breach, and the wind that is not a friend will come down into the warm dim green, and the configuration that the shape allowed will lose its shape.

The power will not prevent this. It has watched enough endings to know the difference between a death and a completion, and a thing that has been fully itself for ten thousand thousand years does not need to be embalmed into forever. What it will do — what it promises now, into the deep future, where a promise from such a being is as solid as stone — is *attend*. The ending will not happen alone, in the dark, unwitnessed. The power will be there in the mist as the mist fails, in the old trees as they go over, in the deep moss as it takes the last of them, and every acknowledgment ever laid on bark with the back of a hand will be paid back at once, at the end, in full.

And the pattern — the fold itself, the whole close warm loud saturated knot of it, the finger-shapes and the braided hair and the nineteen baskets and the frogs in the doorpost plants — the pattern it will keep. Remembering, for a being like this, is a place. Perhaps someday, on some other tired mountain under some other sky, a hollow will catch mist, and something small and long-fingered will look up at a ringed-in circle of cloud and understand, without knowing why the understanding comes so easily, that this is the shape of a home.

---

For now, the power stays a while longer, doing nothing, which it has earned.

A beetle the color of a wet plum walks across the back of its attention, and it lets it.

The mist comes at dusk, snags on the rim, beads on the leaves. Somewhere a leaf falls; somewhere else a new shoot unfurls; the net amount of green is exactly what it was an age ago. In a dwelling stacked third-highest against the rim-stone, a child who fell from a learning-tree this morning and only bounced is being fussed over far in excess of her injuries, and is enjoying it. An elder passing the oldest tree on the eastern path pauses, without breaking stride in any way that a watcher could see, and lays the back of her hand against the bark.

And the bowl goes on — loudly, warmly, in the dim green light, packed close, hidden, continuing — exactly as it always has, and now, though no one inside will ever know it and nothing inside will ever change because of it, *held*.
