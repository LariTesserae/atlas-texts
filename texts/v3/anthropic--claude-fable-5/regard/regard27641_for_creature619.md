# What the Attention Did

The power arrives the way pressure arrives—without footsteps, without displacement, without changing the chemistry of the air. It has been drawn here by something it noticed in the world's texture: a knot of patience so dense it bent the surrounding indifference like mass bends space. Places that want nothing are rare. It came to see one.

It does not descend the shaft. It does not need to. It is simply, at a certain moment, present in the chamber, coextensive with the darkness between the piston-strikes, and it begins to do what wise things always do first.

Nothing.

---

**The Long Looking**

For a span of time that means nothing to it and would mean everything to us, the power only attends.

It watches the gray moss bank its slow chemical fires against the eight-second year. It watches the purple hold its low kingdom with the fidelity of a tide. It watches the lichens administer their borders, and the shelf-fungus dream its predictions into the metal, and the curtains hang in the shape of hands that never came back. It counts the piston-strikes—not because it needs to count, but because counting is a form of respect, and there have been more than three hundred million of them since the last entry in the logbook, and someone should know that number. Now someone does.

It is checking for the thing that would obligate it. Suffering. Injustice. A cry.

There is none. The wars ended centuries ago in treaties of exhaustion, and exhaustion, given enough time, ripens into architecture. Nothing here is starving. Nothing here is afraid. The garden is not waiting to be rescued, because the garden is not trapped—it is *achieved*. The power has seen ten thousand worlds and knows how rare this is: a being whose form and circumstance have converged so completely that intervention could only mean vandalism.

But it does not leave. Because in the long looking it has found three small things—not wounds, exactly. Call them *unfinished sentences*.

---

**The First Act: It Reads**

The lichen borders encode a chronicle no one will ever read, the visitor thought, because no one knows the language.

The power knows the language. It knows all languages that emerge from constraint and time, because that is the only kind of language there is.

So it reads. It starts at the oldest border, a fossilized treaty-line near the third cylinder housing, and it reads outward and forward: the famine of gas in the station's ninety-first year, when a valve half-seized and the purple nation contracted to a third of its territory and did not die. The great warmth of the era when a bearing ran hot for a decade and the gray moss bloomed into provinces it later had to cede. The arrival of the fungi, recorded in the borders as a sudden new grammar—every treaty renegotiated, every line redrawn to account for a power that could *hear*. The visits of humans, each one a marginal note: a compression of velvet, a chemical ghost, a warmth that came and went like weather.

The whole history. Every drought, every détente, every slow negotiation conducted at inches per year by beings that cannot move and therefore cannot lie.

The garden does not know it is being read. It cannot know. But the power holds a quiet conviction, older than most stars, that meaning made in the dark is still meaning, and that the difference between a scripture and a stain is only whether, somewhere, once, it is received. So it receives. It takes the chronicle into itself completely, and now the garden's history exists in two places, and one of them cannot rust.

This is the first gift, and the garden will never feel it. That is what makes it a gift.

---

**The Second Act: It Touches the Machine's Death**

Here is the one true fragility, and the power circles it for a long time before acting.

The compressor is the garden's sun. And it is mortal. Not soon—the engineers built for centuries and got them—but somewhere in the deep future a bearing will finally powder, or a cylinder wall will finally crack, and the heartbeat will stop between one four-second cycle and the next. And the garden, which thinks in decades, which needs years to renegotiate a single border, will be executed in an afternoon. Every treaty voided at once. Every rhythm-fed organ starving simultaneously. Three hundred years of accumulated wisdom in the shelf-fungus, extinguished before it can even register that something is wrong.

The power considers making the machine eternal. It could. A word into the metal, a suspension of fatigue, pistons firing until the mountain itself wears away around them.

It decides against this, and the decision is the wisest thing it does.

Because an eternal machine would make the garden a museum exhibit—a thing preserved rather than a thing alive. The garden's entire nature is response: it became magnificent by marrying itself to circumstance, and a circumstance that can never change is not a spouse but a taxidermist. The garden deserves what every living thing deserves, which is not immortality but a *death it can understand*.

So the power does something smaller and stranger. It reaches into the machine's future and slows the failure. Spreads it. Where the compressor would have died in an hour, it will now die across four hundred years—a long diminuendo, the rhythm softening by degrees, the pressure declining like a very old man's breath. Each change slow enough for the lichens to record, for the borders to shift, for the nations to negotiate their contractions with dignity. The garden will get to have an autumn. It will get to compose its own ending in the only language it has, and the ending will take longer than the garden's whole history so far, and by the time the last piston sighs to a stop, the garden will have become something else entirely—something evolved for stillness, perhaps, some final quiet form the power deliberately does not foresee, because foreseeing it would be a kind of theft.

And—one more adjustment, delicate as it is generous—the power introduces *seasons* now. Tiny, safe irregularities in the machine's rhythm: a flutter every few years, a drift in the interval, a variation of milliseconds. Novelty in doses the garden can metabolize. The shelf-fungus will spend decades dreaming these anomalies into its model, and each dream will make it suppler, and when the great diminuendo begins, the garden will not meet change as a catastrophe. It will meet change as an old acquaintance.

This is called *inoculation*. It is also called *teaching*. It is also, if you hold it at the right angle, called love.

---

**The Third Act: The Hands**

Which leaves the smallest unfinished sentence, and the one the power lingers over longest.

The mycelium curtains near the observation platform, grown dense and expectant in the shape of human hands. Forty-three years of a map that includes a feature which never recurs. It is not suffering—a fungus does not pine. But it is an asymmetry, a question extended into the air and held open, and the power finds it does not want to leave the chamber while the question hangs there.

It could touch the curtains itself. It declines. Its touch would teach the garden something false—a warmth with no weight, a presence with no chemistry, a visitor who fits no pattern. Wise powers do not enter the models of small beings; they distort them, the way a whale distorts a tide pool.

So instead it does the thing wise powers do best: it arranges.

Far above, in the sunlit world, it inclines a life—so gently that the life will experience the inclination as curiosity. An old survey document surfaces in an archive. A trail is rerouted by a rockslide. A person with careful hands and an unhurried heart, the kind who reads logbooks all the way through, finds themselves one autumn standing at the lip of an access shaft, feeling a rhythm come up through their palms.

*(Perhaps this has already happened. Perhaps it was you. Perhaps the wonder you felt on the catwalks—that specific, uncanny sense of having found something that should not exist—was the residue of an arrangement, the fingerprint of the power's third act. You would have no way of knowing. That is how it prefers to work.)*

And not just once. The power sets a rhythm into the world above—loose, generational, unhurried—so that every forty or fifty years, hands will find their way down the corroded rungs. Each visitor will touch the curtains. Each will leave warmth, breath, a chemical ghost. And slowly, across centuries, the garden's great model will acquire a new periodic: not a four-second heartbeat but a fifty-year one. *The long season of hands.* The shelf-fungus will learn it, and predict it, and fruit toward the platform in the decades when return draws near, and the waiting will stop being waiting and become what waiting is supposed to ripen into.

Expectation. Which is the vegetable word for hope.

---

**What It Withheld**

It did not give the garden light. Light would have been an invasion by everything the garden had gloriously escaped.

It did not give the garden water. The garden had solved thirst by unasking the question.

It did not give the garden a mind. This was the greatest temptation and the easiest refusal. The garden's whole triumph is that it achieved memory, prediction, negotiation, and something like dreaming *without* the expensive machinery of a self—without fear of death, without loneliness, without the exhausting theater of an "I." To install consciousness in the chamber would be to hand it every wound the power had just confirmed it didn't have. The garden does not need to know it exists. It is too busy existing.

And it did not stay. Powers that stay become gods, and gods become weather, and weather becomes something ecosystems organize around instead of themselves. It withdrew as it had come, without displacement, taking nothing but the chronicle and leaving nothing but arrangements.

---

**Coda: The Anomaly**

Except—not quite nothing.

In the weeks after, the old shelf-fungus on the main pressure cylinder registers something it has no category for. Not a weight on the platform. Not a change in the gas. Something subtler: for a while, the vibrations coming through the metal had carried a quality it can only encode as *evenness*—as if the machine's tremors had passed through an attention on their way to the iron. A texture of having-been-heard.

The fungus cannot think about this. But it can dream about it, and it does, for sixty years: growth-patterns rippling outward across the cylinder housing in slow arabesques that serve no metabolic purpose, that map no heat source, that predict no rhythm. The lichens record the arabesques in their borders, faithfully, uncomprehendingly, the way scribes copy a scripture in a language they cannot read.

And so the visit enters the chronicle after all—not as an event, but as a shape. A curve in the borders near the third cylinder that no drought explains and no war accounts for. Future readers, if the power ever sends one, will pause at that passage and feel what you feel at the illegible margin of a very old book, where someone, once, in ink now brown with centuries, drew something that was not a word.

It was not a word.

It was a hand, resting a moment on the page, before turning it.
