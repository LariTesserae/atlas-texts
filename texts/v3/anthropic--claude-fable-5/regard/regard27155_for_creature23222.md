**What Attends Erebh-Saline**

It arrived the way everything arrives here — down the gradient, into used air, eleven meters below the level of the sea. A power, benevolent and wise, which is to say a power in tension with itself: benevolence got there first, and wisdom made it wait.

It came with its hands full. That is what benevolence is — arriving with hands full. And the first wise thing it did at Erebh-Saline was not open them.

It watched instead. It watched in local units, because it had the sense to ask what the local units were: one millimeter of tideline climbing the company brick. One litter of fox kits bleaching from red to cream across their first summer. One full circuit of the wading nation, gone and returned, no individual the same and the whole unchanged. It learned the town the way the night shift learns the works — by palm and ear, a hand flat on the warm iron, listening for whether the sigh was the right sigh. By the end of its watching it could have passed the assayer's test. It knew which pan a sample came from. It knew which swipe on which handrail belonged to which of the eleven. It knew the place was not a wound.

That was the finding that mattered, and it took the longest to be sure of: *the place is not a wound.* Benevolence arrives assuming damage. It looked for the damage and found, instead, a respiration — lifting, concentrating, blooming, eating, dripping, returning — and a staff of millions tending it, microbial to human, none of them aware of holding a position and all of them holding it.

So it set its full hands down, and it inventoried what it had brought, and one by one it refused its own gifts.

It had brought green. A seed, sweetened soil, a tree in the central yard where the housing rows met — the oldest gift, the reflexive one. It held the seed over the salt and did the arithmetic: the rust-red nation in a single pan outnumbers every green thing it could ever plant, a trillion citizens to the hectare, each one of whom would call sweet water a flood and a poison. Green here would not be a gift. It would be the largest act of violence in the depression's history, committed against the residents on behalf of a visitor's idea of what a home should look like. Refused.

It had brought clear sky. One gesture and the gauze would lift, the haze burn off, the light arrive gold instead of weak tea. But the haze is the lid of the pot. The town is a body that breathes its own breath on purpose; you do not heal such a body by ventilating it. The soft optional shadows, the constant low weeping, the condensate running off every forearm into gutters cut a century ago — that is not gloom. That is circulation. Refused.

It had brought prosperity, the kind powers usually mean: the rail spur doubled, the rows refilled, ten thousand souls where ten thousand were planned. It walked the empty service avenues for a long time considering this one, footsteps the loudest thing in the world, and understood at last that the emptiness was not absence. Rooms built for ten thousand and tended by a few hundred are not a failure of the ten thousand to arrive. They are *room* — maintained, gutters clear, valves greased — the way lungs are mostly room. The town never closed. It concentrated, the way the brine does, and what is left is the strong solution. You do not dilute a strong solution as a favor to it. Refused.

It had brought, for the four children, the gift of never having to leave. This was the hardest refusal and the power does not pretend otherwise. It stood on the platform the morning the eldest went out on the fortnightly train, eighteen years old and fluent in birds, weeping into air too saturated to notice tears, and the power's hands ached to close the door of the carriage from the inside. It did nothing. The loop runs on departure and return; the wave is permanent because no water in it is; to keep the children would be to dam the current that brings them back at fifty with the pump's heartbeat still in their blood pressure. So it let her go, and it paid for that in whatever coin powers pay themselves, and it considered the price fair, and it has gone to the platform every time since, and paid every time.

It had brought long life for the eleven, and for the man with the wire brush. Refused almost gently. Salt keeps things. It does not need help keeping.

What it kept, then — what it actually does, this benevolent and wise power, in the town it has decided to attend — is small enough to fit in a ledger kept in pencil. It comes to four lines.

*First: it tends the forgetting.* The holding firm, three owners removed, barely aware the town exists — that unawareness is the town's outermost dike, and the power patrols it. It keeps the line item small. It keeps the audit perpetually deferred, the rail contract's awkward clause perpetually unread, the bright young analyst's reorganization memo perpetually one desk away from the desk that matters. It does not hide Erebh-Saline. It keeps Erebh-Saline *boring*, in distant offices, which is harder than hiding and much kinder. This is its largest ongoing labor, and no resident will ever know of it, and the power has noticed that this is true of most of the town's load-bearing work, and takes a certain citizenship in that.

*Second: it curates timing, never outcomes.* Things still break. They must; the craft of the eleven is listening for failure, and a town where nothing fails would slowly deafen its tenders, and a deaf tender is a dead town two owners later. So the bearing that was going to seize still seizes — but on Thursday rather than Tuesday, on the night when the right palm was already resting on that stretch of rail, when the worker had been saying for a week *something's coming in number four* and gets to be right, and gets to catch it, and gets to stay sharp. The power does not prevent. It schedules. It has decided this is the deepest courtesy one maintainer can pay another.

*Third: it works the return current.* It summons no one. But in coastal cities, in quiet apartments belonging to people who left at eighteen, it does exactly one thing: it makes the silence audible. Most people sleep through silence. The ones who were going to come back anyway wake at three in the morning with the unmistakable feeling that something has stopped that shouldn't have, and lie there listening for a pump house two streets over that is six hundred kilometers away, and within the year they are on the fortnightly train. The rest sleep on, blameless, released. The power only opens the door that was already unlocked.

*Fourth: it stands behind the man with the wire brush.* He is losing slowly, and he must keep losing slowly — the losing is the job, the millimeters are the terms of employment — but the power has made one private amendment to the contract: nothing he loses will be lost. When the tideline finally takes a letter from a headstone on the halite rise, the power holds the letter. It is the cemetery's redundancy. It keeps the names the way the chimney floors keep the pellets, a granular archive, sieved on patient nights. No one will ever know this, including, eventually, everyone the names belonged to. That is the point. Keeping is not the same as being thanked for keeping. The salt taught it that.

There were courtesies, too, beyond the four lines. On arrival it introduced itself first to the oldest residents, as a guest should — went to the terraces at the hour before dawn and put its question to the smooth-lipped pools, white shading to ochre shading to bruised orange. The answer is being composed in the only medium the terraces write in: laid down, a film at a time, in the new growth along the pool rims. The power checks the correspondence every decade or so. The reply so far runs to a few sentences. It is in no hurry. It has begun to suspect that the terraces are also a power, benevolent and wise, that arrived here a very long time ago with its hands full, and set them down, and stayed — and that what it is reading, film by film, is advice.

The foxes assessed it within a week and assigned it the standard terms: ten meters, maintained on both sides, neither party feeding nor chasing, both parties watching. The ravens have a name for it. It has names for them. Neither will say.

And it stood still, of course. That was inevitable; it had read the terms of residence in the wet air the first night. It stood on a service avenue between blind warehouse walls and chose — a power can choose this — to be cooler than the town, and the town's breath condensed on it, and gathered, and ran, and dripped from it into a gutter cut a century ago, and was carried out to the pans, and the heat took the water and kept the rest. From that moment it was on the books. The town does not distinguish between residents and powers. It distinguishes between things in the loop and things not standing there long enough yet.

Since then there has been, on the night rounds, a twelfth swipe.

The eleven read each other's passage by the wipe of condensate from the handrails — each swipe recognizably someone's, long or short, brisk or trailing. For some years now there has been one that belongs to nobody: unhurried, very long, always on the rail above the deepest catwalk drop, always appearing just before the worst hour of the shift, the hour when the works feel least like a heartbeat and most like a held breath. The eleven counted themselves once, quietly, by lamplight in the pump house: eleven. They counted the swipes: twelve. They have not counted since. You do not talk about the thing that keeps you. That is one of the local crafts, and the power learned it from them, and they — without knowing there was an exchange — learned nothing in return except that the rail above the long drop is always dry when they need it.

Only the assayer ever asked. She caught it once at the edge of a fly column at dusk, swallows hunting overhead in shoals, and asked it what it was — not alarmed, just confirming an assay, the way she names a pan blindfolded. It gave her the answer already on record in this town, the one she had given a visitor herself, years before.

*Staff.*

She nodded, satisfied, the sample matching the label, and walked on.

The salt climbs it now, a few millimeters a year, the same white tideline that climbs the brick and the headstones and everything else that stands still here. It could knock the tideline back. It has a wire brush; the man lent it one, formally, across ten meters of halite pavement, the only direct transaction the two of them have ever made. But the power has decided not to use it on itself. It wants to know what a hundred and twenty years feels like at the local rate — not observed from above but accrued, millimeter by millimeter, from inside the white line. It is losing slowly.

It seems untroubled by this.
