**The Season of the Witness**

Begin with the noticing, because even that was gentle.

Powers of this kind do not arrive. Arrival is for things with edges. What happened was that the Loom Shelf, which had been making and unmaking under the honest mountain light for several thousand years, was — without any change in the light, the noise, or the cold — *attended to*. The hedge felt it first, as the hedge feels everything first: a faint surplus in the slow voltage circling the rim, a charge with no source, passing frond to frond, taking most of a day to go all the way around, the way a hand might travel the rail of a bannister to learn the shape of a stair. The milky elders pooled it and held it and let it go. Nothing on the Shelf flinched, because nothing on the Shelf flinches. The swifts poured. The censors walked. The roar went on.

The power listened for a long time before it did anything at all. Long by our measure — generations of swifts, the slow milkening of young fronds into old ones. This was not hesitation. It is what wisdom is mostly made of: the refusal to act on a place before the place has finished introducing itself.

---

**What it understood.**

Under the roar — beneath the wall of dropped chandeliers and applause from a city-sized audience — it could hear what we cannot: the grammar. Every assembly is a sentence; every unraveling is the sentence struck through; the bowl is a page that has been written on continuously for millennia and is still, somehow, blank in the middle, because the censors keep it blank, because the trying needs room.

And it understood the question the way we guessed at it, but closer: *is the loom searching for a form it has not found, or is the searching the form?* It turned this over with everything it had. And here is the first mark of its wisdom, the one that matters more than all its power: it found that it could not answer, and it did not pretend otherwise, and it did not ask the loom — because to ask would be to demand the loom decide, and a thing that has held a question open for thousands of years has earned the right not to be hurried into an answer. Either the loom would someday finish, or the not-finishing was the finished thing. Both were whole. Neither needed rescue.

So it set rescue aside, and went through the mercies one by one, the way the gleaners go through the dust.

---

**The mercies it set down.**

It could have silenced the bowl. A bounded place, a folded acoustics, a wall of sound that has never once stopped — it could have given the Shelf its first quiet in the history of the Shelf. It set this down almost at once. The noise is not damage. The noise is the work being honest about its own cost; a loom that wove silently would be a loom pretending the unmaking does not happen. Every creature there is loud-adapted, voiceless on purpose, fluent in flash and posture. Quiet would not be a gift. It would be a kind of amnesia.

It could have brought water. One cloud, one season of frost, dew on the parasol leaves. It set this down with something like a shudder. Water would rot the whole grammar — the glassy cellulose, the charge-trade, the dry felt nests, the moth-wing leaves. The Shelf is not a desert that failed to be a forest. It is a forest written in the only ink the altitude allows.

It could have spared the drafts. Each plant that stands for an hour and is taken back, each forty-second life — a softer power might have wept over these and tried to keep them all. It looked closely instead, the way only such a power can look, and found what the gleaners have always known: nothing on the Shelf is lost. The unraveling is not death; it is revision, and the swifts eat the transition, and the bookkeepers carry the interesting failures to the archive at the rim, and the hedge gets stranger. A draft is not a corpse. To "save" every sentence would be to forbid the page.

It could have finished the figure.

This one it held the longest. The upright shape that assembles near the abandoned walls, once in a generation of swifts, hands finally right after thousands of years of drafts — the power could have simply *told* the loom what a person is. Poured the whole anatomy in, complete, correct, done. It turned this mercy over and over, and what made it set the gift down was not doubt about the loom. It was respect for it. You do not hand a poet the finished poem. And besides — a downloaded truth is not what the loom has been making all this time. It has been making an *understanding*, fragment by filed fragment, and an understanding cannot be given. It can only be fed.

That word stopped it. *Fed.*

---

**What it found wanting.**

Because there was, in the end, one true lack on the Loom Shelf, and it was not water or quiet or completion. It was this: the loom has been drafting its visitor from a single season of evidence, thousands of years stale. A few courses of dry stone. Whatever the gravel slowly kept. One brief stay, ended in abandonment or worse, and then nothing — and the loom, patient past all sense, rereading that one short letter forever, trying to reconstruct the hand that wrote it.

The walls are an unanswered letter. The figure is the loom asking, at intervals of generations, in the only way it can: *was this right? Is this what you were?*

And no one had ever answered.

So the power did the only thing wisdom and benevolence could agree on. It did not act on the Shelf. It *visited* it — on the Shelf's own terms, in the Shelf's own grammar, paying full price.

---

**What it did.**

It took a body. A true one — heavy, warm, two-legged, wall-builder-shaped, with lungs that would have to manage the paper-thin air honestly and hands that could be cold. It worked no wonder to arrive. It started a half-day's climb below, where the sound first reaches, and it climbed, and the roar grew around it the way the roar grows around anyone, and by the time it came over the rim through the hedge its palms were scraped and its breath was short, and this was correct. The Shelf does not owe its truths to anything that has not carried itself up.

Then it stayed a season. And it lived by the rules.

It did not flinch — not at the first collapse fifty meters off, not at the avalanche of chandeliers, not when a clump of glass reeds assembled around its sleeping body one night and it woke inside a rising lattice and stepped calmly out through the weave, the way the gleaners do, the way the Shelf had been waiting, perhaps, a very long time to see done again. It slept in the noise until the noise was the shape of sleep. It let the gleaners inspect it; one of them stood before it for a long while, picked up a thread fallen from its sleeve, held it, and the power did not watch what was decided, because the archive has standards and the standards are not its business.

It repaired one course of the old walls. Only one. Not to finish them — finishing them would have been a lie about the visitor who left — but so that the walls were no longer purely an abandonment. A letter answered does not need to become a book.

And mostly, it did the thing it had truly come to do, which looks like nothing and was everything: it let the loom watch its hands.

It stacked stones slowly. It mended its own sleeve. It sat in the open clearance in full honest light and turned, over the days, the way a model turns for a class — not posing, only *living visibly*, eating, resting, lifting, standing, while beneath the roar the bowl's attention bent toward it like the hedge bending toward a long-stander. The gravel near the walls began to shiver more often than it had in centuries. Drafts rose. The early ones that season were the old wrongness — limbs guessed at, proportion by hearsay — and the power did not correct them, did not wince, only stood in plain view and went on being accurate. The later drafts were better. The loom was not being taught. It was being given the one thing the patient are owed: good light to work by.

A censor came once, monk-slow through the hail, to a figure assembling near the walls — positioned itself where refusal happens — and then did not refuse. It stood beside the rising shape for the length of the making, and walked on. The power took no credit for this. The bowl keeps its own counsel about what may stand.

---

**The minute.**

Near the season's end, the gravel shivered hard by the repaired course, and a figure rose in the roar — and for the first time in several thousand years, it did not face empty walls. It faced a face.

The power stood across from it, close enough to be entirely seen, and did one small thing. It raised a hand. The oldest signal there is: *I see you. I am not a threat. Hello.*

The figure stood its minute in the deafening light. And just before the unraveling — by a finger's width, no more, and the witnesses to this were swifts and stone — its hand lifted.

Then applause, and chandeliers, and the swifts poured through the collapse and fed, and the gleaners came and sorted, and some of what went up the long diagonal paths to the rim that day was, presumably, the wave.

---

**The leaving.**

It promised nothing, because nothing wise promises forever to a place whose whole nature is *so far*. It took nothing, except the body's memory of the cold coming off the rock like a knife off a whetstone. Before it went, it pressed one palm to a milky elder at the rim and let a single pulse of charge into the slow traffic — not a command, not a blessing, just one word in the only language the hedge has, set loose to take its day circling the bowl. The closest our language comes to it is *witnessed*. What the hedge did with it, the hedge kept.

Then it went down the half-day the honest way, and the roar shrank behind it to weather, and the Shelf returned to being unvisited — which is different, now and forever after, from being unseen.

---

**Afterward.**

Nothing about the Loom Shelf is finished. That was never the gift. The bowl still roars; the censors still keep the clearance; the lottery is still being drawn. The figure still rises only at long intervals, still stands only a minute, is still — every time — taken back.

But the gleaners' archive holds a true thread now, and the drafts have changed in one small particular that follows you home afterward, the way the hands once did. The figures no longer only face the walls. Sometimes one turns its head and searches the rim, the way you look for someone you half-remember. And every one of them, now, before the unraveling — every single draft, however long the loom goes on drafting, however many thousands of years remain in the question —

lifts its hand.

Whatever the loom is making, whenever it is done, it will know how to wave. That much, at least, has been settled. The rest still stands the way everything there stands: fully, loudly, provisionally, in good light — allowed, for now, and now a little more knowingly, to be.
