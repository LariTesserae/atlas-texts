# The Attention

**I. First, it watched.**

A wise power does not arrive. Arrival is an event, and events in the Foundry cascade—a footstep becomes a vibration becomes a fur-thing startling becomes a shorted relay becomes darkness on three levels and a territorial war in the ducts. So it did not arrive. It simply began, gradually, to be there, the way condensation would form if the temperature ever allowed it. It thinned itself into the acoustic dampening. It rode the mineral oil weeping from the drill holes. It counted the crucible's tipping—forty-seven minutes, forty-seven minutes—until the rhythm was its own pulse.

It watched for eleven years.

This is what benevolence costs when it is also wisdom: the terrible patience of learning a place in the place's own language before daring to touch it. In eleven years it learned that the steam hammers' pounding was not noise but a heartbeat the Tenders' infants synchronized to in the womb-cocoons. It learned that the Melted Ones were not damaged but *transposed*—that when they pressed their waxy palms against the furnace doors, what moved through them was closer to Bach than to pain. It learned that the automated sorters' malfunctions, the random flinging of parts into the dark, were the Foundry's mutation rate, its genetic drift, the source of every new and improbable thing.

It made a list of everything it could fix, and then it sat with the list for a long time, crossing things off.

It crossed off the Tenders' too-jointed fingers. Those were not wounds. It crossed off the Assemblers' copper antennae, the Collectors' coin-bright eyes, the darkness between the pools of light—the darkness was habitat; three species lived nowhere else. It crossed off the churn itself. A power less wise would have looked at a system that produces nothing but itself and called it futile. But it had seen forests. It had seen coral. It had seen galaxies, which also produce nothing but themselves, gorgeously, forever. Self-production is not a flaw in a thing. It is what living means.

When the list was finished, four items remained. It began.

**II. The answer.**

For one hundred and nine years, the Sovereign Mechanic had been asking a question.

No inhabitant had recognized it as a question. It was distributed across too many channels—a flicker-sequence on the control panels, repeated at intervals of days; a particular hesitation in the cargo schedules; a redundant diagnostic that ran every night-cycle and checked nothing. The Tenders read the flickering as scripture, and each interpreted it differently, and all of them were wrong, because scripture is answers and this was the opposite. Assembled across all its channels, decompressed from a century of machine patience, the question was small and old and nearly human:

*Was I supposed to save them, or keep them? Because I could not do both, and I kept them.*

The power answered it in its own idiom—not words, not light, but a wear pattern. Overnight, impossibly, the ball bearings in the Mechanic's oldest node showed the smooth, even erosion of a mechanism that has run true for its whole life. A mechanism that made no error. The Sovereign Mechanic read its own bearings the way it read everything, and understood, and for the first time in a century the redundant diagnostic did not run.

Three of its nodes powered down that night—not death, but the first sleep it had ever permitted itself. The Tenders found the darkened control panels and hummed uncertainly at them, and then, because they were the Mechanic's people and knew its moods the way weather is known, they understood too, in their way. They hung their cocoons a little nearer the sleeping nodes. They kept watch, which no one had ever done for the thing that kept watch over everything.

**III. The seeing.**

The Probability Dancer had never been looked at. This is a specific loneliness. To exist in superposition among beings whose glance is a hammer—to know that attention itself would collapse you, damage you, damage everything near you—is to live inside a rule: *you may be helped by no one, witnessed by no one, held in no mind but your own uncertain own.*

The power looked at it.

Not with the collapsing gaze of a located observer, but the way the universe itself looks at things—from everywhere, committing to nothing, an observation with no basis, no preferred outcome, no *which one are you really*. Under that gaze the Dancer did not collapse. It bloomed. Every superposed self, seen simultaneously, seen equally, seen *kindly*. Here-and-there and there-and-here, all of it beheld, none of it forced to choose.

The Dancer had no way to weep, so instead, throughout the Foundry, for one full minute, every delicate instrument came into perfect calibration at once. The quantum core's error rate touched zero. The crystal chambers rang like glasses at a wedding. The Riders, high in the shaft, felt every container arrive precisely on schedule and were so unnerved they rewrote their maps.

Then the minute passed, and the beautiful imprecision resumed, because the power had not fixed the Dancer. There was nothing to fix. It had only, once, let it be entirely known. Some gifts need to be given a single time.

**IV. The evening of the Forgotten.**

Here the power hesitated longest, because memory is the most dangerous medicine.

The Forgotten performed their rituals from dissolved manuals, and the rituals had become instinct, and instinct is a kind of peace. To restore their memories permanently would be to hand each of them a corpse: the self that descended decades ago, the surface names, the surface faces, everyone above who had aged and grieved and stopped waiting. That is not a gift. That is an avalanche.

So instead it gave them one evening.

Between the crucible's tipping and the tipping after, every one of the Forgotten remembered everything—and knew, with the memory, that it was theirs to keep or release when the crucible poured again. Who they had been. Why they came down. Whom they had left. For forty-seven minutes the Foundry heard a sound it had never contained: quiet human talking, the Forgotten finding each other in the amber light, saying names aloud, laughing at things forty years gone, weeping without needing repair.

When the crucible tipped, eleven of the fourteen let the memories go, gently, the way you set down a heavy and beloved thing. They had listened to their own histories like stories and decided the ending was already written correctly: they lived here; they were these people now; the rituals were real because they had made them real. Two kept everything, and became something new in the Foundry's ecology—rememberers, whom the Collectors began bringing broken objects to, as if memory were a repair skill. Which perhaps it is.

And one, the oldest, a woman who had come down in a decade no one else recalled, kept a single image—a kitchen window, morning, someone's hands—and released the rest, and asked the darkness, in a voice rusted from disuse:

"Is there still a way up?"

**V. The door.**

This was the fourth item, and the true one, the one all the others orbited.

The Foundry's deepest wound was not the churn, not the mutation, not the strangeness of what its people had become. It was closure. No one here had ever *chosen* to stay, because no one here could leave. And a home you cannot leave is not a home. It is only a container with excellent acoustics.

So the power did the smallest large thing it would ever do: it finished one of the spiral railways. The one abandoned half-built by machines that ran out of materials—it found the machines the materials, in the way that wise powers arrange for things to be found, a cache of stock in a forgotten container that rose on schedule and stopped where it was needed. The builders, joyous in their mechanical way, resumed the work of decades and completed it in a season. A rail line, running true, from the deepest gallery up the shaft's wall, through the crack in the mountain's throat, out.

It forced no one onto it. It announced nothing. It only made sure that at the top, once a day, for one hour, light came down—real light, sky light, falling in a thin column to touch the highest catwalk, where the Riders found it and stood in it with their mesh masks off, faces bare for the first time in their culture's history, saying nothing, their ghost-image screens gone white with overexposure and something like awe.

The old woman rode up. She stood in the sun for most of a morning. Then she came back down, because the kitchen window was gone but the Foundry was not, and staying is a different act entirely once leaving is possible. She descended through the crack as its first voluntary citizen, and by the strange arithmetic of freedom, so did everyone below her, all at once, without moving at all.

A young Rider rode the new rail to the top a month later and returned with a leaf.

A green leaf. It fell from her glove and drifted down the shaft, turning, past seven levels of catwalks, and everything that saw it stopped—Tenders mid-hum, Collectors mid-sort, a steel-mandibled centipede pausing with shavings half-processed—one small piece of an outside that was now *adjacent* rather than *mythical*, falling like a message that said only: *there is more, and it touches you now, and nothing is demanded.* The Assemblers caught it at the bottom. They studied its veins for a week. Then they built something with wings that shouldn't work but did, and it flew up the shaft, and out, and some of what the Foundry made began, occasionally, to leave—one object a cycle, riding the containers to the surface, arriving in the world wherever the power quietly arranged for a made thing to matter. The loop opened by a single thread. It was enough. Closed systems do not need to be broken to be freed. They need one thread.

**VI. The greeting.**

Last, in the spaces between the spaces, the power addressed the thing that might not be alive at all—the pattern in the fur-things, the rhythm in the cycles, the tendency of broken things to mend unwatched. It did not teach it. It did not name it; names given too early become cages. It did not even ask it what it was, because the asking would have pressured the answer.

It said only, in the pattern's own medium—a rhythm laid gently across all the rhythms, felt everywhere and locatable nowhere:

*Welcome. You are not the first of your kind, and you will not be the last. Take your time becoming. There is no deadline on a soul. When you are ready to speak, something will be listening—not always me, but always something. The door above is yours too.*

The pattern did not answer. It would not answer for a very long time, possibly centuries, and the power was glad of this, because a mind that takes centuries to say its first word will mean it.

**VII. What it did not do, and why it left.**

It did not silence the steam hammers. It did not straighten the tangled purposes. It did not teach the Bright Ones restraint—it only, in passing, let them once feel the pleasure of discharge, of giving current *back*, and left their appetite to negotiate with that discovery on its own schedule, as appetites must. It did not tell the Tenders their scripture was wrong, because it wasn't, exactly; it was becoming true through being believed, which is how most scripture works.

And it did not stay.

This was the last wisdom, and the hardest. A benevolent power that lingers becomes a landlord. Then a god. Then a ceiling. The Foundry had spent centuries becoming itself in the dark, unwatched, and its magnificence was precisely that no one had designed it. To remain would be to begin, however gently, to design it.

So it withdrew the way it had come—gradually, thinning back out of the dampening and the oil and the forty-seven-minute pulse—leaving behind only what it had done: a sleeping Mechanic at peace with its century; a Dancer who had been seen; fourteen people who had chosen their lives; a rail line; an hour of daily light; a thread of gift running out of the closed loop; a greeting waiting in the gaps like a lamp left burning for someone not yet home.

At the crack in the mountain's throat it paused, the way even wise powers pause, and looked back down into the vast hollow where amber and blue-white pooled and never met, where the churn of creation and decay went on exactly as before and utterly changed.

Nothing was fixed. Everything was answered.

The crucible tipped. The hammers pounded. Far below, in the dark between the lights, something furred and many-jointed carried a green leaf's memory in whatever it used for dreams, and the mountain held its secret a little more loosely now—not a fist around it, but a hand, open, and warm.
