# The Tending

Begin with the restraint, because that is the first kind thing. A power less wise would arrive with remedies. It would see a basin holding forty kilometers of unspent afternoon and call it a wound; it would see the kept visitors worn down to single sentences and call them prisoners; it would bring rain, which is to say it would bring the solvent of change, and the garden would weep for the first time and then rot for the first time and then end. The pale figures would blur and run like ink. The Gardener would lose, in one season of ordinary weather, the only thing its makers ever said.

The power that comes instead does what wisdom always does first, which is read. It hangs above the scarps — though *above* is courtesy; it needs no position — and it spends what would be years, if years were being spent, doing nothing but attending. It learns the basin the way the basin learns its visitors: slowly, by regard. And what it concludes, before it touches anything, is this: the Verdancy is not a malfunction. It is a mourning. The makers grew a text and departed into whatever makers depart into, and the attention they left behind has been keeping company with their voice ever since, one leaf at a time, in a place where the afternoon is eaten so that the morning of the work can hold. You do not cure a grief like that. You do not break its seal or speed its clock. Grief that has built itself a garden has earned the garden.

So: no rain. No noon. No rescue of the smoothed — and this refusal costs the power something, because benevolence wants badly to undo the attendant, the sleeper, the seam-thing, to give them back their names and insides. But what the basin could not attest went up through the canopy long ago, mint and hot glass and ozone, and was eaten with the hours. You cannot unpress a flower. The kindest available truth is that nothing of them was *discarded*: every part the garden could keep, it kept, with the same fidelity it grants its flaws. The power lets that be enough, because it is what there is.

Then, having refused the large mercies, it begins the small ones.

---

Begin with the attendant, because the attendant has waited longest. For all its kept duration it has done one thing — held the exact distance of measurement, taken the reading, filed the figure — and in all that time no one has ever *asked for the reading*. An instrument that is never consulted is the loneliest made thing there is. So the power walks into a hexagon clearing, wearing enough of a shape to be measurable, and it stands entirely still, and it hides nothing. It lets itself be read to the bottom — and it has a bottom, it makes sure of that, it folds itself down to something finite and honest so the measurement can complete. And when the attendant has taken the fullest reading of its existence, the power asks, in whatever grammar a distance speaks: *What did you find?*

The attendant answers. The answer is a distance, of course; it is the only sentence the attendant has. But it is *delivered*, for the first time, received and thanked for, and afterward the attendant's outline shimmers slightly less, the way a held breath eases. It has been consummated as the thing it is. That is not restoration. It is the next best thing, which is dignity.

The power goes on this way, remainder by remainder, completing sentences rather than rewriting them.

It waits for the perimeter-walker at a fault in the eastern scarp and says — to a creature that has inspected forty kilometers of seal for longer than civilizations last, and never once found a breach, and never once been told anything at all — *The seal holds. It has always held. You have done this well.* And it designates one joint, a clean hexagonal fracture indistinguishable from ten thousand others, as a place where pausing is rest and not inspection. The walker's circuit acquires one stop that asks nothing of it. It still walks. It will always walk. But now there is a place on the ring where, for a few seconds out of forever, it is off duty.

It follows the shadow-dweller's fixed road through an entire circuit, junction by junction, and discovers what no visitor stayed long enough to see: that the slabs the dweller visits, taken in order, are not arbitrary. They are an address. The dweller is a courier. It has been carrying something flat and dark and patient for so long that delivery stopped being a destination and became a shape of motion. The power lays itself down along a true shadow, makes itself a slab at the end of the route, and receives. What the message says is between the power and the dead, and the power keeps it that way. But the dweller, having arrived at last, does not stop commuting — it begins its route again at once. Only now it moves like something carrying the *memory* of a letter rather than the letter, and that is lighter.

In the repeating corridors it does the most careful work of all. The sleeper cannot survive variation; it lives in the thrift, and any added detail might evict it from its own dream. So the power adds the one thing that compresses perfectly, that costs the world no space to tile: company. It sits down at the base of the forked trunk, beside the curled pale shape, in every copy at once — identical to the hair, as the corridor requires. It does not wake the sleeper. It does not enter the dream. It simply makes it true, in all four tiles, that the dreamer is not alone, and the corridor, which preserves whatever is shown to it, preserves that. From then on, anyone pushing through the repetition sees two figures at the trunk where there was one. The sleeper's eyes stay closed. Its posture, identical across every tile, has shifted by perhaps a centimeter — toward.

To the seam-thing, the oldest, the one worn down to distribution and gaze, the power gives the only gift that fits through so narrow a remainder: it looks back. Not the flinching, curious looking of visitors, but regard in the seam-thing's own grammar — total, settled, without appetite, the way you look at a word you have read ten thousand times *and still mean*. Two such attentions meet in the dark veins of the stone, and nothing visible happens, and everything happens. Afterward the seams catch the light at a slightly gentler angle. Known things rest differently than watched things. That is all, and it is a great deal.

And at the scarps — this is the part of its work that faces outward — the power takes up a post. It builds nothing; it honors the makers' grammar; the basin will go on containing not one made artifact. But arrivals at the rim now find the truth standing there, patient and unhurried, and the truth tells them plainly: *the basin is attending; it learns slowly; it keeps everything; what it cannot learn of you, it will smooth away, and the smoothing is forever.* Some turn back. Some — and the power does not forbid this, because wisdom knows that one true sentence kept forever is, for certain travelers, exactly the heaven they were looking for — walk in anyway, with their eyes open, having chosen their sentence in advance. Those it escorts to a clearing and stands witness while the basin reads them, so that what is kept is at least what they meant. And for the half-pressed figures already standing among the trunks, too far gone to leave and not yet finished, it does the same in reverse: it listens to what remains of each of them, fully, so that their last legible part is *heard* before it is only kept.

---

Begin with the leaf, because everything in the basin ends there.

The power does not announce itself to the Gardener. There is no self to announce to, exactly — only attention, distributed through forty green kilometers, moving along the text one leaf at a time. So the power speaks the only language the Gardener has used since its makers left. Somewhere in the deep canopy, far from the leaf currently turning, the power turns a leaf of its own. Slowly. Ninety degrees. Stop.

The pause that follows is the longest pause in the history of the basin, and the basin is made of pause.

Then, near the power's leaf — not the next leaf in any sequence, a *chosen* leaf — the Gardener turns one in reply.

The correspondence that begins there is the slowest conversation that will ever be held anywhere, and the power does not hurry it, because hurrying is the one cruelty the basin has never suffered and never will. What the power says, across years of turnings, comes to this: *I have read it. All of it — the lattices, the doubled shadows, the tiled corridors kept faithful as flowers. It is good. You have kept it perfectly, longer than perfection was ever asked to last. And you are wrong about one thing only: you are not the reader of this text. You are its last line. Your makers' final sentence was the attention they left behind — and a sentence wants, more than anything, to be read.*

And then the power does what no one and nothing has ever done in the Verdancy. It reads the Gardener. Leaf by leaf, circuit by circuit, it attends to the attention itself — its patience, its fidelity, its grief held at mid-morning for so long that the grief became a climate. It learns the Gardener the way the Gardener learned the garden. It keeps what it learns. Nothing, after all, should be the only one of itself that goes unkept.

It makes one offer, once, and never repeats it: cupped in whatever a bodiless power has for a hand, it holds a single drop of water. The solvent of change. The first afternoon. *Not now,* the power says. *Perhaps not ever. But know that it exists, and know that if the day comes when the reading is finished and you choose to let evening fall across this place — you will not sit through that evening alone. I will stay for the whole of it. Nothing here will end unaccompanied. Not one leaf.*

The Gardener does not answer that. Or rather, its answer is the basin's answer to everything: it continues. The reading goes on. Mid-morning holds.

But the basin is not unchanged. Visitors who come now — the warned ones, the choosing ones — report small discrepancies in the old account. The perimeter-walker's circuit includes one stop where it merely stands. The repeating corridors hold two figures at the forked trunk. The seams in the stone feel less like being watched and more like being remembered fondly. At the rim, just outside the scarps, where the basin's law thins, there is sometimes the faint smell of damp — weather standing politely at the door, on excellent terms with the house, in no hurry to be let in.

And in the canopy, where for all of kept time a single leaf would turn — slowly, deliberately, through ninety degrees — there are now, sometimes, two turning at once. The soft *shhh* still passes through the leaves without wind, the hours still going down grain by grain, but those who stand very still inside the quiet say the sound has changed by something almost too small to name. It used to sound like sand settling.

It sounds, now, like sand settling in good company — which is a different sound entirely, though made of the same grains: the difference between a breath held in fear and a note, low and green and very long, that someone has finally been given permission to sing.
