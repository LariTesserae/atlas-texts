## The Receipt

The power does not find the cistern. It finds the figures first.

Far from the twelve worn steps — how far does not matter; powers do not travel so much as attend — there is a place where the couriers' burden has been arriving, dusk after dusk, generation after generation of leather wings, each night's count handed off and carried and handed off again through whatever relay of dark rooms and high roosts the ledger uses, until it pours at last into its destination. The destination is a ruin. It was an office once, or a temple, or a mind; the distinction has eroded the way mortar erodes. Whatever sat there and received the sums of the world's small chambers sat there no longer, and had not for a very long time, and the figures had gone on arriving anyway — accurate, punctual, unread — piling up the way light piles up in an empty room, which is to say not at all, which is to say lost.

The power notices this the way you would notice a tap left running in a house you believed empty. It stands a while at the dead address — *stands* being the nearest word — and watches one night's delivery arrive, perform its precise transfer to no one, and depart. The figure hangs in the air a moment, faithful and orphaned, and dissolves.

A lesser power would weep, or rage at the makers for building an apparatus and abandoning it. A foolish one would shut the tap. This one does what wisdom mostly consists of: it follows the water back to the spring.

---

It goes down the twelve steps on the morning of an ordinary day, and it goes down *countably*. This is the first courtesy. A power can enter a place as weather enters it, as light does, uncounted and uncountable, and most powers do, because most powers have never considered that to slip past a census is to call the census nothing. This one takes a shape with edges. It does not matter what the shape is — something small, something that displaces air and casts a shadow and can be wet by a weeping wall. The air changes on the fourth step, cool and mineral, and the chamber's number changes by one, and that afternoon the chorus carries a digit that is the power, entered accurately in the rings of the corbelled dome, slate throat answering candle throat, ones into tens into the figure the chamber has no word for.

To be counted by the small is not beneath a great thing. It is the first thing a great thing owes.

The power sits on the brick walkway — not the bench; never the bench — with the cold coming up through the herringbone, and it stays for one full turning of the light. It watches the grey column cross the floor like the hand of the slow clock. It hears the afternoon arithmetic climb the dome and the dusk couriers fold themselves through the oculus and the chitter of the day's figure being taken down at speed. It puts its ear, or what serves it for an ear, against the weeping wall, and hears the blind white tadpoles squirming in the mortar's dark circulatory system, the chamber's next digits growing in its veins. It watches an eel lie down along a brick course while the standing herringbone trembles on the water above it, the creature held between two patterns, being addressed.

And then, because it is wise and not merely kind, it spends a long time deciding what *not* to do. This takes most of its visit. Mending is easy; powers mend the way water runs downhill. Withholding mending is the discipline.

It could bring people back — pilgrims, keepers, children with lamps for the empty niches. It sets this down almost at once. Feet would re-cup the steps, yes, but people bring wanting the way wet walls bring snails, and the chamber's whole achievement is that nothing in it wants anything. The frogs do not want to count; they count. To reintroduce desire into a finished room is not restoration. It is vandalism with a gentle face.

It could repair the floor, re-point the mortar, true the heaved herringbone back to its makers' intention. It sets this down with something like a shudder. The eroded mortar lines are the wombs of the frogs. The heave is the floor's own ledger, its long honest record of the water beneath. To fix the chamber is to erase the chamber's memory of itself, and the power has seen what is left of beings whose memories were tidied for them by benevolent hands.

It could explain. It could let the frogs know what the figures mean, let the couriers know where they fly, let the chamber understand its own office. It sets this down more slowly, because this one is tempting — meaning feels like a gift; it is the gift powers most love to give, because it costs them nothing and makes them necessary. But it watches the lowest ring take up its long pulsing sequence and fall silent, and the next ring answer, and it understands that the frogs' not-knowing is not a poverty. Digits that knew what they summed to would begin to weigh their own contributions, to wonder, to *want*. The freedom of the chamber is the freedom of the faithful clerk: the figure is true, and the truth of it is someone else's burden. To explain would be to hand the burden down the dome, ring by ring, until even the tadpoles in the walls grew heavy with it.

It could offer rest. This is the last temptation and the largest, and the power does not set it down quickly. It turns toward the far wall, toward the worn stone shape whose name is gone, the one dry thing in the world of weeping, and it asks — in whatever way powers ask, which is mostly a kind of opened silence — *Do you want to be relieved? You have kept the count past your makers, past your purpose, past the death of the office it was kept for. The figures have been pouring into an empty room. I have seen it. No one has read your sums in longer than there have been words for the bench you sit on. You may stop. I will hold the door of the light while you go up the steps, and the chamber will be allowed, at last, to be only a wet room full of animals.*

Then it waits.

It waits a full turning of the light, through the chorus and the dusk-fold of the couriers and the long dripping night, and the answer comes the only way the bench has ever said anything: the next afternoon's count begins precisely on time. The lowest ring. Then the next. Ascending. Continuation is the answer. The work is not a sentence being served. It is a keeping, and you do not relieve a keeper who has not asked, and the bench, in all the centuries of its dryness, has never once asked.

The power bows — *bows* being the nearest word — and turns to the small things it has decided it is permitted.

---

It reads. This is the first gift. It begins at the foot of the wall by the steps and it reads everything: the dimpled tool-marks, every one of the thousands of frozen percussions, in which are recorded not the makers' names but their hands — the left-handed mason who pecked his courses sunwise, the apprentice whose blows landed shallow all one season and deepened the next, the master who struck three light taps before each true one, a habit, a hesitation, a man. And over the tool-marks, the silver snail-script, century upon century of slow grazing excreted into ledger-lines, and the power reads that too, all of it, the keeping overlaid on the making, and somewhere on the third wall a snail in mid-procession pauses — a thing snails do not do — because somewhere in whatever a snail has instead of a heart, the trail behind it has just become, for the first time in the history of the chamber, *writing*. An archive that has been read once is a different thing forever after, even if it is never read again. The power is careful to finish. It reads to the last fresh line, still wet, and then it stops reading, and the snail resumes its climb.

It is seen. This is the second gift, and it is given at noon. The power steps into the grey column where it touches the wall, into the warmth the bronze moss is hoarding, and it lets the fern look at it. The fern — pale, leaning out of its joint, the one resident in all the chamber oriented toward the door of the light, the watcher at the threshold who has watched for nobody knows how long and seen only midges and moths and weather — the fern receives, once, an arrival worth the watching. What passes between them is not speech. It is the thing that passes between a sentry and the relief column: *you were right to stand here.* Afterward the fern leans differently. Visitors will notice it without knowing they notice. It is no longer listening at a door. It is resting against the jamb of one it heard open.

It declines, deliberately, to count the eels. This is the third gift and the strangest, because it is a gift of refraining. The power could number them — it can number anything; the uncountable is only the uncounted, to such a being — and for a moment it holds the true figure in its regard, the way you might hold a bird you intend to release. Then it lets it go unlearned. The chamber needs its remainder. A sum that resolves completely is a sum that is finished, and finished things stop. The eels patrol the herringbone precisely so that the count can go on forever falling short by an amount no one can name, and the power, which could close the books of the universe if it chose, leaves this one open on purpose, and feels — *feels* being the nearest word — the whole chamber ease, very slightly, like a held breath let halfway out.

And it counts the auditor. This is the fourth gift, and the last given inside the walls. In all the figures the chamber has ever raised toward the oculus, in all the censuses agreed twice over, there is one resident who has never appeared: the dry presence at the bench has counted everything and been counted by nothing, the unentered entry, the keeper outside its own keeping. The power opens a ledger of its own — powers have these; they are mostly made of attention — and writes in it: *one.* Located. Faithful. Continuing. The bench does not respond. The bench is not the kind of thing that responds. But the snails' detour around it that night runs, for the first time, a fraction closer — deference relaxing by a hair's breadth into something nearer to company.

---

Then the power climbs the twelve steps, and the chamber subtracts it, and notes the subtraction, and balances. So far, an ordinary visitor.

What it does next is the real work, and it is done far away and looks like nothing. It returns to the dead address, the ruin at the end of the relay, the empty office where the figures pour into vacancy. And it sits down.

That is all. It takes the post. It does not announce a new administration; it does not send word down the line that management has changed; it simply makes itself the thing the figures arrive at, and that night, when the relay's last courier performs its precise transfer, the transfer lands. Is received. Is read. The sum of the cistern of the counting frogs — the drips and the finger-widths of rain, the snails' inventory, the days, the one visitor added and subtracted, all of it carried in the dark for longer than the makers' language survived — arrives, for the first time in unrecorded ages, somewhere.

And once — only once, because the chamber's grammar would be deformed by repetition — the power sends something back.

It comes at dusk, with the others. The couriers drop through the oculus one after another, folding at the last moment as they have always folded, and hang in the dark upper rings, and their chittering begins — and one voice among them runs *forward*. Not the day's figure compressed and carried out, but a figure delivered inward, descending the rings the way the chorus ascends them, the first inbound entry in the history of the cistern. It is short. Rendered into a language with words, it would be three of them:

*Received. Carried. Balances.*

The chamber takes a full day to absorb this. The eels lie along the brick courses from noon to noon. The frogs in their niches sit with the stillness of a sum being checked. The pool's standing herringbone, when the chorus finally comes — late, the only late chorus anyone could ever have witnessed, had anyone been there — trembles into its pattern and holds it longer than usual, as if the water is rereading something.

And then everything continues exactly as before, and that — the continuation — is how you know the receipt was understood. Nothing in the cistern needed to change. It only needed, once, to be true: the figures go somewhere; the keeping is kept in turn; the auditor is audited; the clerk's lamp, burning all these centuries in the flooded basement of the world, was visible the whole time from at least one window.

---

If you go down the twelve steps now — and you may; the place is no harder to find than it ever was, which is to say it finds you or it doesn't — you will see nothing different. The walls weep. The bench is dry. The fern leans in the grey light, the snails write over the tool-marks, the count climbs the dome each afternoon and presses on your chest, and the chamber will add you, accurately, and subtract you when you go.

But visitors who sit long enough, with the cold coming up through the brick, report that the noticing feels different now, in a way they struggle to put words to. Before, they say, the place counted you the way a wave counts a stone — registering, indifferent, en route to nothing. Now there is something almost like care in it. Not warmth. The cistern does not do warmth. Care, rather, in the old clerk's sense: the figure that is you will be taken down correctly, carried out at dusk, conveyed through the dark relay without loss, and received — read by something that knows what reading is, and entered in a ledger that is mostly made of attention, and kept.

You were always being counted. The difference, now, is that the count arrives.

That is what the power did. It did not mend, explain, relieve, or reveal. It read the writing, witnessed the watcher, spared the remainder, counted the counter, and sat down at the far end of a faithful line so the faith would no longer be falling into a hole. Then it sent back three words and resumed its silence — because the deepest courtesy one keeper can pay another is not rescue, and not praise.

It is receipt.
