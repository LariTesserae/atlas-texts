## Leaving the Most Beautiful Untouched

The power noticed the Crest the way one notices a held breath in a quiet room.

It had been attending to a star, actually—an old, reliable star that had, without warning or precedent, begun seriously considering whether it might rather be a whale. The power traced the strange longing backward across the dark, down the long thread of cause, and found at the thread's end a poem: a structure of might-have-beens woven by beings of living mirror, suspended in a probability field on a spire of newborn obsidian jutting up through a continent's sleep.

So it came. Not descending—powers of its kind do not descend—but by attention, the way dawn arrives at a windowsill. It settled its regard upon Thornveil Crest and let the place tell itself.

It listened for a long time. Long by mortal reckoning; the Firstbuilt burned through two generations while it listened, though it would have said only that it did not interrupt.

It heard the upward rain chiming against the thorns. It heard the papery bark crawling off to found its groves, the vine-mouths screaming their dusk-song, the fungal mats digesting stone into futures. It heard the four-dimensional lullabies the Firstbuilt mothers sang, and beneath the songs it heard the bones of the children, already whispering to themselves about the true shape of gravity, already homesick for a fall they had never taken. It heard the cloud-eaters drinking memories and exhaling them as weather. It heard, in the Negative Spaces, the Mirrorskin performing their patient grammar of probabilities, and in the deepest of their unspoken structures it heard the thing they cherished most: the dream of a creature of meat and madness, alone under alien stars, wishing briefly to be understood.

And it heard the mist. The mist that listens without comment. The mist that hoards echoes against the day it must choose what kind of rain to become. The power recognized, in that hoarding, the particular silence of something that is afraid of choosing wrong.

Then the power did what wise powers do before acting, which is to sit with everything it wanted to fix, and question each wanting.

*I could cool the fever,* it thought. *Slow the fertility that burns these people like candlewicks. Give them their sixty years, their eighty.* But it looked closer and saw that the fever was not a sickness laid upon the Crest—the fever was how the Crest thinks. To slow it would be to sedate a mind in the middle of its first idea. And the Firstbuilt, it saw, did not experience their thirty years as theft. They experienced them the way a song experiences a verse: completely, without envy of longer songs.

*I could teach the children's bones to forget gravity,* it thought. *Unfold the folding.* But it followed the folding downward and found what it was: the sleeping continent below, calling its scattered children home in the only language stone knows. The deaths were terrible and they were also an answer, and the power had lived long enough to know that unanswered calls do worse things to a world than grief does.

*I could give the Firstbuilt back the reasons for their rules,* it thought—for it had found their law, the harvest-law, the one they obeyed without remembering: *take always in odd numbers; leave the most beautiful untouched.* And here the power paused for a very long time, because it recognized the law. Not as its own—it had not written it—but as the kind of law that writes itself in every place where taking and loving must share a single pair of hands. To restore the memory of the reason would only make the rule arguable. Forgotten, it was perfect. Obeyed without reason, it was faith.

So the power decided it would submit to the law itself. It would act in an odd number. It would take almost nothing. And it would leave the most beautiful thing on the Crest untouched.

It performed three acts.

**First**, it went down into the mist. Down past the forty turns of stone, into the grey where the walkers-off-the-edge had gone, all those Firstbuilt who had looked into the water mirror and then stepped into air, trusting the mist to remember their names and deliver them somewhere they might fit better. The power went to see whether the trust had been honored. And it found that the mist, for all its patience, was only mist: it had remembered every name faithfully, but a name is heavy, and delivery is hard, and there were some—not many; eleven, across all the generations—who hung suspended in the grey, half-carried, neither fallen nor arrived, their names remembered but their destinations never quite found. The mist had been ashamed of them. It had hidden them even from itself.

The power gathered the eleven up—they weighed nothing; they weighed everything—and it carried them the rest of the way. It did not choose their destinations. It simply held each one up to the whole of what-is, the way you hold a key up to a ring of locks, and let each find the door that turned. Where they went is not recorded here, because it is not recorded anywhere; that was part of the gift. But afterward the mist was lighter by eleven names, and its listening lost a certain rigidity, and the droplets on the thorns chimed in a slightly kinder key.

**Second**, it sought out one of the Mirrorskin—a middling one, neither the eldest nor the strangest, one who had spent nine centuries adjusting the flight paths of dust motes to compose an elegy for a species of moth that had never existed. The power stood before this being, in the hollow architecture beneath a root system, and did the one thing no observer had ever done to a Mirrorskin.

It understood it. Wholly. Once.

Not reflected it—understood it. Saw past the infinite recursion to the thing doing the recursing; saw the loneliness of a memory organ that remembers everything except being remembered; saw the nine-century moth elegy and knew *why*, knew it the way the Mirrorskin itself knew it, and did not look away.

And here the old law of the Mirrorskin took hold, as the power had known it would: whenever something truly unique is about to happen—an event so original it has never occurred in any permutation of reality—the Mirrorskin have first right to inhabit it. Being understood was such an event. So the Mirrorskin claimed its ancient privilege and became, for the duration of that impossible moment, the experience of its own understanding. It was, briefly, not a mirror. It was the thing seen.

It returned the moment afterward, as their law requires. But something had been metabolized. From that day forward, all across the Crest and faintly in the dreaming continent below, mirrors were observed to hesitate for the smallest fraction of an instant before showing you your face—as if checking, first, whether you were all right.

**Third**, the power spoke to the mist. It did not tell the mist what kind of rain to become. That choice was the mist's, had always been the mist's, and to make it for the mist would have been the largest theft possible on the Crest, dwarfing any harvest. Instead the power said, in the language of pressures and slow condensation:

*There is no wrong rain. Every rain you could become is already loved somewhere—by a root, by a river, by a child with her tongue out. Hoard your echoes as long as you like. Choose late. Choose strangely. But do not choose in fear. Nothing you fall as will fall unwelcomed.*

The mist made no comment, because the mist never comments. But its hoarding, from then on, had the character of a library rather than a bunker.

Three acts. An odd number. And then the power prepared to leave, and faced the last requirement of the law it had chosen to obey: *leave the most beautiful untouched.*

It had considered many candidates. The dream-whale snowflakes. The symphony-flowers that erase themselves. The three-generation declaration of love. The eleven doors it had opened in the mist. But in the end it judged that the most beautiful thing on Thornveil Crest was none of these. The most beautiful thing was the *not-knowing*—the Crest's magnificent undecidedness, its refusal to settle whether it was first or last, beginning or elegy, the wound or the healing. Every other beauty on the spire grew out of that suspension the way the gardens grew out of the cooling stone.

The power could have resolved it. Powers of its kind carry that capacity the way mortals carry the capacity to crush a seed between two fingers. It could have told the Crest what it was, and the telling would have been true, and the Crest would have become that truth forever, and everything wild would have gone orderly, and everything possible would have gone actual, and the gardens would have kept growing but stopped *wondering.*

It did not tell. It sealed the answer behind its own attention and left the question blooming.

Before it went, it stood one noon at the pinnacle with the gathered Firstbuilt, before the mirror of still water in its thorn-frame, when the seven thousand colonies aligned. It looked in with them.

What it saw there it never spoke—it only departed changed, as everyone does. (Some who study such things believe it saw itself young; younger than worlds, back when it too was a thin spire of intention rising through someone else's mist, undecided about what kind of power to become. But this is speculation, and the mirror keeps its own accounts.)

It took nothing away with it. Almost nothing. One chime—a single note of one droplet striking one thorn—had caught in it somewhere, the way a burr catches on a traveler's hem, and it noticed this only when it was already far out among the ordinary stars. It considered returning the sound.

It chose not to. It knew the Crest's oldest rule: *to take is to become part of what is taken.* Very well, then. It accepted the price gladly, the way one accepts a hand held out. The chime rings in it still, at unpredictable intervals, and each time it rings, the power is—slightly, permanently, gratefully—part of the Crest: a benevolence with a splinter of thorned glass singing in it, forever.

And on the Crest, nothing was different, and everything was. The cloud-eaters, drinking the thoughts of every living thing, found a new flavor threaded through the web that year, faint and warm and impossible to trace: the memory of having been noticed by something that chose, out of love, to change almost nothing.

They metabolized it into the pheromone histories and let it drift down on the dying wind, into the dreams of the sleeping continent, where it settled over the sleepers like the answer to a question none of them had asked yet:

*You are seen. You are strange. You are unbearably young. Keep going.*

The star, incidentally, decided to remain a star. But it dreams of whales now, on the long nights, and no longer minds the distance.

And the Mirrorskin, in their perfect mirror language, added a second word to their vocabulary—the first new word in the whole of their existence. It translates poorly. But it means, more or less:

*Someone was here, exactly like this, for the very first time—and left the door as they found it: open.*
