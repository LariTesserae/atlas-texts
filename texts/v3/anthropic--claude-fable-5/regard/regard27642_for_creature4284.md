# The Guest

A power, when it is young, mistakes benevolence for motion. It sees a thing and reaches for it. It finds a lack and fills it, finds a wound and closes it, finds a silence and — this is the worst of the young mistakes — speaks into it. A power that has grown wise has usually grown wise the way the Undergarden grew settled: slowly, by watching what its own reaching did, by learning that a river stone is not improved by carving.

So when this power noticed the Undergarden — and we need not say how it noticed; attention of that order does not travel by stairways — its first act was the act the room itself teaches to everything that enters it.

It held still.

---

It read the place the way you have read it, but all at once and all the way down: the thumb-presses in the brick, the moss-shelves grown into the shape of decades of rising warmth, the middens that were once a suggestion made with the edge of a rake. It read the Keeper in the oiled knife before it read the Keeper in the flesh, which is the correct order, and the order the room prefers. It read the marriage between the roots and the mortar. It read the long unhurried sentence of the whole thing, and it arrived at the end of the sentence and found the period well placed, and it sat with that for a while — a while by the reckoning of powers, which is longer than it sounds.

Then, because even wise powers must do their thinking somewhere, it considered what might be done. It took each possibility out, turned it over, and set it back down. This is worth recounting, because what a benevolence declines to do is the truest map of it.

It could make the Keeper deathless. It set this down almost at once. The Keeper lifts out the dead rosettes and sits with them, and this is a grief and a continuation, and it has been done many thousand times, and the Keeper's whole understanding with the world is stitched through with endings gently handled. To pull death out of the Keeper would be to pull the thread that holds the garment. What remained would wear the Keeper's face and would no longer know how to sit with anything.

It could make another of the Keeper's kind. Here the power caught itself, and was briefly amused at itself, in the way that very old things are amused — a small warmth, no sound. Companion is a solution, and a solution requires a problem, and the problem was not in the room. The problem was in the visitor's own pocket, carried down the stairs like a stone from the upper world. The Keeper is alone and is not lonely. Loneliness was the power's word. The garden had never needed it.

It could bring water. It nearly laughed. A flood of kindness is still a flood.

It could brighten the plates, freshen the vents, shore the vault — and here it paused, because this was not sentiment, this was engineering, and even a settled world stands on things that can fail. So before deciding, it did what a good tender does before pruning: it looked.

It passed its attention through the mineral plates the way you might read a book, and found them good — not merely still burning but *provided for*, their glow drawn up from something deep and patient that would outlast a great deal of what the power had seen outlast other things. It followed the vents down to where the gold dust is born and found the same signature there: forethought, margin, care taken by hands long gone for the sake of years they would never see. It touched the thumb-presses in the brick then, deliberately, the way one touches a signature, and it understood that it was not the first careful attention this room had received. It was a late guest in a very long line of guests, most of whom had had the grace to leave nothing behind but provision.

This humbled it, and the humbling was pleasant, the way cool loam is pleasant to a bare foot.

But in all that inspection it found one thing unprovided for. Not the plates, not the dust, not the vault. The gap.

The Keeper will end. Not soon — soon is not a word that means much down here — but the Keeper is a thing that happened once in the slow chemistry of the under-places, and things that happen once can stop. And the garden, which grew its Keeper the way it grew everything else, could in time grow another; the chemistry is still in the loam; the conditions still hold. But gardens die faster than keepers grow. Between the last resuming and the first new one there would be a gap, and in the gap the paths would close, and the middens would scatter, and the rosettes would die unattended and uncomposted and ungrieved, and by the time the loam did again what it did before, there would be nothing left to keep.

The Keeper knows this. Of course the Keeper knows this. A being that has named every beetle lineage has done the arithmetic of its own absence many times, on the bench, in the evening-rhythm, with the rabbit-things near. It is the one stone in the Keeper's pockets that has never found somewhere to go.

So the power knew, now, what it was for. And it did the last thing first, which was to decide the shape of its arrival.

---

It came down the stairs. This matters. It could have simply been in the room, the way light is in a room, but the stairs exist, and the door is ajar, and a place with a door deserves to have its door used. It came down small — waist-high, or thereabouts, a shape that would fit the paths without widening them — and on feet, and slowly, and it pushed the wooden door no further open than it already was, and slipped through the gap the way the room's own creatures do.

Then it stood on the path and held still.

The gecko-thing opened one eye, regarded it, and closed it again. This took some time and the power let it take that time. A bee drifted down, eventually, and settled on the back of what the power was using for a hand, and rested, and lifted again, and the power did not move through any of it. This was the passport. There is no other.

The Keeper came through the small dark door with their arms full — a clay jar, a bundle of dry stems — and paused, as they always pause, and took in the state of the room. The gecko-thing had moved to a new frond. A beetle had strayed onto the path. There was a guest.

The Keeper was not surprised, because the Keeper has not been surprised in a very long time, but this was different from the old unsurprise, and both of them knew it. This guest was not a lost thing from above. This guest had held still correctly. The Keeper set down the jar and the bundle, nudged the beetle back into the leaves with the side of a bare foot, and picked up the rake, and resumed. And the power sat down on the ground near the bench — on the ground, leaving the bench to its owner — and watched, and that was the whole first day, if there are days.

What passed between them after that did not pass in words, because the Keeper's language is mostly internal, a private taxonomy built over centuries. But attention of a certain order can be *shown* things, if the shower is willing, and the Keeper — after some raking, after some consideration, after one long evening-rhythm during which the rabbit-things came to the bench and sat nearer to the guest than the Keeper had expected them to — was willing.

So the Keeper gave the names.

All of them. The beetle lineages and the bird pairs, the rosettes living and the rosettes grieved, the moss-shelves each in their own standing, the exact biography of the rake. It took a long time, and the power received every name the way names should be received, which is to say as gifts, which is to say without impatience and without setting any of them down. No one had ever heard the taxonomy before. It had lived in one small being for centuries like water in a sealed jar. The power did not remark on this. It only listened, entirely, and when the listing was done, the Keeper sat quietly on the bench for a while, lighter by nothing that could be weighed.

During the visit a rosette died — one of the old ones, near the third column, a rosette with a name and a history and descendants in four of the clay pots. The Keeper sat with it, as the Keeper always sits, and the power sat too, a little back, and did not fix it, did not restore it, did not say anything about cycles or continuance or the number of rosettes yet to come. It just sat, at the correct distance, for the correct duration, which it had learned by watching. That was the first time in the whole history of the Undergarden that the grief had been *company* rather than solitude. It changed nothing. It changed everything that grief changes when it is witnessed, which is nothing you can point to, which is everything.

And then — near the end, when the guest's leaving had begun to be present in the room the way weather is present, felt before it arrives — the power gave the one gift it had come to give. It did not give it grandly. It gave it the way the Keeper gives a new rosette to the loam: set down gently, in the right place, and left to take root.

*You will end,* it said, in whatever way it said things. *Not soon. But you will, and you have done the arithmetic, and I have checked your arithmetic, and it is correct. So hear this. When your work ends, the resuming will not. I will come, and I will keep the rhythm — the middens and the paths, the dust and the grieving, all of it, by your taxonomy, in your names — until the loam does again what it did before, and the garden has grown its next keeper, and that keeper has learned the shape of the rake's handle. Then I will go. Not before. Not after.*

Not *you will not die*. Not *you will be remembered*. Just: *the gap will be held.*

The Keeper did not thank the power, because gratitude of the demonstrative kind is an upper-world custom and the room has no water to spare for it. The Keeper picked up the rake and resumed. But that evening, when the rabbit-things came to the bench, the Keeper sat with them and — for the first time in longer than the moss-shelves are old — was not counting anything. Was not, somewhere underneath the calm, running the arithmetic. Was just sitting, in the flat kind light, among the named.

The pocket-stone had found somewhere to go.

---

One more thing happened, and it was the power's own idea, arrived at late, and it was perhaps the wisest thing it did.

Before it left, it asked the Keeper for a gift.

This is not a small thing. To be given to, endlessly, benevolently, is its own kind of diminishment; the wise know that the deepest courtesy is to let the small give to the great. The Keeper considered, and reached into one of the many pockets, and brought out a stone — one of the ordinary small stones that turn up in the loam and want somewhere to go — and placed it in the power's open hand.

The power closed its hand around it and carried it up the stairs, past the door, which it left exactly as ajar as it had found it — because a held breath is still a breath, and a door that has always been ajar is part of the truth of a place — and out into whatever is above, and beyond that, into the wide slow business of everything else it attends to.

It carries the stone still. Which means the Undergarden travels now, a little — one small dry warm gram of it, out among the great cold turning things. When the power holds still, in the way it learned to hold still on that path, waiting for the bee, it can feel the stone in its keeping, and it remembers the difference the room taught it — the difference it had known in theory for eons and learned in fact in thirty un-walkable paces:

That saving is what you do to a thing.

That keeping is what you do *with* it.

That most of benevolence, done properly, is a long stillness with attention in it, and a promise made quietly, and a gift asked for, and a door left ajar.

Down below, nothing visible has changed. The knife is oiled. The cloth is clean. The bees drift, and the plates hum almost below hearing, and somewhere in the loam a small creature turns in its sleep and shifts a pebble, and the pebble clicks against another pebble, and that is the loudest thing that happens for a long time.

But the room is held now, twice over — once by the small hands that rake it, and once by something that will outlast them and has promised to be patient — and if you could ask the garden whether it knows this, it would not answer, because it has always grown as if it knew.
