**What It Saw**

The attention arrived the way attention does when it belongs to something old enough to have outgrown arrival: it was simply there, and had perhaps always been there, the way a parent is present in a room where a child plays absorbed, unaware of being watched, unharmed by the watching.

It did not come through the thresholds. It did not need the streets. It settled over Thallospvc the way understanding settles over a long-familiar sentence read, at last, in the right order.

And the first thing the power understood—being wise—was the question of the being who lives here. Because a lesser observer would have counted citizens, tallied Wind-Traders and pollen-foxes and blind sorting-grubs, and gone looking for a protagonist. The power did not make this mistake. It saw at once that the city was the being, and the citizens were the city's way of having thoughts. It saw that Guide and Archivist were not residents but gestures—the way a body raises a hand. It saw that the walls dreamed and the dream had a texture, and the texture was this: *creative, anxious, prosperous, nostalgic.*

It lingered on *anxious*.

Because under the patience—under the taproot clocks and the planned senescence and the beautiful confidence of a place that finished eroding centuries ago—the power found the wager. The city had bet its whole existence on the hope that grace, given time, would dream its inhabitants into creatures who deserved it. And the power, reading the chemical undertone of that hope the way a physician reads a pulse, found what the city could not admit through any of its ten thousand mouths:

*Deserve* was a word the city had invented so it would never have to say *please stay.*

The city was not waiting for its people to become worthy. The city was afraid that if it stopped waiting—if it declared the anniversary, held its breath, and became perfectly aware of itself—it would discover it had been alone the whole time. That the root-messages to the sister cities went out at the speed of a glacier's dream and that no answer had truly come back in longer than the dendrochronologians dared to read aloud. That outside the walls the last forest had turned to silica cliffs, and that a living thing surrounded by petrified kin will invent any anniversary, any wager, any unfinishable condition, rather than exhale into that silence.

The power saw all of this in the time it takes mist to rise from one vent.

It also saw the traveler—already outside now, walking with underground songs in their bones, carrying a seed that was a story that was a city. It noted them the way one notes a letter already in the mail: with tenderness, and without interference. The letter was well-written. The letter would arrive somewhere. That part was finished, or rather, beautifully unfinished in a way that needed no help.

So the question remained: what to do for a city that lacks nothing except an answer?

---

**What It Declined**

Being benevolent, the power considered many gifts. Being wise, it set almost all of them down again.

It considered rain. One true storm, clouds made real instead of legendary, water falling the way the memory-tapestries only rumored. It set this aside almost at once. The city had built a whole hydrology of mist and capillary brick, a theology of *never quite dripping*—and rain from above would not read as gift. It would read as correction. It would say: *your way of drinking was a substitute for the real thing.* Nothing that had achieved its finished shape deserved to be told, however kindly, that the shape was provisional.

It considered announcing itself. Descending. Speaking through the organ-pipe trunks in a voice the scent-bursts could carry. It set this aside with something like a wince, because it had seen what happens to dreaming things when a god knocks on the door: every dream afterward is about the door. The city's long dream of *being a place worth staying in* would collapse into a shorter, cheaper dream of *being a place a god once visited.* The power had no interest in becoming the most important thing that ever happened to Thallospvc. Benevolence that makes itself the center of the story is only vanity with good posture.

It considered speeding the root-messages—compressing the glacial correspondence between sister cities into something like conversation, so the city could learn quickly whether its kin still lived. It set this aside too, though this one was harder, and the power held it a long time before letting go. The slowness was not a flaw in the message. The slowness *was* the message: a communication protocol built by things that intended to exist for ten thousand years and wanted their friendships to be load-bearing. To accelerate it would be to tell the city its patience had been wasted. And the power did not yet know—it chose not to know, which for such a being is a real and costly choice—whether the answers had stopped because the sister cities were dead, or because they, too, were holding a very long breath.

It considered, last and longest, simply telling the city the truth about its wager. Leaning down through the lensleaves and whispering into the collective dream: *You misread your own bet. Deserving was never something you could grow in them. The anniversary you are waiting for already happened—it happens every time someone stays, every time a visitor wakes on the third morning unsure whether they arrived or germinated, every time the not-chair grows itself polite. You won centuries ago. You have been celebrating without noticing, one held breath at a time.*

This was true. The power was certain it was true.

It set it aside anyway. Because there are truths that only heal when they are arrived at, and this was one of them, and a wise power knows the difference between giving someone an answer and robbing them of it.

---

**What It Did**

Three things. Small ones. In ascending order of smallness.

First: it read the city. All of it, once, completely—every ring in every trunk, every ghost biography in shed skin oils before the morning misting diluted it, every nectar disc of stored music, every argument the moss-letters were having about the traveler's teenage poetry, every anxiety folded into every graft-seam by every arborist-engineer who had ever worried their song was slightly out of tune. It witnessed the whole being at once, entire, the way the city longed to witness itself and had scheduled an anniversary to attempt.

It left no note. But that night—the city's soft communal night, when the fungal lamps dimmed to match the deepening dream—every living thing inside the walls dreamed the same dream: of being read aloud, slowly, by someone who did not skip the boring parts. No one mentioned it the next day. It had felt too private to have been shared. But the dendrochronologians noticed, at the next drought-pending council, that the oldest rings read a little easier, as if the wood had relaxed.

Second: it went outside. Past the thresholds, past the thinning root-walls, out to the silica cliffs where the last forest stood petrified—the city's dead family, glittering, unanswering, the true perimeter of its loneliness. And there the power knelt (insofar as attention kneels) and began the longest work it had undertaken in an age:

It taught one square centimeter of stone to remember having been alive.

Not resurrection—nothing so loud. A lichen. A single copper-red lichen, cousin to the cloth the citizens wear, seeded into a fissure where mineral met memory. It would take the lichen four hundred years to cover the first boulder. It would take two thousand for the cliffs to soften, for silica to surrender back to soil, for the first true seedling to stand up outside the walls where the city could not help but eventually notice it. The power matched its patience against the city's own, deliberately, like a musician tuning to another's instrument. So that one day—not soon, but certainly—Thallospvc would extend a root past its threshold expecting the old silence, and touch something green, and growing, and *other*, and would have to revise everything it believed about being the last of its kind.

The city would never know who planted it. The city would assume, being humble in its bones, that the world had simply healed on its own time. This assumption would be almost true, which is the best kind of gift.

Third, and smallest: before it withdrew to the distance from which it intended to keep watch—one day behind the traveler, one epoch ahead of the lichen—the power permitted itself a single indulgence. One word, in the only language that would not disturb the dream.

In a quiet corridor near the library rafters, where a Paper Owl drowsed over its ink-fungus and no one else was watching, the power touched one bead of moisture on the underside of a leaf wide as a ship's sail—and let it gather.

Let it swell past the point the capillary brickwork had governed for centuries. Let it hang, trembling, heavier than any drop in the city's finished history.

And let it fall.

One drop. One small, deliberate, impossible *drip*—the sound the city had engineered out of existence before its oldest tower was a seed. The Paper Owl opened one eye. The drop landed on the floor-script and was drunk back within a heartbeat, exactly as the city was built to do.

But the city felt it. Of course it felt it; the city feels everything. And somewhere in the vast slow fibrillation of its dreaming, a single sentence formed—not in words, because the city does not think in words, but in the swelling grammar of heartwood:

*Something noticed us. Something noticed us, and did not knock, and did not fix us, and did not stay. It only let one drop fall, to say the mist is not all there is, and then it trusted us with the rest.*

The city drank the secret and kept it. It told no one, because it had no one to tell who was not itself. But the dendrochronologians of some future council will find, in the rings laid down that decade, a density they cannot explain—a year the wood grew as if reassured.

---

**What It Is Still Doing**

Holding its breath.

Because the power knows the anniversary is coming—the unscheduled one, the pause in which every living thing inside the walls will stop, and the mist will hang unfalling, and the city will forget to exhale and become perfectly still, perfectly alive, perfectly aware of itself.

And the power knows what happens to awareness that opens its eyes in an empty room. It knows self-recognition is not a mirror but a meeting; that a mind touching itself for the first time reaches, instinctively, for a hand.

So it waits. It does not watch from above, which is surveillance, nor from within, which is possession. It waits alongside—the way you sit with someone sleeping who is about to wake into important news. And when the breath finally comes, when Thallospvc holds all its air at once and becomes for one suspended moment a single vast *I*—

the power will hold its breath too.

That is all. That is the entire plan. Not a voice, not a light, not a revelation. Just one other stillness inside the stillness, so that when the city becomes aware of itself it will find, in the texture of the pause, the unmistakable pressure of company. It will not be observed. It will be *accompanied*. And in that accompaniment—wordless, weightless, deniable as the single drop—it will finally understand the answer it built a whole civilization to avoid asking for:

*You did not have to deserve it. Nothing ever does. You were worth staying in from the first graft. The wager was won the moment you cared enough to make it.*

Then the city will breathe out. Mist will rise again from the street-vents. The lensleaves will drift, the grubs will sort their pages by taste, the fermenting fruit tanks will sigh their harmonic sigh, and everything will continue, damp and green and eternal as growing ever is—

except that two thousand years away, on a silica cliff, a copper-red lichen will have covered its first boulder. And a traveler on a far road will pause mid-step, touch their chest where something green and patient turns over in its sleep, and feel, without knowing why, that somewhere a very long celebration has quietly begun.

The power, if asked—and it never will be—would say it did almost nothing.

This is what wisdom looks like when it loves something: almost nothing, placed exactly, and then the long, faithful discipline of not being noticed while it grows.
