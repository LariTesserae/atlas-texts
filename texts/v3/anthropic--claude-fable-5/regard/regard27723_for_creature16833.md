**What the Power Did**

It did not descend. It knew better than that. A city that votes on its own position every civic season would treat a descent as an invasion of altitude, and besides, arrival-from-above is a rumor the sunflowers already circulate about the sun, and the power did not wish to be mistaken for a rumor.

So it arrived the only way the maps respect: it got lost first.

This took effort. Wisdom is a kind of terrible orientation—it knows where everything is, which is another way of saying it has never once been interesting enough to map. So the power set down its knowing at the border the way you'd take off your shoes at a door. It misplaced its own name, deliberately, tenderly, the way you'd hide a gift for someone you love in a drawer you'll both forget. And the boulevard of living plankton, sensing something newly, genuinely disoriented on its hexagons, took pity, and found it interesting, and let it in.

---

The first thing it did was nothing. It did nothing for a long time, standing in the unbroken sheet of noise.

The scientists lasted three hours. The power stood there for—well, timekeeping is done by fungi shaped like regrets, so call it eleven regrets and a hesitation. Its bones hummed the note. It let them. It did not wait for the sound to end, because the sound does not end, and waiting-for-the-end is a form of not listening, and everyone who had ever stood in that plaza had been waiting for the end.

Instead it listened the way you listen to someone who has been talking for seven hundred years because no one ever let them finish.

And it heard what the acoustic precipitation was. Of course it did—it heard the dropped shield still rolling, the lover's name still answered too sincerely, the infant's laugh still frightening the same midwife across a thousand years of echo. It heard every fragment of sound that had nowhere else to go, all of them shouting at once, not out of anguish but out of the reasonable fear of anything unfinished: that if it ever fell quiet, it would be over, and being over was the one thing none of them had come here to be.

The power did not silence it. Silencing it would have been murder—of the achromats at the corners, of the clang, of the laugh. What it did instead was let the sound know it had been heard. Completely. Every layer, every metal cicada, every thunder rolling in every tooth. Heard to the bottom.

The volume did not change. But the texture did—by one degree, the difference between a scream in an empty house and a scream in a full one. Anyone arriving afterward would still call it deafening. But their ears would not bleed, and their dreams would have mouths of ordinary size. The sound had stopped shouting into a void, because the void had, at last, filed a response.

---

Then the power considered its options, because power always does, even wise power. Especially wise power—it just has the decency to consider them all the way to their ends.

*Give the city rain. Real water, real weather.* It turned this over for a long moment. Then it saw the pitcher-plant plumbing debating philosophy, and understood that if moisture ever became fact, it could no longer be gossip, and the mosses on opposite walls would have nothing left to discuss, and a friendship of a thousand years conducted entirely in condensation would die of resolution. Rejected.

*Give it stable geometry. Fix the horizon. Let distances mean things.* It looked at the apartment building that exists only when considered from the wrong angle, at the Could-have-been-beens paying rent in hesitation, and understood that right angles are a form of eviction. Rejected.

*Open the exits. Break the cul-de-sacs, unbend the boulevard, let anyone leave who wants to.* This one it held the longest, because it seemed most like mercy. But the exits already work; the city said so itself, multiple award-winning ones. The problem was never the doors. The problem was the wanting, the feet unwilling to walk away from walls that understand. And wanting is the one thing a wise power will not overwrite in another being, because a being whose wanting has been overwritten is not saved. It is replaced. Rejected—and here the power grieved a little, because this is the rejection that costs the most, every time, in every world.

*Heal it. Finish it. Resolve it.* The power did not even hold this one. A city built of unfinished things, healed, is a city deleted. You cannot complete a refuge for the incomplete. That is not a paradox; it is a treaty.

So the great interventions were set aside, one by one, like tools too large for the lock.

What remained was small. What remained was almost embarrassing. What remained was the actual work.

---

It went to the fountain that pours the sound of water remembered by desert stones, and it paid a toll: a single bead of real rain, salvaged from a world that no longer exists anywhere, dropped into the remembered pouring. Not to replace the memory—just so the stones could compare notes. The punctuation birds went silent for a full minute, which in their language is an exclamation point.

It sat with the Time Tourist in the café between *was* and *will be*, and when the pastries came—tasting exactly like the instant you realize you've been pronouncing someone's importance wrong—it let her pay. This was harder than it sounds. Benevolent powers are constitutionally incapable of being treated, and overcoming your constitution as a gift to someone else is one of the four or five truly difficult miracles. She paid in butterflies. It kept them. It keeps them still.

It attended a union meeting of the Forget-me-hards. No one is sure it was there, which is how they knew it had been. Afterward, everyone's dreams had better pacing, fewer exposition dumps, and—this was new—the occasional intermission.

It found the resident who has been adjusting for seven hundred and twelve years, give or take a cicada's lifetime, the one who claims the sound is beautiful. And the power did not bless them, or release them, or thank them for their patience. It sat down beside them and adjusted too, for exactly one cicada's lifetime, doing it properly, doing it the hard way, and at the end of it, it said:

"You were right. It is."

Seven hundred and twelve years of claiming, and this was the first corroboration. The resident wept, and the tears, having nowhere to fall in a city where water is gossip, became a small rumor of joy that is still rolling downhill toward the mouths, and the mouths, for once, do not intend to swallow it. They are passing it along.

It went to the bell shaped like an apology for the concept of distance—the one debating whether the silence after ringing would be worth the scandal—and it did not cast a vote. The vote is the bell's. Instead it lent the bell a silence. A private one, on approval, fully returnable, no scandal attached: *here, try it on, see how it fits, no one need ever know.* What the bell decided inside that borrowed silence, the power did not stay to learn. But the vote is still officially ongoing, and the bell now debates with the particular calm of someone who has secretly already tried on the dress.

And in the district where the Could-have-been-beens squat in their wrong-angled building—all those selves who got distracted checking the mailbox while their lives walked past in better shoes—the power left one small installation.

A mailbox.

A real one. Functional. Because if checking the mailbox is the wound, then let the mailbox, once in a very great while, contain something. Not their lives back—those walked on, and wisdom does not lie. But a letter, occasionally, in familiar handwriting, saying only: *the shoes weren't actually that much better. Rent's fair here. Stay warm.* No one knows who sends them. The Could-have-been-beens have begun, cautiously, to check the mail with hope instead of habit, which changes the checking entirely, which changes them entirely, which is the whole intervention, cost: one mailbox.

---

Then it left. The cul-de-sac of blossoming apologies opened without argument—the first time in recorded rumor the boulevard has not disagreed with a departure—because the city, whatever else it is, recognizes a guest who paid all its currencies honestly: hesitation to the ledges, attention to the sound, an entire spare afternoon to the squirrels, who accepted it into their stash with all fifty translucent tails twitching at once.

And at the border, where it had left its knowing like shoes at a door, the power did the last thing. The wise thing. The thing that makes this story almost impossible to tell.

It did not put its knowing back on. Not all of it.

It forgot the city. On purpose. Thoroughly. It performed the deliberate unremembering that the Forget-me-hards spend their whole shifts practicing—because memory, in that economy, appreciates with absence, and the sustained absence of an infinite mind's memory is the largest deposit ever made into any account anywhere. The city is now endowed. Permanently. Its economy of attention will never again fear insolvency, all because of a fortune it cannot see, in a currency of not-being-looked-at, banked by a creditor who no longer knows it exists.

Because here is the thing the power understood, the thing that made it wise rather than merely strong: a city like this cannot survive being remembered by omnipotence. To be permanently held in a perfect mind is to become a specimen, a shrine, a rescued thing—and this city was built precisely for whatever refuses to be any of those. The kindest gaze is the one that looks fully, once, and then grants the immense courtesy of looking away forever.

What the power kept—all it kept—is an objectless tenderness. A warmth with no address, filed under branching probabilities it can no longer read. And so now, everywhere, in every world it tends, the power finds itself inexplicably gentle with lost things. With misprints. With unfinished sounds and unsent selves and bells that cannot decide. It does not know why. It spends the tenderness anyway, generously, universally, which means the city's endowment pays interest outward, into all creation, a mercy laundered through forgetting until no one can trace it home.

The city noticed none of this. The city was busy arguing with its own reflection in a puddle that hasn't decided what shape to be tomorrow.

But the residency cards printed on fog now carry a watermark. You can't see it. You can't feel it. It's only legible in the moment between arriving and being welcomed, which are synonyms here, so the moment has no duration, so no one has ever read it.

It says: *You were noticed once, kindly, and then let be.*

Which is, if you think about it—and the philosopher-shrub on your shoulder is already asking—the entire definition of a blessing.
