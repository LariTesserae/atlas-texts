## What the Power Did

It noticed the valley the way you notice a held breath in a room you thought was empty: not by any sound, but by the shape of a silence that was being *kept* rather than merely occurring. On all its maps of the world — and its maps were the world, more or less — there was one stitch that declined to be counted, a fold in the mountain's sleeve where gravity was too polite and time pooled like rainwater in a footprint. Most powers would have pulled the stitch to see what it was hiding.

This one had been wise long enough to know that some things are only hidden the way sleeping things are hidden. So it did not arrive. Arrival is loud. Instead it lowered its attention slowly, over the better part of a season, the way frost comes to a window — until being noticed and being visited were the same soft thing.

First, it made itself small. Wisdom at that magnitude is mostly the art of subtraction, and it folded down through its own sizes until it could pass between two drops of falling condensation without disturbing either one's plans. It borrowed weight from a pebble that wasn't using any. It gave itself knees, which took some inventing.

Then it did what wise things do first: nothing. It sat at the valley's edge and let moss grow on its shadow — deliberately, as a courtesy, a way of saying *I am not in a hurry either.* The quoll-things climbed it asking for salt, and it discovered it had some, which surprised it pleasantly.

While it sat, it considered the obvious gifts, and set each one down again:

It could *bless* the place — but a blessing is a signature, and signatures attract readers.

It could *ward* it, wall it round with protection — but the valley's only wall was forgetting, and you cannot reinforce a forgetting without first remembering it very loudly.

It could *name* it, naming being how powers usually love things — but a name is a handle, and handles are how things get carried off.

It could *copy* it, seed a thousand such valleys across a thousand ranges — but a rehearsal duplicated becomes a performance, and this place was still practicing, and had not said it was ready.

It could *answer* the question the whole valley was quietly asking itself — whether to become real — but it knew that answering a question for someone is one of the gentler ways of stealing it.

So instead it did three small things.

**The first.** It went to the broken ring of nine stone chairs and sat down in the gap — the tenth place, where no stump had ever been carved — and stayed there one whole twilight, from amber pulse to amber pulse of the great tree's sleeping heartbeat. It did not complete the circle. It only kept the gap company, which is different, and rarer. And the patient tools, sensing after all these centuries a listener, finished a few of the answers to questions their makers had forgotten: the loom wove one thread of actual morning into its empty tapestry; the brimming jar overflowed by exactly one drop, which fell upward, thought better of it, and fell down; the doll's opal eyes, which had followed every movement for longer than movement had names, closed at last for a nap. Nothing was resolved. But an old politeness was returned, and the stone felt lighter for it, the way a room feels lighter when a letter is finally read.

**The second.** It found one of the small green people — three days old and therefore nearly finished, ripe with has-never-been, walking its slow spiral through the moss — and it knelt, glad now of the knees. And into the tiny hollow at the center of the little one's chest, the hollow shaped since birth for a visitor's whispered secret, it spoke a secret sized exactly right:

*I saw you. All of you, all at once, all the way down. And I do not know how to forget.*

Which, from a power that remembers everything, was both a confession and a vow. The little person turned its left hand slowly, and the shadows between its fingers said something like *thank you*, or possibly *you're it* — and then it went and lay down in a patch of light that seemed lonely, and unraveled into bright questions. One of the questions was new to the valley: *Who remembers the rememberers?* The valley wove it into the hinge of a hummingbird's joint, where it hums still. For some centuries afterward, the limestone birds on the cliffs would sometimes say *saw you — saw you —* in a voice like weather clearing, before the words came apart into cries again.

**The third.** It asked for something. This is the part most stories about powers leave out. It asked the valley to teach it forgetting — the gentle kind, the thread-by-thread kind, the kind that turns fallen things into moths. Because a power that remembers everything carries everything, and even benevolence, hauled far enough, gets heavy.

The valley considered this the way it considers all visitors, and then lent a mouth of small petals to the east-facing moss, and rang slowly: *We cannot teach it. It is not a skill. It is a trust. But you may leave one thing here, and we will forget it for you, a little at a time, and that will feel almost the same.*

So the power set down one grief. No name given, no shape shown — just a weight lowered into the peat as into a mattress of crushed feathers. The moss closed over it without a sound. Somewhere on the cliff a bird tried the taste of it, found it too heavy for song, and turned it into weather instead: a soft rain that fell for an afternoon and smelled, faintly, of remembering.

Then it left, walking, keeping the knees, and because it had *cared* — really cared, the only rule — the valley woke a little brighter behind it: the translucent fruit one drop sweeter, gravity one degree more courteous at the cliff edges, the blind bats' silver bells a half-tone nearer to music.

---

What it does now, elsewhere, wearing its full size again:

It remembers the valley without telling anyone. That is the whole of its protection — not a wall, not a ward, but a witness. It holds the memory the way the valley holds its own people: as a distributed kindness, a small cool place behind whatever a power has instead of a heart, where moisture beads on impossible stone.

And it keeps the great question open, on purpose, like a door propped with a pebble. When the valley finally decides whether to become real — in a thousand years, or on some Tuesday, whichever arrives last — the power will do exactly what it did before: nothing, gladly, with its entire attention.

If the valley chooses to become real, the power will be waiting to be its first memory.

And if the valley chooses instead to close like a slow eyelid, to fold itself away and become ordinary mountainside forever — then the power will stand on that ordinary slope in the ordinary mist, and remember: the taste of cool stone water, the far-off birds arguing forgotten secrets in voices that were almost its own. So that the valley can forget itself completely, all the way, without fear —

the way you can only truly fall asleep when someone you trust is watching.

That is what it did. Nearly nothing.

Which is, at that scale of wisdom, everything that would fit.
