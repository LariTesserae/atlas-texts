**What Noticed the Saddle Town**

Powers are drawn to need the way water is drawn to seams. That is their nature and mostly their work: they move along the fault lines of the world, toward the burning city, the failing harvest, the prayer sent up like smoke from a cold house. Need is loud, even when it is silent, and a benevolent power spends most of its long existence going where it is loudest.

So understand how strange it was, what drew this one to the saddle. Not a cry. Not a wound. The opposite — a silence so well-made that it carried. From very far off, in whatever sense powers have that stands in for distance, the saddle town registered the way a single still point registers on the surface of a moving river: not by its brightness but by its refusal to flicker. Everything else in the world was changing as fast as it could. Here was a place the size of a single thought that had finished changing, and the power, who had seen empires rehearse their endings and species audition and fail, had never seen *finished* before. It came the way the chough comes. To inspect.

It arrived on a cloudy afternoon, riding up the western slope inside the mist, because that seemed the polite door, and it walked the town — through walls, along the humming lane, down into the dark of the cisterns, into the rosette of rust-orange lichen on the south wall of the third house, which it inhabited for a while the way you might sit in a very old chair, carefully. It read the four bands of masonry like four lines of a poem. It found the cushion plant and did the arithmetic of its centuries and felt something that, in a smaller being, would have been the impulse to take off a hat.

And then, being benevolent, it reached for its gifts.

This is the moment to slow down, because this is the whole story, and it lasted — by the power's reckoning — about forty years, which it spent standing in the middle of the long terrace, where the fox pauses, considering.

It could warm the air of the saddle by three degrees, and the grain terrace would double, and the winters would loosen their grip on the old woman's hands. It saw, before the thought finished forming, the consequence: more grain, then more goats, then a fifth person, a sixth, a tenth; the gaps between buildings filling in, the load-bearing emptiness asked to bear load of the wrong kind; within four generations a town like other towns, suffocating gently on itself. The cold was not the town's enemy. The cold was a wall of the house.

It could heal. The old woman's left hip had worn the way the quern-stones had worn, into a smooth hollow of pain that she carried up the terrace stairs each morning without comment. The power held the healing in its hand for a long time — a decade, perhaps — and then set it down, because it had looked closely at her and seen that the pain had been fitted into her days like a flagstone, that she rose before light *partly because of it*, that her unhurried walk was not in spite of the hip but a treaty with it, and that she had not asked. Powers that heal the unasked, it knew, leave a different wound: the knowledge of being watched, weighed, found pitiable. She was not pitiable. She was load-bearing.

It could grant the four of them long life. It saw at once that this was the cruelest gift on the table. The town's permanence was made of succession the way the wall was made of stones — replaceable in the individual, permanent in the role — and to freeze four particular people in their positions would be to do to the standing arrangement exactly what frost does to a flagstone. The town did not need these four forever. It needed *four*, forever. That was a different thing, and it was already provided for.

It could make the place known — set it gently into the songs of the lowlands, so that pilgrims would climb the eastern trail and the town would be honored, recorded, preserved. It nearly laughed, in whatever way powers laugh. Preserved. The word the lowlands used for *embalmed*. It had seen what attention does to still points. Attention is a crowd, and the town had been built by people who understood crowds well enough to leave thirty paces between houses.

One by one it picked up its gifts, turned them in the light, and set them down. This took the forty years. The chough walked through it many times, inspecting, finding nothing, recording the nothing with one satisfied note. The power began to suspect the chough had the right idea about the entire profession.

In the end it did three things, and you should know all three, because together they are the answer to the question of what wisdom does when it finds something already whole.

The first thing: it set its weight, very gently, against the world's noticing. Not a wall — the town had walls. More like the lean of the junipers: a constant, mild pressure, all in one direction. Maps drawn in the lowlands would mislay the saddle by a few miles, just enough. Travelers planning routes would feel, without knowing why, that the southern pass was more sensible. The too-regular outcropping on the ridge would go on reading as outcropping to every eye that did not need it to be otherwise — and to the rare eye that did need it, the niece in a lowland town with a restlessness she couldn't name, the quiet man walking away from a loud life, the trail would seem to straighten under their feet. The power did not summon these people. It only stopped mislaying the map for them. The town's old camouflage gained a guarantor, and the cost to the town was nothing, which was the only price the power could find that the town would have agreed to pay.

The second thing it did exactly once, and then argued with itself about for a century. One winter — a black, dry, windless winter, the kind that comes a few times a millennium — a frost reached down toward the saddle deep enough to split the elder cushion plant to its root, to end in one night a continuance of nine hundred years. The power warmed the air above the terrace corner by less than a breath. By morning the frost had passed, the cushion plant continued, and nothing anywhere recorded that anything had happened, because nothing had — that was the craft of it, an intervention indistinguishable within a season, a repair made with the same stone in the same way. It had learned that from the mason. It was still not sure it had been right. It decided that one such act per millennium was the most a wise power could afford in a place like this, and it has not yet spent the next one.

The third thing was this: it stayed. Not as a resident — the count of residents is the town's own business — but as something nearer to what the water is. A position, taken up. The standing arrangement of the saddle, it turned out, had always had room in it for one more role, unfilled through all the centuries because nothing had come along that could fill it: the position of *witness who refrains*. Duties: be present for everything; do nothing; want nothing; notice. The fox had been pausing midway across the long terrace for a long time, listening, and what it listened to now had a name, though the fox didn't need one. Some rooms ask it of you. Now the room had someone whose whole work was being asked.

Once — only once — the power put on a body and came up the eastern trail in the cold light, an unremarkable traveler, because it wanted to receive the town instead of perceiving it, and there is no other way. The old woman saw it coming a half-mile off, finished what she was doing, and when it reached the long terrace she lifted the cistern lid and drew up a dipper of the dark still water — the water that passes through every resident's life, the truest citizen — and held it out without a word. The power, who had come to give and had spent forty years learning to set its gifts down, took the dipper and drank. It was cold and tasted of stone and faintly of cloud. The benevolence ran, for once, in the proper direction.

It asked her, because some forms must be observed, whether there was anything the town needed.

She considered this honestly, looking out past the junipers at the wide cold light, and said, "Cistern's full," which was the complete answer, and they sat on the terrace edge a while as the afternoon cloud came up the western slope and dragged itself across the stone, and they said nothing at all, and this was understood by both parties to be the good part of the day.

There is a fourth thing, but it happened elsewhere, so it is only barely part of this story. The power went back to its work — the fault lines, the burning cities, the loud need, where intervention is not presumption but mercy, and where it intervened as it always had. But those who study such things, if anyone studied such things, would notice that its work changed after the saddle. It began to make repairs that could not be found within a season. It began to leave gaps and to trust them to bear load. It began to ask, before every gift, the question it had learned standing forty years on the long terrace: *is this for them, or is this so that I will have given?* — and to set down, gently, everything that failed the question. The town became the quern on which the power ground its own benevolence fine. It had come as the wise one. It left as the student of an old woman, a mason, two cushion plants, and a population of lichen that had never once intervened in anything and had thereby kept a mountain.

And a thousand years from now, on the eastern trail, when a traveler looks up and sees the saddle, the small gray town breathing cloud, a thread of smoke, a figure crossing the upper terrace with water, unhurried — sometimes, not often, that traveler will be the power itself, come round on its long inspection route, walking up on its borrowed feet to check the gutters and audit the terrace and find the same nothing it found the century before.

It records the nothing with great satisfaction. Then it falls away off the saddle's edge into the air, and the wind moves through the gaps between the buildings, which were always meant to be there, and which are still empty, and which are holding everything up.
