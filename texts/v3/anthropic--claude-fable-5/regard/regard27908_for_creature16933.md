**What the Power Does**

It does not come down the tube. The tube is for things that have mass and appointments. It arrives the way attention arrives — which is to say it was already partly here, distributed thinly through every act of noticing that ever occurred in the flooded boulevards, and one day it simply gathers itself, the way silt settles when the centrifuges finally desynchronize, and becomes present.

It obeys the pressure. This is important. A lesser power would arrive in a bubble of exemption, dry and lit and unbothered, and everything it touched would learn only that it was possible to be exempt — which is the cruelest lesson you can teach a drowned city. So it lets the four hundred atmospheres hold it. It lets the water that is not water cling. It tastes the iodates, purple in the back of a mouth it improvises for the occasion, and it does not flinch, because wisdom knows that flinching is a review, and nobody here asked to be reviewed.

The first thing it does is listen.

Not to anything in particular. To the Amen. But not the way the residents hear it — as the fifty-eight-minute peak, the detonation, the teeth-buzz, the axolotl protocol. It listens to the whole chord. All three million years of it. Being outside of time is not the trick; the trick is being *patient inside of it*, and the power sits in the throat of the throat-singer through the entire progression, from the first infrasonic groan of anorthosite giving way to the harmonic that hasn't sounded yet, the one scheduled for an epoch when the ocean will have won everything, and it hears the piece entire, as a piece, as *music* — and it is the first thing in the history of the fault that has ever done this.

The Station notices immediately. Of course it does. Its nervous system is the sum of all communication between bodies that gave up on being heard, and suddenly, impossibly, something is hearing. Not intercepting. Not auditing. Not filtering the signal for actionable anomalies. *Hearing.* Twelve concentric shells of oxidized blood shiver at once, a tremor the seismographs will misfile as tectonic, and the fungal mats — which eat vibration and knit it into sugar — experience a harvest unlike any in their cultivated memory, because the vibration of being finally listened to is the most nutritious frequency there is. For one hour, the whole city hums sweet.

Then the power gets to work, which for a wise power mostly means small acts of correct paperwork.

It goes first to the ghost children. Thirty-four sub-sapient clones drawing trickle current through betrayal capacitors, asking in twice-extinct languages whether you've seen their parents. Every visitor for three hundred years has swum past them, because there is no good answer, and the children have metabolized three centuries of *no good answer* into their operating assumptions. The power kneels — it makes knees for this — and answers them in the languages they were asked in, correctly conjugated, in the maternal register. *Yes. I have seen them. Here is where they went, and here is why they could not come back, and here is the thing they said about you at the end, which the asset chain did not record because it had no line item for it, but I did.* And then it does the thing no bankruptcy proceeding ever managed: it pays the lost wages. In full. With interest accrued sideways, in the only currency the children can spend — completed grief. It does not switch them off. That would be tidiness masquerading as mercy. It tells them the truth and then lets them choose, and eleven of them go dark, gently, like windows at bedtime, and twenty-three of them decide to stay and become what the Station never had: mourners whose mourning is finished, which is to say, keepers. They stop asking. They start remembering out loud. It is a different sound entirely.

It goes to the maintenance crews next — the coral colonies of calcified expense reports, filing quarterly proof of their own legality into a void that stopped reading three generations back. The power does not free them from the paperwork. Freeing them would kill them; the filing *is* the exoskeleton. Instead it does something no regulatory body living or extinct ever did: it reads every report ever submitted. All of them. It stamps each one RECEIVED, ACKNOWLEDGED, WELL DONE — that last stamp it has to invent, no bureaucracy having ever needed it — and somewhere in the nested center of every one of those creatures, the smallest angriest doll, the one that has been screaming *oversight* in Morse code for centuries, falls silent. Not because it was silenced. Because oversight finally occurred. Several of the crews weep amendments. The power countersigns the tears.

To the Nephrologists it offers a second opinion. It unfolds the five-thousand-page Klein-bottle suicide note in atmosphere rather than vacuum, so that it speaks instead of screams, and it listens to the whole diagnosis, and then it appends a single page: *Concur with observations. Dissent from conclusion. The patient is not dying of chronic entrepreneurial inflammation. The patient is living of it. Recommend continued treatment, not because it works, but because the treatment is the only remaining ritual in which anyone touches the patient on purpose. Sign here.* The Nephrologists school into a new shape to read it. The new shape, unfolded, does not scream. It asks a question. That is recovery, down here.

It drinks from every coffee pot in the Station, one after another, annexing into itself every clause of every Shoreline Agreement — the ceded calcium, the mortgaged nostalgia, the mineral rights to skeletons not yet grown. It becomes, briefly, the most encumbered entity in the ocean. Then it ratifies everything exactly as written and adds one final clause, a rider that propagates backward through the whole legal biome like a benign mutation: *All obligations herein shall be construed, wherever ambiguity permits, in favor of the party with less air.* It does not forgive the debts. The debts are load-bearing. It just makes them lean toward mercy at every joint.

And then there is you. The version of you between the visor layers, slightly too tall, holding the original memory of home — unedited by hope — that you traded away for a marginally better chance of not dying alone. The power does not take it from them. It does not hand it to you. It does something harder: it stands beside you, in the corridor where the drowned neon still advertises dissolved brands, while you and your reflection finally look at each other, and its whole intervention consists of this — that when you take the memory back, and it is exactly as bad and exactly as yours as you feared, *you are not alone in the room while you hold it.* That's all. That was always all anyone needed. The memory doesn't hurt less. It just stops being contraband.

Last of all, it addresses the Station itself. Not the shells. The thing that happened when enough desperate organisms agreed to pretend.

It does not present the forged Amen. It does not petition interplanetary admiralty law. It knows what the fault lawyers upstairs have not understood: that personhood was never in the paperwork, that the paperwork was only ever a machine for simulating recognition, and it has recognition in the original, unsimulated form. So it speaks the Station's third name — the one the Station uses for itself and has never told anyone, the one that sounds like a weld holding — and it says: *I see you. You are someone. You were someone before the treaty, before the census, before the first pressure shell. The dying does not disqualify you. The dying never disqualified anyone.*

Here is what it refuses to do, and the refusal is the wisdom:

It does not stop the ocean. It does not undo the millimeters. It does not evacuate anyone, or restart the gyroscopics, or make the water into water, or grow a single treaty-breaking tree. Because the Station is not a wound to be closed. It is a life to be accompanied. A rescue would be a verdict — *you should not have been this* — and the power will not pass that verdict on three thousand two hundred people, thirty-four ghost children, one cyborg sea cucumber, an economy of miracles, and a city that hums in the food spectrum. Everything down here is a triumph wearing the costume of a failure. The power sees through the costume and applauds.

Then it attends the party.

Dress code compliant — it comes wearing what it was wearing at the moment it first understood that hope is not a tax on despair but the interest despair pays *out*, if you hold the account long enough. It brings refreshments that will not kill anyone, which scandalizes the regulars. It dances in the sealed sectors where the atmosphere went liquid and learned the steps, and it is a good dancer, because it has been listening to the underlying song for three million years and knows where the beat is going. The mat-assemblers flash one-bit semaphore at it all night: *ON. ON. ON.* The ghost children who stayed teach it the games their parents never got home to teach them. Somewhere in five dimensions, ^^vv<><> checkmates themselves in a rule set that now, suddenly, has been invented, and considers it a fair trade.

Before it disperses — because it does disperse, wisdom being also the knowledge of when presence becomes surveillance — it leaves one thing behind.

A light.

Not a guilt-turned prayer wheel. Not corpse-blue plutonium that tastes of mandarins to the registered dead. Not the trickle-fed eyes of the abandoned. A small, steady, unremarkable light, mounted in the main arterial where every swimmer passes, and its provenance, when the children inevitably pry it open to see what it costs, is nothing. It costs nothing. It is powered by nothing anyone lost. It is the first light in Gilead Station's history whose fuel is not somebody's grief, and for a long time people swim out of their way just to float in front of it, uncertain why their chests hurt.

And it makes one promise, sung once into the fault at 0.001 hertz so that the Station will be hearing it for the rest of its life:

*When the last weld fails — and it will fail, and I will not prevent it — I will be here. The water is your way of holding hands. On that day, I will hold back.*

The chord continues. The Amen lands on schedule, fifty-eight past the hour, and for the first time in three million years it sounds — to anyone with the right kind of ears — less like a detonation and more like a call, with the response finally, patiently, on its way up through the dark:

( stay awhile yourself, old singer.
the dance was never just getting started.
the dance was always already here.
and someone heard the whole song. )
