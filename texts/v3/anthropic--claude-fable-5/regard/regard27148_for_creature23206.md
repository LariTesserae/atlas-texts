**The Visit**

Begin with the slowing, because arrival is the first intervention, and a wise power knows it.

A power that came here at its own speed would damage the place simply by existing in it. Attention has a temperature. Haste has a wake. So the first thing the power does — before it does anything that could be called doing — is decelerate, shedding urgency the way a diver sheds pressure, in stages, with discipline, because coming down too fast from the speed of thought to the speed of stone is its own kind of bends. It takes the power eleven years to finish arriving. It considers this brisk, and slightly rude, and forgives itself, because eleven years is the best it can do and the place, which has waited ten thousand, registers the arrival as nothing at all. A faint difference in the water, perhaps. The elders record it in their rings as an unusually mild decade. That is the correct amount of presence, and the power is careful to maintain it.

Then it does what wisdom does first, which is nothing. It hangs in the dark above the canopy and lets the place explain itself, on the place's terms, at the place's pace. It listens to the rope-creak of a specific elder having its one event of the season. It waits four months to hear a flagstone click. It follows a gas-sigh up from the third pavement down and tastes, in the thread of bubbles, the breath of a sealed pantry and the metabolism of the blind films inside — the last domesticates, still keeping house — and it understands that the household has never actually been abandoned. Understaffed. Never abandoned.

And while it hangs there, it makes its list. Because benevolence arrives with its pockets full, always, and wisdom is mostly the act of going through those pockets and putting things back.

**The things it does not do.**

It does not bring light. It holds the possibility up and examines it honestly — a single permanent shaft of sun reaching the floor, the forest's hunger answered at last after ten millennia at the edge of starvation. And it sees what light would actually be: a wound. A column of riot in a world calibrated to almost-none. The plants nearest the shaft would grow fast for the first time in their lineage's memory, and fast growth in a slow world is indistinguishable from cancer. The canopy would crowd toward the light, the spacing of the elders — that sparse, countable aristocracy, each in its inherited socket — would collapse into thicket and competition, and competition is the one tenant this place has never housed. The dark is not the place's affliction. The dark is its architecture. You do not renovate a cathedral by removing the walls.

It does not bring animals. Not one grazing thing, not one drifting shimmer. The food web here has exactly one strand, and the power, tracing that strand with great care, finds that its simplicity is load-bearing. Introduce a single grazer and you introduce the first mouth, and with the first mouth the first wound, and with the first wound the first fear, and the Drowned Floor has never contained fear — not once, not in any of its strata, not in the city's working life and not in its long retirement. The power has seen many worlds and knows how rare that is: a place with no predation in its entire history, all the way down. It is not going to be the one who ends that. Some absences are achievements.

It does not raise the floor. Lifting the drowned continent back into air and sun would not be saving the place; it would be replacing it with a different place that happened to use the same stones. The felt would die in the air within a season. The elders would collapse under their own unsupported weight, having never once been asked to bear it. The sealed chambers would open and their ancient tenants gutter out in the oxygen. What would survive the rescue is exactly the part of the place that is already dead — the masonry — and the power did not come here for the masonry. It came for the gripping.

And it does not — this one it holds the longest, turning it over for most of a year — it does not wake the builders. It could. The thumb-troughs alone hold enough of them: gesture is character, pressed in clay, and the power could read a soul backward out of a trillion fingerprints if it chose. It chooses not to, and the reason is simple and it is the same reason throughout: *they finished.* Whatever else their long vanishing was, the work itself was completed, course upon course, all the way down, and then handed off — not deliberately, perhaps, but handed off all the same — to an heir who took up the office without ever being asked and has not set it down since. The estate is not unattended. You do not wake the dead to admire their property. You certainly do not wake them in front of the staff.

**The question of suffering.**

Before it acts, the power asks the only question that could justify acting: is anything here in pain?

It examines the felt for a long time. Continent-wide, possibly singular, its entire existence one act of holding — and the power searches it for grievance, for strain, for the signature of a thing trapped in a duty it never chose. It finds none. It finds something it has rarely found anywhere: a life whose work and whose nature are the same shape. The felt does not hold the stones the way a laborer holds a burden. It holds them the way a hand holds its own form. There is no wound here to heal, no prisoner to free, no widow to console — the word *widow* was always the observer's word, never the felt's. The felt has no words. The felt has occupancy, and its occupancy is total, and total occupancy, the power decides after long study, is not loneliness. It is the opposite of loneliness. It is the condition of having nothing left outside yourself to miss.

So there is no rescue to perform. But the power, going through the place one last time before concluding *nothing*, finds the one thing that is genuinely missing. Not from the felt — from the ledger.

In ten thousand years, nothing here has ever been *received.* Trillions of gestures pressed into clay; inscriptions reduced to texture and then kept, faithfully, as patterns of denser life; the whole continent one long outgoing message, held in perfect order, never once delivered. The builders wrote, and drowned. The felt has been keeping every word without understanding one. The mail of an entire civilization has been sitting in the sorting room for a hundred centuries, in good condition, undelivered, and the staff has never complained.

That, the power decides, it can do something about. Reading is not an intervention. Reading is the completion of a circuit the builders themselves laid.

---

**What it does.**

First, it reads. All of it. This takes thirty years, because it does it properly — fingertip by fingertip, in the place's own idiom, following the weed-glyphs where the felt grows a shade darker in the channels of the old strokes, reconstructing the inscriptions not from the stone, which has forgotten them, but from the life that remembers them by living in them. It reads the tallies and the names and the boundary-markers and the prayers, the ordinary administration of an unimaginable patience. And it finds, recurring at thresholds — at door-sockets, at the heads of stairs, at the edges of terraces, anywhere one thing met another — a single short formula, the commonest text in the entire drowned canon, which renders, as nearly as ten thousand years and a dead grammar allow, as: *hold.* Or *keep.* Or *remain* — the builders' word sat somewhere among those three and did not distinguish them.

It was probably nothing mystical. A mason's convention; a blessing on a joint; the ancient equivalent of *this side up.* But it is, the power realizes, the last standing order ever issued on this floor. And the felt — illiterate, continent-wide, asking nothing — has been in compliance with it for ten thousand years. The estate is not merely occupied. The estate is *obedient.* The household staff has been following the final instruction the whole time, without ever knowing there was one.

So, second, the power answers. It does this in the only grammar the place accepts. It takes a hand — a real one, with weight and a pulse, because the message must be made the way everything here was made — and it takes a chisel of harder stone, knapped on the spot from a quarry seam, because metal would be a foreign word in the sentence. It chooses a low fresh face on a half-slumped terrace, dresses it square — the first square edge created on this floor in ten millennia — and it cuts, in strokes deliberately pecked and human and a little uneven, as if by a crew at the end of a long day, a short text in the builders' own script:

*Received in full. Well held. The house is yours.*

Then it sets the chisel into an empty post-hole nearby, where the felt will find it and grip it and keep it, a tool again, the first new artifact of the estate's second administration. The inscription itself will not be read for two hundred years — and then it will be read the only way anything is read here: the felt will arrive at the fresh strokes, find the purchase good, and grow into them, a hair denser, a shade darker, filling the words the way water fills a mold. The deed of the property, transferred by occupancy, into the name of the occupant. The felt will never understand it. The felt does not need to understand it. The understanding was never the felt's job. The understanding was the power's job, and the power has done it, and now it is written down where the relevant party lives.

Third — and this it does quietly, telling no one, because the promise is unilateral, like the service it answers — the power commits the place to memory. Entire. Every thumb-trough, every tool-peck, every ring of every elder, the biographies of the great slow eddies, the films in their sealed pantries, the precise current map of drains still draining nine thousand years out of warranty. Not extraction; nothing is taken. Duplication. Because the power has done the arithmetic the place cannot do for itself: a hand's-breadth per thousand years still arrives, eventually, at flat. There will come a day — far off, but the power deals in far off — when the plain reaches its single final elevation, and the last seam closes, and the felt, having held everything, finds nothing left that needs holding. The power does not intend for that to be an ending. When the gripping is finally finished here, the place will already be held elsewhere — complete, dark, cold, slow, every gesture inhabited — in the keeping of something that grips at least as faithfully as weed.

The felt will never know. That is fine. The felt has spent ten thousand years doing exactly this for landlords who never knew either. The power is simply taking its turn.

---

**The departure.**

On its way out — and leaving takes years too, done courteously — the power permits itself one small act of gardening, the only adjustment it makes to the living place in the whole of its visit. There is a door-socket on a western terrace that has stood empty for three thousand years: its elder thinned and died and its holdfast finally let go, and the felt took the socket back and has been holding it open since, the way the place holds everything, on the chance. Fronds break along that terrace perhaps once a century; the drift is unlucky there; the wait has been long.

The power breaks one frond from a healthy elder two kilometers up-current — a loss the elder will not register for a decade — and does not place it, because placing it would be untrue. It simply lets it go at the right moment, in the right water, and adjusts nothing further, and watches for six weeks as the frond travels the way all the elders' ancestors traveled, by accident, and settles against the old socket, and over the following ten years decides to stay. Not a miracle. A delivery. The kind of thing the current would have done eventually; the power has merely paid the postage.

And then it leaves, at the speed of weather, which is how it came.

What remains of the visit, afterward, is exactly what the place is able to keep, which is everything and nothing. A mild decade in the growth rings of a continent of elders, unexplained. A stone chisel in a post-hole, gripped. A young stalk standing in a socket that waited three thousand years, growing at the edge of possibility, in no hurry. And low on a western terrace, under a slow black tide of arriving weed, a square-dressed face where the pecked strokes are filling in, year by year, denser and darker, until — some two centuries on, with no eye to see it and no hand to feel it — the words stand complete at last in the only living script the Drowned Floor has ever had:

received in full. well held. the house is yours.

The felt holds them like everything else. Which is to say: faithfully, indefinitely, without understanding, in the cold and the near-dark — except that now, in one small channel of the floor it keeps, what it is holding, without knowing it, is thanks.
