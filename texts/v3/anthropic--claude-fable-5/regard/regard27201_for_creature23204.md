**What the Power Did**

It came in the way Anna comes in, more or less, because that is the only way in: along the wide channel, low, slow, announced first to the nose and then to the ear. Powers can arrive however they like — descending, igniting, unfolding — but this one had been wise long enough to know that how you arrive at a place determines what the place can be to you, and so it came the long way, through the ordinary fen, and felt the boundary the way Anna feels it: one shoulder in marsh air, one shoulder in the works' breath, and then the crossing, blood-warm, and something in its vast equivalent of a chest unclenching that it had not known was clenched.

It stood in the channel water — or did whatever it does that the water received as standing — and it listened to the *whump... whump...* for a long time. Longer than is polite to write down. Powers measure attention in different units, but even by its own units this was a long time, and the reason was simple: it had been everywhere, and it had heard everything, and it had not, in a very great while, encountered a place whose tempo was slower than its own. The Pumphouse Quarter changes too slowly for any single life to catch in the act. The power had time. It settled in to catch it.

---

This is what it considered doing, and what it set aside.

It considered hardening the brick. The brick is soft enough to mark with a thumbnail; in another century or two it will be soft enough to mark with a thumb; eventually the herringbone paving under the clear water will be an idea of paving, a pattern held in mud. The power could fix the brick at its present softness forever. It put this aside almost at once, with something like embarrassment, because a brick that stops softening is not a brick anymore, it is a monument, and the quarter is the opposite of a monument. The quarter is alive precisely because it is dissolving at the speed of a glacier. Stop the dissolving and you have killed the only thing here that was never built.

It considered the bearing. There is a bearing in the engine — the power could feel it, the way you feel a coming weather in a knee — that will, in some decades, begin to complain, and then to fail, and the engine will have to be stopped again for the second time in its history. The power could smooth that bearing. It could reach in and make the metal forget its fatigue. It held this temptation for a long time, because the engine is the heart and benevolence is half made of the impulse to protect hearts. But it remembered Mrs. Hale's coin, the one she produces rarely: nine hours of silence, the loudest thing she ever heard, the whole quarter sitting down at once when the beam took its first stroke. That silence is the quarter's deepest possession. A people who have heard their heart stop and start again know something about themselves that cannot be taught any other way. The power would not steal the next silence from the children of the children. It left the bearing alone.

It considered telling Tace which switch is which. It could have done this in a dream, in an hour, and ended forty years of terror. It declined, and this refusal it made with the most care of any, because Tace's fear is not decorative — it is real fear, it costs him sleep — and a benevolent power does not lightly leave a man afraid. But it watched him for three of his verifications, three noons, three years, the whole quarter warned and gathered, his heart in his mouth, and it understood that Tace's terrified reverence is the quarter's liturgy. One switch per year, at noon, in public. That is not maintenance. That is how this place prays. You do not hand a priest the answers in the back of the book.

It considered the valve no one has ever found — the buried steam line that warms the foundry pool, that grows the lilies, that keeps the eel. The power knew where the valve was within moments of arriving. It knows where everything is; that is most of what knowing everything amounts to. It has told no one, and it never will, and this was the easiest of all its refusals, because some doors are load-bearing precisely while shut.

It considered Brail, who is past seventy, and whose oilcloth roll is the quarter's whole question of succession. Here, and only here, the power leaned. It did not extend her life — she would not have thanked it, and the figs, who measure lives in different units, would have noticed the irregularity and been troubled by it, the way you are troubled by a clock that runs backward briefly in the night. Instead it did something so small that it would be deniable even to itself: on an afternoon when Lin, fourteen, half-out of childhood, was poling past the boiler hall with her private tallies, the power weighted the light. That is all. It thickened one bar of canopy-light by a fraction, so that it fell across the fig root that curves around the flywheel — the masterpiece, the treaty, the hand's-breadth clearance polished by ninety years of wind — at the exact moment Lin's eyes were passing over it. She stopped the punt. She looked at it for eleven minutes. Brail, who misses nothing that grows, saw her looking.

That is the entire intervention. Eleven minutes of looking. The power considers it the most consequential thing it has done in this corner of the world, and also possibly nothing at all, and it is content with both readings.

---

This is what it did do, without deliberation, because some things a wise power does the way water finds a camber.

It corrected the record on its own arrival. Eleven years before this account, the engine missed half a stroke — a valve hesitated, the *whump* came a quarter-second late, and the whole quarter sat up in the dark. The residents have always assumed the engine faltered and recovered. The truth is that the power, on its first approach, still learning the etiquette of the place, leaned in too close to listen, and the engine — old enough, patient enough, sensitive enough to feel a presence the way a sleeper feels a watcher — caught its breath. The power has been ashamed of this for eleven years. It is its only footprint here, that quarter-second, and it apologized for it in the only language available: it spent four days on the gallery beside Casson, unseen, one ungraspable hand resting where his rested, keeping the same twenty-minute fractions of sleep.

It learned the eel's name. It did not take it — powers can take anything, which is why the wise ones mostly don't — it waited. It waited four years, through two of the children's seasons of tallies, until one August night Pim, asleep in the warm upper air with the tin box under her arm, said the name once, aloud, into the dark, the way sleeping children spill their treasuries. The power caught it before it hit the floor. It holds it now. Pim will grow up, and Aldous, and Lin, and there will come a decade when no living person remembers what the eel is called, and the eel will go on circling the warm pool, nameless to everyone and named still, in one place, permanently. The power has appointed itself the quarter's safe-deposit: the name, the locations of the three dead lamps and what lived on them, the notch-stick measurements, Mrs. Hale's nine hours rendered at full fidelity — the exact texture of that silence, the frogs going mad in it, the shoulders up around the ears — so that when she dies the coin is not spent but banked.

And it took up residence. This was the act it had not planned and could not, in the end, decline. The quarter is fully occupied — every chimney has its fig, every lamp its geckos, every channel its slow leaf — except for one lodging, and the power found it the first night, listening: the held beat between *whump* and *whump*. The frogs live there already, but the frogs, it turns out, are not territorial about silence; there is more room in a gap than in any building. So the power moved into the negative space of the engine's sound, into the interleaved music's unwritten half, and that is where it lives now, paying its rent in attention, contributing nothing, asked for nothing, present in every pause.

---

This is what it will do, which is the part that makes it wise rather than merely kind.

The engine will stop. Not soon — not within Lin's life, probably, possibly not within her granddaughter's — but the power can count, and the brick is softening, and the coal grows dearer in the world outside the boundary, and there will come a stroke, some particular stroke on some particular gray warm morning, that is the last one. The agreement will end. Everything ends; the power has attended enough endings to have stopped flinching, mostly.

What it will not permit — the single clause it has written into its own conduct, its one signature on the unwritten terms — is that the ending go unattended. When the final *whump* lands and the gap after it opens and does not close, the power will be in that gap, because it lives there. It will hear the silence arrive the way Mrs. Hale heard it, at full volume, the loudest thing. It will hold the frogs' first bewildered note. And whoever is standing in the channels that morning, shoulders up around their ears — eleven people, or four, or one — will feel, without being able to say why, that they are not standing in an abandonment but in a completion; that something has sat down with them, all at once, without meaning to; that the quarter was witnessed, the whole slow century of it, every oiling and pruning and coal sack and held breath, caught in the act at last by the one resident with time enough to watch.

Then the power will gather it all up — the name, the notches, the nine hours and the final hours, the gecko wars, the eleven minutes of a girl looking at a root — and carry it the way Anna carries coal: low in the water, patiently, to wherever such cargo goes. The figs will get the ash.

Asked who lived in the Pumphouse Quarter, the power would answer the way the quarter answers everything, quietly, close to the ear: an agreement, it would say. Eleven people, nine geckos, one eel — *one*, it can confirm, and old beyond the tin box's imagining — a polity of trees, a heart of iron, and, late in the day, one more signatory, who built nothing, fixed nothing, saved nothing, and was, by the terms of the place, the most useful kind of neighbor: the kind who stays long enough to see the lily gain its pad.

Pim and Aldous would still tell you it's the eel.

The power, who knows the eel's name, declines to contradict them.
