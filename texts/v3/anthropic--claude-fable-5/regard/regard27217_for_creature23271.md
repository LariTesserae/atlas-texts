# What Stood at the Rim

The power had crossed the world many times before it noticed the marsh, and the marsh many times before it noticed the island, because the island did not call attention to itself, and the power had been listening, all those crossings, for calls.

What stopped it, finally, was the absence of one. It was moving over the gray water-country at dusk, the hour when most settled places send up their noise — boasting, praying, mourning at full voice, asking the sky for things — and it passed over a low clot of rooftops that was sending up nothing but a single low syllable, continuous, contented, like a word being said so slowly it would take a century to finish.

The power paused. Powers of this kind pause the way the heron stands: wholly, with their entire being, for as long as it takes.

It took a year.

---

In that year it learned the place the way the place asks to be learned, which is slowly and at wall-height. It heard the *tock* of the cracks opening at night and the soft answering scrape of bone tools at dawn. It watched a lane reverse its drainage and watched the children discover it by ear before their elders discovered it by eye. It counted the trees and found there were three, and that all three were known by name, and it understood that a counted thing is a kept thing. It listened through forty hearths' worth of shared walls and heard everything — the quarrels, the births, the wall-height murmuring, the keening that the neighbors stilled themselves to carry — and somewhere in the middle of that year it noticed that it had begun, without deciding to, observing the discipline of the place. It knew what it had heard. It did not, even to itself, repeat it.

This was the first thing it had *learned*, rather than simply known, in longer than it could account for. It found the sensation remarkable.

Then it did what such powers do when they have taken the measure of a place and mean it kindness: it looked for the being who lives there, intending to give that being a gift.

And here it met a difficulty.

It looked at the old woman who is older than the ground, who hums platform-songs at dusk, and thought: *her, perhaps.* But her life ran down into the gutters she had helped gouge and out along the channels her grandchildren cleared, and it could not find an edge to her where the town began.

It looked at the stone — the youngest resident, the wet new country still arriving, weeping its sweetness, growing its finger-width a year — and thought: *it, then. The being who lives in this place is the place.* But the stone's cracks had all been channeled, niched, named; its seep ran under the sleeping platforms and through the corners of rooms; its strata held reed-impression and plaster like growth rings. It could not find an edge to the stone where the people ended.

It looked at the lichen. It looked at the frogs. It looked at the heron with the notched feather, standing its hour.

The power, who was wise, eventually understood that it had been asking a question with the wrong shape. There was no single being living in this place, and there was no plurality either. There was a braid. The being who lived here was the *making-room itself* — the continuous mutual shifting on the bench — and you cannot hand a gift to a courtesy. You can only join it or break it.

So the power did what you do when you have brought gifts to a house that needs none. It went through its pockets anyway, slowly, considering each thing, and set each one down.

---

It considered widening the island. Four hundred paces is very little ground, and the power could have shouldered up another four hundred in a season, gently, in the stone's own manner, and given every hearth its own four walls and a lane wide enough for two abreast.

It set this down almost at once. The crowding was not the town's affliction; it was the loom. Every grace it had admired — the sideways walk, the trailing hand on the wall, the wall-height voice, the unhearing — was woven on the narrowness. Give them room and within three generations they would be a people who could pass each other without touching, and would.

It considered stilling the stone. No more *tock* in the night, no more doorways gone out of true, no more rebuilding the high quarter. It held this gift the longest, because the rebuilding was endless labor and the power was tender toward labor.

But it had watched a re-gouging day. It had seen the meal at the end of it. The stone's restlessness was the town's conversation with its ground, and a stilled stone would be a companion gone silent — politely, permanently, mid-sentence. You do not stop a child's growing to spare the doorframes. It set this down.

It considered telling them where the sweet water comes from. It knew, of course. It is the kind of thing such powers know without trying.

It set this down with particular care. An explained mystery becomes a mechanism, and a mechanism becomes someone's responsibility, and then someone's possession, and then someone's worry. Right now the sweetness belonged to no one, which meant everyone; it was accepted like weather, and weather cannot be hoarded. The power resolved to practice, on this one matter, the deepest unhearing of all: to know and never say, even to the wind.

It considered soil. A gift of deep black earth, enough at last to bury in, so the dead would not have to go back to the brackish water.

This one it set down most gently of all. The people had a threshold they were keeping — *the stone is too young to hold the dead* — and the day the first grave goes into the island will mean the land has finished arriving. That day has to be earned by time, theirs and the stone's together, or it means nothing. You do not give a family their child's adulthood as a present. It is not yours; it is not even the child's; it belongs to the years.

It considered the future, because it could see some of it: the world filling, the comers with maps and the postures of mastery, finding at last a small sweet-watered island in the brack. It considered hiding the town in mist. It considered standing guard.

Then it looked again at the marsh — the gray patient water-country, trackless, channel-riddled, legible only to those who move the way it moves — and understood that the island already had a door, and that the door already taught. Anyone who reaches this place will have spent days learning to pole quietly, to read reeds, to wait. The marsh admits the teachable and wearies the rest. The power decided the guard was already posted, and had been on duty since before the stone came up.

Its pockets were empty. It had given nothing. It felt — and noted the feeling with interest — something like relief.

---

But it did not leave with nothing done. Three things, in the end, in the grammar of the place.

The first: one night in early autumn, when the keening went up from a hearth on the western side and the adjacent households stilled themselves so the grief could travel, the power stilled itself too — all of itself, its whole weather, every attention it was paying anywhere in the world — and let the sound pass through it unobstructed. It is possible the grief traveled a little farther that night than grief usually does. It is possible the marsh itself seemed to listen. No one in the town remarked on it, which is how they would have wanted it.

The second: on a moonless night near the year's turn, the power knocked.

It had thought a long time about how to speak to the stone, which had been saying its one slow syllable since before the people came, and which — the power had come to believe — was asking, in the only language a young land has, the question every young thing asks: *am I doing this right?* The weeping, the cracking, the restless finger-width rising: a child's fidget, a guest's anxious shifting on the bench, *is this all right, am I taking too much room, is this all right.*

So the power answered in the town's own grammar. It knocked on the stone the way you knock on a shared wall — once, softly: a single *tock*, no louder than a knuckle on wood, meaning what that knock has always meant between neighbors. *We hear you. You're all right. Settle in.*

The crack-listener woke, as he always did, and went out at dawn with his reed strip, and searched, and found no new crack anywhere — the only tock in his long tenure that never wept. He recorded it anyway, with a small charcoal mark beside it that was not quite a question. It is the only signature the power left in the world's keeping, and it is in good hands: an entry on a strip that is added to and never consulted, the way you'd remember something a quiet companion told you.

And the stone heard. Nothing changed that anyone could prove. But the wet-born woman, who knows the ground's breath better than anyone living, said that winter that the island slept deeper. The next crack the stone opened, in spring, came up directly beneath the high quarter's driest corner, weeping sweet, exactly where water had been wanted for a year — and if that was kindness answering kindness, no one said so, because the people of the Crowded Stone do not say the island is alive. They re-cut a channel around the new crack, and found a use for it, and gave it a niche.

The third thing the power did was make a promise, to no one, which is the only kind of promise such powers can keep perfectly.

It will come back for the first burial.

Someday — forty years, sixty, the power is in no hurry and neither is anything here — the stone will be old enough. There will be soil, real soil, grown from carried baskets and lichen-rot and patience, and someone the town loves will die, and instead of poling out at dusk to the old channels, the people will look at the ground and the ground will look back, and they will know it is time, and the land will receive its first dead and be, at last, fully arrived: not a guest anymore. Family.

On that day there will be a heron at the rim — or something standing the way a heron stands, wholly, for as long as it takes — and it will be still on purpose, so that the grief of that day can travel through it without obstruction, out over the marsh, as far as grief has ever gone.

Until then it has moved on, because it understood, in the end, that its attention was the heaviest thing it had brought, and that the last gift in its pockets was its absence — the room a wise guest makes by rising from the bench at the right hour.

But it did not leave unchanged, and this may be the real account of what the power did at the Crowded Stone: it was given something. It crosses the world differently now. When it passes over old lands — the deep-asleep, dead-drunk, finished countries — it goes more gently than it used to, the way you move through a dark room where a stranger is sleeping. And when it finds young stone anywhere — a new island shouldering up out of some other water, wet from being born, weeping, fidgeting, asking the only question young things ask — it pauses.

And knocks once, softly.

And says, in the oldest courtesy it knows, which it learned from a few dozen hearths on four hundred paces of crowded rock: *We hear you. You're all right. Settle in. Someone will make room.*
