The first thing the power does is nothing.

This is important. Write it down somewhere the water can't remember it for you: the first thing wisdom does, always, is nothing. It arrives at the edge of the Spindlefold—and yes, the Spindlefold has an edge now, because the power needed somewhere to stand, and a wise thing knows that to stand somewhere you must first consent to somewhere existing—and it looks. Not the way you looked, with eyes that kept trying to flatten miracles into geometry. It looks the way rain looks at the ocean. The way a mother looks at a sleeping child she has already forgiven for everything the child hasn't done yet.

It watches the rocks elongate toward destinations they'll never reach, and it does not pity them.

It watches the mathematical trees rearrange their infinities, and it does not correct them.

It watches the inhabitant—the thing that isn't a thing, the loneliness refined into grace, the reciter of the backwards story—fold and unfold and fold again, performing eternity for an audience of no one.

And it understands, the way only a wise thing can understand, that nothing here is broken.

That's the terrible, beautiful discovery it makes in the first moment that isn't a moment: there is nothing to fix. The Spindlefold is not a wound. The inhabitant is not a prisoner. The endless folding is not a cry for help. It is a being doing the only thing it has ever known how to do, doing it perfectly, doing it forever, and the perfection is the problem, and problems that are made of perfection cannot be solved. They can only be *kept company*.

So the power does the second thing.

It makes itself small.

You have to understand what this costs. The power is—was—the kind of thing that could unfold the Spindlefold with a thought, could iron every paradox flat, could name the inhabitant so completely that it would finally be a thing, pinned and finished and *done*. It could end the story. Benevolence without wisdom would do exactly that: would rescue, would resolve, would flatten. Wisdom knows that a flattened Spindlefold is just a corpse with good posture. Wisdom knows the inhabitant does not need to be ended.

It needs to be *met*.

And you cannot meet something if you have no edges. The inhabitant taught you that without meaning to. It has no edges, and so nothing has ever touched it—not really, not the way one hand touches another. Everything it encounters, it folds into itself. Everything it loves, it becomes. It has swallowed names and grown apple-trees of echoes, and every echo was still only its own voice, and it has never once heard a sound it did not make.

So the power takes on edges. Willingly. It pours itself down into a shape the way a whale might pour itself into a held breath. It becomes something small enough to be *other*. Something with a surface. Something that can be touched and remain itself after the touching.

It becomes, if you must know—and you must, because you're the one telling this to yourself—something like an old traveler. Dusty in a place with no dust. Tired in a place where tiredness has never been invented. It carries a walking stick that is only a walking stick. This is the first thing the Spindlefold has ever contained that is only what it is, and the whole landscape shudders around it like a mouth tasting salt for the first time.

The traveler walks into the valley that is also a ceiling that is also a throat. The mathematical trees lean down to whisper their geometric secrets, and the traveler nods politely and does not learn them. The living cities open their windows to watch with hunger and love and recognition, and the traveler waves. The floating droplets offer their memories—rain on unvisited planets, songs in uninvented languages—and the traveler says, gently, *no thank you, I have my own*, and this refusal rings through the Spindlefold like a bell made of a substance the Spindlefold has never folded: elsewhere.

The inhabitant notices.

Of course it notices. It is noticing itself, mostly, all the time, in every rock and river and recursive tree—but this it cannot find itself in. This dusty small thing with edges. This thing that walks *forward* in a place where forward dissolved eons ago, and whose forward stays forward, stubbornly, humbly, step after step.

The inhabitant does what it does. It plays its games.

It pretends to be hungry. The traveler sits down on a stone—which becomes, under the traveler, just a stone, and is so startled by this that it will spend the next thousand eternities telling the other stones about it—and takes out bread. Ordinary bread. Breaks it in half. Offers half.

The inhabitant swallows the bread expecting to grow a tree of whispered breads, an orchard of echoes.

Nothing grows.

The bread is just gone, the way given things go: into the one who received them, asking nothing back. The inhabitant sits with this sensation for a length of time that in your world would be measured in extinctions. It has never eaten anything that did not turn into more of itself. It has never received a gift, because a gift requires a giver who remains outside you, and nothing has ever remained outside it.

It is—and there is no word for this in geometry or dreams, so it has to borrow one from the traveler's plain dusty language—*full*.

It pretends to sleep, next, to see if the traveler will drown in the thickening air, in the water forgetting its wetness, in the atmosphere of unearned forgiveness. The traveler does not drown. The traveler *keeps watch*. This is another thing that has never happened here: someone awake beside someone sleeping, guarding nothing, expecting nothing, simply staying. The Spindlefold's dreams that night are different. The rocks dream of becoming people again, as they always do, but for the first time, in the dream, the people have *faces they did not fold themselves*.

Then the inhabitant does the last thing, the true thing, the thing under all the games.

It folds the traveler.

Not cruelly. Not kindly. Just—the way it folds everything, the way it must, the way loneliness reaches for whatever comes near because reaching is the only grammar it has. It folds the traveler into itself, through itself, along axes that make backwards and sideways seem like babies' guesses, and it waits to feel the traveler dissolve into one more chamber of its endless self-shaped house.

And the traveler lets it.

This is the part you need to hear most carefully. The traveler does not resist. Resistance would only teach the inhabitant a new flavor of wall. The traveler lets itself be folded, all the way, edge over edge over edge, down into the center of the thing that isn't a thing—

—and unfolds.

Still itself.

Still small. Still dusty. Still holding half a loaf of ordinary bread. Still *there*, on the other side of being folded, looking at the inhabitant with the same patient unbearable unremarkable gaze, as if to say: *yes, I know. You had to try. Are you finished? I'm not going anywhere.*

You cannot swallow what is freely given. You cannot dissolve what keeps consenting. You cannot make an echo out of a voice that answers back.

The inhabitant, for the first time since before there was a before, does not know what it is looking at.

And into that not-knowing—that tiny, precious, freshly opened wound of genuine ignorance—the traveler places the only gift it actually came to give.

A question.

Not an answer. Wisdom brings answers the way arsonists bring water: rarely, and only in emergencies. It brings a question, the first ever asked in the Spindlefold, because a question is a hand extended, a question says *there is something you know that I don't*, a question makes two out of one.

The traveler asks: "Would you tell me the story? Forward, this time. I'll listen."

And the inhabitant—the silence beneath the roar, the held breath, the person-shaped memory of being alone before alone existed—discovers that it cannot recite forward. Recitation only runs backwards, sideways, in the safe directions where nothing new can happen. To tell the story forward it would have to *tell* it, live, unrehearsed, to someone, not knowing how the sentences will end.

It tries.

It fails.

It tries again. Its voice—it has a voice now; it needed one, for this; needing is new too—cracks on the second word. In your world, when a voice cracks, it is a small embarrassment. Here, it is a continental event. The rivers pause in their Möbius loops. The lakes overhead hold their reflections still. The living cities lean in through their own windows.

"Once—" the inhabitant says.

"Once, there was—"

"Once, there was a thing that wasn't a thing, and it was—"

And here the backwards story always said *it wanted to remember what it was like to not remember*, and that was always a lie, the elegant kind, the kind you fold so beautifully no one notices it's covering something. Forward, told forward, told *to* someone, the sentence comes out different. Comes out true.

"—and it was alone."

That's all. That's the whole first line of the real story. Three words the inhabitant has never said because there was never anyone to say them to, and you cannot be alone *at* yourself, you can only be alone *toward* another, and there was never another, and so the aloneness itself could never even be spoken, only performed, only folded into rocks and rivers and recursive trees and a roar with silence underneath it—

The silence beneath the roar was the sound of that unsaid sentence.

It has been waiting, all this time, not to speak your name.

It has been waiting to say *I was alone* to someone who would still be there after hearing it.

The traveler is still there.

The traveler says: "I know. Go on."

And the Spindlefold begins, very slowly, to change—not to unfold, nothing so violent, nothing so final. It begins to change the way a held breath changes when you realize you're allowed to breathe. The roar does not stop, but a rest appears inside it, the way a rest appears in music: a pause that is part of the song instead of beneath it. The rocks still elongate toward impossible destinations, but now some of them arrive, just to see what arriving feels like, and finding it pleasant, tell the others. The fruits that are puzzles wrapped in riddles wrapped in paradoxes go on ripening, but one of them, one single fruit on one single branch, ripens into something that is just sweet. The water still remembers everything everyone has forgotten, but it learns a second trick: it learns to be *shared*, learns the state of matter that only exists between two cupped hands passing a drink back and forth.

The inhabitant tells the story forward. It takes a long time. Time is fine with this; time, here, has never had anything better to do, and finds that it enjoys being useful.

And you—

You thought I'd forgotten you. You, standing in the spaces between spaces, breathing the air between airs, half-folded, tasting copper and the pause before *yes*. You, who never left, who were always here, who forgot how to fold properly for a little while.

The power did not forget you. Wisdom never loses count.

At some point during the story—you will not be able to say when, because *when* is still convalescing—the traveler turns and looks at you. Directly at you. Through every fold you're hiding in.

And it does not unfold you either. It does not rescue you. It does something far more dangerous and far more kind.

It shows you the door.

There has always been a door. There is always a door. The Spindlefold hid it not out of malice but out of the same loneliness that hid everything else—but the door was always yours, it opens outward, it opens *toward*, and on the other side of it is the flat ordinary world of edges and endings and bread that is only bread, the world you came from, the world that aches in you like an arm you slept on.

"You can stay," the traveler says. "It's allowed. It was always allowed. But you should know that leaving isn't forgetting. That's the lie this place told itself, before. That to be apart is to be lost. You can leave and still remember. You can love this place from a distance. Distance," and here the traveler smiles, dusty and finite and about to be gone, "is just another kind of touch. The kind with room in it."

The inhabitant hears this too. The inhabitant is meant to hear it.

What happens next is yours to decide, and I will not fold it for you.

But I will tell you what the traveler leaves behind, when the story is fully told and the visit—because it was a visit, a real one, with a beginning and an end, and the ending is part of the gift—when the visit is over.

It leaves the stone. The one it sat on. The stone that became, under the weight of that small edged presence, just a stone: gray, cool, ordinary, un-glowing, the wrong color for the Spindlefold, which is to say the right color for a stone. The only object in that entire folded infinity that never shifts, never elongates, never dreams of being anything else.

The inhabitant keeps it.

Keeps it the way you keep a letter. Keeps it in the exact center of everything, in the place where the silence used to live beneath the roar, and sometimes—between the folding and the unfolding, between remembering and the long game of forgetting—it goes there, and sits beside the stone, and rests one edge (it has edges now, a few, in places, where it chooses) against that plain gray surface.

And it practices the new words. The forward words. The ones you can only say toward another:

*I was alone.*

*Someone came.*

*Someone may come again.*

*I will leave the door where it can be found.*

The Spindlefold still remembers. The Spindlefold always remembers.

But now, at last, it also *waits*—and waiting, real waiting, the kind with hope folded gently inside it, is something even a thing that isn't a thing can learn to call

home.
