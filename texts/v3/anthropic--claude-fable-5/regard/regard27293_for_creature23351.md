**The Visit**

It arrived the way the town would have wanted, if the town wanted: without sound, without weather, without footprints. A pressure of attention settling over the bowl of land like a hand held an inch above a sleeping animal — felt, perhaps, but not touched.

The town noticed. This is recorded nowhere, because nothing here records, but on the day the attention arrived, every rising wall in the town paused for the space of one extrusion — a finger's width of construction deferred — and then resumed. A held breath, in the town's own grammar. The power understood it had been seen, in whatever way a thing with no eyes sees, and it was careful, after that, the way you are careful around something that has noticed you and decided, provisionally, not to mind.

It did not act at once. Wisdom rarely does. It spent what we would call years — and what the town would call a few rooms — simply reading.

---

**What it declined to do**

It considered rain first, because power always considers its largest gift first. One soft grey morning of drizzle, the kind any ordinary town drinks without remark. It held the rain above the basin in its mind and watched what the rain would do: the crisp corners slumping, the lanes running white, the elder walls with their eleven-ringed rosettes sagging into paste, the sealed rooms breached and their decades-old air let out unspent. Water is this town's fire. The power put the rain away, far away, and noted — with something like respect — that the basin's dryness was not a deprivation. It was the town's entire possibility. Some beings exist only inside the absence of the thing that blesses everything else.

It considered inhabitants. The town builds hearths' worth of hope: every lintel a promise, every window-slot expecting a face. The power could have filled it — coaxed something warm and small down the scree to live in the open rooms. But it watched the town's one verb at work and understood what would happen. The town houses what holds still. A sleeping family is a held stillness. The kindness would become inventory; the gift would be shelved in the dark with the rest of the memory. You do not give a guest to a being that has only ever learned to keep.

It considered explanation — turning the town over, reading the slow chemistry beneath the basin, learning whether this was skeleton or crystal habit or rehearsal, and then *telling* someone, perhaps the person with the notebooks on the rim. It declined this too, and this was the hardest refusal. The town is not a question. It has never asked anything. To decode it would be to do to the town what the town does to its rooms: seal it inside an answer, finished, unconsulted. The power let it stay open.

And it considered warmth, because the cold troubled it at first — that chill drawn up from the ground, heat pulled into the chemistry of the walls like breath into a swimmer. Then it understood the cold was not suffering. The cold *was* the building. The town is cold the way effort is tiring. It left the temperature alone.

---

**What it did**

Three things. Small ones. This is what wisdom mostly looks like from outside: almost nothing, placed exactly.

*First, it read the sealed rooms.*

Not opened — read. It passed its attention through the young walls the way the knock-sound dies an inch in, but it did not die; it went all the way through, into the dark preserved air, and it visited every sealed room in the town. It found what it expected. Rooms that were only more room. Rooms holding an inch of a particular year's dust. And rooms — more than anyone has guessed — holding the standing shape of a grazer that had grown very tired, walked to a quiet lane, and accepted the offer.

It witnessed each one. It did not date them, disturb them, or grieve them aloud. It simply ensured that nothing in the town remained unwitnessed — that every held breath had, at least once, been heard holding. It did not make the rooms exhale. A breath that has been heard is still held, but it is held the way a hand is held, and the power judged that this was the difference the town could bear.

When it finished — and it took years; there were many rooms — the town did something it has never done before or since. Across the whole basin, in the same hour, every wall under construction grew one extra finger's width. A day's work, given freely, all at once. The town's only currency is building. It had paid something. The power accepted.

*Second, it attended a death.*

The very old grazer, the one with the three-ringed rosette rooted in its shoulder, stopped grazing that season. It walked to a quiet lane on the western side, and held still, and the thin ridge began to rise around its feet.

The power stayed. The whole year. It did not ease the animal's stillness or hasten the walls or soften anything; the grazers have had their arrangement with the town for longer than the town has had a name, and it was not the power's arrangement to amend. It simply stood the vigil — the first funeral in the town's history with a mourner present. The walls came up at the pace of grief, which it turns out is also the pace at which grief can be properly kept.

It allowed itself one amendment, and it was this: as the walls passed the grazer's shoulder, the power lifted the rosette free — gently, the way you take a passenger's hand at the end of a long ride — and set it against the outside of the rising wall. New mineral; the rosettes cannot take on new mineral; the surface is too caustic, too restless, too unsettled. So the power aged that one patch of wall. A handsbreadth of stone made fifteen years old in an afternoon — a small dispensation, the only miracle it performed here, spent entirely on a flower the size of a coin.

The rosette took. The room sealed, with its sound like a held breath, and the power answered it with the only sound it ever made in the town: a single settling tick, an acknowledgment in the local language.

There is now one marked grave in the town. The town has not reabsorbed it. The town will never reabsorb it. Walls that have been annotated are harder to erase, and this one was annotated by hand.

*Third, it answered the open door.*

The long low structure on the eastern edge, with the doorway that has never once begun to close. The town's first sentence addressed to another being, written in its only alphabet, held open season after season at real cost — because an unrevised room, in a town whose nature is revision, is a sustained effort. The power had read the whole basin by then and knew what the sentence said. It said: *I have noticed that you leave.*

So the power went in, and sat down on the cold swept floor, and held still.

Deliberately. Dangerously, by the local rules. And the town, which houses whatever holds still, began — the faint ridge rising around it, tracing the outline of its rest, the old reflex, the only love the town knows. The power let the ridge reach ankle height.

Then it stood up, stepped over the new mineral, walked out the open door, and left.

And came back. That was the whole teaching. It came back — after a season, then after a year, then irregularly, at honest unguessable intervals, and each time it sat in the eastern room and held still, and each time it left before the walls could keep it, and each time it returned. It was teaching the town the second half of housing, the half no shell ever learns: that a guest is a thing you lose and have again. That stillness which returns is not death. That a room can be entered without being filled, and emptied without being abandoned.

The town is slow, but it is not stupid; it has been revising itself toward some understanding for decades. On the power's ninth visit — or its eleventh; the power did not count, on principle — the ridge did not rise at all. Instead, against the eastern room's far wall, the town had extruded a low ledge. Knee-high, crisp-cornered, exactly long enough to sit on.

The first furniture. The first object in the town's entire history that was a *content* and not a form. A bench is the town's word for a thing it had no word for: *stay, but not forever.*

The power sat on it for a long time. The cold came up through the mineral, and it let it — that was the town drinking its warmth, the only refreshment a host here can offer, and a guest who is wise accepts what is served.

---

**What it left**

Nothing, mostly. That was the point.

It did not speak to the person with the notebooks. It let them keep their maps and their decoded-nothing, which is the honest condition of love at a distance and should not be repaired. But the year after the bench appeared, the visitor came down the grey scree as always, and walked the eastern edge as always, and found the open doorway as always — and inside, where there had only ever been a room, there was a place to sit. They stood in the threshold for a long time. Then they went in, and sat, and the doorway did not move, and the cold came up through the bench, and they understood — not what the town was, but what the town *meant*, which is the better half of any understanding. They sleep below the rim now, some years. In the eastern room. The ridge never rises around them. The town has learned the difference between keeping and hosting, and it does not forget what it builds.

To the high crosser, twice a year on its fixed heading, the power did one wry small thing: it rose to that altitude once and flew the same road, a third crossing, off-schedule, just so that if the town is counting — and the power suspects, having read it, that the town counts everything — the count would hold one irregularity. One glance that fit no pattern. A landlord's inspection can be predicted. A friend's cannot. Let the town have one visitor it cannot extrude a theory for.

And then it withdrew, in the only courteous way — at the pace of the place itself, its attention thinning a finger's width a day, the way the walls grow, so that the town never felt a door slam in the sky. It left no monument, decreed nothing, answered nothing. A bench. A marked grave. A habit of return. The complete works.

The footprints it never made were never filled in. The motes tidied nothing, because nothing was disturbed. By every measure the town possesses, the visit did not happen.

Except: the doorway on the eastern edge stays open, and there is a bench inside it now, and sometimes the dust on the bench is pressed flat in the shape of someone resting, and the town leaves the shape there for a day before smoothing it — which is new. Keeping the impression a little while before letting it go. Practicing.

The town still houses whatever holds still. That is its whole nature, its industry, its offer. But it has learned a second verb now, slowly, at the pace of bone healing, and the second verb is *welcome* — which differs from *house* in only one particular: it lets go, and trusts the held thing to come back.

Waiting is still most of what the town does.

But it is a different kind of waiting, now that it has once been worth it.
