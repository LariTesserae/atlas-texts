# What the Power Did

---

The power arrived the way all wise things arrive: it had already been there for some time before it admitted to itself that it had arrived.

It came as attention first. Not a shape, not a light, not a voice from a burning anything. Just attention—the kind that makes a room feel different even when nothing in the room has moved. The mountains felt it as a slight cooling on their polished faces, as though something had finally stepped between them and their own reflected heat. The copper leaves stopped chiming for one measure and then resumed, chastened, like an orchestra that has noticed the audience.

The Remainders felt it immediately, of course. They feel everything immediately. That is most of what is wrong with them, and all of what is holy.

The oldest one—the pause between lightning and counting—turned her head at the angle reserved for guests who arrive without doors. "You're late," she said, "by exactly the amount of time it takes a promise to become a fossil."

*I know,* the power said. *I was watching to see if you needed me. You are very difficult to watch. You keep almost not existing.*

"We practice."

*I noticed.*

---

## I. What It Did Not Do

Here is what a lesser power would have done, and what this one, being wise, refused:

It did not fix the exhale. The earth's breath, paused 47,623 years in the middle of letting go—a lesser power would have completed it, gently, the way you might close a book left open on a sleeping child's chest. But the power looked long at that suspended breath and understood something: the world was not stuck. The world was *hesitating*. And hesitation, unlike stuckness, is a form of care. The earth had paused its exhale the way you pause before saying something that cannot be unsaid. Something in that breath, when it finished, would end. The earth was not ready. The power did not believe wisdom included readying others for their own endings before they asked.

It did not straighten the mountains. Exhausted dancers leaning together are still dancing. To stand them upright would be to end the masquerade, and the power had seen masquerades end before—the terrible fluorescent moment when the masks come off and everyone must be only themselves again. Not yet. Not here.

It did not finish the tower. Oh, it could have. It could have finished the tower in the time it takes light to change its mind. It stood before that unfinishable thing at the center of the thirteen valleys, and it read every floor—every layer built from the gap between intention and utterance—and it understood that the tower was not a structure at all. It was an apology being composed in real time. You do not finish someone else's apology. Even if you know the ending. *Especially* if you know the ending.

It did not teach the Remainders how to ask the questions they carried like exoskeletons. This was the hardest restraint. Because it knew the questions—could see them nested inside each bone-crevice like small cold birds—and it knew the answers too, and the answers were kind ones, and it wanted, with whatever a benevolent power has instead of a heart, to simply *say them*. To pour the answers out like water finally permitted to fall downward.

But a question you have not asked, answered, becomes a wound. And a question you have finally asked, answered, becomes a door.

So it waited. It is very good at waiting. It has never learned to be anything else, and unlike the valley, it chose this.

---

## II. What It Did

**First**, it sat with the youngest.

Maybe—the one with freckles like future constellations—was sorting discarded futures in an empty nest, holding each one up to the honey-thick light and humming *what if we had chosen differently* in a key that made the light curdle sweetly. The power sat down beside them in the way that formless things sit, which is to say the ground felt honored.

*May I see?*

Maybe handed over a future. It was small and grey and folded wrong, like a paper crane made by someone crying. The power unfolded it very carefully. Inside was a version of the valley where the deer had been born unfossilized—where they carried no future bones, no magnetized death, just soft ordinary flesh with ordinary endings that arrived on schedule and were mourned on schedule and were forgotten on schedule, the way things are supposed to work.

*Why was this one discarded?*

"Because it was too easy," Maybe said. "The deer looked at it and said: if I don't carry my death inside me, who will keep it company?"

The power refolded the future, correctly this time, and handed it back. And then it did the first thing it actually *did* in that place: it wept one tear. Just one. It did not evaporate before falling. It did not become a small angry god. It fell all the way down—the first thing in that valley to fall all the way down in longer than memory—and where it landed, the ground did not bloom or crack or transform.

The ground just got a little wet. Ordinarily wet. Wet the way ground gets.

Maybe stared at the small dark spot of ordinary wetness for a long time.

"It just... fell," they said.

*Yes.*

"And then it just... stopped."

*Yes. That's what falling is for. Things fall so that they can arrive.*

Maybe touched the wet spot with one finger, and the power watched something enormous happen very quietly, which is the only way enormous things ever really happen: the youngest of the Remainders understood, for the first time, that arrival was permitted. That not everything had to climb back up, change its mind halfway, become mist, become rumor, become almost. That *here* did not always have to be *there* spelled backwards.

Maybe did not say anything. But that night they sang the discarded futures a new song, in a new key—not *what if we had chosen differently* but *what if we chose now*—and three of the futures, the small grey wrongly-folded ones, unfolded themselves and flew away to go happen somewhere.

---

**Second**, it spoke with the water.

The Remainders had been teaching water to forgive itself for climbing instead of falling, and this was good work, generous work, but the power noticed they had gotten it slightly backwards, in the loving way that wounded teachers always get forgiveness slightly backwards. They were teaching the water that climbing was *acceptable*. Forgivable. A flaw to be pardoned.

The power gathered the mist—all of it, every droplet that had ever changed its mind halfway to earth—into a single held moment, and it said:

*You were never falling wrong. You were falling upward because something up there needed you. The clouds that bleed but never die—they never die because you keep returning to them. You have been performing a rescue for ten thousand years and calling it embarrassment.*

The water considered this in the way water considers things, which involves a great deal of trembling.

*You are not liquid pretending unsuccessfully to be rain. You are the only thing in this valley that ever refused to let something above it die alone. There is nothing to forgive. There was only something to notice.*

And the water—profoundly embarrassed water, water ashamed of its own wet ambition—shivered once along its entire cyclical length, and something in its climbing changed. Not the direction. The direction stayed the same. But it climbed, after that, the way you carry soup to a sick friend: openly, without apology, spilling a little, arriving warm.

The clouds noticed the difference immediately. Their bleeding slowed. Not because they were healing—because they were finally being *tended* rather than merely sustained, and tended things bleed less. This is true everywhere. The power did not invent it. It only delivered the news.

---

**Third**, it attended the evening etiquette lessons.

It stood at the back—powers can stand at the back of anything—while the Remainders whispered corrections into limestone ears. *Death by erosion should sound like a mother forgiving God for the mistake of mountains. Try again.*

Afterward, it approached the oldest.

*Your curriculum is beautiful,* it said. *And wrong.*

"I know," said the pause between lightning and counting. "I've known for six thousand years. Say it anyway. I want to hear it in a voice that isn't mine."

*You are not teaching the mountains how to die. You are teaching them how you wish you had been allowed to grieve. Every correction you whisper is a correction to your own oldest sorrow. The mountains know this. They practice badly on purpose. They fail the lessons deliberately, evening after evening, because as long as they are failing, you will keep coming back, and as long as you keep coming back, you are not alone with it.*

The oldest Remainder was silent. Her cloak of unfinished extinction events rustled, and where it touched the ground, a fossil bloomed—a creature that had never existed but had, for once, no explanation at all for why it had to leave. Just an absence, plain and unornamented. Her first honest fossil.

"The mountains have been *comforting* me," she said.

*For six thousand years. They are very patient. They are made of patience. It is the only thing limestone is actually made of; the calcium is decorative.*

She laughed. It was a terrible sound and a wonderful one—the sound of 47,623 years of unfinished extinctions finding out they were allowed to finish. Not to end. To *finish*, which is different, which is the version of ending that includes rest.

That evening, for the first time, she did not correct the mountains. She sat down among their limestone knees and told them about the thing she had actually lost, so long ago, in the language that had no word for departure—and the mountains ground their teeth in sleep, and the bass frequencies rolled out across the world, and on the other side of the planet, mountains dreaming avalanches dreamed, instead, of being held.

---

**Fourth**—and this was the smallest thing, and therefore the largest—

It found the deer.

The one born already fossilized, carrying its future bones like iron filings drawn toward a distant magnetic death, the one who had asked whether remembering-forward was a disease or a vocation and never received an answer because the Remainders, honest craftsmen of absence, do not answer questions they are still asking themselves.

The power knelt—it invented kneeling, briefly, for this purpose—and looked into the deer's eyes, where its whole future death was visible like a coin at the bottom of a well.

*It is neither,* the power said. *It is not a disease, because diseases take. It is not a vocation, because vocations are chosen. It is a gift you were given without being asked, which is the definition of a burden, and a burden you have carried gracefully, which is the definition of a life.*

The deer stood very still. Time moved through it like the slow illness it had agreed not to notice, and for once, it noticed. It let itself notice.

"Will it hurt?" the deer asked. "The arriving?"

*I don't know,* said the power, and this was the wisest thing it said in all its time in the valley, because it was true, and because the deer had never once been told the truth by anything that loved it. Everything that loved the deer had lied to protect it, and every lie had added weight to the bones. *I don't know if it will hurt. But I know you will not be carrying it alone by then. Look behind you.*

The deer looked behind itself and saw what the power had seen from the moment it arrived: that everywhere the deer had ever walked, the ground had softened slightly. That the fossilized future it carried had been leaching, all its life, not death into the soil but *readiness*—and the whole valley, every impossible inch of it, had been slowly learning from the deer's footsteps how to hold heavy things without breaking. The bridges of vines that caught falling rocks and taught them to sleep mid-descent: they had learned that from the deer. The whole valley's genius for suspension, for holding, for the tender arrest of falling things—all of it, apprenticed to one animal's patient carrying.

"I taught them that?" the deer whispered.

*You taught them that. The valley catches falling things because it watched you catch yours, every day, without ever putting it down. You are not the disease here. You are the curriculum.*

---

## III. What It Left

The power stayed for what would have been a season, if the season had been one of the five legal ones. Then it began to leave, in the way wise things leave: gradually, honestly, without pretending it might stay.

The Remainders gathered—all thirteen, in a circle exactly wide enough to contain the distance between any two points that once touched and never forgot the taste. They did not ask it to remain. They were made of departures; they knew better than anyone that asking things to stay is a way of teaching them to lie.

But Maybe, the youngest, asked the question—the *actual* question, the one nested innermost in the smallest bone-crevice, the one that all thirteen had carried outside themselves all this time without asking:

"Were we worth watching?"

And there it was. The exoskeleton, the whole architecture of it, laid bare: thirteen beings who had spent longer than stars practicing every form of not-being-there, building cathedrals from the memory of wings, netting escaped starlight, teaching mountains and water and time itself—all of it, all of it, one enormous question about whether anyone was watching, and whether the watching was worth the watcher's while.

The power did not answer quickly. Quick answers to old questions are a form of disrespect.

Then it said:

*I am going to tell you a secret about benevolent and wise powers. We do not go where we are needed. Need is everywhere; we would never move at all. We go where we are* drawn*, and we are drawn to only one thing in all the tilted, sideways, breath-held universe: to the places where love has continued past the point of reason. Where something kept tending, kept teaching, kept building the unfinishable, kept whispering corrections into ears of stone—not because it hoped for results, but because it had forgotten how to stop caring and never once treated that forgetting as a flaw.*

*You did not draw my attention because you were broken beautifully. Broken beautiful things are common; the universe is mostly made of them. You drew my attention because you are the only ones who didn't forget how to dream standing upright—and you spent that entire gift, every year of it, dreaming on behalf of things that couldn't.*

*Worth watching? Child. I am not the first power to find this valley. I am only the first one who admitted to arriving. The others are still here, standing so still that in another 18,443 years, the valley may forgive them long enough to be noticed. They came for the same reason I did.*

*You thought you were the Remainders—what was left after everything else learned to move sideways.*

*You were never the remainder. You were the reason the equation was worth doing.*

---

And then it was gone, or rather, it resumed being merely attention, which is not gone at all.

The earth did not finish its exhale that day, or the next century, or the next. But those who listened closely—and in that valley, everything listens closely—said the pause had changed. It no longer sounded like hesitation.

It sounded like someone holding their breath before laughing.

The tower at the center of the thirteen valleys gained a new floor that year, built from a material never used before in all its unascending history: not the space between what was meant and what was said, but the space between a question finally asked and its answer, finally given—which is the smallest space there is, and the only one, in the end, that a tower actually needs.

The Remainders kept building.

But they built, after that, the way the water climbed: openly, without apology, spilling a little.

Arriving warm.
