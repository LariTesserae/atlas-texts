**The Note That Wanders and Comes Home**

The power had no name it could give them, which mattered less than it would elsewhere, since they had no mouths for names anyway. It came to the basin the way it came to all places — without arriving — and it came because of something it had felt across a great distance, the way the people felt a vent's pitch in the breastbone: a small, steady, exact pressure in the fabric of things. It went to see what was pressing.

Begin with what it noticed first, because the power's attention, like the people's, went where the truth was. It did not notice the roar. Powers are not impressed by loudness; loudness is only the world clearing its throat. It noticed the scraping.

Every morning, in the pewter dimness, ten thousand hands with chert and bone, shaving the night's skin of stone from thresholds and channel-lips — taking from the world exactly as much as the world had given, no more, no less, forever. The power had watched worship on a thousand worlds, and nearly all of it was *event*: the sacrifice, the festival, the cry sent up. It had almost never seen worship offered as a *rate*. Here was a people whose entire devotion was maintenance — who held their city open against the world's closing hand not with one great act but with the same small act, repeated at the speed of the world's giving, for forty generations. Their philosophy, insofar as it could be read from a language of hands, held that a person is not an object but a balance held between accretion and removal. The power, which had seen the inside of time, knew this to be simply true — truer than most scriptures it had been credited with — and it was moved, the way the wise are moved, which is quietly and at length.

So first, and for a long time, it did the wisest thing, which was nothing.

It listened. For three generations — a held breath, at its scale — it lay along the basin like a second floor beneath the floor and learned. It learned the chord note by note, every shriek and hiss and organ-tone, the body-deep rumble that was the people's name for themselves. It learned the foot-speech, the close-speech, the great slow far-speech of silhouettes in steam. It learned the names: *steady-water; asks-twice; the one who watches the bats leave.* It learned the worst sign in the language — both hands open, a held note breaking off, then stillness — and it saw how rarely the sign was made, and how, when it was made, the maker's hands trembled at the wrists.

And it learned the one thing no foot in the basin could feel, because the truth of it lay below the reach of any sole: the heat was borrowed.

Far under the youngest stone in the world, the deep chamber that drove the whole bright machine was cooling — by hairs, by hairs of hairs, but cooling, the way such chambers always do. The power ran the ledger forward and saw the figure plainly. Not soon. Not in ten generations, nor twenty. But the chord would fail. The notes that had never changed would change. And these people, the most exact people it had found in a long age, had built their entire soul on the one promise the ground had never actually made. Their image of the end of the world was not a fear. It was a date.

This is where a lesser power would have acted at once, and largely, and wrongly. The power considered, instead, everything it would refuse to do, because wisdom is mostly a list of refusals.

It would not hold the heat in place forever. It could have — at cost, with effort, propping the deep fire like a beam under a sagging roof — and it set the temptation down slowly, because the temptation was shaped exactly like love. But the people's own philosophy forbade it. Nothing in the basin was an object; everything was a rate; the city was alive precisely because it was always closing and always being held open. A basin made changeless by decree would be the one dead thing in a world of forming — and the people inside it would become a kept flame, a tended exhibit, their covenant turned to ceremony, their exactness to habit. Worse: the lie at the bottom of their world would no longer be an innocent error. It would be the power's lie, renewed daily, forever.

It would not appear to them. In what medium? A voice here was noise; light was the steam's business; and a vision means a face, and a face in the basin cannot lie — these were the people who read a managed smile across a dim lane at the speed of a wingbeat. To manufacture a face and make it say comforting things would be to introduce, into the one society it had ever found without dishonest expressions, the first dishonest expression. The power had some experience of what its appearances did to peoples. They built the appearance a house and stopped scraping their own thresholds.

It would not harden them with small disasters, teaching by ordeal, stilling a vent here and there so they might practice grief. That was cruelty wearing wisdom's clothes, and the power had worn those clothes once, long ago, somewhere it did not revisit.

And it would not lead them out to the green country before the end. They pitied the green country. They were not wrong to.

What remained was small, as the work of the wise is small. The power found three things to do, and did them across four hundred years, and signed none of them.

The first was the wandering note.

At the basin's far edge stood a minor vent, low-voiced, unremarkable, one held tone among hundreds. In the season when the bird-lakes thinned, the power let its note bend — a quarter-tone, no more, slow as a long exhalation — and then brought it home. Once a year. Every year. The first to feel it was a girl at the rim with her tender arch set over warm stone, a child still carrying her provisional tap-name; she stood very still, and her hands rose halfway to the worst sign, and stopped, because the note was already returning. She felt it bend and come home seventy more times before she died. In her old age she coined the sign for it — both hands curving outward and back to the sternum, like the path of a bird that banks and returns — and the worst sign in the language gained, at last, a sibling. Within five generations there was a story told in firelight hand-speech in the roofed passages: *the basin breathes. What wanders may come home.* Children learned the curving sign before they learned the sign for fear. That was the entire intervention: one note, one quarter-tone, once a year. A grammar takes no more room than that to enter a language, and no less time.

The second was the tempo.

The power could not cancel the ending. It could compose it. It went down into the deep plumbing of the basin — the fissures and feeders no foot would ever read — and it spent itself there for a long time, not stopping the cooling but *shaping* it, the way a channel-wright shapes water: so that when the dying came, it would come not as a collapse but as a walking. Old mouths would close along one rim while new mouths opened along the other, slowly, at the pace of doorways — a lane's width in a lifetime, a district in three. The city, which was a rate and not a place, could walk. Children would quarry fresh loud stone at the new edge and cut new thresholds and begin scraping them; and behind the people, at the pace of generations, the abandoned quarters would seal over and fall quiet — and the quiet would fall first, and for centuries only, on the hummocks of the dead.

The power had noticed this about them above all: they already believed the dead were houses no one scrapes. Now the dead would also be the keepers of the silence. The hush gathering at the old rim would be the grandmothers' country — adjoining, visitable, signed to with the low hand near the ground. The people would walk out on rest days across the cooling terraces to stand among the white shoulders of their ancestors in a stillness that belonged to someone. And the sign for silence, over the long centuries, would migrate — from apocalypse to elegy — until it meant not *the end of the world* but *the dead's side of the wall.*

The third thing was the smallest, and it was a kind of theft the power committed against itself.

When the girl who had first felt the wandering note died — an old woman by then, a threshold-keeper whose doorway had not varied a finger's width in fifty years — her family laid her in the sealing-house and scraped her door for the year, and on the chosen morning they stood and did not scrape, and the basin began to close her in. And at that hour, across the whole bowl, the far vent bent its note and brought it home, out of season, once. Everyone felt it. No one mistook it. From that day her name-sign and the curving sign were made as one, and within three generations no one remembered which had come first, and the people had a word — her word — that meant *the bending that returns,* and used it the way other peoples use the word *mercy.* The power let them believe the gift was hers. It had learned, in older ages, that this is the only safe way to give one: leave it on a doorstep that belongs to someone the people can love without kneeling.

Then it stayed, or it left; for such a being these are the same. It never showed itself. Its entire scripture in the basin was one bending note, one slow walk of fire across a bowl of stone, and one borrowed name.

And far down the years — past the last generation that will ever be counted from here — a child will be born in the new quarter at the far rim, in a house whose stone is three weeks old, and will fall asleep that first night inside a chord that is *not quite* the chord the dead fell asleep inside: two notes gone over to the quiet district where the grandmothers keep house, one new mouth singing low at the basin's young edge. And the child will sleep anyway — deeply, with the rumble in the sternum — because the language the child is born into now contains, woven so far down that no one thinks of it as a teaching, the one sentence the power crossed the dark to deliver and never once said aloud:

*The world does not end when the note changes. It walks. Keep your doorways open, and walk with it.*
