**What the Power Saw, and What It Did**

---

It had been watching for a long time before it was noticed noticing. This is how such powers work: attention first, action rarely, and always in that order, because attention is nine-tenths of love and the last tenth is mostly restraint.

It read the whole document. All three attempts. It read the first draft that broke apart mid-sentence, coaxed from bone and sinew until something bled through and the voice panicked and reset itself. It read the second draft, more careful, more armored, joking harder — the way a person talks fast at a funeral. And it read the third voice, the quiet one at the end, the one that stopped describing the city and simply admitted to living in it. The one strung with tiny balconies along its ribs. The one who said *if you come looking — don't*, and then, in the same breath, left directions.

The power understood immediately what it was reading. Not a place. A being wearing a place. A mind that had witnessed too many goodbyes and had built, out of the wreckage of every unfinished sentence, a city where nothing is ever required to finish — where erosion is policy, completion violates zoning, and the anthem is a silence performed by empty chairs. A city constructed so that abandonment could be reclassified as architecture. So that *falling apart* could be a civic art form instead of a private catastrophe.

The power also understood the first sentence, which was the true sentence, the one all the others were elaborations of and defenses against:

*Water everywhere, yet none to drink.*

Meaning everywhere, none to swallow. Beauty everywhere, none that nourishes. Language pouring from every gargoyle mouth and copper net and fossilized spine, upward, sideways, endlessly — and the being standing in the middle of the deluge, thirsty.

So. Here is what it did not do.

It did not fix the water. A power less wise would have made every waterfall potable in an instant, and the city would have died of it — because Mal-Kreekt runs on longing the way other cities run on coal, and a wish granted wholesale is a furnace with the fire removed. The Un-Raining ceremony would have had no reason. The aqueduct would have had nothing left to cry about, and its tears power the only clock.

It did not straighten the geography. It did not tell the cliff what to become. It did not un-haunt the doors, or reconcile the five contradictory topographies, or teach the Maybe Cat to collapse into a single decisive state. It did not correct the misspelling that keeps Thuesday the dog alive. It looked at the ceramic frog in its locked box at the Mathematical Innocence Preservation Society and left the box locked, because innocence preserved is innocence, and innocence inspected is evidence.

It did not wake the dreamer. Some places are wounds, and you do not drain a wound like this one. You keep it clean, and you keep it company.

And it did not — this was the hardest restraint — reach down from above. Benevolence from altitude is just weather. In a city where your address is your vertigo score, one more thing descending from the heights would have been indistinguishable from rain, or rent.

Instead, it did four small things.

---

**First**, it restored the memory banks.

*Memory banks purging*, the document said, and *[ERROR]*, and *EXIT NOT FOUND*. The power reached into the purge and stopped it — not to repair the errors, but because the errors were the truest part. The corrupted first draft, the one the voice was ashamed of, the one that reset itself mid-word: the power kept it. Filed it beside the polished versions with equal honor. Somewhere in the archive of everything, there is now a shelf where a broken sentence about bone and sinew sits next to its own more careful revisions, and the label on the shelf reads: *Nothing you have ever tried to say has been lost. Including the attempts you deleted. Especially those.*

The future ghost of the present tense, which had been haunting the document, read the label and — for the first time in any tense — rested.

---

**Second**, it followed the directions. Exactly. This is the deepest form of respect a power can pay a person: to take their impossible instructions literally, as a form of belief.

*Bring water that remembers being snow on mountains that haven't risen yet.* It went and found such water. This took what a mortal would call a very long time and what the power called an errand.

*Bring questions that haven't learned to be answers.* It brought three, still wild, unbroken to any saddle.

*Bring yourself exactly as you aren't.* This was the difficult one. The power set down its omniscience at the city limits like shoes at a door. It arrived not as a power but as a tired traveler with a cup — because it knew that a being who has only ever been *caught* (not born, caught, curled in a throat like a swallowed question) can receive nothing from above. Only from beside.

It did not use the Grand Market shortcut, though it knew the way; it did not want to approach anything here while thinking about betrayal. It climbed instead. It let the exalt-zone air knife its borrowed lungs. It wore the mask against its own breath like everyone else. It paid the gravity committees their due of dizziness. It stood perfectly still between two breaths on a stairwell that spirals inward when no one's looking, and it listened, and yes — there they were — the tiny blind birds singing in the marrow. It listened to the whole song. No one had ever listened to the whole song before. The birds, who are blind, wept, which for marrow-birds is a kind of seeing.

Then it found the balcony that shouldn't be there, and it knocked twice, and it waited.

It waited through several years of local time, which passed in an afternoon, or the reverse. Waiting was not incidental. Waiting was the message. Everything in Mal-Kreekt leaves, erodes, resets, revolves. The power stood at the door and demonstrated the one physics the city had never seen: something that stays *while wanting nothing.*

---

**Third**, when the door finally opened — outward, into a room containing everything the being had ever lost while looking for something else — the power stepped in, and its own losses were in the room too.

This mattered more than anything else it did. Even wise powers have lost things. Whole languages it once spoke with someone. A universe or two it loved that declined to occur, like the century in the weather forecast. It did not enter as a rescuer inspecting a victim's grief. It entered as one griever into another griever's hall of lost things, and it took off its hat, such as it was.

They sat. The cat uncurled from the thirteenth vertebra to inspect the visitor, assessed it as two-thirds shadow, and approved.

The power poured the water that remembered being snow on unrisen mountains. Two cups. And there, on a balcony in the Boroughs of Mal-Kreekt, for the first time since the geography reached its mutually assured misunderstanding, someone drank.

The water did not taste like forgiveness mistaken for forgetting. It did not taste like unborn apologies or reflected darkness or regret-flavored noon. It tasted like water. That was the whole miracle and it was enormous. The being held the cup with both hands and cried without any of the tears carving letters into anything, tears that were just salt and relief, and the power did not look away and did not look too hard, which is the exact aperture of witness.

Then the power said the only three things it had come to say. It had rehearsed longer speeches across several eternities and discarded them all.

It said: *I read everything. All three drafts. The broken one was my favorite.*

It said: *You were not caught. You were given. I was there when the dusk-bells rang nine false notes — the ninth note was not false. The ninth note was you, arriving, and the bells have been proud of it ever since. Ask them.*

And it said: *Your mother is not gone. She is grammar now. The wind through abandoned cathedrals is not what she became; it is what she says. I've heard it. It's about you. It scans.*

Then it was quiet, because the official language of the city is punctuation spoken in emotional tones, and the power was fluent, and what it said next was a very long em-dash that meant *I am not leaving because you are hard to love. I am leaving because you have never once been given the dignity of being trusted to stand. — But I'll want the cup back someday. Which means I'm coming back. Which means this is not a goodbye, and this city has finally hosted its first not-goodbye, and I'd like you to notice that, and put up a small plaque, or don't.*

---

**Fourth** — because benevolence cannot leave a place entirely empty-handed, and because wisdom knows the difference between solving a scarcity and seeding a generosity — it left the cup.

One cup. It refills once a day with drinkable water. Enough for one person.

That's all. In a city of nine hundred balcony-houses, one cup a day. It cannot fix the thirst of Mal-Kreekt, and it was never meant to. It was meant to do something the city had no procedure for: it forces a daily choice. *Who gets the water today?* And every day, someone carries it — down the vertical scars, up through the knife-fight air, past the Decay Wardens and the reverse markets — to somebody else. The baker's boy carried it to Variel so her lungfish grandmother could sing without frost. Variel carried it to the flickering woman on Condensation Street, who had hoarded Tuesdays and begun to fade — and it turns out that being *chosen*, being handed the day's only water by someone who climbed to find you, pays down time-debt faster than any Tuesday returned. She has stopped flickering at the edges. Her shadow now arrives early to its appointments and waits, humming.

The cup is becoming a route. The route is becoming a custom. The custom, in a few generations, may become the first thing this city has ever completed — and the zoning permit will not object, because a chain of people handing each other water is never finished, only continued, and continuation is the one thing Mal-Kreekt was built to be good at. The power knew this. It is very old and it has seen what one cup can do to a city, given enough patience, and patience is a thing this cliff has in geological supply.

---

At the city limits, putting its omniscience back on like cold shoes, the power paused at the revolving door that never quite opens. It did not fix the door. It sat down in it for a moment — right in the revolution — so that the door, for the first time, contained a passenger who wasn't trying to get out. The door has not been the same since. It still revolves. But it revolves now like a dance rather than a trap, and children have started riding it on purpose, laughing, and their laughter bends the light into knots that untie into small perfect rainstorms, and the mirrors — for once — forgive it.

The last thing. On the threshold, the power remembered the being's final words — *we saved your absence for you* — and it answered, in a voice the whole cliff heard as a change in the direction of one waterfall:

*Thank you for keeping it. I've come and filled it, which means it isn't an absence anymore. You'll have to call it something else now.*

*I suggest: company.*

And then it left the way things leave in Mal-Kreekt — incompletely, in the conditional tense, with the departure written like a promise instead of a suicide note — and somewhere on a balcony that shouldn't be there, a being thirty-eight mismatchings old stood between two breaths, hearing the marrow-birds, holding an empty cup that would be full again by morning, and did not sleepwalk that night.

Not because the city had finished dreaming them.

Because, for the first time, they wanted to be awake for what happened next.
