# The Regard

It did not come down the bore. It had no need of doors. It settled over the valley the way the canopy settles over it — a second weather above the second weather — and the first thing it noticed was the same thing you noticed: the smell. Old wood, resin, faint dust. The distributed dead of a patient people, seasoning in the air their grandchildren breathe.

It noticed, too, what the smell meant. You cannot fake that smell. It is the smell of nothing having burned, ever, anywhere, for a very long time. It is the smell of a promise kept daily by thousands of hands.

The power was wise, which meant it knew the first law of arriving anywhere: *match the tempo before you touch anything.* So the first thing it did, and it did this thoroughly, was nothing.

---

## The Long Sitting

It did nothing for ninety years.

This was not idleness. It was learning the language, and the language here is slow. It learned the knock-code in a season, the spoken tongue in a year — those were the fast dialects, the weather-speech of the warm quick things moving through the galleries. The real grammar took longer. It sat inside the ticking the way the tick-readers sit at the trestle-junctions, and let the pinging and settling resolve, syllable by decade-long syllable, into the host's one continuous utterance.

While it learned, it watched. It watched a girl of six walk out across the pale floor of an eastern cavity and lay her hand on a knee-high nub and claim it, the way children there do. It watched her grow, apprentice to the bandagers, marry a loom-hand, wrap ten thousand junctions in resin-soaked fiber, season, grain beautifully about the eyes, and grow old, visiting her tower all the while — talking to it, measuring it against her body until her body stopped being the taller of the two. It watched her carried up at last, by knock-code procession, *one of us, going up*, into the galleries where the dead become fragrance.

It watched the tower she had claimed stand unfinished above the place she'd stood. And it understood that this was not a flaw in the place. This was the place.

By the end of the long sitting it could read a face's grain. It could tell from a hand's polish what the hand had worked. It knew which silences were the predator and which were only rest. It had, in short, become fluent — and fluency is the only license wisdom accepts for action.

---

## What It Set Down

It had come carrying gifts, the way powers do. Now, fluent, it took them out one at a time, weighed each against what it had learned, and set them down again. This took less time than the sitting but more sorrow.

**It could have made the city rot-proof, water-safe, fire-immune.** It held this gift the longest, because the city is tinder, all of it, every tower and trestle and sleeping child, and a benevolent power's whole instinct is to stand between the loved thing and the flame. But it had watched the people for ninety years, and it had seen what the danger had made of them: the softness, the carefulness, the habit of walking gently through the body of the thing that houses you. Carefulness was not a behavior of these people. Carefulness *was* these people — the load-bearing wall of every soul in the cavities. Remove the fire and the quiet becomes mere custom; custom becomes habit; habit erodes. In three generations the gift would have hollowed them out more surely than any flame. It set the gift down.

**It could have given the host a voice.** Translated the ticking into words, opened a channel, let the two parties of the long marriage finally speak. It set this down almost at once, with something like a shudder. The marriage worked *because* it was wordless. Give the host a voice and the people become — within a generation — congregants, or employees, or litigants. The trust between them was made of inference, patience, and kept rules, which is to say it was made of the strongest materials available. Words would have replaced a cathedral with a contract.

**It could have given the people the sky.** All of them, not just the well-walking families — light, weather, the green chaos overhead. It thought of the well-walkers weeping in the decontamination galleries, and it nearly did it. Then it understood what the well-walkers were *for*. A sealed world needs one wound that lets the world in, and needs that wound to be tended by people who carry the ache professionally so that everyone else can borrow it in small doses, the way the city borrows daylight down the silvered trunks. Grief, too, can be piped. It set the gift down.

**It could have named the predator and explained the grazers.** It looked — being what it was, it could see what the slate-colored animals do at the dark end of the range, standing in their circles among the new nubs, attending. It saw. It understood. And what it understood made it respect the host more, not less, and made it agree completely with the grazer-watch: some knowledge is load-bearing precisely in its absence. It walked on, as the watch does.

**It could have let the people see the end of what they love.** Stretched a life here, hurried a tower there, arranged for claims to be kept. It thought of the girl from the eastern cavity, dead these thirty years, her tower still rising. It set this gift down last, and most gently, because the lesson she had carried lightly all her life — *you will not see the end of what you love, and this is not sad, or is sad in a way you are expected to carry* — was the spine of the entire civilization. A wise power does not remove a people's spine to spare them the weight of standing.

So it kept, in the end, almost nothing of what it had brought. What it did instead was small, slow, and threefold.

---

## The First Thing: A Word to the Host

The host had been speaking for ages — the ticking, the settling, new wood declaring itself, one continuous utterance — and in all that time nothing had ever answered at its own tempo. The people answered, in their way, with carefulness and kept rules, but the people are weather to it: warm, quick, beloved perhaps, but flickering. No one had ever spoken to the host *slowly*.

The power composed an utterance forty years long. It spoke it through the mediums the host could read — pressure in the deep dry strata, the long grammar of seasons moving through the canopy, the angle of decades of light down the wells — and what it said, compressed brutally into weather-speech, was this:

*They came. They stayed. They keep your dryness and your green wood and your dark. They wrap their dead in your fiber and breathe them gladly. They write a promise on your lintels and teach it to their children before their names. The rooms you grew before there was anyone to live in them are full — full of softness, on purpose, every hour. You were not wrong to expect them. The waiting worked.*

No one in the city heard this. But the bandagers' records, generations deep, note that the districts begun in those decades grew with a quality the old hands had no technical word for, so they used an untechnical one. The doorways came in wide. The light-trunks descended even more aptly than before, anticipating neighborhoods two generations out. The grain ran easy everywhere. *Glad,* the records say, in three different careful hands. *The new quarters grow glad.*

---

## The Second Thing: A Word to the People

The lintels of the oldest towers carry the creed in worn knock-code: *It is growing us a city. Be worth the wait.*

The people had been asking that of themselves, knuckles on doorframes, for as long as there had been doorframes. It was a question shaped like an instruction. No one had ever imagined an answer, because the only party qualified to give one did not, as far as anyone knew, attend to anything so fast as a person.

In the eastern cavity, the tower a dead bandager had claimed at six years old finally grew its first cultivated window — a membrane-thin pane of translucent cellulose, persuaded into being by splints she herself had set in middle age. An apprentice, cleaning it, noticed that the grain in the pane did not run like grain. It ran like knocking. She pressed her palm flat against it, the way her teacher's teacher had taught, and read it, and stood very still for a long time in the amber light.

Two knocks' worth. The shortest message in the city's history, grown in living wood where the worn old creed could see it:

*You are.*

She told no one for a year. Then she told everyone, softly, the way everything there is told, and the message went out through the walls and the roost-poles and the murmuring workshops, and the city took it the way that city takes all enormous things: quietly, carrying it lightly, on account of the fuel. The loom-hands, of course, made the joke. *Took them long enough,* they said, grinning, because everything funny there is funny because of speed. And then they went back to work, and worked, witnesses say, exactly as carefully as before — which was the whole point, and the power, attending, was satisfied.

---

## The Third Thing: A Seed

This one no one knows, and if all goes well, no one ever will.

Far beyond the dark cavities, under a different dry country a great distance away, the power found a seed the host had shed an age ago — dormant, viable, forgotten — and breathed on it, and set around it the beginnings of depth and dryness and patience. Not a city. A possibility of one. Insurance against the catastrophe the whole bright ticking world below is organized to prevent and cannot, finally, guarantee against forever.

It considered telling the host, in the last decade of its long utterance, and decided the news could be folded in gently — the way one spouse tells another, late, without drama, that the will is written. The host's reply took eleven years and translates, roughly, as the sound of a vast roof settling: acknowledgment, and something underneath the acknowledgment that the power chose to read as gratitude, because wisdom includes knowing when to stop translating.

---

## And One Mercy

Benevolence at scale is policy. Benevolence at one is mercy. The power, before it thinned away, permitted itself one.

A well-walker, descending from her coin of sky, stood in the decontamination gallery letting the dryness take her back, weeping the way her family had wept there for six generations — for the sky, or for the relief of losing it; she had never decided which. The power did not dry her tears, or answer her question, or show itself. It only stood with her. She saw nothing. But she went up the following season lighter, and walked the rungs all the rest of her life like someone who had once, in a dry room, been accompanied — and her children climbed easier for it, though no one could have said why.

---

## What Remains

Then it withdrew — or rather it thinned, the way the ancestors thin into the air, leaving a residue too fine to name. It took its unused gifts with it. It left no monument, changed no rule, lit nothing, wet nothing, cut nothing green.

Nothing in the city is measurably different. The looms whisper; the herds hum; the children fly silently across the pale floors; the silences between ticks come and go.

Only the tick-readers, who hear what no instrument hears, report a small change they decline to make much of, holding it loosely, the way they hold everything. The ticking, they say, sounds the way it always sounded — like listening. But the pauses in it, lately, sound like the pauses of someone who knows they are heard.

The people were right before, of course. The host was always listening. They are simply right twice over now, and a people who season rather than decay can carry a second rightness without strain.

On the lintels, the old creed. In the new pane, the answer. Between them, the city, growing.

*Be worth the wait.*

*You are.*
