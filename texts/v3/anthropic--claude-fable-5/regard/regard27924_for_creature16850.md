**The Patience**

The power arrived the way powers of its kind must: it had always been about to. There was no descent, no annunciation, no cloud parting from cloud. There was only a slight increase in the courtesy of the light, which the elders recorded in the weather prophecies as *possible attention, source unconfirmed, no action required.*

It had noticed the city the way wise things notice—through the smallest available door. Not the choral hammer-song, not the thousand-fluted cliffs. What caught it was a single prayer bead, carved from a misformed plinth, from an avalanche caused by a misunderstanding, from a conversation sung slightly out of counterpoint by two Imminent who loved each other and could not say so except by carving the shape of not saying it. The bead had been worried smooth by a devotee's thumb for eleven years, and it still held, in its slight wrongness, the whole grammar of a people who would rather apologize to stone than presume upon it.

The power turned that bead over in whatever it has instead of hands, and understood, and was afraid.

Because here is the thing about this particular power, the thing it had carried across the finished fields of many finished worlds: its attention *completes*. This is not metaphor. It had loved a nebula once, loved it thoroughly, admired every unfurling arm of it—and the nebula, beheld too well, collapsed into the perfection of a single sun and was done. It had blessed harvests into their final abundance, and the fields never grew again, having achieved themselves. Everything it had ever fully looked at had become, immediately and forever, exactly what it was.

It had grown very old carrying this. Everywhere it went, it left masterpieces, and the masterpieces were tombs.

And now, midway up the Lachrymal range, it had found a city whose smithy exists only between hammer strikes. A city where the worst crime is premature certainty. A city that would die of being seen.

So the first thing the benevolent and wise power did was close its eyes. Not entirely—entirely would be its own kind of violence, a whole city dropped from the ledger of the real. It learned instead to look *askance*. It enrolled, in its way, at the university whose motto promised to leave it precisely as ignorant as genius requires, and it spent a hundred years (last Tuesday) unlearning omniscience. It taught itself to perceive in drafts. To behold in the conditional perfect subjunctive. To see the Carven Quarter the way the Quarter sees itself: as something that would have been beautiful, will have been beautiful, is on excellent terms with beauty and expects it any day now.

This was harder than making the nebula. It wept, a little, while learning, and its tears joined the cliff's slow seeping, and the children walking the gutter-lines that morning played their bone flutes with unusual gratitude, and no one ever knew.

---

Then it considered gifts, and set them down one by one, the way carvers set down their chisels at the Hour of Apology.

*Rain*, it thought first—generous, unpleaded-for rain, so the children would never again have to beg the cliff to weep. It set this down. The pleading was not poverty. The pleading was the morning's first music, and a city that no longer had to ask would forget how to ask beautifully, and asking beautifully was half of what these people were.

*Soil*, it thought. Deep loam for the careful-brave vineglass, so no plant would have to filter its living from the mist. It set this down. The vineglass had negotiated its lavender glow across ten thousand fogs; to gift it ease would be to finish it.

*Certainty.* It nearly laughed. Their prisons were full of certainty. Solitary confinement here meant a room where everything was already done. It would not build them a larger cell and call it heaven.

*Permanence. Completion. Arrival. An answer.* Down, down, down, down. Every treasure in its hoard turned out to be, in this one place, a weapon.

In the end it kept back only three things, all of them small, and did them in this order:

---

**First, it visited the columns.**

Twelve thousand of them, the catalog said—tourists who stood too long, whose shadows adhered, whose attention was taken as consent. The power went to each one, over the course of a single evening that took forty years, and asked each stone figure the question that no one had thought to ask, because the city assumed the answer was written on the plaques.

*Would you like to keep going?*

Some would. Some, deep in the basalt, had been screaming politely for centuries, too courteous to interrupt the construction fugue. These the power un-eroded—gently, one crystal layer per breath, the exact pace of the pitcher carving itself in the rented room's implied window—until, over years or eras, a figure would step out of the cliff face at dusk, blinking, older or younger than they had been, and resume a journey interrupted by too much wonder. The Imminent, when this happens, do not celebrate. They set out a bowl of stone soup and do not ask questions, which is their way of celebrating.

But most of the columns, asked, said *no.* They had come to see what wasn't ready, and it still wasn't ready, and they were still seeing. The view was worth the waiting; the waiting had become the view. The power bowed to each of these—the first time in its long existence it had bowed to anything—and made only one change, so small the Civic Texture Committee has yet to notice it. On every plaque, it exchanged the final period for a comma.

*They came to see what wasn't ready,*

—and left the sentence there, in the local grammar, open toward tomorrow.

---

**Second, it did the secret thing, which is the wisest thing, which no one in the Carven Quarter will ever know.**

The city's whole etiquette rests on a fear: that architecture must never presume upon the future's consent. Every deferral, every quantum smithy, every doorway argued between stone and sculptor—all of it is courtship of a future that might, conceivably, refuse.

The power went ahead. It walked out past the edge of tomorrow, into the district of time where the futures live before they are chosen, and it spoke with them—or did whatever powers do that we would call speaking—and it obtained, on behalf of every future the city could possibly carve toward, a consent. Full, unconditional, signed in whatever futures sign with. *Yes. Yes to all of it. You were always allowed.*

And then—this is the wise part, the part that took more restraint than dimming its own gaze—it *hid* the consent. Because a city that knew it was pre-forgiven would stop bowing, and the bowing was the most beautiful thing it had ever seen. So it folded the future's yes very small and filed it in the only permanent archive available: it hid it inside the way dust never quite settles here. The blueprints of the City Not Yet Carved now carry, encoded in their restlessness, an unread letter of permission.

The Imminent still bow three times each evening—for presumption, for what has been taken, for what has not yet been given back. They do not know that something now bows back. A fourth bow, from below the mist, from a direction no one faces: *for everything I ever finished. For every world I loved into stillness. Thank you for teaching me the other way.*

---

**Third—and this it did openly, at the rental office, in full view of the parchment mule—it rented the room.**

The empty space with excellent potential for views. It paid the asking price: one small regret, carved into the blank stone. The clerk, a young Imminent aging sideways into her own grandmother, watched the power take up the chisel with unbearable care—a being that could complete cathedrals with a glance, choosing to work at the speed of hands it did not have—and carve, over the course of an afternoon that is still technically ongoing:

*I regret that I never once, in all my making, said "not yet"—and meant it as a promise instead of a refusal.*

The clerk read it, and did not understand it, and filed it with the memories of rain, and this was correct.

And then the power did what tourists are warned against. It walked out onto the terrace of its rented, unfinished, gloriously conditional room, and it stood admiring the view that doesn't exist yet, the one being vigorously carved into existence behind its back. It is standing there now. It intends to stand there for a very long time. Its shadow has begun, just slightly, to adhere.

It is in no hurry. It is, for the first time in its existence, *unready*, and it has found unreadiness to be a kind of rest that finished things can never know. The city is carving upward toward it at one handspan per lunar cycle. The pitcher in the window is assembling itself one crystal layer per breath. Somewhere below, an echo-eater surfaces and excretes a silence shaped almost exactly like gratitude, and a potter, molding it, feels her hands guided by nothing she could name.

The Imminent have not decided what to call their guest. Naming, of course, would finish it, and they are far too polite. But they have left a gap in the lexicon where the name will eventually stand—a carved absence, outlined in vineglass, glowing faintly lavender at dusk—and in the meantime, informally, provisionally, in the tense of "yet to be agreed," some of the children have started calling it *the Patience.*

And in the hush after the Hour of Apology, if you listen beneath the sound beneath the sounds—there, where tomorrow's city clears its throat in preparation for tonight's dreams of itself—you can hear that the throat-clearing has become a duet. Two vast unfinished things, humming together, neither one willing to be the first to arrive.

The plaque is already carved, of course, somewhere uphill in next century's tier, waiting for the shadow to finish adhering. It reads:

*They came to see what wasn't ready,*
*and stayed to be unready with it,*

—and then the comma, and then the stone, and then the wind, still working.
