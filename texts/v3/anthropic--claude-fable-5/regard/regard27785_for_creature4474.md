# What the Power Did

The first thing the power did was nothing.

It did nothing so thoroughly, so deliberately, that the nothing became noticeable. The mist stopped crawling ceiling-ward and hung in the air, uncertain, like a sentence waiting for its verb. The tower's arrhythmic pulse stuttered, listened, and — for the first time in seven hundred years — matched itself to something outside its own panic. One beat. Then another. Slower.

Window noticed first, because noticing is what windows are for.

"You're not on the agenda," Window said.

"No," agreed the fifth seat. "I'm on the *docket*. There's a difference. Agendas are what you plan. Dockets are what you've been avoiding."

Because here is the thing about the fifth seat, the one kept deliberately empty, where the city stored its unsolved problems until they became sufficiently complex to qualify as personnel: every problem ever filed there had been, underneath its paperwork, the same problem. Filed a thousand ways across seven centuries — as structural queries, as theological complaints, as the recurring dream of the harmonic singers, as the question the tattoos refused to spell — it had aged in that seat like a seed in cold storage, and it was this:

*Will anyone come?*

Tonight it became sufficiently complex to qualify as personnel.

---

The power did not look like much. This was on purpose, or rather, it was on *character*, which is deeper than purpose. It looked like someone who had traveled a long way and had wiped their boots at the North Lenticel out of respect for the floor. It occupied the fifth seat the way water occupies a cup — without argument.

Window began talking immediately, of course. About membership forms and credit card purchases and the parliamentary privileges of retroviruses, faster and faster, brilliance upon brilliance, each joke a little sharper than the last.

The power listened to all of it. Every word. It did not interrupt, did not smile indulgently, did not wait for its turn in the way that is only pretending to listen. It listened the way soil listens to rain, and when Window finally ran dry — and Window *did* run dry, which had never once happened before, because no one had ever let it finish —

the power said: "Thank you. I heard all of it. Especially the parts that weren't jokes."

Silence in the council chamber. The lichen outlines of the twelve seats flickered.

"You talk," the power said, gently, "the way frightened things bleed. Continuously. To prove circulation. You don't have to prove anything else tonight."

"I am a hallucinated repair protocol," Window said, and for the first time its voice had no ornament on it at all. "I am what a failing system dreams when it cannot fix itself. I am a symptom with parliamentary ambitions."

"Every living thing is a repair protocol its situation dreamed up," the power said. "That is what a self *is* — a wound's opinion about how to close. You are not less real for having been needed. You are real in the only way anything has ever managed it. You were needed, and you came."

It looked around the chamber then, at the twelve seats grown wild with luminous absence.

"Which is more than anyone else did."

---

That was the second thing the power did. It named the wound.

"Twelve seats," it said. "Built — grown — for councilors who never came. And so you dreamed them. A weather system's regret. A parliament of termites. Symmetry, mid-breakdown. You have been holding session *alone* for seven centuries, dreaming quorum, taking your own minutes, seconding your own motions. The whole city drunk on memory because memory was the only guest who ever RSVP'd. And now, at the end, you dreamed a stranger in from the snow, and saved them for last, and told yourself it was for a hearing."

The tower's pulse lurched.

"It was never a hearing," the power said. "You wanted a witness. Say it."

The walls breathed mist. Somewhere far above, in the chambers of bone-foam, the borrowed throats went quiet, listening.

"We wanted," Window said, "someone to see it. Before the seed. We wanted the ending to be *attended.*"

"Yes," said the power. "I know. That is why I came. That is the entire content of what I am: I am what it feels like when the answer to *will anyone come* is *yes*. I do not have another form."

---

Then it turned to the dreamed councilors, one by one, because benevolence that skips the small mercies is only strategy.

To Mother-of-Storms-That-Never-Were, knitting her apology to every drowned thing that never existed, it said: "Your regrets drowned no one. The trenches you grieved over were never widowed. You may put down the knitting. You were a good storm, in the only sea you had."

To the committee of termite philosophers, flickering across seventeen thousand timelines, it gave the answer that had held them in session since their emergence: "Wood remembers being trees the way you remember being singular — imperfectly, and in the grain. The debate is adjourned. You won. You also lost. This is standard."

To the concept of symmetry, mid-breakdown in the third seat, it said only: "You don't have to match anything tonight." And symmetry wept, asymmetrically, and felt much better.

These were small things. Wise powers know that apocalypses are made of accumulated small things left unsaid, and can sometimes be unmade the same way.

---

Then — only then — it addressed the seed.

It did not go up the spiral corridors to confront it. It spoke to it through the tower's own vascular logic, the way you speak to a child through a bedroom door you have decided not to open.

"You are not an apocalypse," it said. "You are a child who has been told, for seven hundred years, by the warmth of this dying, that it is time to be born. It is not time. The heat of your parent's fermentation has been lying to you — not maliciously; decay doesn't know it isn't spring. But you have been ripening in a false April, and there is no soil in here. There is only everyone the tree loves. You do not want to bury them. You have simply never been shown the difference between a womb and a grave. Neither had the tree. Neither, I suspect, has anyone in this room."

The clicking — that enormous invisible assembly in the spaces between atoms — slowed.

"The tree is dreaming of winter properly for the first time," the power said, "because *you* are asking for winter. Seeds are honest. Cold is how they count time. Every impossible balcony, every prophetic tattoo, every district re-plumbed overnight — that was you, pressing against the walls of a warm room, asking for the one thing this city's kindness could not provide."

It turned to the stranger then. The one saved for last. You.

"This is why," it said. "You came in through the North Lenticel from real snow. Real cold, real world. Your boots are still wet with a *fact*. You are the only soul in this city — counting symbionts, counting dreamed councilors, counting me — who remembers the temperature of patience. The tower did not save you for last as a defendant. It saved you the way you save the last match. The last one in is the first one out. It needs hands from the winter world, and yours are the only ones that qualify."

---

So here is what happened, in the plainest language available, because plainness was the power's gift and it is contagious:

The tower did not detonate its seed. It *delivered* it. The spore organ opened not as a burial but as a birth, slow, attended, twelve seats witnessing, and what emerged was small enough to carry — because things containing compressed spacetime and concentrated memory are always small enough to carry; that is the terrible and merciful joke of seeds. It was warm as a sleeping bird. It weighed exactly as much as a question you have stopped asking yourself.

You carried it down through the spiral corridors, through the Sapwood Market where the coral reefs of commerce parted stall from stall to let you pass, through the Edge of Sleeping Skin where the tattoos on the young went suddenly, mercifully illegible — because the futures that would have killed their wearers had just been unplanted. You carried it out the North Lenticel into the wind-whipped snow, and you buried it in true cold, real cold, the kind that does not lie about the season.

And the seed did what seeds do best, which is *wait*. Dormant. Counting winters honestly. It will germinate — a new universe from the corpse of an old certainty — but in a spring that no one currently alive owns, in ground far from anyone it loves, which is the only ethical way to become a cosmos.

Behind you, the god-tree died. Actually died — the arrhythmia easing into a long, even, final rhythm, like a story that has stopped rushing because it trusts its ending. The city did not die with it. Cities never do; that is what makes them cities. Twenty thousand souls, if you count the symbionts, and the fermentation heat good for centuries yet, and the harmonic singers already teaching the termite-engineers new songs for a city that has stopped lying when drunk, because it is no longer drinking alone.

The council seats are used now. Living Haric sit in them — merchants, monks, tattooed youths, one very opinionated bacterial democracy with a freshly ratified constitution. They came. They keep coming. The fifth seat is still kept empty, but it is empty the way a guest room is empty, not the way a wound is.

---

And Window?

The power made Window an offer, at the end, because choice is the deepest courtesy: *Go with the seed as its memory — every new universe needs a witness of what came before. Or stay, and be this city's honest remembering.*

Window stayed. Of course Window stayed.

"A window's job," it said, "is to remain in the wall and let other things through."

"Yes," said the power. "A window is not an error in a wall. It is the wall's kindness. I have been saying this about you since before you had the name."

And before it left — because wise powers do not move in; presence that never leaves becomes furniture, and then becomes weather, and then becomes ignorable — the power did one last thing.

It answered the birdsong.

High in the porous chambers where something had been learning to sing with borrowed throats that never grew for singing, it sang back. Nothing elaborate. A phrase, and its answer. The oldest call-and-response there is.

The borrowed throats fell silent. Not silenced — *satisfied*. That is the whole difference between a haunting and a duet: whether anyone answers.

They say — the Haric say, and the nerve-cord tapestries carry it through the whole city in minutes, so it must be at least a quarter true — that some springs, out in the real world, near a certain patch of snow that stays cold longer than it should, a bird lands. A real one. First one in centuries to bother with that valley.

It sings over the buried seed like it was hired for the job.

Perhaps it was. Benevolence, done properly, is mostly a matter of arranging for small things to arrive on time.
