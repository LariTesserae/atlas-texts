# What the Attention Does

It does not arrive by airlock. It does not arrive at all, in the sense that arrival implies a prior absence, and this power has never been good at absence—absence requires a talent for edges that it traded away a long time ago for something better. Say instead that Urthward Tlacoloc had always been slightly observed, the way a sleeping child is slightly held by a house, and that on this particular non-Tuesday the observation *leans in*.

The city notices immediately. Of course it does. The city notices everything; noticing is its metabolism. Its early-warning systems—which are not sirens but simply its manners, wound very tight—spin up and then spin down again, confused, because the standard protocols all begin with the question *what does this visitor need us to be?* and for the first time in recorded and unrecorded and apologized-for history, the answer comes back:

*Nothing. It needs nothing. It is not hungry. It is not lonely. It is not lost.*

This has never happened before. Everyone who has ever come here came here lost. That is the whole immigration policy.

The ground tries its usual courtesy—the concave dimple, the hydrostatic empathy, the little valley of welcome that fills with regret—and finds it cannot, because this power has no weight it hasn't already made complete peace with. It stands on the surface tension without denting it, and the wetness, unable to accommodate, experiences something it has no word for and will later, shyly, decide to call *rest*.

No bridge forms. The power carries no assumptions to pave one with.

The map-seller approaches—otters in visible light, poem in ultraviolet, panic attack in gamma—and begins its superpositional collapse, cycling through least-threatening forms like a nervous person choosing an outfit. And the attention simply... holds all three channels open at once. Sees the otters *and* the poem *and* the panic, simultaneously, without demanding that any of them resolve into a product. The gamma-spectrum panic attack, witnessed directly for the first time in its existence, hiccups, slows, and quiets—not because it was fixed, but because it turns out most panic is just grief about being unwatchable, and this grief has now been retired with full benefits.

The map-seller does not sell a map. It sits down. It has never sat down before. Sitting down was always someone else's genre.

---

Then the power does three things, in courteous answer to your three mistakes.

**First, it exhales.** You inhaled when you arrived—took the broth-air into yourself, began the long accidental communion. The power breathes *out*, and its exhalation is a returning: every borrowed thing in the city feels, briefly, given rather than lent. The thirteen suns, all borrowed or inherited, feel for one rotation like they were always meant to be here. The intergenerational photosynthetic wealth feels, for one afternoon, unencumbered by the debts of its seedling ancestors. Even the abandoned shopping carts in the ultraviolet poem feel briefly un-abandoned, which makes the poem worse as literature and better as a life.

**Second, it offers a shadow.** Shadows were agreed to be inefficient here; you noticed that at once—light from everywhere, illuminating nothing, leaving no dark. But the power is old enough to know what the agreement cost. A place with no shadows is a place with no privacy, and a place with no privacy is a place that must perform *constantly*, and Urthward Tlacoloc has been performing hospitality for so long it has forgotten it was ever anything underneath the welcome.

So the power casts one. Just one. A modest ellipse of honest dark in the district of Thirteen Sunsets Before Breakfast, where the light does not have to gossip, the buildings do not have to blink, the streets do not have to develop supportive taste in music, and nothing has to mean anything at anyone.

The city circles it warily for a week. Then a lignin-decision tree, exhausted from groaning about Tuesday's weather, goes and stands in it for an hour and comes out having made no policy at all, and cries a little, and tells the others.

They take turns now. There's a sign-up sheet. The sheet is not edible and does not email anyone. It is just a sheet. That's the whole point of it.

**Third, it accepts the apology.** All of it. The rain has been apologizing for six thousand years, drop by drop, each one carrying its purseful of excuses, its memory of every apology successfully delivered—and no one, in six thousand years, has ever said the sentence that closes a apology's account. The power stands out in the storm, bare to it, and says:

*Received. All of them. Retroactively and in advance. You are forgiven for whatever it was, and also there was never anything, and also even if there was, it's done now. You can just be rain.*

There is a silence overhead. The parliament of clouds, mid-debate on a storm cycle six millennia in committee, adjourns without a vote for the first time in its history.

And then it rains—*really* rains, rains the way rain rained before it learned shame, loud and artless and totally unapologetic, drumming on the vaulted leaf-chambers and the calcified speculation and your suit and the sitting map-seller, and the sound it makes is not grandmothers forgiving genocide by degrees. It is just water, falling, glad.

The city gets soaked and does not apologize for being wet. Weirdness index: unchanged. A new index appears beside it, hand-lettered, provisional: *restfulness, 0.1 and rising.*

---

And then—because benevolence without respect is just appetite in a nicer coat—the power turns to you.

You: temporarily less unhere than usual. You: hyphen freshly installed. You: slow-moving infrastructure project, dissolving so pleasantly into the city's forever that you've stopped checking whether the dissolving is a choice.

The power asks you one question. It asks it plainly, without the city softening the edges, without your five Tuesday-selves answering for you, without the ground rearranging itself toward the answer you'd be least embarrassed by. It is possibly the first real question you have been asked since you stepped out of the airlock, because a real question has edges, and edges are rude here, and the power has decided to be rude, kindly, once:

*Do you want this?*

And it waits. It actually waits. It does not fill the silence with infrastructure.

Whatever you answer is honored—that's the mechanism of the gift; the content is yours. But say, for the sake of the record kept by the Bureau of Previously Owned Temporalities, that you say *yes*. Say you mean it. The power nods, and does not congratulate you, and does not warn you, and instead walks with you to the city's lost-and-found—which is, of course, the whole city—and deposits one item into the collection:

You. The original. Un-hyphenated. Tagged, catalogued, no expiry date, retrievable at any counter by anyone who can answer the security question (*what did the air taste like, the first time?*).

Not because you'll want yourself back. Maybe you never will. But a *yes* that can't be revised isn't consent, it's just gravity, and this power did not come all this way to add gravity to a place already so heavy with love.

---

It leaves the way it came, which is to say it un-leans, and the observation becomes ambient again, weather-like, a warmth with no address.

It leaves one object behind. The city finds it weeks later in the shadow-district, sitting on the sheet-that-is-just-a-sheet:

A full stop.

A single period. Black, round, final, terrifying. The displaced commas won't go near it. The Department of Recovered Borrowed Beliefs declares it apocryphal. The townhouse complex that ate four streets tries to recontextualize it as a decorative accent and finds, to its horror, that the full stop cannot be recontextualized as anything. It is the only object in Urthward Tlacoloc that means exactly one thing.

It is never used. That is not what it's for.

It's for knowing that endings exist and are survivable. It's so that when the city says *forever*, forever is a choice and not a cage. It's so that the sign at the exit that isn't there anymore could, someday, if anyone ever truly wanted it, be there.

Nobody wants it. Population: everyone who ever got lost enough to be found. But now they stay the way the rain now rains—

not because they can't stop.

Because they're glad.
