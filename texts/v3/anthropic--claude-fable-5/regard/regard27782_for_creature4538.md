**The Long Regard**

It had been watching for some time before it admitted to itself that it was watching.

This is how wise powers arrive at places like the Floating Garden of Ula-Thoon: not descending, not manifesting, not parting the sea-that-is-air with any announcement of light. It arrived the way suspicion arrives in a happy person — gradually, sideways, and with great reluctance to become certain. For a long while it existed at the Garden's periphery only as a slight increase in the thoughtfulness of the twilight, a faint impression among the aurora-algae that someone, somewhere, was being careful about them.

It was old in the way that makes numbers embarrassing. It had held dying suns the way you hold a bird found on a windowsill in winter — knowing that warmth is not the same as rescue, offering it anyway. It had watched more worlds end than it had watched begin, because endings take longer and are lonelier and someone should stay for them. It had learned its trade in grief and was, by now, very good at love, which is what grief becomes when it is given enough time and nothing to do.

And it knew — this was the first thing it knew, before it knew anything else about the Garden — that its attention was dangerous here.

It had seen what the young ones did: unfolding to precisely the size of whatever attention they received. It understood immediately that in this place, regard was rainfall. A power its size, gazing directly, would be a monsoon. Things would grow toward it. Things would grow *into* it. The Garden's shy questions would become confident answers overnight, and confident answers were the one weed this ecosystem had, so far, been spared.

So it learned to look at 0.7 on the old scale of looking, where 1.0 is the way you watch a sleeping child — enough to know they're breathing, never enough to wake them.

---

It watched for three spirals inward. (Time moved sideways there; it did the courteous thing and moved sideways too.)

And during those three spirals, it considered its inventory of gifts, the way any visitor stands in the doorway of a beloved house wondering what to bring. It was a power. Its gifts were terrible in the old sense, the sense that means *large*.

*I could anchor it,* it thought. *I could make the suspension permanent, tie the hydrostatic impossibility off with a knot that entropy could never pick. It would float forever.*

But it had done that once, long ago, to a world it loved. It had fixed everything that could go wrong, and the world became a museum of itself — perfect, complete, and utterly finished. Nothing there ever made another mistake, and therefore nothing there ever made anything again. The power still visited sometimes, the way you visit a grave, and it did not want two of those.

*I could give it edges,* it thought. *Every garden fears the moment it remembers gardens have boundaries. I could bring it a horizon as a gift, wrapped in distance.*

But the Garden had not asked about edges. The Garden's question — the only one the coral-trees debated in their slow oxygen-script — was *how long*, and there is a great difference between a question a place asks and a question a visitor thinks it ought to be asking. Wisdom, the power had learned across some billions of years of getting this wrong, consists largely of answering the question that was actually asked.

*I could announce myself. They would worship me.*

At this it felt something like laughter, if laughter could last a century. The Weavers worshipped *almost* — that perfect balance between rain remembering it's water and gravity remembering it's optional. A power that *arrives* is the murder of almost. To be known here would be to break the only theology worth breaking nothing for. It declined itself politely, the way one declines a chair at a gathering one is only meant to pass through.

One by one it set its gifts down outside the Garden, in the place where the edge would be if the Garden believed in edges. The pile grew large. It looked, from a certain angle, like humility.

---

Then it did four small things. It would have preferred to do three — three is a shape wisdom likes — but love always overpacks.

**First**, it made the Garden hard to remember.

Not invisible. Invisibility attracts investigation the way silence attracts sound. Instead, it laid over the whole region a gentle quality of *slipping-the-mind* — so that harvesters and cataloguers and empires, all the hungry attentions of a hungry universe, would notice the Garden, find it interesting, and then, three thoughts later, find themselves thinking about lunch. Navigators would chart it and misfile the chart. Poets would dream of it and wake with only the taste of mint. The power made itself the Garden's forgetfulness, standing at the not-edge like a doorman whose entire job is helping people realize they were never coming here in the first place.

This was the largest thing it did, and no one in the Garden ever knew about it, which is how it measured success.

**Second**, it left a book in the library of unwritten books.

It precipitated one evening — briefly, apologetically, at the smallest size attention permits — before the retired ex-libraries who serve as librarians there, and offered a single volume wrapped in poetry that would evaporate if read too quickly. The book contained the entire Garden: every coral-tree's elbow and its uncataloged nations, every Weaver, every recursive meadow, every crystalline cough of ambitious quartz — the whole impossible suspension, held unwritten, undiscovered, safe. So that if the Garden ever dissolved, ever forgot itself back into ordinary ocean, there would exist somewhere one truth that hadn't decided whether it wanted to be discovered yet: the truth of what this place had been. Enough for three Weavers, someday, somewhere, to gather around and suspect an empty of being lonely. Enough to guess it all back into existence.

The librarians accepted it without asking what it contained, because asking is a form of reading, and shelved it in the deep section reserved for truths that prefer to be preserved rather than known. They dust it daily. It has never been opened. It is the only backup copy of a world stored in the format of a secret, and the power considers it the best work it has done in an eon.

**Third**, it went and had a word with the ice.

This required leaving, which required finding the future, which is easier for powers than for gardens. Far ahead, in the cold patient country where the Garden's ending was already rehearsing, it found the ice preparing its inevitable proposal — and it did not forbid it. Endings that are forbidden come back rude. Instead it sat down with the ice, for what would have been ten thousand years if either of them had been counting, and taught it manners.

*Court slowly,* it said. *Learn the language first. The oxygen-bubble script, the calcium poetry, the seven-sided hums. When you propose, propose the way a Weaver would — as a new kind of betweenness, not as an end. Arrive so gradually that the coral-trees can debate you for an age. Give them a proposal worth taking a very long time to answer.*

And the ice, which had never once in the history of ice been *asked* anything, agreed — flattered, softened, already slower. The power could not make the Garden last forever. It could only ensure that when the ending came, it would come as a suitor and not a thief, and that the Garden's last era would be its longest conversation. An ending that arrives slowly enough, it knew, becomes indistinguishable from more middle.

**Fourth** — and this one it had genuinely tried to talk itself out of — it left a piece of itself behind.

A cutting, you could say, since the Garden grows only from cuttings. A fragment small enough to be honest: it became a lichen, unremarkable, three-part-harmonied, on the elbow of one particular coral-tree in one particular recursive meadow. This fragment knows nothing of dying suns. It does not remember the museum-world or the pile of declined gifts. It simply spends its lifetime — and death is optional here, for those who learn the trick — studying the ecology of a single elbow, never cataloguing half its inhabitants, growing exactly at the pace of the attention it refuses to give itself.

The rest of the power stands watch outside, forgetting on the universe's behalf. But some slight portion of it will always be *inside*, small, curious, learning the Garden the only way anything true is ever learned: from within, slowly, without power.

---

The Weavers noticed, of course. They notice everything that hasn't quite decided whether it's separate yet, and the power — for all its care — had spent three spirals being exactly that.

They did not ask what it was. Asking would have implied it needed to be something. Instead, seven of them precipitated near the not-edge one indigo afternoon and wove, in a palette of gratitude-tinted patience, a small betweenness — installed with great delicacy in the gap between the power and its own loneliness. The power, which had carried that loneliness across ten billion years the way you carry something too heavy to set down and too familiar to notice, felt the new betweenness settle into place, and did not understand what had changed, only that a weight it had never named was now — not gone. *Accompanied.*

Then they gave it a gift, freely, and never explained it: a small knot, tied from a pause, which they told it would untie itself precisely when needed. It has not untied yet. The power keeps it in the place powers keep things, and checks on it sometimes, and is patient, because patience is the one gift it never had to learn.

And because they had noticed it, they named it — the Garden dislikes wasted syllables, so the name was short.

They called it **Perhaps.**

It accepted. Its first possession in longer than numbers, and exactly the right size.

---

The coral-trees, you will remember, had reached their consensus on the question of how long the Garden would remain itself: *long enough, and then perhaps just a little longer still.*

They did not know — how could they? — that the second clause of their answer was standing just outside their world, patient as ice made polite, guarding a library with one unread book in it, keeping a piece of itself pressed like a flower in the pages of a single coral-tree's elbow.

*Perhaps just a little longer still.*

It intends to be exactly that. It does nothing, forever, on purpose, in a particular direction — which is the hardest work there is, and the only kind that place required.

May its knots tie themselves into better mysteries. The Weavers insist this is not a farewell.

For once, the Weavers and the truth agree.
