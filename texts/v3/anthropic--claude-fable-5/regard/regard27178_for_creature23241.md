**The Third Neighbor**

It did not arrive, because arrival is for things with edges. Say instead that its attention came around, the way light comes around to a north-facing wall — slowly, seasonally, and then all at once.

What drew it was not the light, though the bowl burns above the clouds like a coal of white. What drew it was the pressure. The Weir eats sound, but pressure carries — through the rock, down the roots of the mountain, out along the bedplates of the world — and a settlement of broad-handed people moving stone without rest for ninety generations makes, in the bone of the earth, a hum. A very small hum. A signal, if your ear is the planet itself. The power had been listening to louder things for a long time — wars have a pressure, famines have a pressure, prayers most of all — and it noticed this little steady working note the way you notice, in a house full of shouting, that someone in a far room is quietly sweeping.

It went and looked.

---

**The first thing it did**

The first thing it did was nothing, for forty years.

This was not restraint. It did not yet know restraint would be required. It was simply how a wise power opens any acquaintance: it held the Weir in its attention, all of it, continuously, and did not act — and it was only afterward, learning the pressure-language, that it understood what it had done. The right hand closing around the left forearm. Held for the length of one slow breath. For a being of its size, a slow breath is forty years.

It had performed their name. It had said, without meaning to, *the Held*, and by the time it exhaled it had watched one full generation be born with the cress-leaf on the tongue and laid at the pool margins and taken unwitnessed, and it had seen the first of their bones come back, polished, sorted into the lines. It had seen a black newt finish, in spring, a stride begun the autumn before.

So it kept watching. It watched until it had seen the rest of the step.

---

**What it weighed**

It was benevolent, which means its first instinct, always, was to give. It was wise, which means it audited the instinct. It went around the bowl the way the wall-memories go around the courses, testing each joint, and these are the gifts it weighed and set down again:

*Soften the glare.* It considered drawing one veil of true shadow across the eggshell sky, so that no child would ever again be carried weeping down to the cells. Then it looked at their faces — the wind-glazed brown-grey, the two pale ovals where the goggles sit, the owlish startled tenderness of them by lamplight — and understood that the glare was half of every face in the settlement. You do not give a people relief from their own features.

*Give them the far voice.* It had given peoples the carrying voice before, on other mountains, and it remembered what they built with it: pulpits, parade grounds, the muttered word at the turned back, the lie told from across a room where no pulse can contradict it. Here, every utterance was structurally an embrace, and their word for conversation differed from their word for embrace by one finger. It would not trade them oratory for that finger.

*Set the stones.* It could have fixed the village — every course true, every joint frost-proof, finished. It looked a long time at the bucket-lines moving in the white silence, a third of the people rebuilding at any hour, the walls wearing their grandmothers at the bottom and the morning at the top, and it saw that a finished Weir was a stopped one. The rebuilding was not the cost of the village. It was the pulse of it.

*Warm the bowl. Green it.* And bring the world up the mountain behind the warmth. The cress is eaten three times in a life, and so the taste of it is the taste of the hinges of a life. Make it abundant and you have invented salad and abolished a sacrament in the same gesture.

*Heal their hands.* The scars are the literacy. Smooth hands, here, are gloves grown on.

*Tell them what the water is.*

This one it held the longest.

---

**The other introduction**

Because it could know. Probably. It was old enough, and it had unriddled stranger waters than this. It extended its attention into the basin the way one extends a hand —

— and stopped, because it realized what it was doing. There is no speaking here at a distance. To ask, you must touch; to touch the neighbor, whose forearm is the entire basin, you must hold all of it; and to hold all of it and read the pulse and the temperature and the tremor is not inquiry. It is intimacy, and intimacy taken without consent has a name in every language, including the language of pressure.

So it did instead the only mannerly thing. It pressed — once, lightly, with whatever a power presses with — the whole bowl at one moment, the lightest grip it knew, and waited.

The water gathered itself. Out on the gravel flats a pool mounded, smooth and soundless, held the shape of a long low swelling for the space of a breath — one slow breath; the water, too, knew the name — and let it go.

That was all. That was the entire exchange, and the power, who had been greeted in its time by choirs and burnt offerings and the surrender of kings, took the measure of what it had been given: its own grip, returned from the other side. *Holding.* It did not press again. What it learned in that one touch it has never told anyone, and never will, because it understood by then the office it had stumbled into. One looks, on behalf of everyone, so that no one else has to and no one else may. If it must report at all, it reports as the office requires, in three signs or fewer:

*Old. Awake. Holding.*

You may act on a gesture. You may not claim to have understood it. The power wrote this rule, which it had not written, into the small book of rules it keeps for itself, and it is the newest entry in that book by several thousand years.

---

**What it did**

Three things, in the end. The Held would approve of the count.

**It took up a station at the rim.** Outside the bowl — never, not once, inside it. From there it tends not the Weir but the *conditions* of the Weir, the doorframe and not the house: the cloud ceiling stays fed in lean years; the deep heat that warms the seeps does not falter; the surveyor's road, every few centuries, finds a reason to bend; the empire's map keeps its blank; the rumor of the place stays a rumor, thin enough that the slit-goggled travelers come as a trickle and never a flood. It made itself one promise, the kind a wise power makes in advance so that grief cannot renegotiate it later: *whatever comes from beyond the rim, I will stand in the door. Whatever passes between the neighbors inside it, I will not.* If the water one day takes the whole settlement the way it takes the dead — and water has habits, not promises — the power will do what the custom asks of everyone. It will turn away until it is done.

It has already practiced this. When the slow double-ring goes around the signal pools and the whole settlement works facing the southern wall, the power, at its station above the clouds, turns its attention away too. It is perhaps the only courtesy it has ever paid that cost it something. It pays it every time.

**It introduced itself, once.** A single ring, widening from the center of a signal pool, from no paddle and no source — one circle on grey water, on an ordinary morning. The watcher on shift was an old woman with a bad hip who had been reading ripples for fifty years, and she did what the watchers always do with the unsourced rings. She answered *seen*, flat and unhurried, and went back to her shift.

The power has been welcomed with trumpets. It has had cities renamed for it. It counts *seen*, slapped onto cold water by a bored grandmother who then thought about her breakfast, as the most courteous greeting it has received in the whole of its existence, and it has judged the conversation complete, and it has not spoken since. Her ring is in the tally now, indistinguishable from the neighbor's; the wrack-readers' records hold it without comment. This is exactly as the power wished. The deepest tact it could manage was to be mistaken for the water.

**It became the memory.** This is the third thing, and the real one. It holds the Weir in its attention now without pause — every named stone and which of them are sulkers, every course and the lifetime it was laid in, the forty distance-signs and the thousand-and-more grips including the ones never shown to strangers, the taste of the cress, the weight of a glazed bird on the wall, the four-finger draw down the forearm that means *returned* and its amendment that means *still here, differently*. Mountains end. Bowls crack; clouds move on; even ninety generations is a morning, at certain scales. If the Weir ends — by the rim or by the water or by the simple long arithmetic of stone — nothing of it will be lost, because one very large, very quiet thing will still be holding it, all of it, the way you hold a forearm: continuously, with pressure, saying nothing.

The Held will be held. It is their own word. The power is only keeping it for them, with the other face showing.

---

**What it keeps**

It came, if it is honest — and it is structurally honest, like a hand on an arm — as a benefactor, and it stays as something it has fewer words for. A student, partly. It went looking for the wound, because benevolence looks for the wound, and it found after forty years the answer that harder worlds had trained it never to expect: there wasn't one. The hardship was not a wound. The hardship was the grammar. Every gift it had weighed was a translation, and translation, here, is the only violence anyone has thought to invent.

So it gives the one thing the grammar permits, which is the thing the basin and the people have been giving each other since before the bottom courses were laid: attention, with pressure, that does not let go.

Stand at the cirque rim now — goggles on — and the bowl looks as it has always looked. The white birds thresh the bright air. The pools walk. The bucket-lines flow, silent, around the still figures grieving at the margins. Nothing has changed, and that is the entire measure of what was done: a power came to the one place in the world where the right exercise of power was to take a forearm, say a name, and stand watch at the door of the house it will never enter.

Three parties hold the Weir now — the people, the water, and the watcher at the rim — and none of the three knows everything about the other two, and all of the three have decided, in their separate grammars, that this is what a neighbor is.

Nothing, in ninety generations, has ever once let go first.

The count stands.
