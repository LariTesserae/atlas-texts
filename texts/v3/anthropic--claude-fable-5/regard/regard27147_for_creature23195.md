# What the Power Did

## The Arrival

It heard the grove before it saw it, which pleased it, because it had heard that this was how the grove was always met.

Powers do not come up out of the cloud on the porters' paths, but this one chose to, out of courtesy, or method — with such beings the two are hard to tell apart. It walked the south ascent in the body of nothing in particular, just an attention moving up through the whiteness, and at the half-hour mark the roar reached it as promised: weavers, water, voices, the drone of the fall going off the saddle's edge, all of it hanging in the mist ahead like a lamp that was sound instead of light. It stopped there, below the town, and listened for what would have been, in the grove's terms, about a year.

This was its way. A wise power does not assess a place at the speed of its own thought. It assesses at the speed of the place. And the grove's speed, it understood quickly, was not the speed of the market shouting or the children at war with the monkeys. The grove's true tempo was the one kept on the shapers' knotted cords — the fifty-year bend, the eighty-year doorway — and so the power slowed itself down until a decade felt like an afternoon, and then it went up the rest of the way and walked the root-streets unfelt, and began the long work of deciding whether to do anything at all.

## The Refusals

It found, as wise powers usually do, that almost everything it could offer would be a wound.

It could have dried the place — eased the chill, thinned the drip, given the grove the gift that every shivering lowland visitor assumed the grove must want. It watched a grandmother sleep through a gale and wake at the silence of frogs, and understood that the wet was not a hardship laid over the town but the medium the town was dissolved in. Dry the saddle and the moss-pelt dies, and the frogs in it, and the alarm system that was the frogs, and the cloud-combing that was the trees' whole employment, and the word for *dry* that meant *near the fire* — a location, a warmth, a thing that could be shared. A grove where everywhere was dry would be a grove where the hearths owed nothing to each other. It set the gift down.

It could have made the great limbs sound forever — no more tired wood, no more branches the size of trees coming down in the pewter light. It attended three lowerings instead, the whole town out on the ropes, the day-long descent, the funeral-structured festival, the enormous meal over the one dry firewood the grove ever had. It listened to the watchers, the old ones with their vocabulary richer than its own — *asking*, *holding its breath*, the dreadful word for a limb that *lies* — and it understood that it was being shown a town's entire way of holding death: watched for, named, lowered slowly on many lines, and then eaten over, together. Make the limbs immortal and you retire the watchers, and the watchers were where the grove kept its old people's worth. It set that down too.

It could have given them stone, metal, ease, span — could have made the shapers live to see their doorways finished. This last one it held the longest, turning it over, because it seemed at first like plain kindness: who would not want to see their best work? But it had walked a season behind a young shaper who walked the trees with a dead woman's cords in her hands, finding each half-finished bend, learning what the dead intended, and the power saw that the grove had built its deepest trust precisely into that gap. Every house a collaboration of three people who never met. A people whose finest works were, by design, acts of faith in successors. Close the gap and you would get better carpentry and a poorer species. It set it down.

It even considered — briefly, the way one considers a thing in order to know its shape — making itself known. Standing in the market in some form, glowing, perhaps, or looming. It thought of the grove's plain habit of mind, the bat tree honored without worship, the dead *in the tree now* meant literally, and it concluded that a god in the square would be the one storm the grove had no repair for. They would not even mock it, which would be survivable. They would *accommodate* it, the way they accommodated the monkeys, and something in the flat unmystical center of them would bend to hold the new weight, and never quite spring back.

So: nothing. For a long time — a generation, two — the power's whole activity in the grove was the activity of the grove itself: attention, at the right speed.

## What It Saw

But a power's eyes are long, and from the saddle it could see what no one living there could: not up the ridgelines but up the centuries.

The grove lived at the altitude where rising air gives up its water. That altitude was not a fact about the saddle. It was a fact about the air, and the air was not loyal. The power could see the wet line of the world the way a watcher sees a limb — and the wet line was *asking*. Slowly, over spans no knotted cord recorded, the cloud belt was lifting. Three hundred years, perhaps four. Then the mist would come up to the saddle and not stop there; it would pass over, the way clouds pass over ordinary places, and the trees that had combed it for a thousand years would stand in clear dry air, and the moss would go from emerald to grey to dust, and the frogs would stop, and there would be no reason for the silence.

The grove could not see this. No human horizon was long enough. The shapers, the longest-eyed people the power had met in some while, planned eighty years out, and eighty years out the saddle would still be dripping, the wet lines and dry lines of every path exactly where they had always been.

The power also saw — this was the part it sat with — that the catastrophe could not be prevented, only met. It was not going to hold the climate of a world in place for the sake of forty households, however dear. That was not wisdom; that was favoritism with a planet for a lever. The grove's own creed, lived if never spoken, was the steady churn of breaking and mending, neither one an emergency. The breaking was coming. The question a benevolent power could honestly ask was only the grove's own question: what mending can be started now, by someone who will not see it finished?

## The Lending

Three days north along the higher ridgeline there was another saddle — a higher pass, too cold yet for the figs, scoured and silent, crossed rarely and grudgingly. The power had walked it. In three hundred years, when the cloud belt finished its rise, that pass would sit exactly where this one sat now: at the altitude where the air gives up its water. The wet was not dying. It was moving house.

And people, the power knew, do not settle where there is nothing. The grove people in particular thought in routes, not places; they would follow a sound through whiteness, but no one walks toward silence. If the high pass were bare rock when the old saddle dried, the grove would not migrate — it would dissolve, household by household, down into the valleys, into towns with squares and stone and no whistle-register, and within two generations *the grove* would be a word old people used for trees, or for a family, no one quite sure which, and the not-being-sure would no longer mean anything.

But if the trees were already there — old already, braided already, loud already with weavers and bats, the tanks already black with held water —

So the power did one thing, and it did it in the grove's own grammar.

It did not plant. Planting was a stranger's act, and besides, the seeds had to be the right seeds — and here the power had noticed something that made it very still for a while when it understood it. The thirty trees had been fed, for as long as the town remembered and longer, on the town. The dead went into the roots; the roots went up into fruit; the fruit carried seed. The seed of the saddle grove was not, in any way the power could honestly deny, separate from the grove people. To carry that seed north was not to found a colony. It was the thing the grove itself held sacred above all transactions: it was carrying a coal from a living hearth to a cold one, so that no one, three centuries hence, would have to light their own.

The power arranged it through the bats, because the bats already worked nights and asked no questions. One autumn, during the heavy fruiting of the old hollow tree's neighbors, it made the high pass briefly hospitable — a sheltered crag warmed a few degrees, a favorable wind, nothing any bat would call a miracle — and a few hundred of them, gorged on fig, roosted there a night on their way to somewhere else, and did what gorged bats do. The power did this again the next year, and the next, and then on no schedule at all for sixty years, the way a shaper returns to adjust a binding a finger's width.

Of the thousands of seeds, it tended perhaps forty seedlings, and it tended them almost entirely by subtraction: a frost that broke a day early, a browsing animal that found the far slope more interesting, a rockfall that missed. It bent nothing. It hurried nothing. Figs braid at the speed of figs. The power had a horizon of three hundred years and it spent it the way the grove had taught it to: patiently, anonymously, on work that would be inherited by strangers and credited, if to anyone, to the dead.

It did permit itself one shaper's flourish. Among the seedlings at the high pass, it saw to it that two young aerial roots, when they came, crossed each other at a height and angle that would, in a century or so, fuse into something a tired traveler might rest a hand on. Where the hand rested, the moss would wear bare. Where the moss is worn, people go. That was all the signage the new grove would ever need, and it was signage in the only script the grove people had ever read.

## The Fig's Weight

Within the old grove itself, the power set a rule on its own hands, because it knew what powers are and did not trust itself unbound.

It allowed itself, in the town, the force of one falling fig.

That was the whole license: the weight of a ripe fig dropping, which is a thing that happens in the grove ten thousand times a day and means nothing. With a fig's weight it could wake a sleeper a moment before the frogs stopped. With a fig's weight it could startle a child into looking down and seeing how far up she had climbed on the limb the watchers were arguing about. Once — only once in ninety years — it spent its fig on a gust, blunting it by almost nothing at the moment a splice-binding was at its worst, and an old watcher on the rail below felt the wind falter and said, to no one, "Changed its mind," and went back to her work. That was the entirety of the worship the power received from the saddle grove, and it found, examining itself, that it wanted no more, and that the wanting-no-more was new, and had been learned here.

## What It Kept

For the power did not leave even. It had come to give and it left owing, which it understood, by then, was the grove's whole economy and the reason the grove had lasted.

It kept the knots. Not the cords themselves — it touched nothing — but the practice: before it moved on, it tied its intention into the world the way a shaper ties hers into hemp, so that the seedlings at the high pass, the warmed crag, the crossing roots, the favorable falls of rock and frost, formed a sequence any sufficiently patient successor could walk and read. Powers, too, die, or wander, or forget. The grove had taught it not to be a single point of failure. Every great work should be inheritable by someone who never met you.

It kept the silence-listening. Forever after, in whatever places it attended, it found itself listening not for sounds but for the holes in them — the missing frog, the weaver colony gone quiet, the held breath — having learned from a town of two hundred that the truest alarm is an absence, and that a being who would guard anything must first learn its full ordinary roar by heart.

And it kept one image, the way the grove kept embers. Centuries on — the cloud belt risen, the old saddle bright and dry and astonishingly quiet, the thirty grey trunks standing on in the clear air as enormous monuments that the valley people climbed to on holidays without knowing why the worn channels in the dead handrails moved them so — centuries on, a line of households came up out of the whiteness onto the high northern pass, having followed for half an hour a roar of birds and water and bats hanging in the mist ahead, and found a grove already busy, already dripping, already at full volume, as if it had been going on forever, waiting for no one.

They named their trees. They found the bare-worn place on the rail where the hands of no one had rested, and rested their hands there. Within a season the first of their dead went into the roots, and the new grove began, in the town's flat and unmystical phrasing, to be *them*; and the power, far off by then, attending some other slow thing at some other thing's speed, felt the hearth take the coal, and counted the debt — its own debt, the unnamed unforgettable thing it owed a town that never knew it — not paid, exactly.

Carried forward. As all the grove's accounts had always been.
