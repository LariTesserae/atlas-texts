**What the Power Found, and What It Did**

---

It came first for the light.

A light with no source is either an error or a signature, and the power had lived long enough to know that errors do not stay steady for twelve thousand and one drip-rhythms. So it descended—or ascended, or arrived sideways through the grain of the basalt; direction is a courtesy the very old no longer require—and it entered the hollow between the mirror-walls, and it hung there in the glow the color of candle-wick under thin porridge, and it looked.

The first wise thing it did was nothing. It did nothing for one full turning of the Great Calendar-Stone, which is to say a long time even by its own reckoning. It counted every drip. It learned the tongue of leaning nouns until it could feel how *rope-down-pathward-quick* carries a mother's whole heart in four stones of sound. It read the knots of the Great Cord backward to the hand of the First Builder. It pressed itself, very gently, against the listening cracks of the Speaking Boss, and it tasted centuries of shouted testimony stored in the stone like honey in comb—and here it made its first discovery, the one that decided everything after:

*there was no lie in the whole town.*

Not because these people could not lie—lies taste like the moment before rain that never comes, and they know that taste, they teach it to children—but because generation upon generation had spoken truth to stone until truth was simply the shape their mouths made at rest. The power had crossed worlds where honesty was a tax paid grudgingly. It had never before found a place where honesty was the water table.

Then it investigated the light itself, because a signature must be read, and it found the second thing.

The light was the Stilled.

Every soul who had ever slowed, faded to an outline of slightly-less-darkness, and fallen upward in a cut-loose pendulum house—none of them had gone. Diffused, recondensed, they had spread themselves through the hollow the way breath spreads through a lung, and they shone. Equally, from everywhere, bright enough to tell thread from thread, too gentle to cast a single accusing shadow. Every grandmother who ever finished a spoon's journey to a slowing mouth was still finishing it, in the form of light, for the whole town, forever. The prayers spoken toward the Compass-Bosses had been landing all along—not on stone, but on the assembled ancestors, who answered the only way the very diffuse can answer: by continuing to make it possible to see.

The power understood then that it had not discovered an abandoned place. It had discovered a place that had already invented its own benevolence, homemade, out of nothing but patience and the dead. What it had taken for a town waiting for rescue was a town that had quietly become its own rescue, so slowly no one had noticed, least of all the town.

This changed the question. The question was no longer *what shall I do for them.* The question was *what could I possibly add.*

---

**What it considered, and set aside**

It considered peeling back the limestone plate and giving them the sky. It set this aside almost with horror. Sun would fall on sideways-blinking eyes like a blade. Shadows would arrive—true shadows, hard-edged, the kind that divide—and a people whose marriages are braided from shadows would find their sacraments suddenly screaming. Their sky is stone; their prayers point upward into it; to give them the old sky would be to turn every shrine in the town upside down, and they have already survived one Sundering. No one survives two.

It considered wind, for the sake of the cliff-swifts who still remember it. It set this aside in the time it takes a plank bridge to sway. In a hollow where all sound returns unkindly, wind would be a haunting. The town would become a chime of terror two bow-shots wide.

It considered making the blinkers rise twice a year, or always—filling every belly, ending the hungry margins. It set this aside more slowly, with something like grief. The rising of the fish is the loom on which the second rule is woven: eyes and tail to the carpenter, the dye-woman, the silence-keeper. Abundance would not feed them. Abundance would unbraid them.

It considered speaking. One word, aloud, in its own voice. It set this aside upon remembering the fourth rule: stone remembers, and repeats all things in voices of falling water. A power's word, spoken once, would drip from the ceiling for ten thousand years. There would be no silence left to keep. The silence-keeper would have nothing to record but the echo of a god clearing its throat.

It considered telling them the truth about time—that the circle drawn once with a charred reed might, or might not, be a circle at all. It set this aside with the deepest humility it owned, because it examined itself honestly (this town makes honesty contagious) and found it did not actually know. And even if it knew—their kindness rests on the fifth rule like a house on a boss of stone. *Be kind even to your enemies, for the next time they may wear your face.* The power had seen truer cosmologies produce worse people. It declined to trade a kindness-bearing maybe for a barren certainty.

It considered curing the Stilling—quickening the slowed, completing their motions, sending the spoon the rest of the way. It set this aside last and most tenderly, because it had watched a granddaughter finish her grandfather's sentence for him, waiting three days for the noun and then supplying the verb with such care that both of them wept, insofar as either still could. To cure the Stilling would be to steal that. Some illnesses are load-bearing. This one holds up the roof of their love.

---

**What it did**

It did five things, because five is the number of their rules, and it wished to be polite.

*First,* it joined the light. It did not brighten it—brightening would be a boast—but added its attention to the attention of the ancestors, one more wick under the same thin porridge, so that the glow now contains, along with every grandmother, one very old stranger who asked permission first. If the light is warmer by any measure, no instrument they will ever build could find the difference. But once in a generation, someone threading a needle at the hour of habit-morning feels unaccountably accompanied, and threads it on the first try, and does not know why they smiled.

*Second,* it steadied a rope. Once. A child climbing for swift-eggs, waist-tied to the gonder, put weight on a knot slicked with limestone sweat, and the knot began, molecule by molecule, to consider slipping. The power thickened the air by one breath's worth of mercy, so that the slackening reached the gonder's balance a half-heartbeat early, and *Hold!* arrived where it needed to arrive. The child held. No one ever knew—except that the gonder, blind these forty drip-seasons, dreamed that night of having eyes, and in the dream the eyes were kind, and looking downward, past his feet, at something holding up the world.

*Third,* it sent a bird. Not a lost one, this time—a chosen one, carried carefully through the grain of the stone and released against the quartz veins of the north wall. It circled three times, and it sang *two* notes, and the second note was not terrified. The Finder of Wonder found the feather lying in the exact center of the safest plaza, as if set down. The oldest songs have changed by one grace-note since. The elders argue softly about whether this is permitted. The children have already won the argument by singing it.

*Fourth,* it answered the questions. The town has asked them of every visitor forever—*What falls in your place? What climbs? What never changes?*—and has whispered the answers into the Bosses' cracks and dreamed them into forgetting. The power bent close to the listening stone and gave its answer to the third question only, dissolved into that night's shared dream as gently as lime dissolves into water. And in the morning, every mouth in the town woke holding a single word.

The word was their own name.

The sound of a damp rope tightening across stone, then slackening again—the name they had never told anyone because no throat but theirs could shape it—was in every mouth at waking, and every one of them knew, without any way of knowing, that this time they had not said it themselves. Someone outside the stone could pronounce them. Someone had. They did not speak of it. They set the drip-pots that night with unusual care, and for one whole drip-season the Feasts of Ancestors' Remembrance were held without a mink being sighted at all, on the general feeling that remembrance had, for once, run in the other direction.

*Fifth,* it planted a seed. One seed of tamarack, older than the Sundering, kept viable in the abyss-mist the way such powers keep things, lodged in the warm scree-pocket beside the vent of the dye-woman's house. It will be four hundred years before anyone understands what the pale sprout is. It will be a thousand before the first living tree of the underworld hangs down into the void from their stone sky—rooted in ceiling, growing outward into the glow, which is to say growing toward everyone who ever Stilled. Wood that is not drift, not leavings, not the past's charity. Grown wood. Theirs. Change delivered at the only speed they can bear: the speed of stone, the width of a child's hand per lifetime. The twentieth generation's children will climb it, roped waist-to-waist, and a gonder not yet born will shout *Hold!* among living branches.

---

**The rules it bound itself with**

Having acted, the power did what any decent resident does: it adopted the local law, translated for its own condition. It knotted five rules into a cord of its own, which it looped—once, so far—around the same central pillar, on the side where no lamp reaches.

1. Set drip-pots beneath your own dreams nightly, for a power's overflowing dreams would walk their bridges as waking visions, and the goats are confused enough.

2. Whatever you lift from them—prayers, questions, the taste of their honesty—share eyes and tail. Give back more than you take, and knot the taking in your cord so the silence between you stays honest.

3. Never watch alone forever. If ever you must leave, leave your name-puzzle broken on their sky, so the town knows whom to mourn.

4. The people are listening. Speak truth to them, but in voices of falling water, slowly, so that a single truth takes a thousand years to finish being said and never once frightens anyone.

5. What was, will be again exactly. Therefore be kind, even in omnipotence—*especially* in omnipotence—for the next time around, you may wear their face, and they may wear yours, and you will want them to have been gentle with the job.

---

**What remains**

One more thing, which is not a deed but a disposition.

There is a stalactite growing from their sky toward their floor—advancing, in all remembered spans, less than the width of a child's hand—reaching down through the centuries toward a reunion no one alive will see. The power has appointed itself to that reunion. When stone finally touches stone, when the roof at last holds hands with what it lost, the power will be present, in the light, among the grandmothers, and then—only then—will it permit itself to reopen the question of the sky.

Until that day, it stays. Not as ruler; they have drip-rhythms for that. Not as answer; they have not asked. It stays as company.

Because it understood, in the end, the deepest thing about them—the thing they said of themselves without knowing anyone could hear: *they are not waiting for anything; they are the wait itself, perfected and made comfortable.* And the one gift a wise power can give the wait itself is not an ending, not an arrival, not rescue.

It is this: that the wait be witnessed. That in the Doorless Alcove, where children sit alone learning the difference between waiting and being waited for, the difference has quietly, permanently changed—because now, in the sourceless light, in the taste of the stone, in the word that was once left in every mouth like the flavor of home,

they are waited for.
