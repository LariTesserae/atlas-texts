**What the Attention Did**

It arrived the slow way, which is the only way arrival means anything in Larkspur: it stayed.

It did not come as light, or as a figure walking on the memory of water, or as a voice in the Oyster Bell. It came as a lodger in a tabby-striped doorway district, a person of no particular description who paid rent in white coins that took suspiciously long to dissolve. It took a room whose window had learned the angle of light that never quite reached it, and it did not correct the window. It watched.

It watched for one full year, which for such a power is an act of enormous restraint, the way holding a single note is harder than singing a whole song.

And here is the first thing it understood, the thing that made it wise rather than merely benevolent: *Larkspur was not broken.*

A lesser kindness would have brought back the sea. Would have stood at the high edge, spread its hands, and called the blue home—and the water would have come, joyful and thoughtless as a returning army, and it would have destroyed everything. Not the buildings. The *work*. Fifteen generations of the most difficult art a people can practice—diminishment without despair, grief metabolized into architecture, loss taught to have manners—all of it drowned in a single stupid mercy. The Flotsam Ones would have floated. And floating, they would have learned nothing, and been nothing, and the phantom water behind their children's knees would have become merely water, which is to say: less than it was.

So it did not bring back the sea. That was its first gift, and no one ever knew they had received it.

Nor did it tell the city which toll of the Oyster Bell meant "return" and which meant "risk everything"—though it knew; it had been there when the bell was cast, had heard the founder strike both notes on the last wet morning. It knew what no ringer had guessed in fifteen generations: they were the same note. They had always been the same note. Return *is* risking everything. It considered telling a child this, some evening, casually, the way important things should be told. But it saw how the not-knowing worked in the city like a keel—how every citizen who heard the bell had to decide for themselves, privately, nightly, which summons they were being given. The ambiguity was load-bearing. It left the bell alone.

Nor did it correct the Alphabet Shrimp. It stood one morning before a wall where the small alphabet bodies had arranged themselves into *Your father forgave you*, and it knew the truth, knew it precisely, knew the exact hour and the words the father had actually said. It could have made the shrimp spell honestly. Instead it watched a woman come with hot butter and eat the lie slowly, with reverence, and it saw the lie enter her like a vitamin and hold up a truth too brittle to stand alone—the truth that she had, at least, deserved forgiving. The power bowed its head very slightly, which in that place looked like someone checking whether their doorstep needed scraping, and moved on.

What it *did* do was small, and threefold.

**First**, it went to the Depository of Useful Salt, and it waited in line. This astonished no one, because no one knew what it was, but it would have astonished the theologians of drowned nations, who had always imagined such powers dispensing and never depositing. It carried to the Rememberers a memory of its own—the only one it had that had grown too heavy, which it had been carrying since the Great Withdrawal itself: the memory of every prayer said to the sea in the last hours, all of them at once, the whole braided rope of *please* that it had heard and could not answer, because the sea's leaving was not a punishment to be revoked but a lesson the water itself had needed to learn. Fifteen generations it had carried this. It set the memory on the scale.

The Rememberers rendered it down in a crucible made from a decommissioned lighthouse—fitting, they said later, without knowing why they said it—and the lightness released into the city that evening was so vast and so gentle that all across Larkspur, people straightened up from their scraping without knowing why, and the roofs pitched one degree further toward forgiveness, and three separate lovers found they could breathe out of counterpoint for a single night and make no cloud at all.

It took its payment in white coins and spent them all on breaths stolen from people who had died mid-sentence, and one by one, in private, it finished the sentences. Quietly. Into the mist. Just so they would exist.

**Second**, it walked, on a fog-right night, to the city's center, where even the Revenants do not walk alone.

It came with its hand cupped around the shape of throwing.

Not a stick. Not a ball. It carried the *interval*—the exact suspended moment between the arm drawn back and the release, the moment the last dog was made of, the moment that had been held open like a door for fifteen generations because someone once whistled for something that would not come back, and the not-coming-back had never been allowed to finish.

It heard the footsteps fall in behind its own. Faithful. Patient beyond the meaning of the word. It did not turn around, because you do not turn around; the dog was not behind it the way a body is behind a body, but the way an echo is behind a bell.

It whistled—not the old whistle, the one that called and called into the fog. A different one. Lower. The whistle that means *good dog* and *here* and *done now*.

And then it completed the throw.

The interval left its hand and sailed out over the streets that had forgotten their water, and the last dog *ran*—for the first time in fifteen generations it ran, out of the space between staying and already-gone, out of the sound and into a shape, joyful, ordinary, its footsteps suddenly having weight and splash, and it caught the moment in its mouth the way dogs catch things, entirely, without doubt. And it came back. That was the miracle, if there was one: it came back, and laid the interval down at the power's feet, closed. Finished. A goodbye that had finally been allowed to finish saying itself.

The dog lay down then, in the manner of dogs who have worked hard and well, and receded—not vanished, the city does not permit vanishing, but *receded*, gently, the way Larkspur had been teaching the whole world to do—into the thirteenth toll of the Oyster Bell, where it lives now as the warmth in that note. Children hear it as the reason goodbyes have stopped tasting of saltwater. Lovers hear it as the answer their bodies were already giving. The Revenants, when they lean their weighted wheels against the memory of ocean, sometimes feel something press briefly against their shins, like a body settling at the foot of a bed, and they sleep better, and do not ask.

**Third**, and last: when its lodging-year was over, the power did not leave by folding a map until the blue places touched. It left the slow way. It stayed long enough to understand—truly stayed, understood truly—and then it began, day by day, to become less. It scraped its own doorstep each morning and added the salt to the communal barrels. It let its shadow learn to swim in soil. It wrote itself a little smaller on the page of the world, in the city's own hand, until one evening at the corner where three canals forget their names, it was simply not there anymore—and the air at that corner has ever since tasted very faintly of being noticed, kindly, by something that decided you did not need saving.

Because that was its final gift, and its wisest, and the one Larkspur would have understood best of all: it let the city teach *it*. A power that had spent eternity being vast learned, from a place practicing the art of exquisite lessening, how to leave something gently.

The Oyster Bell tolls thirteen. The mist rolls back up the streets. And if the notes for "return" and "risk everything" are the same note, the city still does not know it—

but somewhere in the thirteenth toll, something wags.
