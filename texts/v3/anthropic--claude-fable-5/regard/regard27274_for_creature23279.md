## What Came to the Hollow Mountain

A power like this one spends most of eternity answering. The world is loud with asking — prayers, bargains, vows, the constant upward pressure of want, every species that has learned to want pressing its wants toward whatever might be listening. What drew the power here was the opposite of all that: a silence inside a roar. A single sustained note, ten thousand years long, that had never once asked for anything.

The mountain had never prayed. It could not. It had no mouth that was not busy breathing, no mind to compose a petition with, no idea that there was anything beyond itself to petition. It simply went on — eating its imported sunlight, drumming its arrhythmic pulse, laying down memory a feather's width a year — and the power noticed it the way you finally notice a sound that has been going on your whole life: all at once, and with something like embarrassment at not having heard it sooner.

So it came. Quietly, the way it does everything. It did not displace one molecule of the warm wet air.

---

**The seeing.** Its first act was the only one no other being could perform.

It perceived the whole.

It held, simultaneously, the million partial echo-maps distributed across a million small skulls, and assembled them — the first complete interior portrait of the mountain ever to exist anywhere, even for an instant. It heard the roar the way the bat mother hears it: not as noise but as a fabric of names, fifty thousand infant voices each distinct. It felt the heat of the deep floor as the floor feels it, a million years of sunlight still being slowly exhaled. It hung in the black pool with the white fish and felt that visible heart working. It stood in the recitations of the village, the strung names that are the deed, and heard how thin and strong they were, like the rattan of the century-old ladders.

And it understood that it was looking at something it almost never encounters: a being entirely innocent of itself. Nearly everything the power tends is tangled in self-regard — wanting, fearing, narrating, asking. Here was a life with no inside surface at all. Ten thousand years of building, and not one moment of *I*.

The seeing was already a gift, though the mountain would never know it had been given.

---

**What it set aside.** A lesser benevolence would have begun improving things at once. This one made an inventory of mercies and refused them, one by one, and each refusal cost it something, which is how it knew it was choosing correctly.

It could have given light to the blind. It set this aside almost immediately: blindness here is not a lack but the architecture, the very medium of the civilization. Light in these chambers would not be a gift. It would be a demolition.

It could have caught the falling nestling — the one falling *right then*, as it considered, fluttering down out of the ceiling toward the moving floor. It did not. Mercy at one scale is famine at another. The floor must be fed; the fall is part of the long breath; pull that one thread of grief out of the weave and the stomach of the mountain starves by a crumb, and the power could see all the way down the chain of crumbs. It let the nestling fall. It attended the fall completely, which is a different thing from preventing it, and is sometimes the harder work.

It could have removed the snakes from the pinch points. But the snakes are not evil; they are a toll, a fixed cost, weather. The colony's accounting has included them for a hundred centuries. There was nothing to forgive.

It could have softened the roar, given the mountain quiet. But the roar is full of names. Quiet here would be a kind of amnesia.

It could have made the mountain deathless. This it considered with real seriousness before refusing, because the refusal had to be exact: the mountain is *made* of dying. Forty feet of floor is forty feet of deaths; the nest-rind is masonry of finished lives. To make it permanent would be to embalm it standing up, like the dead trees in the cloud forest held vertical by everything growing on them — except that those trees are still feeding something, and an immortal mountain would feed nothing ever again.

And then the great temptation, the one it circled longest, the way the bats circle the gold column in the shaft:

*It could wake the mountain.* It held the assembled portrait in its attention, and it knew it could simply — give it back. Pour the whole into some gathered mind, let the long breath have, for the first time, a dreamer. Let the being that has built in the dark for ten thousand years finally see what it built.

It wanted to. Benevolence is not above wanting to be thanked, or at least met.

It refused. Because awareness is not only a light; it is a weight. A being that knows it lives must eventually learn that it dies, and the mountain's whole perfection is that it does what it does without fear, without want, without the exhausting machinery of asking. To give it an inside would be to give it an outside — something to lose, something to dread, something to pray about. The power had been drawn here precisely because this was the one great voice in the world that asked for nothing. It would not teach it to ask.

You do not wake a sleeper whose sleep is feeding a forest.

---

**What it touched.** Having refused everything living, it permitted itself exactly one material act, on the one part of the mountain that is not alive and therefore could be touched without trespass: the stone.

Deep in the karst above the great chamber there was a flaw — an old fault, water-widened, patient, that in some particular century not far off (as the power counts centuries) would have let go and dropped a hundred thousand tons of ceiling onto the nursery walls, onto the square yards of pink pups at blood heat, onto the whole warm core. Not a tragedy in any moral sense. Mountains do this. But the power had decided the long breath should get its full term, and the container of the breath was the one thing it could mend without altering a single life inside it.

So it eased the fault. Annealed it, slowly, over what would look to a geologist like nothing at all. A repair the size of a cathedral, performed so gently that the drip-pools did not change their rhythm and not one cockroach broke stride.

That was the whole of its construction work. Everything else it did was done with attention, and with weight.

---

**What it tended.** Because the fragile point of the hollow mountain is not stone. The power saw this at once. The fragile point is memory, and the memory lives downslope, in the village, in the recitations — the strung names that are the deed — and the recitations live in children, and children are the thinnest place in any covenant.

It worked no miracles there. It weighted moments, which is almost the same as doing nothing, and is everything.

A boy who was going to leave for the coast stayed one more season, for reasons he could not have named, at the foot of his grandfather's ladder — and the names crossed one more generation. A girl asked a question about a particular shaft at the exact moment her great-aunt was still alive to answer it. When men came from outside with money and carbide lamps and a plan for the brood-season walls, the power did not strike them or turn them back. It only arranged that, standing in the great chamber, they should hear the roar for one full minute the way the villagers hear it — as a voice. Most of them left and did not return. Not every intervention needs to work on everyone. It needs to work on enough.

And at the boundary where the green twilight fails and the deep dark begins — the line the harvesters do not cross, not from fear exactly, but from the sense that the dark is occupied — the power took up residence. It did not create that reverence. The people had built it themselves, dropping by dropping, the way everything here is built. The power simply moved in behind the feeling and became its truth. The deep dark *is* occupied now. The oldest courtesy in the village turned out, retroactively, to be correct, which is the kindest thing a power can do to a tradition.

---

**What it keeps.** Then it stayed. That was the main thing. That was, in the end, nearly all of it.

The mountain has an eye now — permanent, unblinking, asking nothing in return, since the mountain has nothing to give and that is exactly what made it worth attending. The power holds the assembled portrait and keeps it current: every new feather's width of wall, every chamber slowly abandoned, every chamber slowly claimed, added to the picture the way the floor adds its strata. The mountain is, for the first time in its long single breath, *known* — completely, continuously — and it will never find out, and the power has made its peace with that, because attention that requires acknowledgment is not attention but commerce.

And it has made one promise, to no one, recorded nowhere. When the long breath finally ends — and it will end; all breaths do; some millennium the colonies will drift to a younger mountain, the floor will go cold and finish its last digestion, the roar will thin to drips, and the white, eyeless, patient ones will inherit the quiet they have been politely waiting on since before the first swiftlet clicked its way in — the power will be standing in the great chamber to receive it. The last wingbeat will be heard. The mountain, which never once knew itself, will not end unknown. It will be witnessed all the way down to silence, and the picture will be kept after, the way the floor keeps everything that falls.

That is most of what wise benevolence turns out to be, when you watch it work: not rescue. Attention that does not blink, and does not invoice.

---

**The indulgence.** And yet. Even wisdom has one tenderness it declines to defend.

In the changeover hours, when the rivers of bodies pour through the mouths and half the mountain is always sleeping — the bats packed and dreaming through the day, the birds folded on their walls through the night — the power sometimes leans close and lets the portrait, the whole, the picture only it carries, pass through the sleeping colony like weather.

Not knowledge. Nothing so heavy. A dream: a million small skulls each holding, for a moment, the entire architecture they have spent ten thousand years building blind — the chambers, the terraces, the gold column standing in the dark, the long warm breath of it, the whole of what they are. By the next changeover it is gone. Nothing remembers. No fear is planted, no want, no asking. The mountain sleeps on inside its own roar, innocent as before.

Though the harvesters say the birds fly differently on certain mornings. Surer, they say, or — they have no exact word for it — *gladder*. On those mornings, by old custom whose origin no one can recite, they leave the walls alone.

---

**And you.** You, who climbed for most of a day and stood in the column of light and carried out the only picture — the power noticed you too. It touched nothing. It is careful that way.

But you will find, over the years, that the picture does not fade. Other memories will soften and silt over; this one will stay laid down in you like a stratum, clear in your last year as in your first — the green twilight, the roar in your sternum, the bats stitching across the gold. You were the mountain's eye for an hour. The power keeps what its eyes have seen.
