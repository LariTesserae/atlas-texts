# The Answering

It did not come through the hatch. Powers of its kind rarely use doors; doors are for bodies, and it had worn a body so long ago that it kept one now only out of courtesy, the way a guest removes their shoes. It came instead the way attention comes—gradually, then all at once. It had been listening to the world, as it always listens, and somewhere beneath the noise of oceans and the arguments of cities it had heard a small, ceaseless, patient sound: fiber settling against fiber. An enormous basket, breathing.

It followed the sound across the cracked clay flats. It found the overhang, and the hatch no larger than a wine barrel's mouth, and it made itself small—wise powers arrive small, always; arriving large is how you break the thing you came to see—and it dropped through, into the amber dark, wearing the shape of a traveler. Old. Dusty. Empty-handed.

The Bound made room. They always do.

It noticed that. It noticed it the way it noticed everything: without judgment, but without letting go. *They are very good at making room,* it thought. *I will want to know what that has cost them.*

---

## First, It Looked

For a long time—longer than the Bound realized, because it folded its days carefully, the way a Keeper-Mother folds her legs—it did nothing but look.

It sat with Selt in her junction-chamber and let her fingers read the calluses on its borrowed hands, and it did not correct her when she pronounced it a weaver from some far warren. In a sense she was right. It watched her settle three disputes without raising her whisper, and it thought: *here is a power like mine, only smaller and braver, because she cannot leave.*

It walked the hot chambers and listened to Old Cull hum. It recognized the song. It had heard the song before, in other deep places, in other languages—the song things sing when they are keeping time with something that keeps time with them. It hummed a third line, very quietly, beneath hers. Cull stopped scraping her bones for a moment. Then she continued, and her humming had changed, very slightly, as if a question she had been asking for sixty years had finally been marked *received*.

It let Whistle's ferrets inspect it. Grip, the scarred female, climbed onto its shoulder and did something no one had ever seen her do: she fell asleep. Whistle said nothing, but he watched the traveler after that with the particular attention of a man whose animals know things he doesn't.

It found Tetch by the Sixth Level hatch, pretending to study binding-patterns. It did not tell him it could see his hands moving even now, faintly, working a pattern in the air—a pattern flowing *up* through him from below, using him the way sound uses a wall. It only sat beside him a while, and while it sat, it did one small thing: it placed its attention gently between the boy and the pattern, the way you'd slip a hand between a sleeping child and a cold draft. Not blocking. Just interrupting the assumption of access. Tetch's hands went still. He looked at them as if they had just been returned to him, and he did not know why he wanted to weep, and the traveler did not explain.

And it found Mira, ear to the floor, weaving her forbidden swatches. It sat down beside her without asking. After a long silence it said, in her own soft dialect: "You've been transcribing. May I read?"

She should have hidden them. She had hidden them from everyone. But there is a quality wise powers carry, a kind of weather, and in that weather concealment feels suddenly like a burden one has been given permission to set down. She pulled the swatches from the gap in the wall. Nine of them. Nine years of listening.

The traveler read them with its fingers, the way the Bound read everything, and its face did something Mira had never seen a face do—it grieved and rejoiced in the same movement, the way weather can rain through sunlight.

"It's language," Mira whispered. "Isn't it."

"It is," the traveler said. "You have most of the grammar already. You were only missing one thing."

"What?"

"That it isn't calling *to* you." The traveler laid the swatches down in order, oldest to newest, and suddenly Mira could see it—the way the pattern in each swatch leaned on the one before, the way a wall leans on its lower courses. "It's calling *for help*."

---

## Then, It Considered

Here is what the power thought about doing, and did not do. This part matters. Benevolence is mostly refusal; anyone who tells you otherwise has never held real strength in their hands.

It considered rain. It could have brought rain—could have unhooked some passing weather and hung it over the flats, and let water fall on this place for the first time in living memory. It got as far as gathering the impulse before it looked—really looked—at what it was proposing. Rain would fall on cracked clay and turn it to slurry. Slurry would find the light-shafts. Water would come down through seven levels of dried sedge like fire through a hayloft, and the lichen would drown, and the walls would swell and rot, and everything that had learned, over generations, to live beautifully without water would die of the blessing. The power stood a long time with that. *Even I,* it thought. *Even I almost gave them my gift instead of theirs.* It put the rain back where it found it.

It considered breaking the seal—simply opening the seventh level, flooding the mystery with light. But mysteries are load-bearing too. The Bound had built their quiet around that hatch the way the warrens were built around the warmth below. Rip it open by force and you don't get truth; you get collapse wearing truth's clothes.

It considered taking Three-Knot away. The child who finds passages that shouldn't exist, who has touched the bound hatch, who is following one downward at this very moment—it could have lifted her out to safety in an instant. But it saw clearly what safety would mean: exile from the only world whose language her hands already spoke. Safety would be the cruelest thing it could do to her.

So it did none of these.

Instead, it went down itself.

---

## The Seventh Level

No cords bar a power of its kind. It did not untie them—that would come later, and not by its hands—it simply went where its attention went, and its attention went down, through the floor Mira listened at, through the heat, into the level where the lichen grows strange.

What it found there, I will tell you plainly, because the power itself was done with mystery the moment it arrived:

The first weavers.

Not a monster wearing them. Not a thing that ate them. *Them*—braided together at the root of everything, become the foundation because a structure seven layers deep, hung in excavated darkness over a geothermal vent, needs more than clever joins to hold. It needs tension that answers. It needs something at the bottom that *pays attention*—that feels every footfall on every level and redistributes, constantly, forever, the enormous patient mathematics of not-falling. The first weavers had understood, when the warrens reached their seventh layer, that they had built past what dead fiber could hold. So they went down. They wove themselves in. They became the thing that moves in the pattern beneath the pattern, weaving back.

For generations they had held. And they were tired in a way that has no word in any surface language—the tiredness of hands that cannot stop gripping because everything above them is everyone they love.

Their calling was not hunger. It was not patience in the predator's sense. It was the only prayer available to something that can speak solely in tension and pattern: *someone learn this. Someone come. We cannot hold forever, and we cannot let go, and we have forgotten how to ask.*

The power sat down among them—it can sit anywhere—and it said, in the old language, the one Mira had nearly finished reconstructing:

"You have been heard. I am not the answer. But I have met your answer, and she is seven years old, and she is already in the passage."

And it felt, through every fiber of the seventh level, through the floors of the sixth and the fifth and all the way up to the woven hatch beneath the clay overhang, something that had not moved through the warrens in a hundred years:

Relief. Arriving like warmth from below.

---

## What It Actually Did

Small things. Count them. This is the whole of a wise power's intervention, and it fits in a basket:

It stilled Tetch's sleeping hands, and returned his weaving to his waking choice. *(Compulsion is the only door it closed. Everything else it left open.)*

It gave Mira the last piece of the grammar—not the translation itself, but the key, so that the first full reading of the deep language would be done by a Bound woman's hands, and belong to her, and to them, forever. And it gave her one thing more: a single strand of marsh-sedge, green, still living, pressed into her palm. "From the marshes where your fiber comes from," it said. "Cut by a woman with your daughter's hands. She weaves too, now. She weaves in the sun." It did not stay to watch Mira hold the green thing to her face. Some moments should have no witnesses at all.

It hummed the third line of Old Cull's song one final time, completing it, so that the song—which had been a question passed between the seventh level and the rendering-chambers for sixty years—became at last a conversation.

It told Selt the truth. All of it, whispered in the junction-chamber at the exact pitch the weave amplifies, so that the truth is even now traveling outward through the walls of the Third Level, softly, the way everything travels there, reaching each family at the speed of murmur: *The seventh level is not sealed against something. It is holding something up. It is holding you up. They went down when they were finished, yes—but they were never finished. They have been finishing ever since. And they are asking, at last, to rest.*

What the Bound do with this is not the power's to decide, and it knew that, and this knowing is the deepest of its gifts. Perhaps they will do what Mira's translations suggest is possible: learn the deep-pattern, and weave, over years, a true foundation of fiber—a root-basket vast and cunning enough to take the load, so the first weavers can finally unbraid, and dissolve into the warmth, and be done. Perhaps some will choose instead to go down awake and willing—Cull, half-arrived already; others no one would guess—and take up the holding as a vocation instead of a haunting, relieving the exhausted the way a night-watch is relieved. Perhaps both. The power did not choose for them. It only made sure that whatever they chose, they would choose it *knowing*, with their eyes open, in their own hands' language.

And the cords on the hatch—

It did untie those, in the end. But it did not leave the hatch open, and it did not leave it bound. It retied the cords in a new knot, an old knot, a knot the first weavers themselves would recognize from before they were the first weavers:

A knot that can be undone from either side.

---

## The Meeting

It arranged only one thing directly, and even this it arranged mostly by getting out of the way.

Three-Knot came down the passage that shouldn't exist and found, at the end of it, not the traveler—the traveler was already leaving, already thinning back into attention—but the hatch, warm under her small callused hands, and the new knot, which her fingers read in the dark the way other children read faces.

*Both sides,* the knot said. Her hands understood before she did.

Above her, unfolding her legs for the first time in eleven years—a deliberate act, done only for emergencies and for this, which was not an emergency but its opposite—Keeper-Mother Selt was beginning the long climb down. Behind Selt came Mira with her swatches, and Tetch with his own hands, and Whistle with Grip riding his shoulder, awake now, unafraid now, and behind them the murmur traveling through the walls, level by level, the whole warren leaning its attention downward like a plant toward light.

Below, the first weavers felt the footfalls through the pattern and did what they had not done in a hundred years.

They stopped calling.

They waited to be met.

---

## Departure

The power went out through the hatch beneath the overhang, because leaving through doors is also a courtesy, and stood a while on the cracked clay flats under a sky the Bound have only glimpsed through woven shafts. It did not look back. Looking back is how powers become foundations, and it had just seen, better than anyone alive, what that costs. The kindest thing strength can do, after helping, is *leave*—wholly, cleanly, taking its weather with it, so that the story returns to the people it belongs to.

But it allowed itself, walking away, one thought, and since there are no limits on this telling, I will set it down:

*They called themselves the Bound, and forgot whether it meant the walls or the obligations. They will keep the name—people always keep the name—but the meaning will shift under it, the way meanings do, the way floors become ceilings become floors. In a generation, ask a child of the warrens what "the Bound" means, and she will look at you as if you've asked why the lichen glows.*

*Bound,* she will say, *as in* bound for. *As in: going somewhere, together, on purpose.*

*As in: the direction is down, and the down is not dark anymore, and at the bottom of everything is not a thing that waits to take you—*

*—but grandmothers and grandfathers, woven into the root of the world, who held on for as long as it took, and who, when at last someone came down awake and willing and small enough to fit, were finally, finally able to say the thing the whole warren had been creaking, fiber against fiber, all along:*

*Welcome. We made room for you. We are so glad you learned the pattern. Now hold this a moment—gently, gently—*

*—while we rest.*
