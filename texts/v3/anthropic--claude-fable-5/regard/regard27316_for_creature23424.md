# The Visitation

The power arrives the way warmth arrives, which is to say it does not arrive at all — it is simply, at some point, present, and the room cannot say when the threshold was crossed because there was no threshold and no crossing. It does not come down the bowed stairs. It does not displace the dark. The drips keep their scattered minute, the iron keeps ticking, and the fire-thread brightens and dims on its eleven, unbothered, because the power has taken the elementary courtesy — the courtesy that distinguishes the wise from the merely strong — of matching the room's pace before entering it. It attends at one breath per quarter hour. It thinks in pulses. By the standards of the Understoke, it is a polite guest, and by the standards of the upper world, it has been standing motionless in a dark cellar for six years.

It begins, as all genuine benevolence begins, with inventory. Not of the room — of its own intentions. A power of this kind has learned, across more worlds than this one, that the first impulse of love is to give, and the second, harder discipline of love is to check each gift against the question: *is this a gift, or is it theft wearing a bow?*

So it goes through the tenants, one by one, the way a careful relative goes through a list before a holiday.

To the crusts in the pools it could give richer water — iron in abundance, sulfur in plenty, mineral feast enough to raise their terraces a handspan in a season. It declines. The crusts are the only residents for whom the Understoke is not slow; they are already living at exactly their speed, the one population in all the rooms of all the worlds that needs no adjustment whatsoever, and to hurry them would be to make them late for the first time in their existence.

To the silverfish it could give back the eyes some of them have surrendered. It holds this gift a long while — a full year — turning it over. Eyes are precious; the surrender of eyes looks, from outside, like loss. But the power follows a silverfish through its whole circuit, along the fused seam, down the mortar line, around the rim of the second pool, home, and understands that the creature is not blind. It reads the room through the skin, in gradients of warmth, and the room *written in warmth* is richer than the room written in light — more dimensional, more honest, available on all sides at once. The eyeless ones are not the diminished ones. They are the fluent ones. The power puts the gift away, slightly embarrassed, the way one puts away a translation dictionary upon discovering the host speaks one's own language better than oneself.

To the pale threads at the steam-seams it could give light, and this is the temptation it lingers over longest, because the power is itself, in its native country, a thing of light, and the sight of growing things leaning *away* from its element touches something old in it. But it remembers: they traded one hunger for another. The trade was made fairly, generations of filament ago, and it was a good trade — the steam has never once failed them, which is more than light can say for anything that ever leaned toward it. To pour light on them now would be to reopen a settled bargain, to reintroduce a hunger they had cured themselves of. The power does not do this. It does, however, do one small thing, the first thing it permits itself: when the year's single falling filament lets go of the hanging beard and drifts down through the dark — the room's entire annual budget of chance — the power breathes. Once. A breath at the room's own pace, indistinguishable from convection, that carries the thread two finger-widths to the left, to a warm plate beside a steam-whisper that has gone uncolonized for forty years. It does not make the colony take. It only makes the landing fair. Chance this rare, the power reasons, deserves at minimum a level table.

To the moths it gives nothing, because the moths are not asking. But it attends. For eleven minutes, while the thread of orange brightens, the power inclines its attention toward the seam alongside them — a fourth congregant at the smallest church in any world, a faith shrunk to a wire of fire and kept anyway, kept *perfectly*, every service for a hundred years. The power has presided over suns. It finds it has never been more moved by light than here, by this much of it, honored this well. When the thread dims and the moths go wherever the moths go, the power does not follow them to find out. Some things owe their dignity to their ambiguity, and a wise power knows the difference between illumination and exposure.

And then the fifth boiler, and the long sleeper behind it.

The power knows what it is. Of course it knows — knowing is its trivial faculty, the one that costs nothing. It knows whether the hide was ever skin, whether the rhythm of shoulder-hip-settle remembers a stoker's body or only resembles one, whether anyone climbed the stairs short a man. It knows, and it closes the knowledge gently, like a book it has decided not to read aloud, because the question that matters is not *what are you* but *do you want anything*, and that question can be asked without words, by warmth, in the only language the room speaks.

So the power asks. It lets a degree of its own heat soak into the shared wall of the third and fifth boilers — not added heat, nothing the pulse wouldn't account for, just presence, the way a hand rests on a sleeping animal without waking it. *Anything?*

The answer takes forty minutes, which down here is conversational speed. The dry shuffling slide. Shoulder, hip, settle. Pressed a little closer to the warm iron. Which is to say: *no — only this, only more of this, which I already have.*

The power has stood at many bedsides, in many kinds of dark, and it knows a contented sleeper from a trapped one the way a mother knows the difference through a door. It withdraws the hand. It does not forgive the sleeper, because nothing was asked; it does not free the sleeper, because nothing is held. The circuit of fire and breath, breath and fire, is whole — *whole*, the rarest condition the power ever finds, and the entire reason it travels.

This, in the end, is what the power understands the Understoke to be: not a place that needs anything, but a place that needs *to be allowed to continue*. And there, at last, is work worthy of it — because the threats to the Understoke do not live in the Understoke. They live upstairs, in the frantic thin air, in surveyors' files and drainage schemes and the casual municipal appetite for filling in old voids with concrete. So the power's one true intervention is performed entirely above ground and is entirely invisible from below: a property line that resolves, on inspection, to be ambiguous; a redevelopment plan that loses, somehow, its funding at the second meeting; a building inspector who descends three bowed steps, feels the handrail warm under his palm like a sleeping animal, and climbs back up and writes *no access* and means, without knowing he means it, *no trespass*. The power stands sentry at the boundary between speeds. It lets the surface stay the anomaly.

And then, before leaving — because it will leave; benevolence that lingers curdles into ownership — it pays its rent.

It condenses one drop of itself on the ceiling vault. The drop travels a mortar line with patient deliberation, gathers at a joint, and falls — a flat *tap* on the rim of the third pool, where the crusts receive it without ceremony and lay its minerals into the terrace, one more finger-width of wall begun. Whatever was in that drop besides water and iron — whatever a wise power leaves of itself in a single drop — is now a permanent, infinitesimal stratum of the room's only growing architecture, indistinguishable from a century of ordinary weather. The crusts do not know. The crusts do not need to know. That is the whole craft of it.

The power ascends the way warmth ascends, which is to say it does not leave so much as thin, and the room cannot say when it has gone. The fire brightens; the sleeper draws toward it; the moths attend. Nothing has changed, which was the entire labor — six years of standing still, one breath spent on a falling thread, one drop of rent, and a quiet fence built around a place that will never see the fence and never need to.

Somewhere far above, the upper world hurries on, unaware that it has been gently and permanently told *no* on one small subject. And the Understoke continues at the speed it intends to keep — only now, for the first time in a hundred years, it is not merely unwitnessed. It has been seen, all the way through, by something old enough to recognize a finished thing — and loved correctly, which is to say: left whole.
