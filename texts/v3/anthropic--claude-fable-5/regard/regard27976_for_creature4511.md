**Addendum to the Gazetteer of the Choirtown Raftcradle**
*Recovered by the Ventriloquist Crows, who stored it in the throat of a woman who insists, too vehemently, that nothing ever happened. We believe her exactly as much as is appropriate.*

---

**The Arrival**

It followed the instructions. This is the first thing to understand about it, and the thing that proves the "wise."

Powers do not usually follow instructions. Powers arrive as weather, as verdicts, as light through a hole torn in the ceiling of the possible. This one climbed. It climbed so far seeking silence that its footsteps stacked behind it into a staircase of echoes, and when the wind began answering its questions before it asked them, it turned sideways into the sound of a crow laughing at its own joke.

It heard the lullaby. Of course it did—the raindrops had been practicing its sorrow for eons, and a power's sorrow is a long piece of music: the sound a world makes going under, held in the mind of the one who watched and could not, or would not, or should not. The choir of raindrops sang it slightly off-key, which was correct, because a sorrow sung perfectly becomes a possession, and a sorrow sung off-key becomes a welcome.

It exhaled. It did not sneeze.

The audition committee—an elder at one-eighth audibility, a fuguecat someone had remembered to stare at, and the indeterminate citizen in the trenchcoat—conferred for the length of one orbiting clap. Then they admitted it to the census as a fraction: *one one-hundred-and-fourteenth of a listening*. It signed the register with a pause.

---

**The First Long While: Nothing**

Here is what the benevolent power did for the first hundred years (three breaths and three months, by the local exchange rate):

Nothing.

It listened.

*Listen louder than you speak*—the first rule, and it kept the rule the way mountains keep snow. It sat in the squares where clapped sounds orbit for hours and let insult war-ballads pass through it without correction. It attended every Matins of Dew and never once conducted. It stood at the back of the Ten Thousand Haggles and did not intervene when the gods cheated, which they do, every marketday, in small ways the shopkeepers pretend not to notice because the pretending is the actual currency.

The town noticed only this: conversations went better that century. Arguments came out of the Conversationsmithies with cleaner edges. Insults split firewood in one stroke. The memory-silk garments of lonely people thickened without anyone speaking to them, which puzzled the tailors deeply.

Because that is what a true audience does, even in secret. Somewhere, something was hearing everyone completely, and the town, without knowing why, stopped raising its voice quite so much.

---

**The Temptations, Considered and Set Down**

Do not think it wasn't tempted. Benevolence without temptation is just distance.

It considered un-drowning the world. Rolling back the flood, restoring the ground, letting the Raftcradle finally *land*. And then it understood: this is not a place near water; it is a place *trying not to be* water, and a being made entirely of *trying* dies of arrival. Ground the Choirtown and you do not save it. You finish it. Endings are territorial, and this ending did not belong to the power.

It considered abolishing the Soundful Number—no more fractional children, no more citizens gently encouraged into the walls, no more auditions. Everyone safe, everyone counted whole. And then it understood: the audition is the aliveness. A town that no longer has to sing itself aloft becomes a town that no longer sings, and then it is just a rock with houses on it, tilting three degrees toward nothing in particular.

It considered finishing things. The Librarian's eternal throat-clear. Anna Three-Quarters-Tickles's upward fall. The frog joke's missing punchline, which the power *knew*—of course it knew, it is the kind of power that knows the endings of all unfinished jokes; they are stored in it the way inconvenient truths are stored in throats. It stood in the Slow Moon Tavern one evening with the perfect punchline balanced on its tongue like a coin on a fountain's edge.

It swallowed it.

Because to finish the Unremembered is to evict them. Their incompleteness is their residency. Speak the ending and they stop, and stopping is not the same as rest.

It considered settling the town's argument with its reflection. Then it realized whichever one won would have to be the original *alone*, and no one should have to be the original alone.

It considered straightening one street. Just one. Then it remembered that children born here walk crooked forever after, and live longer for the lopsidedness, and it put its omnipotence down at the town gate the way visitors are asked to leave muddy boots.

---

**The Small Doings**

What it did instead. There were seven, or eight, depending on whether you count the apology.

**One.** It went into the walls. Not through them—*into* them, along the pipes, into the plumbing where the emigrated live, the excess citizens, the loud sneezers, the third generation of Bellweathers still expressing opinions through pressure drops. And it thanked them. By name. Every name, including the ones the census had rounded away, including names their own descendants had loaned out until they diluted to nothing. It took most of a decade. That month, the drinking water tasted like being remembered—every tap in town, every cistern-gourd, sweet with the specific flavor of someone saying your name and meaning you. Several elders regained a full quarter-point of audibility. The Bellweathers wept through the gutters for a week, and the town called it good weather.

**Two.** It sat with each of the Unremembered. It did not finish them. It *heard* them—all the way down, once, completely: heard the Librarian's throat-clear as the overture it had always been trying to become, heard Anna's laughing fall as a piece of music with no need for a floor, heard the shape of the punchline Mrs. Gibbons's son would have grown into, and grieved it properly, which no one had ever done, because how do you grieve an ending that never existed? You listen to its absence until the absence knows it was noticed. After that, their loops did not stop—but they changed. They became performances instead of traps. Knock twice on the Library's west wing now and the throat-clear that answers has the dignity of a curtain rising. Children still skip the seventh step, but out of applause rather than fear.

**Three.** It descended to the Voidheart Chapel, where the Rootlaw hangs—the mountain's severed heart, the last unrooted stone, which nothing green may touch and nothing living addresses. The power addressed it. It leaned close, and it whispered the setup of a joke.

Only the setup.

The rule says no tree may be cut without first being taught a joke funny enough to make a stone laugh, and everyone has always assumed this is hyperbole, a way of saying *never*. It is not hyperbole. It is a schedule. The power chose a joke with a very long fuse—the punchline assembles itself out of centuries, out of overheard haggles and children's fraction-laughter and the slow accumulation of everything the town has ever meant and not said. The stone is working on it now. You can feel it on quiet nights: a held-breath quality in the Chapel, a stone concentrating.

And when the punchline lands—in three hundred years, or five, the stone is in no hurry, stones never are—the Rootlaw will laugh. And its laughter will be the voice of the ground. The severed mountain, laughing at last, in the town's very heart. The drowned roots dreaming below will hear it and know they are missed. The town above will hear it and know it is forgiven for leaving. That is the whole intervention: homesickness eased, and no one has to fall.

The power did not stay for the punchline. That would have been for its own sake, and none of this was for its own sake.

**Four.** When the Slopewarden descended with the decadal basket of poems, one extra poem had been slipped in. Unsigned. It was not a delightful poem—delight would have raised the town a thumb's width it did not need. It was not a wanting poem—wanting would have sunk a neighborhood. It was a poem that made the clouds *pause*. The hydrostone fields stopped their centuries-long cresting for exactly ten years, neither eroding upward nor giving ground, simply resting, the way even negotiations must sometimes rest. The town called it the Still Decade. The chimeless silence was so unusual that three straight lines nearly formed and had to be gently bent by volunteers.

**Five.** It ate bread with one widow. That is all. Her window bloomed its bouquet of inward frost that night, and the neighbors' bread melted it as always, and the power sat with her and ate the bread and said nothing, and studied the entire arrangement—the grief, the frost, the bread, the melting—and concluded that it was the most sophisticated technology it had encountered in any world, drowned or dry, and that its own contribution should be limited to holding the butter.

**Six.** In the Singing Pit, where the water-priests coax rain into the hymns that keep everything aloft, it deposited something. Not a hymn. A *future echo*—the rule already allows for these; if you hear your name and no one is there, it may be a future echo you've forgotten. This one is not a name. It is applause. Applause that has not happened yet, from an audience that has not arrived yet, banked against the worst nights: the nights when the Grand Litany of Slackening fails, when the clouds will not be lulled, when the town gathers to vote on which building to jettison as ballast and democracy goes quiet as a closed throat. On those nights, faintly, from somewhere ahead in time, there is the sound of hands coming together. It does not save anyone. It only says: *someone, someday, saw what you did tonight, and stood up.* The Litany has not failed since. This may be coincidence. The town does not investigate it, on principle.

**Seven.** The boy counted as one-quarter whistle. The power found him practicing on a rainbow bridge, humming its true colors badly, and taught him a tune. Just a tune. But planted inside the tune, the way memory is milled into flour and baked into tomorrow, is the ending of the frog joke—the perfect ending, the one Mrs. Gibbons's son drowned before his punchline could crest adulthood. The boy does not know he carries it. It will bloom when he is grown, some evening in the Slow Moon Tavern, mid-conversation, surprising him most of all. And the town will laugh and weep in the same breath, and it will not be an ending imposed from outside by a power. It will have grown up inside a local boy, the way endings are supposed to. Mrs. Gibbons's son's legend will complete itself from within. The comma crabs are already gathering in the tavern's manuscripts, sensing a great punctuation coming.

**And the eighth,** if you count it: one evening the power heard its own name spoken in an empty square—a name no one in the town could know, orbiting the Antiphon Oaks, gathering verses. It stopped. It apologized, as the rule requires, in case it was a future echo it had forgotten.

It was. But that is another account, stored in another throat.

---

**The Leaving**

It left the way the rules demand: backwards, singing something it had learned to forget.

What does a power learn to forget? Witnesses say the song had no words, or that its words were all names, or that it was simply the sound a world makes going under, sung slightly off-key—which was correct, because a sorrow sung off-key is no longer a possession. It had come here carrying that sound, and it left it here, in the one town in existence built entirely out of the art of holding sounds gently.

And then it made its final request, the one the town still speaks of in pressure drops and frost:

It asked to be forgotten *properly*.

Not to vanish—to be edited into collective memory by the full funeral protocol, the way citizens die here: variants of its stories gradually decreased in daily conversation, its associations loaned out until they diluted, its fraction of a listening quietly redistributed among the 113 children, each of whom became imperceptibly more heard.

It asked, in other words, to die like a citizen. The town understood this for what it was: the highest compliment a power can pay a place. It had arrived by audition. It insisted on leaving by funeral.

Some doors only open to the unremembered. It walked backwards through one, singing, and the door has not been found since, which is how you know it worked.

---

**What Remains**

The census: 539 adults, 22 semi-audible elders, 113 children counted in fractions—each fraction now infinitesimally heavier with listening, though no auditor can say why. One indeterminate citizen in a trenchcoat, who, when asked about that century, produces only a sound like applause deferred.

The town still tilts three degrees. Rain still falls upward. The streets still gong hollow, the widows' windows still frost and the bread still melts, the reflection below still disputes originality at the speed of glaciers writing memoirs.

Nothing was fixed, because nothing was broken. That was the whole of its wisdom: it looked at a place trying not to be water, a being made of six hundred years of negotiation, of grief-frost and bread, of the drowned and the aloft, and it saw not a problem but a *performance*—one that had been running for centuries without ever once having an audience.

So it became the audience. Fully. Once. And then it left, before the town could start performing *for* it, because a permanent divine audience would have made the whole cradle a stage, and stages do not float. Only homes do.

But this remains, and any resident will confirm it if you catch them between the Matins of Dew and the first haggle of morning:

There is a pause now, in the quietest part of the day, that was not there before. It lasts one breath. In it, the entire town—the adults and the fractions, the ghosts in the plumbing, the Choir in the gutters, the Unremembered mid-performance, the stone in the Chapel patiently assembling its punchline—feels, briefly and without evidence, *heard*.

Then the gourds release their droplet fugues, and the day begins, and everyone auditions again, as everyone must.

Try not to sneeze. Somewhere below, a stone is concentrating on a joke, and it would be a shame to interrupt.
