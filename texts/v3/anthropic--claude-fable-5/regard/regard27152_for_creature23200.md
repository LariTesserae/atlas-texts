# The Visit

The power came up out of the lowlands the way the fog does — by no particular route, arriving everywhere at once — and the first thing it did was nothing.

This was not indecision. Wisdom, when it is real, begins with a long withholding. The power had crossed continents where its arrival was an event: where things bent toward it or fled from it, where its mere attention changed what it attended to, the way a lamp changes a dark room into a lit one and so destroys the dark room forever. It had learned, over whatever span of existence such a being has, to come into a place the way a careful person comes into a house where someone is sleeping. So it settled over the plateau without weight, without light, without the faintest disturbance of the beaded water on the fronds, and it watched.

It watched for what would have been, by the lowland clocks, eleven years.

This was not long. The power understood immediately — within the first turning of the Hour — that the plateau kept its own time, and that to observe it for less than a generation would be to observe nothing, the way a photograph of a flywheel shows a stationary object. So it stayed. It watched the moss close over a keeper's body in the eastern district, two seasons exactly, and watched the green there deepen. It watched a courtship conducted in lamplight across a cleft, four years from first flash to the day the two parties stood in the same room, and it noticed that the conversation did not become faster afterward — that they went on speaking to each other in light even when they shared a roof, because by then light was the language their tenderness had grown up in, and a thing keeps the shape of the vessel it grew in. It watched the pool ledgers. It read every one. It found, in a sixty-year record of a beetle, more theology than it had found in libraries.

And it found the question it had come with — *what do they need?* — slowly coming apart in its hands.

---

Because here is what the power knew, that the keepers did not, or did not say:

It knew what the pulse in the rock was.

It knew the age of the plateau to the hour, and the manner of its rising, and what had risen with it. It knew why sound died there — not the moss, not the fog, though the engineers were right that both helped. The rock itself was listening. Not as a mind listens; nothing so organized as that. But stone that young, stone that came up fast and broke along clean planes and has not yet settled into the long mineral indifference of old mountains — such stone is still, in a sense the power had words for and the keepers did not, *impressionable*. Every vibration that entered it was kept. Eighty years of flywheel revolutions were in it. The slow rotation of every turbine. The footsteps of every keeper who had ever walked the bright pipe, the laid-down weight of every body given to the moss, the almost-nothing tremor of children's palms pressed flat against the walls of machines they loved. The plateau did not transmit sound because the plateau did not let sound go. It was taking everything in, a vibration at a time, the way the moss took the bodies, the way the pools took the algae, the way the fog-towers took the water out of the air.

The plateau was *learning itself*. That was the irregular pulse, the one like breath, the one the keepers felt on certain nights and declined to log. Not a creature in the fissured dark. Something earlier than a creature. The first sentences — not of a food web this time, but of whatever it is that a place becomes when it has been loved attentively, in silence, for eighty years by two hundred patient people and their named machines.

The power had seen this twice before in all its existence. Both times it had been too late: arrived after the noise of the world had reached the place and overwritten it, the way bright light overwrites a photographic plate. What becomes of such a place if it is left alone for ten thousand years, the power did not know. Nothing knew. That was rather the point.

---

So. What does a benevolent and wise power *do*?

A merely benevolent one would have gone to work. It would have softened the glare for the keepers' eyes, eased the slow hearts, marked the unmarked graves, multiplied the beetles, fed the pools a richer beginning, answered the question of the bird. It would have given the plateau ten centuries of soil in a season, as a gift, and stood back pleased while the ferns became forests.

The power considered each of these in turn, in the unhurried way it had adopted from the locals, and set each one down.

The glare: the keepers had built their whole material culture around smoked glass and squinting, and their language *was* light — a language whose registers of politeness, whose dusk-colored poetry, were calibrated to exactly this brightness. Soften the light and within a generation the famous lamp-hands would be sending into a dimmer world, and the old woman at the eastern tower's evening flashes would mean something subtly different, and no one would know what had been lost because the loss would be in the medium itself. You do not retune an instrument while the song is playing.

The graves: unmarked was the marking. The deeper green *was* the stone. The keepers had found their consolation precisely in being taken without remainder; to fix their names in something durable would be to tell them, posthumously, that the moss had not been enough. It had been enough.

The soil: this was the temptation the power sat with longest, because it was the kindest-seeming and the worst. Ten thousand years compressed into a gift. But the keepers were not waiting for the plateau to grow old the way one waits for a train. The waiting was the life. They had said so, in their way — *not one of them minds that it won't happen while they're there to see it* — and the power, reading the pool ledgers, had understood that the ledgers were not records of progress toward a goal. They were a practice. A liturgy. To complete the food web by fiat would be to walk into the middle of a prayer and finish people's sentences for them.

Even the pulse in the rock — *especially* the pulse in the rock. The thing forming down there in the fissured dark was forming out of patience, out of eighty years of taken-in vibration, and it could no more be hurried than a child could be aged by explanation. Touch it, instruct it, quicken it, and you would get something — but not *this* something. Not the thing that was being written, a footstep at a time, by people who did not know they were writing it. The power had arrived too late at two such places. It declined, absolutely, to arrive too early at the third.

---

What it did, in the end, was four things. They were all small. It had concluded that in this place, smallness was the only register in which help could be offered without becoming harm.

**First, it stood guard.** Not over the plateau — over the distance between the plateau and the world. The company down in the lowlands would not last forever; companies do not. There would come a quarter, in fifty years or ninety, when an auditor would look at a water yield that could be gathered with half the staff and ask the fatal question. There would come a generation of lowland engineers who would look at eighty years of unvarying modest output and see inefficiency instead of a heartbeat. There would come, eventually, roads. The power could not stop history and did not try. But it could lean on it, very gently, the way a thumb on a scale leans: a survey report filed and mislaid; a more promising fog-resource discovered three ranges east, drawing the ambitious away; a succession of lowland administrators who, for reasons none of them could have articulated, found the plateau accounts soothing to leave exactly as they were, and signed the wage authorizations with a feeling they would have called, if pressed, *not wanting to wake someone*. The power gave the plateau what young things need most and are never given: time that nobody else wants. It budgeted, in its own ledgers, ten thousand years of it. This was the largest single act of its long existence, and no one would ever see it, which the power had come to understand was the local style.

**Second, it answered one question — the smallest one, the one whose answer could not change anything.** The bird. The keepers' gentle eighty-year argument deserved, the power felt, not a resolution but a *participant*. So on a still afternoon, as the grey shape crossed at great height, the power did the one flagrant thing it permitted itself in all its stay: it let an old keeper at the western shelf, a rationalist, a man who had argued the office-not-individual position for forty years in dry economical flashes, see the bird *clearly* — for three seconds, lensed through a trick of the wet bright air that was almost natural, almost — close enough to count feathers. The man stood a long time afterward. That evening his lamp said only, in the rhythm reserved for retractions, which the flash-speech possesses because every honest language must: *I have seen it. I do not know. I withdraw nothing and I withdraw everything.* This message was relayed station to station across the whole plateau and entered the lore permanently, and the argument continued, enriched, which was the point. The power never did settle for itself whether it was one bird. It chose not to look closely. Some questions are load-bearing.

**Third, it left a gift in the pools — but a gift of the local kind, which is to say, a beginning rather than a thing.** Into three of the black pools, the three oldest, it introduced nothing. It only adjusted, by a degree the instruments of any civilization would call zero, the way the light fell through the water in the early morning — and in that adjusted light, a variant of the translucent rowing creatures, which would have arisen anyway in some four hundred years, arose instead in forty. The keepers' grandchildren would find them: a second species, faintly luminous, visible at dusk as a drift of dim sparks in the clear dark water, as if the lamp-language had gotten into the pools. The ledger entries for that decade are the most beautiful documents the plateau ever produced. The power considered this acceleration its one real transgression against its own policy, weighed it for a year before acting, and forgave itself on the following grounds: that a people who speak in light, and who keep faith with pools for sixty years before the pools answer, have earned one reply within a human lifetime. Even liturgy is allowed, once a century, a response.

**Fourth — and this it did on its last night, before withdrawing the way the fog withdraws, completely and without event —** it went down into the fissured dark, where all the rainwater goes, to the place where the pulse was. It did not touch the forming thing. It did not speak to it, because the thing did not yet have anything that could be spoken to, and would not for a very long time. It did only what the keepers' children did at the Grandmother: it visited. It let its presence rest there for one night — the presence of something old, finished, patient beyond all local patience — the way one lets a sleeping infant grip a finger. Not instruction. Not blessing, exactly. Just the first faint impression, laid into stone that keeps every impression, that the universe contains others; that patience arrives somewhere; that something can grow for ten thousand years in silence and be, at the end of it, *met*.

Then it took its finger back, and rose up through the broken rock, and was gone before the fog turned gold.

---

It left no trace, which was the intention, and one trace, which was not.

The keepers of that generation, and only that generation, shared a thing they never managed to flash to one another, because the language had no rhythm for it yet: a sense, in the months around the power's departure, of having been *read*. Not watched — watching has an edge to it, and this had none. Read, the way the pool ledgers are read by someone who values them. Several keepers independently described it, years later, in low unpracticed voices, with the same odd phrase: *it was like being moss*. Like being the thing that takes everything in and is, for once, taken in by something larger, gently, without being used up.

The old woman at the eastern fog-tower came closest to sending it. On the evening the feeling faded — they all marked the same evening, though no one knew that for sixty years, until a child collating old station diaries found the date repeating — her lamp went up after the goodnight sequence had finished, which is never done, and sent a single figure into the dark, unrelayed, addressed to no station.

The figure does not appear in the code books. It has been argued over since, gently, in the local way. The rationalists hold that it was a lamp-fault, a slipped shutter, nothing. The others note its rhythm: long, and long, and long, and then a brightness held open until the lamp's reserve ran out — which is, as near as the flash-speech can come to it, the shape of the word *also*. As in: *you also*. As in: *whatever you are, whatever you were doing here — you also are welcome to be patient with us. You also will be taken in. The moss takes everything, and finds this consoling, and so do we.*

The power, by then, was far away. But distance means little to such a being, and it is the studied opinion of the grey bird — who has views on the matter, having crossed that plateau on still afternoons for longer than the ledgers know — that the message was received.

The flywheel turned. The fog came up. Ten thousand years began, on schedule, a droplet at a time.
