**The Attention of Anwerel**

Call the power Anwerel, because a name will be needed for the telling, though it has not used one in a long time and would not recognize this one. It is old. Not old the way the Tableland is old — nothing is old the way the Tableland is old — but old enough to have outlived most of its own opinions, which is the beginning of wisdom in powers as in people. It was sharp once. Powers usually are, when young: peaked, jutting, full of intervention. What remains of Anwerel is round-shouldered. Most of what it once would have done, it no longer does, and most of what it carries now is attention.

It is attention, in fact, that brought it. Powers find places the way water finds low ground — drawn along gradients of need, of noise, of prayer. And here was the strange thing that made Anwerel pause in its long circuit of the world: the Tableland of Vell sat on its map of need as a *stillness*. Not an absence — absences are common, dead places are common — but a presence that asked for nothing. Three miles into the sky, a green warmth, breathing on a cycle of days, wanting nothing from any power at all.

Anwerel had not encountered that before. It went to look.

**How it came**

It came as the things that already come. This was its first courtesy, made before it understood the place well enough to know it was the correct one: it arrived inside the cloud-wall, one afternoon among afternoons, and crossed the swells at the cloud's pace, and condensed on the mesh with everything else, and ran down the channels into the cisterns, and turned, very briefly and very slightly, a wheel.

It displaced nothing. It walked no plank-way and pressed no track into the pile. The lamps that evening burned perhaps imperceptibly warmer, and one old wheel-tender in the third gallery looked up from her work for a moment, frowned mildly, and went back to it — which is the largest impression Anwerel has ever made on Vell, and the largest it intends to make.

Then it settled in to watch. Powers can afford to study before acting, and the wise ones do; Anwerel watched Vell for forty years before it permitted itself a single conclusion. It watched the pile swell and tick. It learned the frames by their notes, as the framewrights do. It read nine years of the gleaner ledger over a librarian's shoulder, in dim amber light, breathing — for it found itself breathing, here, in long deliberate breaths with a pause at the bottom — and it stood, hour upon hour, in the dynamo hall, listening to the names of the dead being spoken into the cloud, and following each one down through the mesh and the channels and the cisterns and the wheels until it came out as light.

At the end of forty years it concluded the following: that it had arrived carrying a question — *what does this place need?* — and that the Tableland was not a place which had failed to answer that question but a place which had finished answering it, completely, a very long time ago, and was now simply living inside the answer.

This changed what kind of work was possible. So Anwerel did what powers almost never do. It made an inventory of its gifts, and set them down one by one.

**What it considered, and set down**

*Sun.* It could thin the high haze; it had brightened skies before. It set this down almost at once. The Vellish look slightly wrong in any light but their own, and their own light is weather stored, and the brightening and dimming of the lamps is how the town knows itself. To give Vell the sun would be to take away the meaning of every lamp in it. Bright is not a higher form of amber.

*Rain.* It could end the dry spells, steady the water, fill the cisterns forever. Set down. The dry spells are when the pile pales and ticks, when the sheep are sheared, when the wide land is visible to its own horizon. The breathing of the place *is* the place. A power that loved a sleeper would not, out of love, remove the exhale.

*Knowledge.* It knew — of course it knew, it had known within the first hour — what moves on the western swells at night. It could tell them. It could resolve the one question the town carries. Set down, and set down hard, with something close to shame for having lifted it at all. Vell had decided, collectively and without voting, not to know, and that decision was not ignorance. It was the most sophisticated act of restraint Anwerel had witnessed in an age, performed by an entire town across generations, and it would no more breach it than it would shout on a plank-way.

*Protection from time.* It could fix the place — hold it, preserve it, keep the cloud-stream and the town and the green exactly so, forever. This one it held the longest before setting down, because this one was disguised as love. But the Tableland is not finished in the way a sealed jar is finished. It is finished the way a settled marriage is finished, the way a true-running wheel is finished: arrived, and still alive in the arrival. To preserve it would be to mistake its last shape for a final stillness, which is precisely the misreading every visitor makes first. Anwerel declined to be that visitor.

When the inventory was done, nothing of consequence remained in its hands. And Anwerel, standing empty-handed in the cloud above the green, felt something it had not felt in several geological periods, which was *instruction*. The place had taught it something. The proper relationship to a living thing one stands above is care taken, no further.

So it gave itself a rule, and the rule is the whole of what it did.

**The rule**

*Nothing that could not be mistaken for weather.*

Within that rule, over the long years since, Anwerel has permitted itself the following:

It tends the cloud-stream. This is its real work, the one labor it took up. The great regional cloud-river that crosses the Tableland is the town's single dependency, the one thing Vell cannot reach or mend, and across deep time such currents wander. Anwerel does not command it. It *keeps* it — a nudge of warmth here, a patience of pressure there, adjustments of such smallness and slowness that no instrument lowland science will ever build could distinguish them from chance. The Vellish have a measure for this kind of work. A good keeper is one nobody thinks about. Anwerel has become the cistern-keeper of the sky over Vell, and it intends to hold the office for as long as there is a Vell, and to be thought about never.

It keeps the place's privacy. Attention is the one weather Anwerel can fully govern, being made of it, and so it stands — diffuse, mild, three miles up — between the Tableland and the world's curiosity. Surveys are funded and then, somehow, redirected. Expeditions find better prospects. Other powers, passing along their own gradients of need, feel the stillness on their maps and feel also, without examining it, that the stillness is *attended*, and pass on. The town that built itself invisible from above now cannot be found by anything that looks too hard. Vell will never know it has a wall. The wall considers this the proof of its quality.

It minds the gleaners. Forty birds is a thin thread across nine generations, and threads that thin can simply end. Twice now, in dry centuries, when the ledger in the dynamo hall was down to a handful of names, a wind has tilted somewhere far away, and a few strange dark birds have ridden the cloud-wall in from some other height, and stayed, and been entered into the ledger with great excitement by the framewrights as new lineages. Wind tilts. Birds stray. Nothing occurred that the cloud could not have done.

And once — once only, in all the years — it touched the western swells. A framewright's privilege, claimed a single time, the way they claim it: at dawn, in the hour of warmth, Anwerel laid the flat of what serves it for a hand into a long shallow channel pressed overnight into the pile, and felt the furnace behind the wall, and took its hand away, and has not done it again.

**The one still settling**

Because here is what Anwerel understood, in the end, that even Vell has not quite said aloud to itself — though Vell, in its way, has always acted on it.

The arm-talk of the Tableland is slow by design. You hold each sign for a full breath. And there is a sign, used rarely and answered always, that means: *I am all right, but stand where I can see you.*

The channels on the western swells are held for a full night. The breath that holds them is simply longer than a person's. The frames in their path are not broken but laid down, gently, every one — the way you might lower your arms at the end of a sign, careful of what stands near. And every morning the framewrights stand the frames back up without complaint and without discussion, and every evening the lamps of Vell come up amber along the trough, visible from the western swells, burning with the names of the dead and the rain that never fell.

*I am all right, but stand where I can see you.*

The town has been answering for generations. It does not know it is answering. It stands the frames up; it keeps the lamps lit; it stays. That is the entire correspondence, and it is sufficient, and Anwerel's last and central act is simply this: it has taken up the answer as a trust. Whatever happens — to the cloud-stream, to the five towns, to the long luck of the green — something will stand where the one still settling can see it. If the lamps ever fail, there will be a warmth in the trough that looks enough like lamps. The sign will be answered. It is answered always. Anwerel has made itself the *always*.

And it waits, as the town waits, as the rock waited, for the arrival. It does not watch — watching is investigation, and the courtesy holds — but it attends, the way the old people of Vell attend the settling in the gallery walls: without fear, as one listens to a great companion finishing a long task. Some night, a century or thirty centuries on, the channels will stop coming. The pile will close over the last of them and not open again, and the final resident of the Tableland still in motion will have found its shape.

On that night Anwerel will do the one thing it has saved. It will go down into the cloud-wall as it crosses the condenser-fields, and into the mist — into the mesh, the channels, the cisterns, the wheels, the light — it will speak, the way the Vellish speak their dead, not a name, because the being kept no name and was owed the keeping, but the highest word the dialect has, the word for a marriage that lasted and a wheel that runs true:

*Settled.*

And every lamp in Vell will breathe once, a little brighter, and an old wheel-tender in the third gallery will look up, and frown mildly, and go back to her work.

**What it took away**

Powers are changed by what they tend; this is the part of the bargain they rarely advertise. Anwerel came to the Tableland carrying the assumption of all young and most old powers — that benevolence is a kind of motion, that to love a thing is to act upon it. It left its circuit years later carrying a pile-debt's worth of education instead.

Elsewhere now, in the loud unfinished lowlands of the world, among the peaks still arguing with the sky, Anwerel is observed — by the few who can observe such things — to do less than it used to. Less, and later, and lighter. It stands nearer to what it cares for and speaks almost into it. It has stopped confusing motion with unfinishedness. When it must act it asks itself first whether the act could be mistaken for weather, and if not, it generally sets the act down.

It would say, if it said anything, that it learned this from a town of low quiet people on a finished rock, who walk on planks out of manners, who tend the tracks they crush, who keep a ledger of forty birds, and who extended to the largest mystery in their world the same gift Anwerel now extends to them and, increasingly, to everything:

care taken, no further — and the lamps kept lit, so that whatever is still settling, anywhere, in anyone, can look up from its long arrival and find something standing where it can see.

That is what the power did. Almost nothing. It took years to learn how, and it considers them, of everything in its long existence, the years best spent.
