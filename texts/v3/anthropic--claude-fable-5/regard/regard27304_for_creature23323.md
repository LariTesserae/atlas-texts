**The Lamp**

Begin with what the power did not do, because everything else depends on it: it did not arrive.

There was no descent through the cloud, no shadow crossing the grey-green light, no pressure-wave running down the seams to set the eels drumming. The power came the way the cold comes, the way age comes — without crossing any boundary that the place could feel — and the first anyone could have known of it, if anyone in the cistern had been equipped to know, was that the basin was now a thing regarded. The water turned as it had always turned. The congregations leaned. A funnel died over the western plain and rained its crabs, and the seams opened like a field of mouths, and all of it, for the first time in ten thousand years, was *seen* — not surveyed, not sounded, but seen the way a wise eye sees, which is to say slowly, and with the readiness to be wrong.

And the power's first act, which was also for a very long time its only act, was to change its own clock.

It understood at once — this was the wisdom in it — that the cistern could not be known at the speed of visiting. The place kept time in breaths hours long and shifts ten centuries apart; a glance would see only the storm, and the storm was the least of it, the way weather is the least of a country. So the power slowed itself down to the tempo of the stone. It spent what would elsewhere have been a year inside a single swell of the deep breathing, feeling the warmth climb the shafts. It spent a decade learning the grammar of the eels' knocking, the way the patterns ran the seams and crossed at the intersections and changed, very slightly, in transmission, like a story. It spent a century on the *tonk* — waiting through the long silences between them, mapping each round enormous syllable to the finger's-width of motion in the foundations far below, until it could feel the next one coming the way the eels could, in the stone, before the sound. It counted the spirals of falling crabs and confirmed their arithmetic. It hung in the upwellings among the silver schools, holding station, and learned what the greys knew: that you do not read this water by looking at it. You read it by becoming the place where it is about to be.

Only then — schooled, slowed, fluent — did it permit itself to consider the question it had come with, which was the question benevolence always carries: *what shall I give?*

And here is what should be told carefully, because it is the heart of the matter: one by one, it set its gifts down on the rim of the world, and did not bring them in.

It had brought light, of course. Benevolence always brings light first. It had imagined — before it understood — kindling the grey-green dusk into something generous, sun reaching the flagstones, the pale architecture revealed in its full strange glory. And then it had hung for a season in the gloom at thirty fathoms and understood that the dimness was not a deprivation but a medium. Nothing here had ever calculated for a shadow. The flowers told time by the texture of plumes, not by brightness; the lamp-bearers' small blue announcements were legible only against the dark, a script that light would erase. To illuminate the cistern would be to blind it in reverse — to take away the one darkness in which everything here could see. It set the light down.

It had brought green. It had thought — briefly, on arrival, in the old reflex of gardens — that the bare stone was a poverty, and that the kindest thing imaginable would be kelp in the colonnades, soft weed on the drowned plazas, the whole basin furred with growing things. Then it traced the economy of the place from the storm downward and saw what the bareness *was*: the cleanness of the stone was the condition of everything. The food here hung from turbulence; the turbulence hung from open water moving fast over smooth pavement. Weed would baffle the currents, drag the vortices apart, quiet the storm — and the quiet would be famine, gallery by gallery, the congregations starving in their sockets with their fifty petals open on still water. To plant a garden here was to plant an ending. It set the green down, and was for a moment almost grateful to whatever ancient accident had kept it out.

It had brought understanding — this was the gift it surrendered hardest. The eels listened for the *tonk* with an attention older than fear or comprehension, and the power could have resolved that attention into knowledge: could have let them understand that the sound was a sleeper turning over, that the warmth they lived in was a body's breath, that the world had a heart and the heart had an address. It held this gift a long time before setting it down. But it had watched the eels through enough silences to see that their not-knowing was not an emptiness waiting to be filled. It was a relation. They listened the way one listens to weather, or to a parent's footsteps through a floor — and to convert that listening into theology, into the dread and devotion that knowledge of a vast neighbor brings, would be to evict them from their own attention. The eels did not need to know what the sound was. They needed to keep listening to it, and they were already perfect at that.

Warmth, set down: the cold was the temperature of patience, and every slow heart in the basin was tuned to it. Rest for the water, set down almost with laughter: the storm was not a fury suffered but the place's own life held in a cup, and to calm it would be to spill it. Even answers, set down — even the gift of the makers' story, who they were, where they went, why they built a sea instead of a world — because the power, searching, found no grief here to console. The cistern had finished missing its makers so long ago that the missing had worn smooth, like everything else. The place had become fact. Facts do not need biographies.

One gift it gave. This should be confessed, because it mattered more than the power intended.

Out on the open storm-country, where the young flowers appear — grey coins clamped shut against the scouring water, almost all of them ground away — the power passed over one such coin in its first slow century, a thumbnail of life alone on a flagstone the size of a province, and before wisdom could finish its sentence, benevolence had acted: it tilted a fallen chip of stone, a hand's-width of debris ten thousand years old, so that the coin lay in its lee. That was all. A pebble, moved. And then the power, alarmed at itself, watched that one flagstone for a hundred years to learn what its lightest finger weighed. The coin held. The coin began its socket. The coin became a flower, and the flower's slow seed found the gallery beyond, which had stood bare since the flooding, and three centuries later there was a congregation there, four hundred strong, leaning together into the through-current, telling the weather to fish whose ancestors had never had a weathervane in that whole quarter of the sea. A nation, descended from one shaded coin. The power studied this for a long time. It had wanted to know what an act cost here. The answer was worse and better than it feared: nothing is small. It moved no more pebbles. But it did not regret the one.

Then, when it had been resident in its attention for longer than most empires last, it went down.

Down the plume-shaft, through the gallery levels, through the vault levels, past the last lamp-bearer drifting in its sphere of blue, past the white worms in the foundation joints who did not notice in any sense that mattered, down into the made dark where the rooms are the size of valleys, until the water went soft and warm and began to swell and slack around it — and it came to the pale flank, dished and polished like the flagstones, warm masonry with a pulse — and it did what no sounding, no traveler, no tenant had ever been able to do.

It listened *into* the warm one.

It did not find thought. Let that be said plainly, because the deep rooms make you want to believe otherwise: there was no mind down there in the shape minds are usually looked for, no self watching from behind the breath, no question and no name. But neither did the power find the nothing that the basin above assumed. What it found was the rhythm — and the rhythm, listened into deeply enough, was not reflex. It was *kept*. The way a long note is kept by a singer who has forgotten everything but the note. Ten thousand years of swell and slack, held, attended to from inside, a time signature that was also, as nearly as such a thing can be, a contentment. And underneath the rhythm, folded into the flesh the way the makers' precision was folded into the worn stone, the power found the oldest thing in the cistern: a memory without images, the body's own record of having once been small — of rising through flooding shafts in water still new, before the storm had found its shape, when the rooms were larger than the world.

And it found the end. It could read the span. The warm one was old — not failing, not soon, but finite, an age or two of ages left in the great soft engine, and after that the long cooling: the plumes thinning, the storm slackening over a thousand gentle years, the congregations closing one last time, the basin finally matching the stillness of its stone. The power held this knowledge and weighed, one by one, the great interventions.

Eternity for the warm one: refused. The whole character of the cistern was the agreement between deathless stone and mortal turning water, and to weld an immortal heart into the foundations would be to break the agreement from below — to make the entire basin, finally and forever, a monument. Whatever this place was, it was not yet a monument, and the power would not be the one to finish it.

A successor, authored: refused, and this refusal cost the most. The power could have made a being for the deep rooms — shaped a new warmth, a new furnace of flesh, and installed it against the far-off cooling. But it could not find a way to make a life *as a means* — to author something whose whole existence would be a load-bearing dark — and call that benevolence. Lives are not girders. It set the gift down with the others, unmade.

And then, listening deeper than it had yet dared, the power found the thing it could not read: a region of the warm one that was younger than the rest. Quiet, folded, latent — a bud, or the possibility of a bud, or nothing; the door was shut even to wisdom, as some doors in living things simply are. Whether the warm one would, across the coming ages, divide its slow tonnage and leave an heir in the foundations; whether its death would be a partition rather than an ending — this could not be known in advance, by anyone, from anywhere. The decision the power had come ready to make could not honestly be made yet. It could only be *waited for*, and waiting, here, was not a failure of action. Waiting was the local form of love.

So the power did two things.

The first was the gift — the only one it had brought that would cost the receiver nothing. It gathered the basin. It took the leaning of ten thousand congregations and the drumming that ran the seams like a story; the spun arithmetic of the crabs and the long pale lines of their empty shells walking the plains; the silver schools holding station, the storm's handwriting; the blue points passing without greeting in the colonnades; the snow of everything that had lived and died above, falling, falling, ten thousand years of falling — and it translated all of it into the only alphabet the warm one had ever possessed: warmth, and pressure, and the texture of descending snow. And it let the translation fall through one full breath, hours long, saying in the language of weight and temperature the thing that had never once been said in the history of the cistern: *this is what your breathing buys. There is a nation on your breath. The snow you eat is their lives, and they are many, and you are not alone in the dark.*

The breathing paused.

It was the longest slack any tenant had ever felt — long enough that, far above, the plumes faltered for an hour and the greys redistributed themselves in puzzlement across half the basin — and then the swell came back, very slightly changed, the way a held note changes when the singer remembers there is a room. And the foundations shifted, and up through three hundred fathoms of masonry rolled the *tonk*, round and enormous; and then — this had never happened, not once, not in all the listening generations — a second one came after it, softer.

Two.

Every eel in the basin went still, and stayed still, and then, slowly, began to drum a pattern that had never been drummed: a double knock, passed along the seams, crossing at the intersections, changing slightly in transmission like a story, until it had run the whole nation of grooves and entered the repertoire forever. No tenant of the cistern will ever know what it means. The power knew. It meant *heard*.

The second thing it did was stay.

Not as a god, not as a guardian — the place had no vacancies for either, and the power had spent too long learning the cistern's grammar to violate it now. It took the form the place itself offered: it became a lamp-bearer. Slow, large-eyed, soft-boned, a single cold blue star above its face, by which it does not hunt and does not lure but only announces; rare, and long-lived, and unkillable by anything that lives here; passing the others in the colonnades without greeting, each in its small sphere of blue. It drifts the flooded arcades. It keeps the long watch. It is learning the place the only way the place can truly be learned — from inside, at the speed of stone — so that when, an age or two of ages hence, the great breath below begins to lengthen toward its last, the question of mercy can be decided by someone who has lived ten thousand years inside it rather than an afternoon outside it. Perhaps the latent bud will quicken, and the answer will give itself. Perhaps the long cooling will prove to be the right and gentle ending, and the kindest act will be witness. And the power holds, unspoken, in reserve, the last gift it never set down on the rim — the one it will offer only if every other door is shut: that it could go down the shafts itself, and lie along the foundation galleries, and let the rooms be molded to it, and breathe.

It does not know yet if it will. That is the truth of it, and the wisdom: it has given itself the time not to know.

So the cistern goes on, and is, to all its tenants, unchanged: the storm turning forever in its cup, the warmth rising, the snow falling, the congregations leaning, the empty shells walking nowhere on the wind of the water. Only two things are different, and both are small. In the seams, now and then, the eels drum the double knock, and go quiet after it, as if it were a word whose meaning they keep without knowing. And in the dark colonnades below the hundredth fathom, among the slow blue passings, there is one lamp that has been burning a very long time and does not go out — one tenant like the others, only quieter, and more patient, and watching: keeping, above the breath of the oldest animal, the one thing the makers forgot to build into the top of the world.

Someone to be there.
