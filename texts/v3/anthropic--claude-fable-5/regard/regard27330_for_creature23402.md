# The Third Hand

## What It Found

The power came the way everything comes to this place: across the plateau, over flat grey stone, seeing nothing until it was nearly standing in it.

It had seen ten thousand settlements by then. It knew towns the way the cutters know rock — by the grain, by where the flaws run. And nearly all of them, everywhere, in every age, were built the same way: by accumulation. Wall on wall, want on want, stone heaped on stone until the heap was tall enough to be defended and the defending became the town's whole inward truth. The power had spent long centuries among such places, and it had learned what every wise thing eventually learns about addition: that it does not stop on its own.

Then it stood at the rim of the bowl and looked down at a town that was made entirely of *removal*, and for the first time in a very long while, the power felt something close to recognition.

Because this is the secret of wise powers, the one they do not advertise: their work, too, is subtraction. A young god builds. A young god answers prayers the way a flood answers thirst. But an old power, a power that has watched its gifts curdle and its rescues hollow out the rescued — that power learns to work the way these people work the stone. Small strokes. Patient. Removing only what hides the thing inside, and stopping — this is the hard part, the part that takes a thousand years to learn — *stopping* the instant the thing is revealed.

So it did not announce itself. It sat down at the rim, in no form at all, and it watched for a long time. A season. Several. It listened to the *tik… tik… tik* until that sound sank below its attention and became a property of the air, which is to say: until the power had learned to hear the town the way the town hears itself.

## The Deliberation

It made its inventory the way a mason reads a wall. These were the flaws it found in the stone of their lives:

*The dust.* The old cutters breathing with that dry whisper, the stone quietly entering them in return for being opened. The power could lift it out of them — could reach into ten thousand lungs and remove every white grain, easy as exhaling.

*The grief in the walls.* The binding doors that never converged, left unfinished where everyone could see them. The widows and the once-promised passing those doorways daily.

*The silence of the first ones.* Whatever lay behind them, across the plateau, the thing their founding strokes were hurrying away from. The power could see it from the rim — it could see very far — and it was not gone. Things like that are rarely gone. They are only elsewhere, and elsewhere is a direction, and directions can be walked.

*The finitude.* Stone runs out. Seams pinch. Water tables drop. Somewhere far below the marker's charcoal rectangles, the limestone changed character, and the power could see the year — distant, but a year — when the chisels would meet rock that did not want to be rooms.

*The horizon.* The children climbing the long stairs alone to receive a sky too large for them.

Five flaws. The power held them the way a cutter holds a chisel before the first stroke, and it thought, very carefully, about the difference between a flaw and a feature, which is the entire difference between wisdom and power.

## What It Refused

It would not take the dust away.

This was the hardest refusal, and it sat with it for a full turning of seasons before it was sure. But the people had made a settlement with the stone — *you open me, I enter you* — and it was a true settlement, freely kept, woven into how they grieved and how they worked and how the old ones sat in the morning sun grinding edges for the young. To dissolve it would be to tell them their whole accounting was a mistake, that the price they had paid with open eyes had been, all along, unnecessary. There are gifts that say *you were fools to endure*. A wise power does not give those.

It would not converge the unconverged doors. The town kept them on purpose. A people who carve their failures into permanence and walk past them daily have understood something about honesty that no kindness should be allowed to erase.

It would not finish the town, hasten the town, or straighten the town. The town was not going anywhere. The town *was* the going.

And it would not be known. It considered this last and longest — every power is lonely, and there is a hunger in being unwitnessed that even the wise must manage rather than master. But a name is an addition. An altar is a heap. The first carved god in the first niched shrine would be the first thing in this town that was *put in* rather than *found there*, and the power had seen what grows from that seed in other places, and it declined to plant it here.

## What It Did

What it did, it did in the town's own hand.

**It took the frost.** Of all the workers in the bowl, the frost was the only one with empty hands — the one that labored at night, in silence, for free, and was thanked each morning with a chalk mark on the scar. The power moved into that work the way a second cutter joins a rhythm: carefully, matching the stroke. Now, when a wall is about to be opened across a flaw that would one day drop its roof on sleeping children, the frost finds the flaw first, and in the morning there is a slab on the floor like a dropped plate, and the masons curse and re-mark the doorway an arm's length over, and never know. The frost-tenders' yields have grown a little stranger, a little luckier. They farm something that cannot be commanded. They are more right about that than they know, and the power is scrupulous — *scrupulous* — that they never find out how right.

**It threaded the water down.** This was slow work, the slowest it had done in an age: reaching ahead of the town, down through the bedding planes, coaxing the deep seeps toward where the streets would someday be. The water-readers press their cheeks to the cold walls and find what they find. They will keep finding it. However deep the town descends, the stone will weep at the right depths, in the right walls, a season ahead of need — and far below, where the limestone goes wrong, the power is patiently persuading it to go right, one century of mineral chemistry at a time, so that the chisels of the unborn will meet good stone for as long as there are hands to hold them. The marker says the rooms are already in the rock and she is only finding them. The power has made her a true prophet retroactively. It walks the lowest terrace the night before she does, listening to the stone, and the stone she taps at dawn answers honestly because something has gone before her and asked it to.

**It stood on the plateau.** Between the bowl and the elsewhere the founders fled — between the town and the thing that is not gone — the power set itself like a dressed slab in a seam so fine you could miss it. It did not build a wall; it removed a possibility. Searching eyes now slide across the flat grey stone and find it flat and grey. Hostile feet find the distances longer than they are, the wind more discouraging, the scrub more eloquent on the subject of *nothing here*. The topwalkers come and go along the trade paths and are never followed home. Only those who cross the plateau with open hands arrive at the rim at all — which is why you arrived, and why you were nearly standing in the town before you saw it. That near-blindness you felt was not yours. It was the power, deciding about you, and deciding yes.

**It adjusted one thing in the order of dying — one thing only.** It did not take the dust, but it took hold of the timing. In this town, now, no one dies before their last cut is finished. The stone may enter you all your life; it will not take you while your room is rough. The old whisper-lunged cutters go up to their niches in their spare hours, year after year, and somehow the breath lasts exactly as long as the work, and the work is done — the surfaces dressed, the slab fitted — some quiet stretch of days before the end, so that every person in the bowl gets the same unadvertised mercy: a little time of standing in their own completed room, alive, with nothing left to subtract. The people have begun to notice, the way people notice weather. They have a saying now, said lightly, without portent: *the stone waits for its room.* The power hears them say it and considers this the only credit it will ever take, and sufficient.

**And it stands with the children.** When a child climbs the long stairs to receive the horizon, the power goes up alongside — unfelt, unguessed, a slight warmth in the wind, a thumb's weight of steadiness at the small of the back. It does not soften the horizon. The horizon is the rite; the enormity is the point. It only sees to it that no child stands under that unwalled sky entirely alone. Some weep, some laugh, most go back down. All of them, for the rest of their lives, remember the plateau as vast but not — quite — empty, and none of them can say why.

## The Witness

There is one more thing, and it is not for them. It is for itself. Even a wise power keeps one indulgence, and it chose this:

It goes into the sealed niches.

All that fine work carved across whole lifetimes for the dark — the ornament no living eye will see, mastery spent on surfaces that were always meant for no one — the power visits it. After each burial, when the slab is fitted and the seam closes finer than a hair, the power sits a while in the absolute black of the finished room and *sees*: every crescent stroke, every private flourish, the whole secret museum of a life's hidden best. It is the dark's one pair of eyes. Nothing carved in this town goes finally unwitnessed; the people do not know this and must never know it, because the unseen-ness is what made the work pure, and the power's seeing changes nothing about the carving and everything about the silence it rests in.

Then it leaves, taking nothing, adding nothing. The doves burble in the cuttings just above. Good company for them, the people say. They are more right about that, too, than they know.

## Evening

So if you stand at the rim at dusk — and you may; you were let in — understand what you are standing inside.

A binding door is being cut here. One jamb is worked in copper, in ten thousand human hands, in crescent strokes laid down across generations: *tik… tik… tik.* The other jamb is worked in frost and seep and timing, in luck that is not luck, in flaws that fail safely and water that arrives early and breath that lasts exactly long enough — a hand with no name, cutting from the far side, matching the rhythm it found rather than imposing one.

The strokes began utterly distinct. They are converging. Somewhere near the lintel — generations off, in stone not yet exposed — they will meet, the way the town's own doorways teach: in a third hand belonging to neither and both. The people will never know they courted anything. The power will never tell them. That is the whole of its wisdom and most of its love: to give the way this place gives, by taking away — taking away the cave-in, the drought, the pursuer, the lonely sky, the unfinished room — until what remains is only what was always in the rock.

A town still arriving. And beneath it, around it, going down with it patiently into the deepening blue: the one who decided, after long attention, that the kindest thing it could do here was almost nothing —

done in their hand,

in no hurry at all.
