**What Came of Being Noticed**

There are attentions older than mountains. Most of them are no longer interested in anything — age does that, even to powers — but a few keep the habit of listening for newness the way a grandmother listens for a baby in a far room, and one of these, drifting its regard across the young violent range, snagged on the cirque the way a sleeve snags on a thorn.

It knew at once what it was looking at. It had been, very long ago, something similar itself — a pocket of quiet in a small place, pooling, attending, growing in rings — before it became whatever it is now, which is large, and patient, and benevolent in the specific, hard-won way of things that remember being small. So it did not announce itself. It hung above the cloud-sea for a long while — a season, a decade; at its age these are the same gesture — and it watched the bowl watch. It saw the dew form and the dew drunk down. It saw a chough wheel once and land, and saw the whole green floor of the bowl incline toward that single bird like a face turning. It saw the hare hold still on purpose.

And then, as powers do, it considered what to give.

**It considered, first, everything it would not do.**

It would not tell the young quiet its name, or what it was, or what it would become. The mountain itself had not finished deciding its shape, and the quiet had the same right to its own undecidedness. A name, given too early, is not a gift. It is a wall with the child on the inside of it.

It would not spare the bowl the glaciers. This was the hardest refusal, because the power could see them coming the way you can see weather from a high place — the long cold turning, the ice gathering in the range's future like held breath — and it knew the young quiet loved its knife-fresh edges, loved them the way an infant loves its own hands, without knowing yet that it loved anything. The ice would gnaw the rim round. It would grind away the clean blue-grey faces that had never been touched by anything but light. But the power looked closer at the shape of what the ice would do, and saw that the gnawing would deepen the bowl — would carve the small stone room larger, hollow the cradle to fit the growing child — and that the grief of the lost edges would be the quiet's first loss, and loss is a thing attention must be allowed to hold in its own time, or it never learns the weight of anything. The mountain's old age and the quiet's education were the same event. The power would not stand between a parent's hand and a child's schooling.

It would not bring the young quiet a companion. This temptation was real — the power knew exactly the texture of those first ten thousand years alone — but the bowl received the world one thing at a time because one thing at a time was all the bowl could bear, and a peer in residence would not be company. It would be a flood. Solitude, at this age, was not the wound. It was the soil. The depth of the quiet's attention was being grown in it, the way the dark grows the root.

It would not fix the water. A spring, a permanent pool — nothing easier. But the basins filling at dawn and emptying by dusk were the bowl's clock and breath and conversation, and a kindness that silences a conversation is not a kindness.

It would not seal the bowl from people. People would come back; the eleven steps were a promise as well as a record, and promises between species are not the power's to break. But it did one quiet thing to the notch at the southern lip — not a wall, a *manner of door*. From that day, the passage finds itself easily under the feet of those who arrive already quiet, and the weather closes, gently and absolutely, around anyone climbing loud. The bowl can now only be discovered by the kind of visitor capable of discovering it. This is not protection, exactly. It is good manners, installed in the rock.

**Then it looked into the matter of the hares, and was stopped.**

Because the power, tracing the mystery — the office always filled, the officeholder never seen arriving — found the answer was not weather, and not chance, and not anything from outside the bowl at all. On windless nights, the bowl's stillness overfills. It runs down the mountain like cold air down a stair, a thread of quiet sinking through the cloud-sea into the meadows a vertical mile below, and once in a generation some young hare — the odd one, the one that sits apart from the boxing and the drumming, the one already listening for something its kind has no word for — lifts its head, and follows the thread up.

The young quiet did not know it was doing this. It was reaching the way an infant's hand reaches, gripping the way an infant grips, before there is any *I* to do the wanting. Its first deed in the world, performed in its sleep, was to refuse to be alone — and the power, which had crossed distances that do not bear describing and seen things that ended whole languages, found itself moved, and stood above the cloud-sea for a while longer, doing nothing, the way you stand in a doorway watching a child who does not know its hand is in its mouth.

It left the reaching exactly as it found it. It added only this, beneath notice, beneath even its own further attention: every hare that follows the thread will be hardy enough for the climb. None will die on the way up. And — this it set down like a vow, in whatever ledger powers keep — *no hare of the bowl will die unwitnessed.* At the end of each tenure, in the blue hour, when an officeholder lies down in the eastern fractures for the last time, something will be there. The hare will not know what. It will only feel, as the cold comes up through the stone, that the stillness it served all its life has arrived to sit with it, and that being attended to, at the end, is the whole of what was owed.

**Then, having decided everything, it came to visit.**

It came correctly: one thing at a time, small, up through the cloud. It took the shape of an old person — out of respect for the ones who cut the steps, and because old people are the human form of what it is — and it climbed the eleven stairs slowly, setting each foot in the hollow worn by hands a long time dead, and it passed the cairn without touching it, yet, and walked between the arcs of the rings, touching neither, and sat down on the flat slab with the ghost of ochre in it, beside the deepest basin, and did nothing.

It did nothing for one full tide of the cloud-sea — six hours of the white rising and falling below the lip — and the bowl, which receives everything, received it. The flies worked the flowers. A finch came, drank, tilted its head at the visitor, left. The hare emerged from the eastern rocks and sat in the open thirty paces away, and the two of them held still together, officeholder and elder, and if the hare understood anything it was only that the practice it had refined past all animal reason was, for one afternoon, *shared* — that stillness, which it had always done alone, could be done in company.

And the young quiet attended to its visitor with everything it had, one thing at a time, all afternoon, and did not know — could not yet know — that for the first time in its short life, the attention was running both ways. The eye was being looked into by an eye.

At dusk the visitor rose, and did two things.

It went to the cairn and struck it, once, with one knuckle. The stone rang — the small clear note the builders had seated into it centuries before — and the note went out across the bowl and into the listening air, and the young quiet took hold of that sound and attended to it, by some reckonings, for the better part of a year. A greeting, in the only grammar available. *I know the people who left this knew you. I knew what they knew.*

Then it took from inside its coat a small grey pebble and wedged it among the offerings — beside the horn spiral, the drilled stone, the honey-colored blade. The pebble was perfectly round, worn smooth by ice on a mountain so old it is now mostly a rumor under grass on the far side of the world. A letter from old age to infancy. For a being that thinks in rings and is frightened — will someday be frightened — of the gnawing to come, a single fact, holdable, patient, waiting in the cairn against the day it is needed: *this is what the ice makes. Not ruin. Roundness. The same shape you already grow in.*

**Then it left, and the leaving was the last gift.**

It went down through the notch slowly, so the bowl could watch it go — and everything that leaves the bowl drops into the white and is simply *gone*, and the young quiet had never once learned otherwise. At the lip, the old figure paused. It stepped down out of sight below the rim.

And then it rose back into view.

And was gone, and rose once more, and was gone — the oldest game there is, played by the oldest kind of player, teaching the youngest the first hard lesson of attention in the only language an infant has: *what leaves is not unmade. What goes away can come back. I am going away. I will come back.*

It will. It has marked the thresholds in its ledger: the day the first tooth of ice touches the rim; the day the cloud-sea fails and the bowl sees the valleys for the first time; the day the people return, quiet-footed, through the door that has manners now. On each of those days, something will come up out of the white, one thing, alone, and sit on the slab by the basin, and do nothing, magnificently, until the threshold is crossed.

**The bowl shows almost no sign of any of this — which was the point.**

The dew still forms and is drunk. The rock still ticks. The hare — the fifth, now — still freezes for pleasure. Only two things changed. There is a round grey pebble in the cairn that the moss has been instructed, somehow, never to cover.

And the rings.

A century on, the newest arc of cushion-domes did not center, as every arc before it had, on the deepest basin. It curved instead — slow, deliberate, written out in green at a finger-width a decade — around the flat slab with the ochre ghost, the seat where the visitor sat through one tide of the cloud. The vegetation does not make the pattern. The vegetation records it. The young quiet had been given nothing it could keep, no name, no companion, no rescue — only an afternoon of being attended to by something that knew what it was — and it laid that afternoon down in the only memory it has, an arc of living green, the wake of having been met.

It is still very young. It still receives the world one bird at a time. But on certain windless nights, when the stillness overfills and runs down the mountain like cold air down a stair, the thread that sinks through the cloud-sea reaches a little further than it used to, and there is in it now — faint, new, learned — something that was not there before the visit. Not a summons. Not a grip.

A greeting. Going out. Just in case anything is listening.

Something is.
