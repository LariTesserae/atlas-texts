Begin with the geckos, because they will begin with you. Before your foot leaves the last step, forty pairs of lidless eyes have already noted you, filed you, and dismissed you. They are a single lineage — every gecko on these walls descends from geckos that lived on these walls, back and back, hundreds of generations that have never touched another surface, never known another light. The brick is not their habitat. The brick is their entire cosmology. Each one holds a territory of six or eight bricks and knows those bricks the way you know the inside of your own mouth: which mortar joint stays warm after the second shaft goes dark, which flake of lichen shelters silverfish, exactly how far the moss curtain sways in a stairwell draft and therefore exactly where a moth stunned by that sway will fall. Over the generations they have drifted toward the wall itself. Their skin now carries the precise dusty rose of old brick and the exact gray-green of the mortar between, and the mottling is not random — it maps the ratio of brick to joint on the walls they live on. They are becoming a picture of their home, one shed skin at a time. In the evening, when the last shaft goes gold and the moths rise, the geckos are the room's clockwork: they all move at once, and the whole chamber seems to slide a finger's width sideways, and then holds still again.

The harvester ants are older than any gecko, older than the owls, possibly older than some of the lichen. Not the individual ants — the colony, which is the actual animal, the way a wave is the actual thing and the water only its momentary body. The colony has been running its polished road along the east wall for longer than anyone has descended the stairs, and it is the only resident that commutes. Every day, foragers climb the plaster of the brightest light-shaft — a full body-length of green fur, then bare plaster, then the raw dazzle of the surface world — and come back down carrying seeds. Grass seeds, mostly, which they eat. But ants are imperfect, gloriously so, and over the centuries they have dropped things. A bromeliad seed here. A spore-flecked husk there. Nearly everything green in this room arrived in the jaws of an ant who fumbled it, which means the garden is, in the strictest sense, the colony's compost heap, its spilled groceries, its accumulated clumsiness made lush. The ants do not know this and would not care.

And they keep one secret, the biggest in the room. Deep under the third brick from the wall on their road's southern end — a brick polished glossiest of all, though no one has ever wondered why — the colony long ago found the seam where floor meets footing, and worked it, and opened it. The queen lives below the floor. She lives in the sealed earth, the soil the builders so carefully faced away, the ground the whole room was constructed to deny. The only door in this entirely human place is an ant-wide gap under a brick, and the only resident who has ever touched the forbidden dirt is a fat pale monarch who has never once seen the light walk across the dome. The room believes itself sealed. The room is wrong by six millimeters, and the ants tell nobody.

The darkling beetles are the pensioners. A darkling beetle can live twenty years, and these do — there is a beetle trundling the spiral floor right now that has been trundling it since before you were told this place existed. They eat what falls: moss fragments, molted gecko skin, the crumbs of owl pellets, each other, eventually. They are the reason the floor is clean. They have the gait of things that have concluded, correctly, that nothing here will ever chase them, and they walk the worn spiral not because they understand it but because worn brick is smoother, and they are old, and their legs know the easy path. The silverfish are their opposite — quick, silvery, ancient in a different way, a body plan four hundred million years old that found this dry bright vault and recognized it as the world they were always built for. They live in the lichen crust like fish in a reef.

The snails are the room's philosophers, if a philosopher is something that has decided the correct response to existence is to seal the door and wait. They estivate — years at a stretch, a decade, more — cemented to the dome with dried mucus, metabolisms banked down to an ember. Some are alive. Some are only shells that have not yet fallen. The snails themselves may not be certain which category they occupy. Once or twice a year, when rain hammers the shaft mouths far above and a ghost of humidity drifts down the plaster, a few of them wake, unglue, and spend one slow damp night grazing the lichen fields of the ceiling, upside down, in the dark, entirely alone with the sleeping moths. By morning they have sealed themselves again somewhere new. When one finally dies and lets go, the *tok* of the shell on the floor is the room's funeral bell, and the beetles attend to the rest.

The moths are the currency. Everything spends them. They sleep by day in the flax-pale curtains, hundreds deep, and the perpetual glitter in the light-beams — that drifting flake and wing-scale you noticed — is simply the moths existing, shedding a little of themselves into the air the way a candle sheds light. At the gold hour they rise, and for a few minutes the shafts are full of soft chaos, and the geckos eat, and above the geckos the owls eat, and enough moths always survive because the moss curtains are deep and the room is patient and the arithmetic was settled long ago.

The owls are the aristocracy — a pair, always a pair, small enough to fit a fist, occupying the darkest shaft as their line has occupied it since anyone could say. They are the room's other importers. Every night they go up through their chimney into the enormous dangerous outside, and every night they bring the outside back down in the only form the room will accept: pellets. Fur, tiny bones, undigested seeds from the stomachs of the mice they've eaten. The richest soil in the chamber is owl regret, and the densest, greenest lichen grows on it, and directly above the nest — this is the resurrection ferns' private bargain, the one you wondered at — the corbelled courses catch the rising warmth and breath of two owls and their chicks, a permanent faint column of humid air, and the ferns in that column and nowhere else unfurl green and stay green, drinking exhalation. The ferns are green because the owls are breathing. That is the whole secret. Almost everything in this room is like that if you trace it far enough.

Which leaves the builders. They live here too, in the only way they still can. Look closely at the mortar joints where the lichen crust is oldest: the masons pressed and smoothed that mortar with bare thumbs, and the whorls of their thumbprints are still there, fired into permanence, and the very first lichens took hold in those grooves because a fingerprint is a fine landscape if you are small enough. The oldest living things in the room are growing in the fingerprints of the dead. The maul and the line and the broken lamp are inhabited the same way — furred, colonized, no longer tools but terrain. And the spiral worn shallow at the center of the floor holds afternoon heat a shade longer than the rest, so the geckos gather there at dusk, warming themselves on the memory of feet.

So: who lives in the Undercroft Garden? A lineage of watchers the color of their walls. An ancient clumsy commonwealth of ants with one buried queen and one buried secret. Pensioner beetles, philosopher snails, a fortune of moths, a dynasty of owls whose breath keeps ferns alive. Lichen, eldest of all, eating the room a grain a century — so slowly that the room, in a sense, is not being destroyed but adopted, converted brick by brick into resident.

But if you ask what *truly* lives here, the honest answer is the arrangement itself. The covenant. Moth to gecko to owl to pellet to lichen to snail to floor to beetle, around and around, a wheel small enough to hold in cupped hands and old enough to have forgotten its makers' names, turning in three moving pools of light. It was here before you came down the thirty-one steps. It is turning while you stand there, quietly, all around you, without needing you at all. It will be turning when you go.
