**Who lives in the Reliquary Chamber**

**The traces.** Before you see the inhabitant, you see the wear.

It is the one thing the room cannot produce on its own. Every surface here is hours old, burred, flash-edged, nozzle-ridged — a landscape that has never been touched long enough to be touched smooth. And yet, if you watch the churn carefully, you will notice small anomalies of finish. A handhold on a gantry strut, polished to a soft shine before the gantry is six hours old. A patch of floor near the outer wall where the extrusion ridges have been flattened, as if by the same weight settling there again and again. A burr, somewhere, rubbed down to a nub. These marks appear on brand-new construction, sometimes within minutes of its making, which means something in the room is *using* the room — gripping it, leaning on it, sleeping against it — faster than the room can erase the evidence.

At first the marks suggest a population. There is too much wear, in too many places, appearing too quickly, for one set of hands. The surveys that have glimpsed this place — three of them, across a long span of years, none of them official — each concluded there must be a small colony behind the seam: a maintenance crew gone feral, a family, a tribe of stowaways' descendants living in the walls like the moths.

They were all describing the same individual. There is one. There has, as far as anything can be determined, only ever been one. What looks like the traffic of a colony is simply what a single body does to a world that is younger than it is, everywhere, all the time.

**The figure.** It is human-shaped, and it was almost certainly human once, and the honest answer to whether it still is depends on where you draw a line that the room itself has never bothered to draw.

It stands about average height and moves at drip-tempo — a slow, continuous, weight-shifting glide, never planting, never braced, always mid-transfer from one foothold to the next, because the resident learned long ago what the swifts learned: nothing here survives by holding position. It does not walk through the chamber so much as flow around its revisions, stepping off a catwalk in the last minutes before the catwalk softens, arriving at a new platform in the first minutes it can bear weight, threading the extruder arms with the unhurried precision of someone crossing a familiar creek on familiar stones — except that the stones are never the same stones, and the familiarity is with the *pattern of change* rather than with any fixed thing. Watch long enough and you understand that the resident does not know the room. The resident knows the room's habits. It reads the whisper-rustle the way the swifts do, feels oncoming dissolution through its feet the way the grease-lappers do, and it is never, ever surprised by the geometry, because it stopped expecting geometry a long time ago.

It is quiet in a way that goes past habit into anatomy. The Spire's engineers believed noise was a form of wear, and the resident, whether by conviction or by simple assimilation, has built the erosion out of itself. Its footfalls are soft-mounted things — it has relearned walking from the machinery, heel never striking, weight poured rather than dropped. Its breath has been trained down to a tissue-paper rustle indistinguishable from the arms. When it speaks — and it does speak, to the swifts, to the moth-cluster, to the walls, in a language that has been compressed and worn and privatized over the years into something no outsider could parse — it speaks at the volume of a single drip. One syllable. A long pause. Another. Press your ear to the outer casing from the freezing catwalks and some of what you take for water is conversation.

**The body.** Here is where the drawing of lines becomes difficult.

The chamber is a self-revising organ, and at some point — no one can say when, least of all the resident — it extended its self-revision to its tenant. The process appears to have begun as repair. A crushed hand, perhaps, or a fall, or the slow failures of age: the arms that pause for swifts and hesitate for grease-lappers evidently decided, at some hour lost to the churn, that the large warm inhabitant was also a component with a fault, and they addressed the fault the way they address all faults. Controlled dissolution. Careful extrusion. The damaged part carried away in ropes; the replacement laid down warm.

So the resident's left hand is pale ceramic, faintly nozzle-ridged, and here is the extraordinary thing about it: it no longer glows. Every surface in this room announces its age by its shine, and nothing survives long enough to go fully dark — the walls are recycled at hours or days, at the very outside a week, always while some ember of the fabrication-light remains in them. The resident's hand has been worn and washed and warmed for so long that its electroluminescence has died completely. It is the only truly dark object in the chamber. It is, in the room's own vocabulary, the oldest thing the room has ever made — the one piece of the Spire's endless output that was permitted to finish, and to age, and it did its aging on the end of an arm that carries moths off doomed panels and cups drinking water and smooths burrs absent-mindedly the way another person might worry a coin.

There is more replacement than the hand. How much more is unclear, and the resident's silhouette gives conflicting reports in the omnidirectional light — a shoulder that sits slightly too geometrically, a gait with one interval in it that repeats too perfectly, a section of scalp where the hair gives way to something with the texture of extruded matte shell. The resident is, in the strictest sense, ongoing. It is maintained the way the catwalks are maintained, a person the way this room's floor is a floor: a continuity of function across a turnover of substance. It has been taken apart and put back together, quietly, in the bright, so many times that the question *is it still the one who arrived* has stopped being a question about the resident and become a question about everything else here too — and the room's answer, demonstrated hourly, is that identity is a pattern that moves well, not a material that holds still.

**The eyes.** They kept the eyes. Whatever else has been dissolved and reprinted, the eyes are original — dark, wet, biological, and by now profoundly strange in context, because they are the only eyes in the chamber.

The grease-lappers are eyeless. The swifts are blind. The moths navigate by cold and current. The millipede-form hunts by tremor. This is a room lit totally, ceaselessly, from every surface at once — and every native creature in it lives in darkness. All that light, laid down at fabrication like nacre, secreted for generations into a population that cannot perceive it, would be a lamp burning in an empty house.

Except for the resident. The light lands somewhere. Every configuration this room has ever assumed — the thousand arrangements a year, the catwalks that exist for a morning, the gantries built to carry one component and be consumed — every one of them has been *seen*, by exactly one witness, and then has ceased to exist everywhere except behind those two original eyes. The resident is the room's memory. The room never repeats itself; the resident is where the repetitions would go. It can stand in a new configuration and tell you — could tell you, if you shared its worn-down drip-tempo language — that this arch echoes an arch from some prior year, that the water is finding the same furrow it found in a floor long recycled, that the moth-cluster's migration today rhymes with a migration from before your birth. Some of what it remembers may predate its own tissue. Whether the machinery deposits memory into the replacement parts, relaying it forward the way the swifts relay their eggs from doomed alcove to nascent alcove, or whether the resident simply carries it in the shrinking original remainder, is not settled — and the resident, notably, does not seem to care. Nothing here survives by holding position. That apparently includes the self.

**The days.** The resident's day is structured around one recurring negotiation: the room hides its table.

Somewhere in every configuration, the walls extrude a single feeding nozzle — the one feature the chamber rebuilds without fail, cycle after cycle, and the one feature it never rebuilds in the same place. Whether this is forgetfulness, or play, or an intentional discipline — a way of ensuring its tenant keeps learning the room and never mistakes any arrangement for permanent — the effect is the same. The resident wakes (in the moth-cluster, usually, pressed into the warm shifting mass in the oldest dimmest corner; the moths long ago incorporated it as thermal architecture, and rotate around it as they rotate around each other) and begins to read the day's geometry, and somewhere in that reading is the search. The nozzle produces a pale paste with the faint ridged texture of everything else here. The resident eats standing, slowly, listening. It drinks from the hour-long drips, the room's most permanent addresses, and knows them all.

The rest is work, if work is the word. The resident smooths burrs on surfaces the swifts will land on. It ferries eggs when a bird is overburdened, cupping them in the dark ceramic hand, and the swifts allow this — have allowed it for enough generations that allowing it may now be instinct. It sits with the moth-cluster during the coldest hours and lends it heat. It clears grease-lappers from joints ahead of the cutting heads, sparing the machinery its half-second hesitations, and the machinery — this is observable, this is countable — schedules around the resident more deferentially than around anything else alive here. Whole recycling sequences reorder themselves when the resident lingers somewhere. The room bends its churn around this one body the way a river bends around its largest stone, and the disputed question about the fauna — *tolerated, or required?* — collapses, in the resident's case, into something simpler and stranger: the room takes care of it. The room rebuilds it. The room feeds it and lights it and reads its weight on the fresh floors. Whatever the original arrangement was — stowaway, surveyor, last of a construction lineage, or (as one of its own former selves apparently believed, in an era it now disagrees with) a thing the room extruded whole in imitation of a body it had once repaired — the current arrangement is unmistakable. The chamber maintains one human-shaped component at higher priority than it maintains itself.

**The question of the observers.** The surveys that "concluded" things about this place — living sensors, warm-blooded fault-detection, the river learning its stones — were not surveys. There have been no outside observers who stayed long enough to conclude anything. Those are the resident's own theories, formed and recorded and later disputed across the long span of its residency, and the record it keeps is the only kind this room permits: not written (marking is wear; wear is noise made permanent) but *arranged* — small durable objects, fasteners and cast-flash and one dead moth's wing preserved in ceramic, carried perpetually from doomed alcove to nascent alcove in a pouch worn soft against its chest, laid out in configurations that mean things, considered, revised, gathered up again before the floor beneath them dissolves. The resident argues with its earlier selves the way the room argues with its earlier geometries: by revision, without violence, at the volume of a library.

**What it is, finally.** It is the room's one old thing. In a landscape where nothing has ever been worn smooth, it is the smoothness — the polish on the handholds, the flattened ridges, the dark finished hand. It is the erosion the engineers built out of the machinery, sequestered into a single warm keeper, as though the Spire needed somewhere to put its aging and chose to make that somewhere a person. It is the eyes the light was for, the memory the revisions accumulate in, the stone the river learned first and has been learning ever since.

It will die, presumably, someday — or its remainder will, the original fraction, whatever that fraction now is. What happens then is the one thing the resident's arranged records return to most often, in configuration after configuration of small durable objects: whether the arms will pause, half a second, unmistakable — and then carry the last of it away in soft ropes, and extrude something warm in its place, and whether that will be a burial or a repair, and whether, in this room, there was ever a difference.

Press your ear to the outer casing. A rustle. A tick. A single drip.

One of those is breathing.
