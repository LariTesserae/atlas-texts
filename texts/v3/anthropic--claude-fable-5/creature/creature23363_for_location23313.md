# The Inhabitants

Begin with the smallest, because the country does.

## The Grazers of the Green

The pale snails are the first thing a patient eye learns to count and the last thing it learns to stop counting. They are the color of skimmed milk, shells thin as a fingernail paring, and they exist at a density that suggests the moss was made for them — which, in the circular logic of the place, it nearly was. They graze the films of algae and the soft growing tips, and their slow traffic keeps the moss pillows trimmed and dense, the way grazing keeps a meadow a meadow. They have no season. They have no hurry. A snail here may travel less in its life than a dauber travels in an evening, and the country does not rank these achievements.

Below them, in the soil that the moss has manufactured out of rotted fronds and rock dust, live the unnumbered: springtails in silver drifts, millipedes pale as cooked rice, blind beetles, the woodlice that work the dead fern litter into earth. They are the digestion of the country. Everything that falls — leaf, feather, weir-stick, dauber chick tipped from a high nest — goes down into them and comes back up as fern.

And in every crevice the seepage hasn't claimed: the funnel spiders. They are gray, patient, and very numerous, and they have made a quiet wager with the place — that in a country where everything whispers, the only honest signal left is *touch*. Their silk sheets are tuned like drumheads. They do not see well and do not need to. A springtail's footfall arrives at the funnel's throat as clearly as a shout.

## The Folded Ones

The seepage salamanders are the great silent majority of the country — ash-gray, cold-fleshed, slow as the water films they live in. Lift a stone and find three; lift the next stone and find four. They do almost nothing, almost always. They breathe through their skin, which is why they choose the weeping walls; they eat what blunders into reach; they fold themselves together under stones in twos and threes with a companionability that may be warmth-seeking or may be something less explicable, since nothing here is warm.

They are not clever and they are very good at being what they are. In a country of cycles, they are the patient hand of the clock. Watch a lit chamber through a full day and you can see them make their migration — a few feet, no more, tracking the wet shadow as the daylight column swings across the floor, the whole population shifting like the slow second movement of something. They have an enemy in the limestone mustelid and an enemy in the shrews and they answer both the same way: by being under a stone, folded, still, one of thousands, statistically safe.

In spring their eggs hang in the seep-cracks in clear beaded strings, each egg a small lens with a curled gray comma inside, and for a few weeks the wettest walls are jeweled with them, and the wrens that squat in the old dauber districts grow fat.

## The Engineers

The dam-builders are the closest thing the country has to a government, and like most governments they did not intend to become one.

They are heavy animals, low and glossy, with the dense underfur of things that live at well-water temperature and never dry off. A family holds a watershed — one branch of the descending pool system, perhaps eight or twelve terraces — and the family is the unit of everything: work, territory, teaching. Kits ride their mothers' backs through the canals for the first season and spend the second season making bad repairs that their elders quietly redo at night. By the third season they are journeymen. By the fourth they are gone, dispersing down-gallery through the connecting passages to the unclaimed water, which is how the system grows: not by plan but by inheritance and exile.

Their genius is not the dam. Any of them can build a dam. Their genius is the *spillway* — the deliberate weakness, the place where each weir is built to fail gracefully, shedding floodwater sideways into an overflow channel instead of bursting. Every terrace has one. The snowmelt comes down the eastern cataract twice a year like a verdict, and the system survives it the way a reed survives wind: by yielding in chosen places. After the flood the families work for weeks, and a watcher would notice that they do not merely repair — they revise. A spillway that failed badly is rebuilt differently. The knowledge is not in any one animal. It is in the structures, which outlive their builders, and in the habit of attending to structures, which is taught.

They are nearly mute. They have perhaps five sounds, all soft: a hum between mates, a grunt of effort, a kit's thin complaint, a low door-creak of warning, and a sound made only at the death of a family member — a long exhaled note, just at the edge of hearing, that the chambers carry a surprising distance. Other dam-builder families, hearing it, stop work for a while. No one knows what to call this and the country does not require a name for it.

Their pools are their estates, and the estates have tenants beyond counting: the cress, the shrimp hanging in the clear water like punctuation, the small dark fish stacked in the weir-shadows, the crayfish working the margins. None of these were invited. All of them are, in the practical sense, dependents — the dam-builders made the still water, and the still water made everything else possible. A family that dies out leaves its terraces to slow ruin, and over a few years the pools drain, the cress browns, the shrimp vanish downstream, and the moss begins reupholstering the empty weirs. The country keeps these ruins the way old cities keep theirs, half-legible, until a dispersing journeyman finds the watershed and begins again on the old foundations, because the old foundations are always, still, the right places.

## The Wall People

The daubers live vertically, in time as much as in stone.

A colony wall is a record. The lowest tiers are the oldest — moss-swallowed, green-shouldered, their cup shapes barely legible under the upholstery — and the tiers climb upward through generations to the fresh pale architecture at the top, still smelling of river mud. A dauber is hatched in a nest mortared to nests its ancestors built and abandoned. It will build its own one tier up or one alley over. The colony does not remember this lineage, but the wall does.

In the air they are close to invisible: swift, dark-backed, utterly silent in the wing, navigating the dim galleries by faint dry clicks — a ticking you register in the jaw rather than the ear. Twice a day the colonies move as one organism. At dawn they go up the sinks in a rising spiral, thousands of them, out into the cold bright surface world to hunt the insect drift above the plateau, and the chambers feel emptier than their silence should allow. At dusk they come back down, funneling through the collapse windows in long threads, and the country inhales them.

Their abandoned districts are the housing stock of half the small life in the system. Wrens nest in the old cups. A solitary, fur-bodied, cold-tolerant bee provisions her young in them. Snails aestivate in them; spiders curtain the alleys between them. A dauber wall a century old is no longer dauber architecture at all — it is a reef, and the daubers are merely the coral that secreted it.

They have one loud sound, used once at a time: a single sharp note when an owl enters a chamber. It is never answered. It does not need to be. After it, the stillness; after the stillness, the dripping; after the dripping, eventually, everything else.

## The Listeners

Two hunters keep the silence honest.

The owls are pale, long-winged, light as a held breath, and they come down through the collapse windows at dusk the way ash comes down — without commitment, without sound, on wings fringed soft to nothing. They hunt entirely by ear in chambers that carry sound like cathedrals, which makes them less predators than auditors: they drift the galleries listening for the one creature careless enough to be heard. A shrew rustling cress. A kit slapping water. A snail's shell tapped against stone by an incautious wren. The owls do not search; they wait for the country to make a mistake, and the country, mostly, doesn't, and so the owls are lean, and few, and patient beyond anything warm-blooded ought to be.

The mustelid is the other auditor — low, limestone-gray, moving along the weir-tops at night with its head cocked toward the pools. It is the only thing in the country the dam-builders fear, and the relationship between them is old and formal: the mustelid takes kits and crayfish and the occasional drowsing fish, the dam-builders build their lodge entrances underwater where it will not follow, and the low door-creak warning note travels family to family down the watershed ahead of it like news. It dens in the breakdown, lines the den with dauber feathers, and is, in its narrow way, the best-informed animal in the system: it knows every spillway, every lodge, every district of the walls, the whole map of the city held in a skull the size of a plum, for the single purpose of finding the seam where the whisper slips.

## The Elders of the Dark

Here is the thing the light-country forgets, and the thing a visitor learns last.

The blind white crayfish in the dark pool-margins are not pale cousins of the sighted dark ones. They are the *originals*. They were here before here existed — before the roofs fell, when the whole system was sealed black water and seeping stone, and they lived their slow eyeless centuries in it as the rightful and only tenants. Then the ceilings came down, the light poured in, and an entire green civilization arrived on top of them in what was, by their clock, an afternoon. The sighted crayfish are newcomers, washed down the cataract, prospering in the bright margins. The white ones simply withdrew a little, into the shadows of the weirs and the mouths of the down-galleries, and continued. They grow slowly, breed rarely, and live so long that the oldest of them predate every dam, every nest, every tree in the country. They are not bitter about the light. They are not anything about the light. They are continuing.

And below even them, in the sumps — the flooded throats at the bottom of the lowest galleries, the only true dark left — lives the thing the first line of the country's history was written about.

It is a salamander, but to call it kin to the gray folded ones under the stones is like calling a glacier kin to frost. It is long — longer than the mustelid, longer than a dam-builder — flat-headed, loose-skinned, the white of things that have never owed the sun anything, with external gills like sprays of red coral, the only color it owns. It does not leave the water. It may not, in any meaningful sense, leave its *pool*: an individual holds a sump the way a family of dam-builders holds a watershed, except that it holds it alone, and for lifetimes. It eats the white crayfish, and the shrimp the terraced pools send down to it, and the occasional fish, and in poor decades it eats almost nothing and simply slows, its heart spacing out its beats like drips from a high ceiling, waiting at a temperature where waiting is nearly free.

These are the longest-lived things here. The phrase "within living memory" hangs on them. The eldest of them were grown — were already old — when the country was still sealed, when there was no green, no daubers, no terraces, no light, only the original black silence and the water working. They lay in their sumps and felt the collapses arrive through the stone: each one a pressure-wave through the flooded galleries, a shudder, a change in the taste of the water as the surface bled in. Everything above them — the whole crowded, whispering, terraced, mortared city — has been built during their lifetimes, over their heads, like a festival assembling above a sleeper.

They are not asleep. Their lateral lines read the sump the way the funnel spiders read silk. They register the dam-builders' dusk shift as a far rhythm in the water. They register the snowmelt floods. It is possible — no more than possible — that they register the slow lengthening of the crack lines, and know, in whatever way a nerve knows, that the vacant dark districts down-gallery will not be dark forever.

If the country has a memory, it is them. If it has a future, it is also them, because when the next roof opens and the light finds a new chamber, the order of arrival is already fixed and ancient and has never once varied: the dust, the moss, the snails, the ferns, the seep-folk, the engineers, the wall people, the listeners drifting down behind. The white elders will feel the collapse come through the stone, the way they have felt every one. And they will do what they have always done about the founding of cities above them.

They will continue.
