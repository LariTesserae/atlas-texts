## The Residents

The census misbehaves from the start. Ask what lives on the terrace and the place offers you candidates in ascending order of doubt, and each one, examined closely, turns out to be partly something else — partly equipment, partly anatomy, partly an appointment that hasn't been kept yet. So begin where the doubt is smallest, and let it grow.

**The mosses** live there, in the way that crops live somewhere. Each cushion is a citizen of the closed economy: it drinks exactly what the weeping cracks allot it, swells to exactly the thickness of a mattress and no further, and holds its border with the bare rock as scrupulously as if the border had been surveyed. Which it may have been. The mosses do not spread. This is the strangest thing about them, stranger than their speed of arrival — moss everywhere else in the world is ambition in vegetable form, a slow green argument with every adjacent surface, and these colonies sit inside their allotments like guests who have read the invitation carefully. They are moss with the striving removed. What remains when you remove striving from a plant is hard to name. Something between a crop and an ornament and a pet: a living thing that has been *relieved* of the one thing living things are usually made of, the fight for the next inch, and has settled instead into a deep, wet, warm competence at simply being moss. They are, in their way, the happiest residents. They are also the least free, and on the terrace these may be the same fact.

**The tree ferns** are younger and have not entirely learned the rules. They grow with the slightly indecent vigor of children in a hot room who believe themselves unsupervised — fiddleheads uncurling almost visibly, fronds reaching into the steam of the pond. But watch a frond approach the airspace of a neighboring colony and it slows. Not wilts; slows. The growth simply runs out of permission. The ferns live in the gaps between invisible fences, and they are young enough to keep testing the fences, and the fences are patient enough to keep being there. In fifty years they will be as composed as the mosses. For now they are the closest thing the terrace has to noise.

**The translucent things in the pools** are where the census starts to fail honestly. Finger-length, glassy, faintly amber in the steaming water; you can see through them to what should be organs and are perhaps organs and are perhaps components — there is a darker thread down the center that could be a gut or could be a wick. They hold still when watched, too still, with the stillness of something that has been inspected before and knows the procedure. But stay an hour. Stay three. They have habits, and habits are the signature of an inner life, or a very good imitation of one. They rest against the warm inflow side of their pools the way cats find the one heating pipe under the floor. They prefer each other's company in the mornings and solitude after the fog. And in the minute before one of the posts sounds its long low tone — that pressure in the chest — every one of them, in every pool, turns to face the same direction, like compass needles, like a congregation, like gauges coming online.

The most economical reading is that they are both things at once: animals that were given a job, or instruments that were given a metabolism, and the distinction stopped mattering some time ago. They are the terrace's nerve endings, its wet thermometers — the place tastes its own water through them. They live there the way a tongue lives in a mouth: indisputably alive, indisputably resident, and not entirely their own. It is even possible they were the first thing installed, before the moss, before the air — that everything else here was calibrated to *their* comfort, and the whole warm dripping enclosure is, at bottom, an aquarium built outward from its fish.

**The gardener** is seen once a day, at the upslope margin, and never well. A low shape, dark and wet-looking, unhurried, that comes down out of the white vapor where the mountain continues and the terrace does not. It never approaches the cliff edges. It leaves no tracks — either because it weighs nothing it does not give back, or because the rock is still warm enough to close behind it, which would mean the gardener is approximately the temperature of the place itself, which would mean it is *of* the place in some way that goes past residency.

Its work is visible only in arrears. The moss colonies are rearranged in the mornings: a cushion thinned here, a border trued there, the spacing — that spacing like a garden no one will admit to keeping — quietly maintained. Grazed or tended; the description's instinct was right that the distinction may not exist, but here is the form the non-distinction takes: the gardener eats only what is surplus. It eats the errors. Where a colony has overdrawn its allotment, where a fern has won an argument with a fence, where growth has happened that was not in the budget — that is what is gone in the morning. It prunes by digestion, and since nothing leaves the terrace, what it eats must come back: as warmth in the rock, as the weeping of a crack on the far side of the shelf, as next month's moss. The gardener is the one mobile vein of the closed circulation. It is the budget, given a body and a route. Whether it enjoys its rounds, whether it is a creature with a duty or a duty wearing a creature, cannot be determined from the available evidence, but it is worth noting that it is never hurried and never absent, and that combination — in anything, anywhere — looks a great deal like contentment.

**The posts** do not live there. Be clear about this, because the temptation is strong: they are warm with a pulse-like warmth, the moss defers to them, the fog organizes itself around them into standing patterns like breath made briefly legible. But a doorframe is not a tenant. The posts are where something that does not live on the terrace comes *nearest* to it — fingertips, perhaps, of the hand the terrace is strung across. When the tone sounds and the steam over the pond changes direction, that is not a resident speaking. That is the landlord adjusting a thermostat from very far away, or from very deep down. In the afternoon fog, when the vapor aligns around the posts in those slow vertical lamellae, you are seeing the closest thing the owner has to a face, and it is a habit of water, and it is not looking at you. It is looking at the inventory.

Which brings the census, finally, to the deepest entry, the one the place told you about first and you mistook for a metaphor. The warmth rising through the soles of your feet — *the heat of an animal that has just lain down*. The summit lost in its own exhalations. The terrace extruded, *decided*. Whatever is growing this house, floor by floor over the centuries, is not absent during construction. It is the construction site. The principal resident of the terrace lives below it and around it and arguably *as* it: the terrace is its newest skin, still cooling, still tender, still sweating the way new skin does. By this reading, nothing on the shelf is an inhabitant in the ordinary sense. The mosses, the swimmers, the gardener — these are flora being installed in a body, the way a body acquires its small internal nations before it can digest the world. The resident is in residence the way you reside in your own most recent inch of growth: completely, and without being findable in it.

And yet. One entry remains, and it is the one the whole place leans toward.

A test plot tests *for* something. The air is held at the warmth of a greenhouse and the thickness of a held breath, two miles above where such air has any business pooling — held, as the description says, as though it had been *asked* to stay. Asked to stay for what? For lungs. Air like that is calibrated air; that density and that humidity are the specifications of a respiratory system. The water is potable and budgeted. The ground, too new for walking, is *for* walking — that is what "too new" promises. The fences that pace the ferns are pacing them toward a layout: open ground, shade, water at intervals. The translucent things hold still when watched because they have been taught that watching happens here, that someone comes through and checks.

So the truest answer to who lives on the terrace may be: nobody, yet. Everything currently alive there is staff, or fixture, or stand-in. The whole small hot dripping place is a held breath in the literal sense — an atmosphere kept in trust. The resident it was decided *for* has not arrived, or has not been finished, or has not been chosen; and the moss keeps its borders, and the gardener makes its rounds, and the pools take the water's temperature four times an hour, all of it maintenance against a move-in date that nothing on the terrace knows.

Note, in this connection, two small facts. The dark bird that rides the thermals outside the meniscus, observing, declining — it declines, perhaps, not from caution but from courtesy. It can see that the invitation is not addressed to it. And nothing on the terrace, in all its sparse and tended life, has yet died. No litter of fronds, no drowned swimmer, no browned moss; the closed budget has no line for decay, because decay has not been needed, because death is a service that gets installed when the tenant does. The first death on the terrace, whenever it comes, will be a kind of ribbon-cutting.

Until then, what lives there is the staff, and the skin, and the waiting. Walk it — carefully, where the ground allows — and you will feel all three at once through the soles of your feet: the warmth of the animal beneath, the diligence of the small lives around the water, and over everything, patient as the fog and as punctual, the meantime, which is the terrace's largest resident, and its most comfortable, and the only one that will have to leave when the door finally opens.
