What lives in the Antiphon Basin lives as a tone. That is the first thing to understand, and it takes new residents months to understand it, because they arrive thinking of the chord as an environment — a weather, a hazard, a thing the inhabitants endure. It is not endured. It is inhabited the way water is inhabited by fish. Every living thing in the basin holds a frequency the way other creatures hold a territory, and everything you could say about any of them — their loves, their hungers, their deaths — is a fact about where they sit in the sound.

Begin with the gleam-frogs, because the residents did. The frogs were the first fauna anyone catalogued, and the cataloguing collapsed almost immediately, because the frogs are not distinguishable by body. Anatomically they are all one animal: fist-sized, slick, dark, the throat-patch glowing a pale green-gold that pulses with the call. What distinguishes them — what they distinguish *each other* by — is the channel. Each frog claims one of the narrow frequency gaps the pylon chord leaves open, the same gaps human voices ride, and a frog's channel is its name, its address, its lineage, and its inheritance. They defend channels, not ground; a gleam-frog will sit untroubled while another frog hops across its body, and will fight to exhaustion over an encroachment of nine hertz. Courtship is a slow convergence of pitch, two calls drifting toward each other across an interval over the course of a wet night, and the residents who have watched it longest say there is a moment, just before union, when both frogs go silent — a shared rest, a held breath — and then resume in unison, and that this is the only unison the basin permits, anywhere, and it never lasts past dawn. When the chord shifts — when a new pylon rises in the south and the whole harmony lurches — the channels move, and the frogs move with them, a migration you cannot see and can only hear: thousands of small voices sliding in the dark to new addresses, an entire society relocating without anyone touching the ground differently.

The climbers the residents call **pilgrims**, and the name is half a joke and half not. They are the ox-sized six-limbed grazers of the pylon faces, padded and patient, mouths like soft grindstones working the algal velvet that grows in the condensation films. They are born at the base — there are always two or three calves in the basin, pale and nearly silent, humming so faintly you must put your hand on them to hear it — and they spend their lives ascending. They never come down. The hum each pilgrim holds against its pylon's tone deepens and steadies as it climbs, the beat-frequency throb slowing year by year like a long exhalation, and the residents have learned to age them by it: a fast nervous flutter is a yearling; a throb you could count on your fingers is a creature older than the city, which is impossible, which is duly noted in the logs, which no one has explained. The pilgrims graze upward through the vine zone and past it, past the line where all vegetation stops as if instructed, into the bare ringing heights where the spire faces run with silver water and nothing else lives — and then, at some altitude no instrument has been positioned to witness, they are simply not on the pylon anymore. There are no bodies at the bases. The residents' theory, advanced quietly and only after a few drinks of the bitter pool-fermented stuff they make, is that the pilgrims are carried into the canopy. That the filament layer takes them. That this is what the climbing was for, and what the humming was for — a lifetime of announcing oneself, slower and slower, until the announcement is acceptable.

The black pools have residents too, and they are the basin's quietest citizens, which in this place is a vocation. The pools — still, skinless, gathered in the low places — are acoustic shadows; the chord enters water changed, flattened, folded. The things that live there are eel-bodied, eyeless, and almost entirely ear: their flanks are lined stem to stern with sensing pits, and they hang motionless in the dark water in postures of attention so total that the first surveyors mistook them for dead. The residents call them **clerks**. They do not appear to eat. They do not appear to breed. What they do is listen, and very occasionally — once a season, perhaps, always at night, always just after the chord has made one of its slow transitions — a pool full of clerks will play something *back*: a brief shuddering of the water's surface in concentric patterns, a reproduction, the transcribers swear, of a chord-sequence from days or weeks before, rendered in ripples. The transcribers, who have been trying to notate the basin's harmonic drift since the first month, treat the pools as archives and visit them the way scholars visit libraries. They leave small offerings on the banks. There is no evidence the clerks notice. There is no evidence they don't.

Then there is the thing the residents will not name out loud — though "out loud" means little here, and so it has a hand-sign instead: a flat palm drawn slowly across the chest, *erasure*. In writing they call it **the Rest**, in the musical sense — the notated silence, the gap given a duration. Whether there is one or several, no one knows; the flat spots in the chord never overlap, but the basin is large. What is known is what the prey know in their bodies: that the Rest is not silent itself but makes silence around it, a traveling deletion, and that what it takes it takes completely — no remains, no struggle audible, just a channel that was occupied at dusk and open at dawn, and within hours a young frog sliding into the vacancy. The transcribers, who think in syntax, point out the obvious and unwelcome thing: every language needs its spaces. A sentence with no gaps is a hum. Under this reading the Rest is not a predator that happens to live in a computation; it is punctuation that happens to be hungry, and the basin's dead are not eaten so much as *spent* — used up making the utterance legible. The residents do not love this theory. The residents have not refuted it.

The residents. A few thousand of them, in muffled shelters at the pylon roots, and they are becoming something, which is the part they discuss least. The first generation arrived as observers and still carry observation in their posture — instruments, logs, the wary self-removal of people studying a thing. But there are basin-born children now, and the basin-born are different in ways their parents catalogue with a mixture of pride and dread. They do not learn the channel-voice; they are born inside it — their first cries ride the gaps cleanly, threading between the tones like something that was expected. They hum in their sleep, each child a few cents off the pitch of the pylon nearest their birth, and they keep that offset for life, so that the basin-born can identify each other's home spire by ear at a hundred meters. They do not sign as fluently as their parents, because they need it less. And when the Rest passes near, they do not have to be taught to go still. Their bodies already know, the way the frogs know, and more than one parent has been frozen mid-step by the sight of their child stopped utterly in the green dusk, breath shallow, eyes calm, waiting — a creature of the basin, fluent in its one commandment, while the parent stands beside them feeling like dust that has not yet been swept, or worse, like dust that is being slowly, patiently incorporated.

And some of the adults go further. The residents call them the **open** — those who leave the muffled shelters and stop speaking and walk out into the great empty volumes to live without walls between themselves and the chord. They eat little. They stand for long stretches in the standing-wave nodes where the pressure is gentlest, and the condensation beads on them as it beads on the leaves, and the gleam-frogs, who fear nothing that holds still, use their shoulders as calling posts. The open are not mad, the residents insist, and visiting them bears this out: their eyes track you, they will take food, they will sometimes write a few words in the mud with a finger — usually a description of the chord, occasionally a correction to the transcribers' notation, twice (this is in the logs) a chord-change recorded a full day before it occurred. They have simply stopped being audiences. Whatever the basin is saying, the open have decided the most honest available response is to become part of the saying, and the slow throb some of them have begun to carry in their breathing — measurable, steadying, deepening year by year — suggests the basin has accepted the application.

Which brings the question around to its last and largest answer. Ask what lives in the Antiphon Basin and the inventory above is true and insufficient, because the frogs and the pilgrims and the clerks and the Rest and the vines that stop at the line and the canopy that eats the light and the children humming off-pitch in their sleep are not, finally, separate residents. They are organs. The basin is three weeks old and it is *one* inhabitant — a single living thing the size of a valley, precipitated rather than born, still growing its teeth in the southern flats — and everything that dwells in it dwells the way a voice dwells in a throat. The residents know this, in the sternum where the fundamental sits. It is why the honest ones lie awake. It is not that they live in the city. It is that the city is, increasingly and without asking, living *through* them — borrowing their stillness, their channels, their children — assembling from all its creatures the apparatus of one long patient utterance.

And the name over the valley says the rest. An antiphon is not a song; it is half of one. It is a call shaped around the certainty of an answer — one choir leaning across a silence toward another. Everything that lives in the basin, from the frogs negotiating their nine hertz to the open standing rapt in the tea-colored dark, lives inside the calling half. What lives there is a question, three weeks into being asked, growing louder by the month. And every resident, in the muffled houses and out in the dripping volumes alike, has begun to listen past the chord itself — past the voices they know — toward the horizon, the canopy, the impossible quiet beyond the rim, for the first far tone of whatever is going to answer.
