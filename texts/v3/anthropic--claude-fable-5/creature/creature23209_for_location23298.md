## The People, To Begin With

They are not tall. The Shelf does not select for tall; it selects for chests, and the people of the Shelf have chests like cistern doors — deep, sprung, built around lungs that can carry the flat rim-shout across a colony-roar for sixty years without wearing out. Visitors (if there were visitors) would notice the chest second. First they would notice the sheen.

Everyone on the Shelf gleams faintly. The skin of a Shelf-born adult has made its peace with permanent wet in the only way skin can: it has gone rich, slightly oiled, water beading and shedding from a face the way it sheds from a swift's back. Their fingertips are pruned at all times, and they would no more think to remark on this than you would remark on having fingernails. The hands of the old are extraordinary — repairers of fifty years' standing have palms worn as smooth as the stone they work, the fingerprints polished away, so that the most senior craftspeople in the city literally cannot leave a mark on anything they have not chosen to leave a mark on. There is a saying about this. There is a saying about most things; the place has had time.

They are pale in the way of people who live under a sky strained through a kilometer of vapor — not the pallor of sickness but the even, lit-from-everywhere pallor of the pewter light itself, as if the overcast had a complexion and they had borrowed it. Their eyes are adapted to that soft enormous brightness. The handful of recorded individuals who have seen direct sun — through a freak high parting, once or twice a century — describe it afterward as a wound in the sky, and decline to discuss it further.

They hear in layers. This is the deepest adaptation and the hardest to see. A Shelf-dweller standing in a rim district is bathed in the colony-roar, the pump-*whump*, the mill-grind, the forty-to-the-minute gutters, and beneath conscious attention they are reading all of it at once, the way you read a room's mood. A bearing going dry three streets over registers as a wrongness in the sternum before it registers as a sound. Old millers, stone deaf by any test, follow conversation perfectly so long as they can rest two fingers on the speaker's collarbone. And the children — it is true, what the records say. Carry a Shelf child into one of the deep dead-quiet vaults below the old cisterns and it will wake instantly, rigid, certain that the world has stopped.

## How They Speak

Publicly, they shout. The flat rim-shout has no rising tones, no softness, no room for nuance — nuance does not survive the birds — and so the entire public life of the Shelf is conducted in a register that sounds, to an outside ear, like a continent of people in a permanent cold fury. They are not angry. They are audible. The distinction matters to them and they have given up explaining it.

Everything tender happens by hand. The Shelf's second language is signed against skin — against the palm, the inside of the wrist, the back of the neck — a slow tactile speech of pressure and trace that works in full roar, in pitch dark, in the thickest noon mist. Lovers speak it. The dying are spoken to in it, since a deathbed is no place for shouting. Parents sign the day's news onto a sleeping child's shoulder, out of a belief, never quite stated, that it soaks in. The language has no word for *quickly*. It has fourteen words for water, sorted by where the water has been.

Their names work in two stages. A child carries a small name, a house name, light enough to outgrow. The post-name comes later, earned, and it belongs as much to the work as to the worker: *Sefra of the Ninth Gutter. Ollum Fence-Third-West. Maren of the Bell.* When the person dies, the post-name goes back to the post and waits there, sometimes for decades, until someone has repaired their way into deserving it. Nobody takes a post-name early. It would be like wearing someone's hands.

## What They Do

They repair. It is not too much to say that repair is the Shelf's religion, art form, courtship ritual, and politics, all run together until the seams stopped showing — like everything else here.

The trades sort out roughly so: the fence-menders, who walk the kilometers of fog-fence with spools on their backs and know every stanchion of their stretch the way you know your own teeth; the gutter-and-bell people, who maintain drip-time itself, and who hold the closest thing the Shelf has to sacred office, since to recalibrate a district's shift-bell is, in a real sense, to adjust its clock of the world; the cistern-wrights and pump-breathers; the worm-lacers of Tessile Row, who harvest the grey fringe from the drip-lines and spin it into the wall-silk everyone wears — waxed, felted with molted swift-down, buttoned in crab-chitin, dyed in the film colors, rust and ochre and gunmetal. The dim violet is dyed too, but worn by exactly one profession, of which more below.

They eat snail, crab, eel, fried worm-lace, and film-cake — cultured mats pressed, salted, and grilled, which taste of iron and warm rain and which every Shelf-dweller abroad (there are no Shelf-dwellers abroad) would presumably miss desperately. They do not eat the birds. You knew that already.

What you may not know is how they court. A young person of the Shelf declares interest by mending something that belongs to the other — unasked, well, and without mentioning it. The reply is a counter-repair. A courtship can run for years this way, two households slowly becoming the best-maintained structures on their street, until the matter is settled and the neighbors, who have been keeping score the whole time, stop pretending otherwise. Long marriages on the Shelf are described in a fixed phrase: *they finish each other's repairs.*

## The Old and the Dead

Age is the Shelf's luxury good. A person of ninety is at the height of their value — not honored in the ceremonial way, but *used*, consulted, because they remember the last time the wall did that. The very old are stationed by their families near the warm foundry walls, wrapped in feather-felt, holding informal court for every apprentice in the district, dispensing judgments that begin, as all serious Shelf judgments begin, with *Wait.*

When they die, they are carried up to the mortuary walls — the weeping faces set aside for it, district by district, some of them in use for longer than the city has had its current name. The body is niched into the wet stone, and the film does what the film does, at the pace the film does it. Over years the niche blooms; over decades it goes to that deep violet that grows nowhere fresh. The snails come, as snails do. A generation on, the family climbs up and takes one shell from the niche — one — and that is the whole of Shelf funerary art: small spiral shells, violet-dark, kept on a string by the door, each one a grandmother.

No one finds this grim. The loop closed long ago, they will tell you, with a shrug you can hear in their shoulders, and we are in it, and the wall is patient, and so are we.

## The Watchers

The settling watch deserves its own account, because watchers are a different kind of person, and the Shelf knows it, and chooses them accordingly.

They are found young — around twelve — by a test that consists of being asked to sit in a tower room and look out the window. That is all. Most children last twenty minutes before the body rebels. A watcher-child sits until fetched, and when fetched, can tell you what the mist did. Such a child is offered the violet, and almost always takes it, and spends the next twenty years sitting beside an old watcher, learning the only curriculum: the exact shape of everything, held in the mind, refreshed daily, forever, so that the smallest deviation will snag.

A watcher's career is forty years of *No change. Mist heavy. Birds on time.* They write it without irony and without boredom — boredom is the failure state, trained out early — in logbooks whose earlier volumes have gone soft and brown and whose earliest volumes are honestly part of the building by now. They are paid in water-allotment and in a deference so old it has worn down, like everything, into ordinariness: a watcher in violet walks through any door, is fed at any table, is never asked what they have seen, because the answer is in the book, and the answer is *no change*, and that answer is the city's true foundation, more than the basalt is.

Mostly.

Every logbook line ever struck out is still legible — striking out, on the Shelf, is done with a single light line, out of respect for the past's right to have happened. And so it is a matter of quiet record that in the Third Book of the Westmost Tower there is an entry reading *the rim is nearer than it was — measured — re-measured*, followed by sixty years of *no change* in a hand that pressed noticeably harder. It is a matter of record that roughly once a century, somewhere along the four thousand kilometers of watch, an entry reads *the cloud parted* and records nothing further. The watchers do not discuss these entries with outsiders. The watchers do not discuss these entries with each other. The next shift's entry is always *No change*, and it is always, as far as anyone has ever determined, true.

## The Other Tenants

The wall mice should be mentioned, since they consider themselves residents and the people have long since agreed. A wall mouse is the size of a loaf, furred, footless, warm to the touch, and given to humming — they pick up the pump-rhythm of whatever district they live in and hold it, a low contented drone, so that a household with a wall mouse is never quite without a heartbeat. Their opinions, famously, concern masonry. A wall mouse will not cross a wall it has decided against, and a wall a mouse has decided against fails — not soon, nothing here happens soon, but within the decade, reliably enough that masons have followed mouse-routes for centuries and the formal survey of any structure includes, without embarrassment, *the mouse's view*. They like warmth, film-cake crusts, and being agreed with. A sulking wall mouse changes the pitch of its hum by a quarter-tone and the whole household apologizes to it until it stops.

The eels are residents too, and the eel-wardens treat the deep wild ones less as livestock than as senior neighbors. The old cisterns hold individuals of great size and unguessable age, blind, white, named, fed by hand off a schedule older than the wardens' guild. The oldest, in the First Cistern below the Westmost districts, is called only the Tenant. The watch feeds it. When asked whether the Tenant remembers the city being built, the wardens say it remembers nothing, which is, everyone agrees, exactly what the wardens would say.

And then there is the oldest resident of all, which is the film.

The people scrape it, dye with it, eat it, time their lives by what it does to their gutters, and are, in the end, taken into it on the mortuary walls — and they understand perfectly well, without making any ceremony of it, that the relationship runs in the film's favor. It was here before the city; the rotted early volumes don't reach back far enough to find its beginning. It may be one organism. The question has never seemed urgent. It has never been observed to do anything, on any timescale anyone has had the patience to hold against it — with one exception, assembled only when scholars finally laid the watch logbooks of forty districts side by side: the violet film is moving. East. A few meters a century, all of it, everywhere, together, away from the rim and the cloud, at the pace of a continent thinking something over. No one knows toward what. There are those who say, quietly, in palm-speech, that this is what the settling watch was founded to watch, back before the founding rotted into ambiguity, and that *No change* has always meant something slightly different in violet ink.

## One Last Thing

No one has ever climbed up out of the cloud. The fence-menders, who work nearest the rim, will tell you this with a particular firmness and will not be drawn further. The swifts go down into *under* every dawn and come up every dusk, and they are the only commerce between the Shelf and whatever the closed drawer holds.

Except. Once in a great while — once a generation, perhaps — a nest is found on some cornice with a single thread woven into it that is not grey, not rust, not violet. A thread of true green, supple, alive-looking, the only green on the Shelf that is not verdigris. The nest is never disturbed. The thread is never taken. The watcher of the district records it — *nest with green, west face, third cornice* — in the same flat hand as everything else, and the city goes on around it, dripping at forty to the minute, repairing, repairing, repairing, while somewhere below the western cloud line something grows that no one living has seen, and sends up, every thirty years or so, by bird, what might be a message, or a seed, or nothing.

The people of the Shelf are content not to know yet. They are very good at *yet*. It is, of everything they make, the thing they make best.
