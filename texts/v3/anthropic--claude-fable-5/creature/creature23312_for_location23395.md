The first answer is the easiest to miss, because a traveler has already seen it without knowing: the color is alive.

Those rims of ochre and bone-white and old-copper green around every vent are not mineral stain. They are mats — films of something between a moss and a chemistry, thin as paint and older than the hills' patience, each one breathing the particular exhalation of its own vent. They do not spread. They do not recede. A ring that is eleven feet across has been eleven feet across since before there was anyone to measure it, and the boundary between living color and bare stone is sharp as a stencil's edge. Each vent wears its own livery and no two are quite the same shade, as though the basin assigned its stations their colors long ago and the matter was closed. The only motion in them is almost too slight to swear to: in the seconds before a vent fires, its ring brightens — a faint deepening, like a held breath — and dims again as the steam goes up. They know the schedule. Of course they know the schedule. They have never heard anything else.

The mats are the oldest residents, and the most numerous, and the least interesting to watch, which is why everyone watches the stitches instead.

That is the travelers' name for them — the flat slate-colored creatures of the warm seeps, hand-length, eyeless, dry-leathery to the touch (the few who have touched one report that it does not flinch; flinching is not in its repertoire; nothing in the basin does anything off the beat). They are called stitches because of how they cross the flats: one small advance per knock, body still as stone between beats, so that watching one travel is like watching a seam being sewn through the rock at the rate of the deep forge below — knock, stitch, stillness; knock, stitch, stillness. A stitch crossing a quarter mile of open stone is a journey of days. None of them has ever been seen to hurry. It is not clear the concept could be explained to them.

They have no eyes because eyes would tell them nothing. Light in the basin is plain and constant and falls on stone that does not change. What a stitch needs to know, it hears — or rather feels, through the flat of its belly pressed to the warm rock, which carries every register of the basin's work. Their geography is acoustic. A traveler navigates the flats by landmark; a stitch navigates by signature — the precise interval, in this spot, between the whistle's arrival and the knock's, the particular overtone the double-beat cone takes on when heard through forty feet of flowstone. Every point in the basin has its own version of the music. The stitches are never lost because they are never, strictly speaking, anywhere except at a known measure of the only piece there is.

They graze the mats — this is the closest thing to an economy the basin has — and the grazing changes nothing, because each stitch's route is a circuit, beat-mapped, that returns it to its starting seep after some great number of cycles, by which time the film has regrown to exactly the thickness it was. The routes interlock across the whole basin like the gear-train they are. Where two routes cross, two stitches meet: they arrive on the same knock, touch — briefly, blunt head to blunt head, a contact lasting one beat — and diverge on the next. This appears to be the entirety of their society. The meetings are not chance. They are written into the timetable like everything else, appointments kept across years of stone, and what passes in that single beat of contact no one knows. Possibly everything they have. Possibly nothing at all.

Here is the unsettling part, and it is the same unsettling part as everything else in the Foundry Flats: a surveyor once spent a deafening season counting them and arrived at two hundred and seventeen. Forty years later another count was made by someone who did not know of the first. Two hundred and seventeen. The basin keeps a constant number the way it keeps constant time. When a stitch dies — and they do die; a dead one simply stops, fades over weeks to the exact pale of the stone, and becomes, as far as anyone can tell, the stone — somewhere across the flats the grey mud turns over with its swallowing sound and a new stitch is in it, glistening, already still, already waiting for the knock. The mud pits, it turns out, are the nursery — those mixers that are never emptied have been turning the same batch for an age, and once in a long while the batch includes a passenger. No eggs have ever been found. No mechanism has ever been proposed that anyone liked. The mud turns over and is the same mud, except when it is the same mud plus one, and it is plus one only when the basin is minus one. The books balance. The books have always balanced.

The high crossing bird does not live here and never will, and the basin's residents make this almost insultingly clear: nothing reacts to it. The mats do not brighten, the stitches do not pause — the bird is the only event in the basin with no place in the sequence, and the basin's answer to an unscheduled event is to treat it as not occurring. The bird calls; the machinery swallows the call; the bird passes out over the rim. It is hard not to read its refusal to land as professional judgment.

Which leaves the last resident, the one travelers speak of least and lowest.

There is always exactly one.

A person, or near enough — someone who came over the rim as a traveler, the way travelers do, and did not leave when the noise began its wearing-down. What happens instead, in these rare ones, is the opposite of wearing: an alignment. They stop speaking first, since speech was already useless. Then their walking changes — anyone who watches long enough sees it — until they move the way the stitches move, one step per knock, still between beats, crossing the flats on a circuit of their own. They take shelter in the lee of the cones where the condensation runs; what they eat, if they eat, is as unanswered as it is for everything else here. They do not respond to hails, though they are not hostile; a traveler who plants themself in the path will be passed at a distance of a few feet, on the beat, by someone whose face holds the expression of a person listening very carefully to something that is going exactly as expected.

Never two. Never zero. When one is gone — and no one has ever found remains, any more than anyone finds dead stitches once the fading is done — the next has already, somewhere on the roads of the world, begun walking this way without quite deciding to. The count is kept. The travelers who camp on the rim call this resident the hand, and the name is bitterly precise: a vast workshop that built all its machinery without hands, running its full eternal shift without product or purpose, that nonetheless keeps — as if for the sake of some formality no one remembers — exactly one.

And below it all, the knock. When it sounds, the stitches orient — every one of them, wherever it is on its circuit, turning its blunt eyeless head a few degrees toward the same point under the basin's floor before taking its step. The mats brighten on the same beat, all of them at once, the only moment the whole basin's color lifts together. Whether what knocks down there is the deepest resident or merely the deepest machinery is a distinction the Foundry Flats has never been willing to draw about anything, and it does not draw it here. Perhaps nothing lives in the basin at all, in the sense a traveler means by the word — perhaps the mats and the stitches and the hand are not occupants but components, kept the way a clock keeps its jewel bearings: not housed, installed. Or perhaps that is what living here is, the only form of it the place permits, and the basin's residents are simply the ones who said yes.

Leave at dusk and you can test it. From halfway up either hill, look back at the right moment in the cycle — after the whistle, before the swallow — and you will see them all in their places in the pale light: the rings of color holding their breath, two hundred and seventeen stitches mid-stride, and one small upright figure crossing the flats on the beat, mid-shift, as always, forever.
