**Who Lives in the Steeped Hamlet**

The hamlet is not abandoned. It has, if anything, a higher population than it ever held in air — it is simply that the census has changed columns. Everyone who lives there now lives there the way the founders did: seriously, territorially, with strong opinions about doorways. The village taught its shape to the water, and the water taught it to everything that swims.

Begin with the char, because they would expect you to. The char are the burghers of the place. They are arctic char of the old landlocked kind, deep-bodied, pewter-flanked, their bellies flushed rose in autumn like men putting on their good coats for the season. They hold the lanes in loose ranks, each fish keeping a station it has kept for years — this one beneath the second lintel of the upper lane, that one in the lee of the trough in the square — and they pass through doorways with the unhurried entitlement of residents, never the curiosity of visitors. They have inherited the village's habits along with its streets. They are calm in the lanes, brisk in the open, and they observe a kind of curfew: at the bell's long brown note they all settle a hand's width lower in the water, the way a congregation sits.

The oldest of them lives in the covered cistern. The cistern was built to hold the village's drinking water, the cleanest and most defended room in the hamlet, and it is still the cleanest and most defended room in the hamlet, which is why she has it. She is enormous by the standards of the basin — old enough that her spots have gone vague, her jaw undershot, one eye pearled — and she does not patrol, because she does not need to. Younger char come to the cistern's stone mouth and hang there a while and go away. Nothing in the lake remembers a time before her. The minnows that crackle and drum around the cistern entrance all day are, in effect, her household noise, the way a great house always has voices in its forecourt; she ignores them with the totality only the very old can manage, and they school in her shadow because nothing that could eat them will come near her door.

The belfry congregation deserves its own entry. The small silver fish that occupy the bell tower — a species of whitefish fry and young roach, mixed, in numbers that change with the year — are the hamlet's only true crowd, its only public. They live in the warmest still water in the village, packed into the top of the tower around the bronze bell itself, and they have built their whole society around the instrument. They feed in its acoustic shadow. They school in the slow eddies its mass creates. And when it sounds, they perform the one civic ceremony the hamlet still keeps: they pour from the four belfry windows simultaneously, a silver detonation in slow motion, scatter to the rooftops, and then, over the following hour, sift home grain by grain, as if the village had rung itself to assembly and then rung itself to rest. No one ordained this. It happens perhaps once an hour, and it has happened, perhaps once an hour, for longer than any fish now living has been alive. It is the closest thing the hamlet has to a tradition, and like most traditions its practitioners do not know it is one.

The crayfish are the night guild. By day they are stones among stones, slate-colored, folded into the cut water-channels that edge every street — channels their builders meant for runoff, and which now run with crayfish instead. By night they come out and *work*. There is no other word for it. They patrol the channels end to end, clearing them of leaf-fall and dead snails and silt; they tick and scrape and rap; they meet at the channel junctions and have brief armored disagreements about precedence; they keep the gutters of a town that no longer has weather. The channels are clean. Nothing else in the hamlet is maintained on purpose, but the channels are clean, because the crayfish have confused inheritance with employment, and the result is indistinguishable from civic pride.

The snails are something older and stranger: they are the masons now. The great freshwater snails — shells the size of a child's fist, whorled, chalky, often a century's worth of growth rings deep — graze the walls in herds of a dozen, moving at the speed of shadow on a sundial. Where they pass, they strip the stone of film and fuzz and leave it bare, and so it is the snails, all unknowing, who keep the chisel marks legible, the doorpost inscriptions readable, the corners of the granite crisp under their green upholstery. The original masons dressed this stone once; the snails dress it forever. There is one curiosity in their work that no one can explain: on the wall of the square, beside the trough, there is a small carved niche — a shrine, perhaps, or a lamp-rest — and the snails do not graze it. They graze around it. The niche keeps its own thin coat of moss, soft and undisturbed, like a thing left respectfully alone, and whether this is chemistry or current or something in the carving that snails dislike, the effect is of a single household in the village still observing an old religion.

The newts are the commuters, the only residents who keep one foot in the upper world. Black-backed, ember-bellied, they patrol the mossed rooftops like wardens walking their rounds, but they must rise to breathe, and so all day they thread up and down between the hamlet and the sky — small dark messengers shuttling along the water column, carrying nothing, reporting to no one, but keeping the road between the two worlds worn smooth. In spring the males flare their crests and dance on the roof-meadows in the caustic light, and the rooftops of the drowned village briefly host more courtship than its bedrooms ever did. Their young are born in the eaves. They are the only citizens for whom the hamlet is genuinely a hometown in the old sense — born there, raised there, returning there to breed.

And then there are the smallest masons, who may be the truest heirs of all. In the silt of the square and along the lane floors live caddis larvae, each one a soft grub that armors itself by building — and what they build with, here, is the village. They cement around their bodies little tubes of whatever the place provides: grains of granite, flakes of the original mortar-less stone, fragments of snail shell, once in a while a sliver of weathered roof slab. Each larva is a hand-built stone house a thumbnail long, walking. The people who cut these terraces are gone, and their architecture is dissolving into a green memory of itself, but at the bottom of the square, by the hundreds, in miniature, in the same local stone, with the same patient fitting of piece to piece, construction continues. Nobody told them. The material told them, the way it told the first builders.

The largest single inhabitant is not an animal. The weed — the dark ribbon-leaved growth that wears the whole town — is, in great part, one organism: a clonal colony, root-thatched through every joint and seam, the upper lane connected to the bell tower connected to the last wall by a single slow green body. By that accounting, the green second hamlet is not a forest but a resident, the oldest one, older than the great char, older perhaps than the flood — a thing that moved into the village while there were still villagers, took a room in a wall joint, and has been, ever since, very gradually becoming the entire building. The hollow archway on the upper lane, the one that is pure vegetation holding the shape of a vanished door, is its masterpiece and its nursery: inside that green vault, in still water, in the body of the arch itself, fry shelter by the thousand each spring. The village's most fragile inhabitants live inside its most faithful memory.

Visitors come from above. Goldeneye ducks dive in summer and loot the roof-meadows, blunt and rude, gone in thirty seconds. A dipper works the inlet stream. Once in a few years an otter finds the lake, and for a fortnight the hamlet endures something it has not known since the flood — a predator that uses doorways — and the lanes empty and the belfry packs tight; then the otter exhausts the easy hunting and crosses the ridge, and the ranks reform, station by station, with the relieved fussiness of a town after soldiers leave.

And there are no ghosts. This should be said plainly, because a drowned village invites them. The people left before the water came — the slow flood gave them decades of warning, and they took their dead and their bells' smaller cousins and their names with them, all but the one bell too heavy to want. What remains is not a haunting but an estate in probate, competently administered: the lake rings the bell, the current sweeps the lanes, the snails keep the stone, the crayfish keep the gutters, the caddis keep the craft, the weed keeps the shapes, and the cold keeps everything honest. The hamlet's residents do not know they live in a village. They live in cover, current, warmth, and stone, and the village is simply what those things are shaped like here.

But the shape persists, and shapes instruct. Whoever lives in the Steeped Hamlet — fish or snail or single sprawling plant — lives a little more orderly than lake life elsewhere, a little more settled, a little more *municipal*, drilled hourly by a bell nobody rings into being, against all biology, neighbors.
