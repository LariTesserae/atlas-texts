**Who lives in the Dovewell**

Start with the obvious tenants and work outward, because the obvious tenants are only the middle of it.

---

**The pigeons** hold the broad waist of the dome, the middle latitudes, and they hold it the way old families hold a neighborhood — not by force but by sheer continuity of occupation. These are not the gleaning, scattering pigeons of open country. They are *of* the Dovewell in a way that has reshaped them slightly: a shade smaller than their wild cousins, broader in the chest, with a plumage that has drifted over uncounted generations toward the colors of the chamber itself. Clay-rose breasts. Wing coverts the grey-brown of old plaster. Here and there a bird barred in white and rust like the mineral streaks on the walls, and the keepers will tell you those are the *wall-marked* ones, lucky, descended (they say) from a hen who nested in a weeping niche and was rained on by lime-water all her brooding life until it ran in her blood.

Each pigeon knows its own niche the way you know your own name — without effort, without the possibility of error. A bird returning at speed through the oculus drops through three hundred feet of vertical confusion, banks once around the curve of the dome, and lands in its fist-sized hollow among forty thousand identical hollows without slowing down. They are never wrong. Squabs are raised in the same niche their parents were raised in when the line allows it, and the deepest, smoothest, most knuckle-worn niches — the ones whose maker's hand-marks were polished away centuries ago — carry lineages older than any human family the keepers can name. There are dynasties in that wall. The pigeons do not know this. They simply continue, which is what a dynasty is.

They are also, frankly, the place's metabolism. The pigeons go out at first light through the oculus in long unspooling threads, feed in fields nobody in the Dovewell will ever see, and come back carrying the surplus of the upper world in their crops and their guts. Everything below them runs on what falls from them. They are the rain of this small climate.

---

**The swifts** are the upper atmosphere, and they are tenants in only the loosest sense, because a swift's relationship to standing still is theological at best. They occupy the high courses near the oculus — the youngest niches, the least worn, because swift feet barely touch them — and they treat the Dovewell not as a home but as the one fixed point in a life otherwise made entirely of air. They eat aloft, drink aloft, sleep aloft in the high dark over the chimney; the niche is for eggs and for the brief annual concession of raising young, and even then a brooding swift gives the impression of a creature gripping the wall against its own nature, like a sailor holding a doorframe in a storm.

But they are the timekeepers. The pigeons mark the hours loosely; the swifts mark the year. They arrive all at once on a day the keepers can predict within a week, screaming down the oculus in that roaring braid, and the chamber's whole sound changes register the moment they're in — brightens, sharpens, gains its rattle. And on the day they leave, they leave *all at once*, the dome's upper third going silent in a single morning, and the keepers say you can hear the dome itself grieve a little, the high empty niches ringing faintly for days afterward at frequencies nothing remaining is producing. That part may be the keepers being keepers. The high niches are tuned sharp, and the wind across the oculus plays them. But it does happen, and it does sound like missing something.

---

**The lower folk** — the bats, the leathery sleepers in the warm wet courses near the floor — are the night shift, and they are the residents the Dovewell seems to have *attracted* rather than been built for. No one pressed those low niches with bats in mind. The bats found them: blood-warm, damp, defensible, and beautifully placed at the bottom of a chimney full of the insects that rise off the steaming floor at dusk. They pack themselves in two and three to a hole, fur to fur, a soft brown caulking in the lowest courses, and through the day they are the quietest thing in the chamber — but put your ear near the low wall and the niches *tick* and *seethe* faintly, thousands of small adjustments, a wall full of sleep.

They pour out at dusk in the opposite spiral to the swifts' descent, smoke running backwards up the column of fading light, and for a few minutes at each end of the day the two populations pass through each other in the chimney — swifts corkscrewing down, bats threading up — and never collide. The keepers consider this exchange the holiest ordinary thing the Dovewell does. Two nations trading the sky, twice daily, without negotiation, in a doorway the width of a wagon wheel.

The bats are also the chamber's defense. Nothing soft-bodied and winged that comes up off the floor at night survives the gauntlet of them, which is why the pigeons — packed at densities that should breed plague and parasites — stay strangely clean. The bats eat the Dovewell's diseases in their larval forms. Nobody arranged this. It is simply one of the many balances the place fell into and now maintains with the indifference of a thing that works.

---

**The floor** is its own country, and the longer you stand in the Dovewell the more you understand that the dramatic populations overhead are, in a sense, just the weather of this country — the source of its rain.

The deep black guano-soil hosts a nation of small things in numbers that make the bird-counts laughable. Tenebrionid beetles, glossy as wet ink, who do the first coarse work of breaking down what falls. Springtails in their millions, a living glitter you only see when the light-column crosses the floor at the right angle and the surface of the soil *shimmers*. Fly larvae in the wettest patches near the basins; the flies they become rarely live an hour past dusk. Pale, eyeless, soft-bodied things in the lowest strata that may have ancestors who arrived on a pigeon's foot generations ago and have been quietly speciating in the dark ever since — the keepers do not have names for these and have never needed any. And binding it all, threading the whole made ground like a nervous system, a white mycelium that fruits twice a year into small grey clay-colored mushrooms which the keepers harvest, dry, and trade upstairs, and which are the only thing the Dovewell exports that the upper world considers a delicacy rather than fertilizer.

There is one snake. There is always exactly one snake — a clay-colored rat snake, thick as a wrist, who lives in a gap behind the lowest course of brick near the stair and works the bottom three rows of niches at her unhurried leisure. The keepers do not remove her. The keepers' grandmothers did not remove *her* grandmother. She takes eggs and the occasional squab and an injured bird now and then, and the pigeons have long since priced her into their architecture: the bottom rows are where the young pairs and the unlucky nest, and losing a clutch to the snake is understood — insofar as a pigeon understands anything — as the rent the low niches charge. When she sheds, the keepers find the skin and hang it from a peg by the stair, and the pegs going up the stairwell carry the papery ghosts of years of her, oldest at the top, like a calendar.

---

**The plants** are residents too, though they are residents the way a pilgrim is a resident — rooted, but oriented entirely toward something passing. The migrating garden in the light-column lives a strange devotional life: every individual plant in it leans along the daily path of the sun-spot, and the whole rosette of ferns and pale grass has, over time, elongated into an ellipse that maps the light's seasonal drift across the floor. The garden is a sundial that grew itself.

The fig sapling deserves its own mention because the fig sapling has *plans*. It is the only thing in the Dovewell with ambitions about the oculus. Figs are patient, root-strong, masonry-splitting things, and this one — sprouted from a seed in some pigeon's gut, fed on the richest soil in the region, reaching up a column of guaranteed light toward a guaranteed sky — is the single greatest long-term threat to the structure, and the keepers know it, and they have not pulled it. They prune it. Every year, a light hand, a negotiation. The standing argument among the keepers is whether the fig will one day have to die or whether the Dovewell, which has absorbed everything else that ever arrived in it, will find a way to absorb a tree. The older keepers favor the second view. The dome, they point out, has eaten stranger things than patience.

---

**The keeper.** One at a time, usually; sometimes a keeper and a learner, which is how it continues. The current one is a small, deliberate person of indeterminate age who lives not in the chamber but in a wide place in the stairwell, second turning, where a sleeping shelf and a lamp niche and a row of snake-skin pegs were built into the wall by some predecessor so long ago that the shelf, like everything else, has gone round at the corners and cupped in the middle from the weight of sleeping bodies.

The keeper's work is small and constant: patch the dome where it weeps too freely, harvest squabs and eggs from the agreed rows on the agreed days (the rows rotate; the pigeons, the keepers insist, keep better track of the rotation than the keepers do), draw off the guano in shallow scrapes that never go deeper than the year's deposit, prune the fig, dry the mushrooms, replace a basin maybe once in a working lifetime. The keeper goes up to the surface to trade and comes back down the same day, always, and keepers have a reputation in the town above for being difficult to talk to in the open air — squinting, abbreviated, vaguely seasick under all that unfocused sound. The Dovewell's acoustics rewire a person. A keeper of long standing can stand in the center of the floor, eyes shut, and tell you from the chord of the dome which neighborhoods are brooding, which niches have newly emptied, whether the snake is moving, whether weather is coming down the oculus before any cloud is visible — because every one of those things changes which resonators are stopped with bodies and which are open and sounding. The keeper does not call this a skill. The keeper calls it *listening to the wall*, the way you'd call reading *looking at the page*.

It is not a lonely post, exactly. The keeper is the only human resident, but the keeper is never for one instant alone, and the keepers who last are the ones for whom that arithmetic comes out as company rather than crowding. They die downstairs when they can manage it. There is a low course of niches behind the stair, deeper than the rest, pressed bigger than a fist, and what is in them is not discussed with visitors, and the bats roost thickest there, and the keepers consider that fitting.

---

**And the dome.** This is the part the keepers say plainly and visitors hear as poetry, but it is meant plainly: the Dovewell itself is the senior resident. Everything else — pigeons, swifts, bats, beetles, fungus, snake, fig, keeper — turns over. Generations of all of them have run through this chamber like water through a basin. The dome is the only individual present that was present at the beginning, and it has spent the whole intervening span doing what living things do: drinking, breathing, warming, mending, shedding, growing slowly less geometric and more itself. The hand-pressed niches were its first cells. The mineral-striped weep-channels are its circulation. The patch-scabs are its healing. The chord it returns to a clap is, depending on whom you ask, either the simple physics of a few thousand tuned cavities or the voice of every singer who ever pressed a fist into the wet clay, held in the wall like a breath held for centuries — and the keepers will tell you that the difference between those two explanations is smaller than you think, and gets smaller the longer you live down here.

What lives in the Dovewell is a household. Not a habitat, not a colony — a household, in the old sense: many lives of unequal size and unequal span, bound by a roof, organized by light and warmth and the slow rain of what falls from above, each member doing some work the others depend on without any of them having agreed to anything. The pigeons feed it. The bats clean it. The swifts time it. The floor digests for it. The snake culls for it. The fig threatens it just enough to keep the keeper attentive. The keeper mends it just enough to let it continue mending itself.

And twice a day, at the exchange of the sky, the household holds its one assembly — the braid descending, the smoke ascending, every empty niche ringing under the wingbeats — and for those few minutes there is no useful answer to the question of who lives there, because the question assumes a difference between the dwelling and the dweller, and the Dovewell, for those few minutes, declines to make one.
