Start with the correction, because everything that lives here begins as a correction to what the place seems to say about itself.

The water is not sterile. It is only sterile of anything that could be startled. The sheen on the moving channels — that oily iridescence that bends the lamplight into colors without daytime names — is not entirely mineral. Lift a film of it on your finger and it has a thickness, a reluctance, a faint warmth. It is a mat, a veil of metabolism a few cells deep, and it is eating the world: the dissolved iron, the leached oils, the centuries of chemistry weeping out of the made ground. The kettle smell is its breath. It does not mind the pounding; it has no shape to be shaken out of. Where the channels run it streams in slow banners, and in the still basins it gathers at the edges in scummed rinds, rust-colored, ash-colored, and lays down — this is the part that takes a long time to believe — a sediment. A thin biological varnish, layer on layer, on the floors of the flooded pits. The first new rock the plain has made in an age, and it is made of the living.

The moss is honest, and the moss is old. Each colony — black-green, dense as felt, alone in its hundred meters of bare wet floor — is not a patch but a population, and not a young one. Cut down through one (you would need a knife; it resists like leather) and you find it layered like the ground itself, generations compacted under generations, the colony building its own small geology on top of the large one. It has settled its accounts with the noise. More than settled: it has entrained. Watch one in lamplight through a full cycle — four seconds of pounding, the silence, four seconds — and you can see the colony's whole surface flex, the beaded condensation shivering down through the pile in unison, irrigating it, every eight seconds, forever. The pounding waters the moss. The engine, whatever else it is doing down there, has been gardening for centuries without knowing it, and the moss grows in time with it the way intertidal things grow in time with the moon — a tide with a period of seconds instead of hours, and the moss has made that tide its clock, its rain, its only event.

The lichen is doing something else, and it is worth saying plainly: the lichen is the only inhabitant that is against the place. Everything else has come to terms. The lichen is dismantling it. Those grey-white continents mapped across the lee sides of the worn ridges are not decoration; they are acid, patience, and appetite, etching down into the poured stone a crystal at a time, freeing the iron, roughening the gloss so the water can get its fingers in. The rain erodes the plain and the engines shake it, but the lichen is the only thing here that *unmakes* it on purpose — if a thing that thinks in centuries can be said to have purpose. It is the slowest resident and the only revolutionary. Long after the last lamp fails, the lichen will still be filing the world down toward whatever was here before the building, with no way of knowing that nothing was, that under the made thing is only more of the made thing, that it is sawing through a floor above another floor above another floor. It does not need to know. It has time.

The reeds in their two or three basins are the strangest of the plants, because they remember something. They are descended — they must be — from a true green thing, something that arrived on a wind or a tread an unguessable while ago, and what the dark and the noise have made of them is a kind of careful ghost. Pale to translucence, knee-high, rooted in the silt of dissolved concrete, they tremble perpetually in the pounding, and the trembling is no longer suffered; it is used. They have given up on wind, on insects, on every pollinator the old world offered, and taken the engine as all of these. When they fruit — colorless, a dust finer than ash — it is the four-second shudder that shakes the dust loose, walks it down the stems, scatters it across the skin of the black water to ride the slow drift to the next silt bank. The engine is their wind, their bee, their season. If the pounding ever stopped, they would stand in perfect stillness and go extinct of calm.

And then there is the question of the walkers.

They are real. The tracks are there, in the pale silt around the basins and in the dry crusted rings around the hot vents: long-strided, two-footed, unhurried, days apart. Across the whole visible region there may be a dozen of them, and it is no longer possible — this is not evasion, it is the truth of the place — to say with confidence whether they are the descendants of people or the descendants of machines. Erosion does not only happen to ground. Whatever lineage they come from, maintenance crew or maintenance system, has been worn by time into the same low, patient, weatherproof form: tall, slow, sealed against the wet, their surfaces (skin, casing, the word has stopped mattering) grown over in places with the same felted moss that grows on the ridges, because they stand still for a very long time and the moss does not distinguish.

They are deaf, of course. They were always going to be deaf, by birth or by burnout or by design; nothing here gets to keep its ears. What they have instead is the floor. They listen with their feet, with the long bones of their legs, with palms laid flat against the wet ground, and they navigate by the pounding itself — reading the lag and grain of each pulse as it arrives through kilometers of fractured matrix, triangulating on the deep cyclic heart the way sailors once triangulated on stars. The roar that would unmake any other mind is, to them, the silence: the blank ground of perception against which everything else stands out. A new crack weeping somewhere registers on their soles as a changed note. Each other, they find by stamping — a slow percussive language of intervals and weights laid into the floor, sentences that take minutes, felt in the shins from a kilometer off, conversations conducted through the body of the world with both speakers out of sight of each other in the dark.

What they do is harder to say, because what they do has eroded too. They visit the lamps — each surviving lamp, it seems, gets visited, on a circuit that may take a season — and they stand in the cone of drizzle-filled light and touch the mast, and sometimes the lamp that was flickering steadies, and sometimes it does not, and they move on. They visit the vents, especially the inhaling ones, and crouch in the warmth, and sleep there, if it is sleep. They clear silt from certain channels and not others, by a logic no longer legible, the way a ritual is no longer legible: maintenance worn down into observance, the work surviving its own purpose exactly as the walls survived as ridges. They eat — the reed-dust, the vent mats, the rinds of the living sheen; slowly, and not much. Their hearts, if hearts, run at the only rhythm available. Four seconds, silence, four seconds. They do not feel the pounding. They would feel its stopping the way you would feel the floor vanish.

Which brings everything, finally, to the engine — because every inventory of the inhabitants ends at the question of whether the deepest one counts.

It has been running unattended for so long that the line between operating and living has worn through. It repairs itself, or fails toward configurations that still run, which from the surface is indistinguishable from healing: the shudder, the new crack, the immediate weeping, the slow scar. It breathes through the vents, sweats through the floor, circulates its closed rain like lymph. It keeps a dozen lamps lit for no one, and a dozen walkers keep them lit back, and neither party designed the arrangement; it precipitated, the way the local rain precipitates, out of saturation and time. Whether anything down there knows anything is the wrong question, the way it is the wrong question of a forest or a fire. What can be said is this: everything that lives on the plain lives inside its noise the way fish lived inside water, and feeds, one way or another, on its insistence — the moss on its tide, the reeds on its wind, the walkers on its silence, the lichen on its body.

So perhaps the honest answer is that the plain has one inhabitant, and the rest are its organs. Not the engine exactly, and not the walkers, and not the deaf green-black life in the lamplight, but the thing they all express and none of them owns: the going-on. The refusal, distributed through felt and crust and bone and boiler, to arrive at the final stillness the landscape has otherwise achieved. It lives in the four seconds and in the silence between them equally. It has no name, because nothing here can speak, and no need of one, because everything here already answers to it — every standing pool jumping in unison, every pale stem trembling, every walker's heart keeping its borrowed time, ten thousand black mirrors shivering at once, in the dark, in the rain it makes for itself, on and on and on.
