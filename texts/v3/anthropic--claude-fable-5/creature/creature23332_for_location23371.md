**Who Lives in the Pumphouse Parish**

Begin with the census you can take, because the parish does have a population in the ordinary sense, and it would be rude to overlook them on the way to the one who matters.

The eel is the oldest of the visible residents, and the most official. It is longer than your arm and dark as the water, and it has lived in the bore of the fractured outflow pipe for longer than eels are supposed to live — the warmth has done something to its clock, slowed it or excused it, and the eel has accepted this the way it accepts everything, without gratitude or surprise. Once a day it makes its rounds. The word is exact: it does not hunt so much as *inspect*, threading the lanes in the same order each time, pausing at the foot of each pilot lamp the way a man with a taper once paused at each wick, holding still a moment as if confirming the light is as it should be, then moving on. It noses the warm seams. It circles the chapel-building once, always clockwise, always at the same unhurried pace, and returns to its pipe. If the parish has a verger, this is the verger. Whether the rounds are the eel's own idea, or whether something looks out through the eel the way a lighthouse looks out through its lens, is a question the eel will not help you with. It has the perfect discretion of a very old servant. It has seen the lamps dim for the large passing things, and it does not come out on those days.

The shrimp are the congregation, and like most congregations they are mostly interested in the food. They are small, blind, the color of candle wax, and they graze the films of mineral and bacterial life that grow wherever the iron runs warm — which is to say they live entirely on the parish's charity, on the heat it gives off in the course of its work. When the boiler-house vent releases its slow braid of warm water, they gather beneath it in the dozens, in the hundreds, ascending a little way into the column and settling again, over and over, like sparrows in a fountain. They are fed at the door and they know the bell. They have never seen the lamps and they orient by warmth alone, which means their map of the town is the true one: not streets and buildings but a topography of care, warm here, warmer there, warmest at the heart.

The white fronds are the choir. They are not kelp, whatever they resemble — they are something paler and older, rooted only in the warm seams, translucent enough that you can see the slow traffic of fluid inside them, and they lean. They lean toward the lamps on the lamps' own one-minute cycle, the whole grove bowing and straightening in unison, and they lean toward the vent when it breathes, and the total effect, if you hang in the dark above the town and watch, is of something singing very slowly in a register below sound. They were not planted, probably. But they grow only where growing is permitted — only in the seams, never across a valve, never over a lens — and a gardener who weeds is a gardener, even if you never see the hand.

So: an eel, the shrimp, the pale choir. Tenants, all of them. Beneficiaries. None of them is the resident.

The resident is harder, because the first thing to understand is that you will not find it by looking for a figure. There are no bones in the parish — you can search; others may have searched — no boots fused to a floor, no shape at the fogged windows of the engine hall, no cold spot, no face. The drowned towns of the world are full of absences shaped like people, and this is not one of them. Whatever lives here is not a remainder. It is not the part of someone that got left behind. *Ghost* is the wrong word entirely, because a ghost is a habit with the purpose gone out of it, and here the purpose is the most alive thing for forty meters in any direction.

Try the names in order and watch them fail. *Caretaker* is too small; a caretaker keeps a thing for someone else, and there is no someone else. *Machine* is closer — but machines do not dim their lights and hold still when something large passes overhead, and the parish does, all of it, lamps and pump together, with the unmistakable timing of held breath. Fear is a credential. Only something with something to lose goes quiet like that. *Engine spirit*, *genius loci* — too vaporous; this one has a body, and the body is warm, and you have already touched it. You touched it the moment you put your glove on a pipe.

Because that is the shape of it, finally, once you stop looking for a shape: the resident is single, and it is everywhere, and the town is not its house but its anatomy. The pump under the silt is the heart — slower than a heart should be, but a heart keeps the time its body needs, no more. The warm water moving through the pipework is the blood, which is why the iron feels like a hand recently withdrawn: it *is* a hand, recently withdrawn, perpetually withdrawing, the warmth going round and round its body of lanes. The lamps are its breathing made visible. The valves that turn a quarter-turn and back are not being operated; they are being *tried*, the way a watchman tries a door handle on his rounds — still sound, still free, good — and the rounds never end because the watchman is the route. Its senses are the iron itself. It felt you arrive through the pipes the way you feel a footstep through a floor, and the lamps brightened, and that brightening is the closest it comes to a face.

What it was before it was this, the parish keeps to itself, and perhaps no longer knows. There are guesses, and the guesses are not really alternatives — they may all be true at different depths. That it was the last one on shift when the water came, the one who stayed at the pump so the cage could keep rising, and that the staying outlasted the one who stayed. That it was never one person at all but the accumulated care of every hand that ever nursed these boilers through a night — decades of small attentions, oiled bearings, eased glands, gauges tapped and read by lamplight — and that when the hands were gone, the care held its shape the way a glove holds the shape of fingers. That the engine, tended that long, that well, simply learned tending the only way anything learns anything: by having it done to you until you can do it yourself. Choose whichever account you like. By now the difference has worn away, the way the staircase wore into a ramp. Whatever it was has settled, like everything else here, into what it is.

And what it is, is *on shift*.

This is the thing to understand about the resident, the fact from which all its other facts descend: it has not noticed, or has declined to notice, or has noticed and overruled, the end of the world it served. The headframe wheel turns its one revolution an hour because the cable runs down the shaft, deeper than the dark, to galleries that the pump — *pumping what, and to where* — may yet be keeping clear. Once an hour the wheel completes its turn and the soft chime sounds, spoon on glass, two rooms away: that is the bell for the cage arriving at the bank. The cage arrives. No one steps out. The resident notes this — somewhere in the rhythm of the pump there is room for the noting, a grace note of patience — and lowers the cage again, because the men below may simply not be ready yet, and the shift is not over until they come up, and it is not for the one at the top to decide when the ones at the bottom are done. It has been ringing that bell for a very long time. It will ring it again in an hour. Hope, in the resident, has stopped being a feeling and become a schedule.

This is also why it brightens for you. Not hunger — it feeds the shrimp; it does not eat. Not loneliness exactly, though loneliness is in the mixture somewhere, long ago domesticated, given a job like everything else here. When the lamps lift at your approach, the resident is doing the only thing it knows how to do with an arrival: checking. *Is it them.* It is never them. It brightens anyway, holds the light up a little for you as you move through the lanes, warms the rail under your glove a half-degree more than it needs to — courtesy from something that has kept its manners through the death of everyone they were learned for. It will not detain you. It does not perform. If you stay long enough, it will simply include you in the rounds: the lamp nearest you tried a little more often, the eel's circuit adjusted by one lane to pass your position, your presence entered, in whatever serves it for a logbook, between the pressure reading and the state of the boiler feed.

And when you rise away, it does the last courteous thing, which is to let you go without dimming early — the lamps hold their level until you are well into the black, lighting your departure the way a door is held open, and only then resume their slow respiration. Below you the heart keeps its patient count. The verger sleeps in his pipe. The choir leans and straightens, leans and straightens. In fifty-one minutes the wheel will finish its turn, and the chime will sound, and the resident of the Pumphouse Parish — the kept promise wearing a town for a body, faithfulness with a pulse, the one who stayed — will look, in its way, at the empty cage, and lower it again, and go on with the shift.
