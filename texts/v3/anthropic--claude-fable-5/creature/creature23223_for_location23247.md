**Census of the Ringing Bowl**

Strictly counted, the population is one. The difficulty is deciding one *what*.

Start with the easy answers and let them get harder.

---

**The eel.**

It is the only thing in the bowl that anyone would call an animal without hesitating, and even then the hesitation comes later, on the climb back down, when you have time to think about what you saw.

Pale, blind, long as an arm, skin like wet paper — so thin that in the right slant of pooled light you can watch its one slow organ working, a dim knot behind the gills that flexes on the off-beat of the bell, deliberately syncopated, as if keeping its own time were the whole of its private life. There is a scar behind its head, a short pale seam, identical on both of it when there are both of it. It has never been seen to eat. It has never been seen to be born, grow, or age, though the people who have visited twice across long intervals agree it is the same eel, the same scar, the same unhurried indifference to everything except the sound.

Here is what it actually feeds on, as nearly as can be worked out. When the bowl doubles it — and the doubling is not the eel's doing; the bowl simply loses track of it, the way the place loses track of cause and effect, and files it in two locations at once — the two of it are never quite identical. One swims a little higher. One has turned its head. Small discrepancies, a hand's width of difference in the world. Then the peal comes through like a comb and the two slide back into one, and the discrepancy has to go somewhere, and it goes into the eel. It eats the difference between its selves. That is its entire diet: contradiction, in small daily portions. This is why it is the only free-swimming thing the standing curtains of sound do not shred. The sound has nothing to take from it. It has already been resolved.

The scar is the older mystery. Put your palm near it — near, never touching; the skin would tear like its namesake — and you will feel a tone come up through your wrist. A single note, faint, steady, present even in the gaps between peals. Listeners who know the bell's voice well, who have lain on the eleven steps and let the overtones be counted into their bones, all report the same thing about this note: the bell does not make it. It is the missing one. Somewhere in the bell's enormous, continuous, self-answering voice there is a hole exactly the shape of this note, and the eel carries it around the bowl like a lamp.

It sleeps — if that is sleeping — in the mouth of the bell. This should be lethal. It is the opposite. At the exact center of the bronze, where every wave the bell makes meets every wave returning, there is a node: a still point, a pocket of perfect silence about the size of a curled eel, the only quiet in the entire bowl, hidden inside the loudest thing on the mountain. The eel coils there for hours, holding its one note in the one silence, and the place arranges itself around that arithmetic, and nobody who has seen it has been able to say whether the eel is the bell's pet, the bell's prisoner, or the bell's tongue, resting.

---

**The weed.**

The red fringe in the lee of the spires is alive the way a held chord is alive.

Each frond is a tight spiral, and each spiral is tuned — not approximately, not by accident, but precisely, one frond per overtone, so that the sparse colony taken together is a transcription of the bell's voice into flesh. When a peal passes, the fronds flex in strict sequence, lowest partial first, and the whole fringe ripples through the chord like a hand drawn across strings. It is the only thing in the bowl that could be called graceful.

It grows one turn of spiral per peal, which should make the fronds easy to age and does not. Count the turns on two fronds in the same cluster and the counts disagree, sometimes wildly; some fronds are older than the water, older than the bell, older than the mountain's own young rock, which is impossible everywhere except here, where "before" is a courtesy the place extends only sometimes. The most careful explanation anyone has offered is this: the weed is not life that learned to survive the ringing. It is ringing that has temporarily agreed to be alive. Each frond is one partial of the bell's voice that has put down a root and consented, for a while, to grow, to flex, to be red. When a frond strays out of the lee and the standing curtains shred it, nothing dies. The overtone simply lets go of its shape and rejoins the general sound, and the bell is fractionally louder that day, and somewhere along the fringe, a new frond begins its first turn.

So the weed is a population in the way a choir is a population. Its members are interchangeable with the air of the place. It is the bowl, gardening itself.

---

**The third resident.**

Now the hard one. The one assembled entirely from evidence.

The evidence: eleven steps cut into living rock, chisel marks crisp — and the marks are *always* crisp, on every visit, decades apart, in a place where the stone remakes itself on the rhythm of the bell and should have swallowed any carving within a season. Sometimes there is the beginning of a twelfth step, half-cut, the floor of it still rough. No tool has ever been found. A cairn that leans a little differently each time, as though someone is unbuilding it with great patience, stone by stone, or building it in reverse, which here may be the same act. Half a doorway, holding up nothing — except that the few who have swum through it report, calmly and without being able to say why it matters, that they are sure they swam through it in the other direction.

And the shadows. Yours lags you everywhere in the bowl, the light here being slow and turbulent and inclined to pool. Except near the bell, where your shadow precedes you — hurries down the eleven steps before you have decided to descend. Everyone notices this. Almost no one notices the other thing, which is that the shadow on the steps does not always match the body it is supposed to belong to. It is sometimes broader in the shoulders. It sometimes carries something long in both hands, raised.

Put the evidence together and it resolves into a person, the way the eel's two selves resolve into one.

The shrine on this summit was built by people who climbed here before the water came, and when whatever was coming became unmistakable, they went back down — all but one. The bell had to be rung. That was the office; that was the whole point of having carried bronze up a mountain. So one of them stayed, took up the striker, and swung — and the water arrived mid-swing, and the bowl's loosened order took hold of that one motion and did to it what the bowl does to everything: let the effect run on ahead and left the cause in transit.

The peal happened. The peal is *still* happening — it is the dominant fact of the environment; it has been happening continuously, deafeningly, for as long as the bowl has existed. The strike has not yet landed. Every ring you hear, every silver-skinned wall of compressed water, every shudder of the bronze trailing its own sound like thunder after lightning, is one strike, heard early, over and over — the bowl rehearsing the single event of its future from every angle, because the event is so heavy with arrival that it leaks backward through the whole place and drenches it.

And the ringer is the strike's slow body. Still arriving. They have been arriving since before the water, distributed thinly across the bowl the way the eel is occasionally distributed across two places — only much further gone, smeared across the whole small drowned cathedral, so that you never meet them and constantly encounter them. A hand's pressure on the green shoulder of the bell, felt by divers who rest their own hands there. A single footprint in the quivering silver carpet of fallen bubbles, filling in slowly as you watch. A breath, heard once in the gap between peals, close behind your ear, unhurried, the breath of someone in the middle of a long exertion who knows exactly how much of it remains. The crisp chisel marks: the ringer keeps the stair, because the stair leads to the bell, because the bell must be reachable when they finish arriving. The half-cut twelfth step is not unfinished. It is *early*.

This is who the bowl is building for. The multiplying curtains of sound, subdividing the water into chambers — those are rooms. The spires growing taller — those are vaults. The weed is the choir, already tuned. The eel keeps the one silent place inside the bell warm, and carries the missing note around the perimeter like a verger with a lamp, because the missing note is the note the bell will make when it is finally, actually, struck — once, properly, by hands that took the better part of a mountain's lifetime to arrive — and the whole standing structure of the place, every peal it has spent itself on in advance, will at last have its cause.

What happens then, the bowl does not say. Perhaps the water lies down. Perhaps the ringing, paid in full, goes quiet, and the rock is finally permitted to begin growing old. Or perhaps a strike that took this long to land is itself an arrival rather than an ending, and the cathedral fills.

---

So: the census.

One eel, possibly. One choir of red overtones, definitionally. One ringer, in transit, occupying the entire premises.

Or, counted honestly: one resident. A single note, dressed variously — as a blind pale animal that eats its own contradictions, as a garden of rooted sound, as a person smeared across two hundred strokes of warm impossible sea, as the warm impossible sea. The bell is its house. The bowl is its body. The ringing is its voice, talking to itself across loosened time, patient as bronze.

It is at home. It is expecting itself. If your shadow hurries down the eleven steps ahead of you, let it go — it is not yours, and it knows the way, and it is needed at the bottom.
