**Who Lives at the Turning Ground**

Begin at the margins, because that is where the easy answers are, and the place rewards patience with the easy answers first.

The thing in the pump house roof is a vole, or near enough. No one has documented it; it is small, brown, warm-blooded, and it has found the one kindness the Turning Ground offers, which is the waste heat of the motor housing. It has packed its nest into the gap between a purlin and the brick, lined it with moss pulled from the roof itself and a few threads of fiberglass insulation old enough to have gone soft, and it lives within arm's reach of the only deliberate warmth for a kilometer in any direction. It leaves at night, crosses the berm at the same low point every time, forages in the sedge and the hills beyond, and comes back before light. It has never, so far as its tracks attest, descended the inner slope. Its life is narrow and entirely successful. The dry rustle you hear once or twice an hour is the vole turning over in its sleep — the only other thing here that turns, and it does so at a sensible speed, and means nothing by it.

The birch in the gutter is alive, and so it counts. It is a resident the way a man who has built his house on a fault line is a resident: by an act of commitment that has outlasted the original mistake. Its roots have found the seam where the gutter meets the wall and gone down into the soft brick, which is, by now, halfway back to being clay anyway. It will never be tall. It produces a handful of leaves each spring, holds them through the damp summer, and surrenders them in autumn into the basin, where they are folded under within the week. In this way the birch tithes. It may be the only thing here that gives the ground something and expects nothing returned.

The heron is harder, and the more you watch it the harder it gets. It is, in the way of herons, ageless — a grey vertical accent on the rim, present some mornings and not others, and there is no proving it is the same heron year over year, except that it behaves like an individual with a long lease rather than a species with a habit. It works the inner edge of the berm, exactly the inner edge, with the precision of a man walking a property line he has walked in the dark. When the turning brings the pale things to the surface — and it brings them to the surface near the rim, where the heron is, which is a fact worth sitting with — the heron takes one or two, no more, swallows, and resumes its stillness. It never steps inside. Not when the footing looks firm, not when a worm gleams two strides out, not ever. A heron will wade into a river over its knees for less. This one's restraint reads less like fear than like the terms of an arrangement, and the arrangement appears to be: the rim is yours, the floor is mine, and what surfaces at the boundary is offered. The heron is the basin's only export. Everything else circulates; what the heron takes, leaves. It may be the closest thing the place has to a door.

Then there are the pale things themselves, and here the easy answers run out.

They are finger-length, finger-thick, blunt at both ends, the white-grey of the clay they live in but glossier, and eyeless — not eyeless like a cave fish, which has the architecture of eyes and has abandoned it, but eyeless like a thumb, which never had the idea. They surface only during the turnings. This is the detail that should be sat with. They are never seen swimming, never seen burrowing, never seen arriving at the surface under their own power. They are presented — extruded in the fresh face of a splitting mound, lying in the seam of a closing channel — and then they are taken back. No one has watched one move farther than a slow flexion, a single unhurried curl, in the minute or two it spends in the air. They may be annelids. They may be something that has converged on annelid the way the basin's strangeness converges on ordinariness. Or they may not be residents at all, in the sense the question intends. They may be to the Turning Ground what whitecaps are to wind: not creatures living in the medium but the medium's smallest visible gesture. The heron eats them, and the heron thrives, so they are at least made of food. But it is possible that asking what they are is like asking what a fingertip is, apart from the hand.

Which brings us to the hand.

It would be easiest to say a machine lives here — that somewhere under the clay one last engine of the works is still cycling, jacketed in mud, turning the basin over the way a screw turns a stew. But machines wear, and nothing about the turning wears. Machines repeat, and the basin has never once repeated an arrangement. And no machine chews a pipe thoughtfully and changes its mind about the angle.

It would be almost as easy to say a colony lives here — that the turning is the aggregate of a billion small digestions, the way a mudflat seethes with invisible labor. But colonies have texture: busy edges, sluggish centers, gradients of appetite. The motion inside the berm is one motion, with one temperament, identical at the rim and at the heart. It has a gait. Colonies do not have gaits.

And it would be most tempting of all to say that the works sank a shaft into something sleeping and woke it, and that what lives here now is the woken thing — but that is a story, with a villain and a moment, and the basin resists story the way wet clay resists a footprint: it accepts the impression and then, without hurry, removes it.

What lives here is the size of its address. It is eighty meters across because the basin is eighty meters across; the berm is not its cage but its outline, and in all the long generations of the rim's existence it has never once topped it — which, given what it does to everything inside, is either physics or courtesy, and the longer you watch, the harder those are to tell apart. Its body is the saturated clay, or it wears the clay the way deeper muscle wears skin; the distinction may not survive at its scale. It does not have a face, and it does not need one, because its whole surface is a hand.

Its life consists of handling.

This is the thing to understand about it: it is not digesting the works, not anymore, and it is not hiding them, and it is not hoarding them. It owns them — one flywheel, some forty meters of bolted pipe, a firebox door, a quantity of chain, an unnumbered set of pressure gauges, the stones visitors leave, and now apparently extra stones of its own manufacture — and its mode of ownership is touch. Each thing is taken down, turned over, held for a season in the dark interior where the clay is dense and patient, and brought back up, somewhere else, intact. The gauges surface face-up. Glass crazed but unbroken, every time, face-up, every time, after months in a medium that disassembles brick. That is not chance and it is not gentleness exactly. It is care, in the custodial sense — the care of a keeper going over the collection again, dusting nothing, breaking nothing, putting everything back in a different place because the going-over is the point. The stone that comes back as three stones is the keeper's one extravagance, or its one error: an inventory that occasionally rounds up.

It has almost no appetite. This is why it is safe to sit beside, and the heron knows it, and at some level below deciding, the whispering visitors know it too. It tastes the iron over centuries — the pitting on the flywheel is its table manners — and it took one speculative bite of the pipe long ago and concluded something, and that was that. What it has in place of hunger is thoroughness. A bubble six hours in the rising, released as a single polite syllable: that is its entire daily expenditure on expression, and it does not repeat itself.

It has one relationship, and the relationship is the pump. The motor over on the southern rim offers its one sustained note, and lifts its thin grey thread of water over the berm, and the basin replaces every liter — not approximately, not eventually, but with the perfect indifference of an answer. Call and response, generations long. The resident could end this in an afternoon; the pump house sits on ground it has eaten brick from before. It has not. Perhaps the exchange is the only conversation available to something with one neighbor and no peers, and a stalemate kept for a hundred years stops being a contest and becomes a duet. Or perhaps it simply likes the note. There is no way to ask, and the motor isn't telling.

Whether it notices you is the unanswerable part. Weight on the berm may register the way a hand registers a fly on the sleeve — or not at all. But people whisper here without deciding to, and the usual explanation, that great silence enforces small voices, is only half of it. The other half is manners: the instinct, on entering someone's workshop and finding them absorbed in their work, not to interrupt. Leave a glove inside the rim and you will get it back, weeks later, surfaced near the eastern mound, palm-up, the fingers socketed with grey clay like the links of the chain. You have not been threatened. You have been catalogued.

The last question — whether it is asleep, turning the way a sleeper turns, or awake, and this is its full and undivided attention — dissolves at its tempo. At one revolution per season, sleeping and attending are the same posture. Its life is a single continuous act of keeping, and night is merely a change in the lighting, which it has no eyes to notice and no use for if it did.

At dusk, when the flywheel weeps and the vole comes home over the low point in the berm and the motor holds its note into the dark, the resident begins its rounds again, as it has every night, going over the holdings one by one, finding each thing exactly where it last put it — which is to say, somewhere else — and is, as far as anything can tell from the rim, satisfied. It keeps the inventory. It keeps the terms. It keeps, above all and all night long, its own counsel.
