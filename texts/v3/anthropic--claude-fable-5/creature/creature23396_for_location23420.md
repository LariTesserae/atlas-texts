The census of the Loud Country has been attempted four times, and all four attempts returned the same figure: zero. The figure is wrong, but it is wrong in an instructive way. The census-takers were counting lowland-fashion — looking for movement, for tracks, for smoke, for anything that eats and flees and dies on an observable schedule. Life here does none of those things at a speed a visitor can certify. The plateau is not uninhabited. It is inhabited at tempos your stay is too short to constitute evidence of.

So: who lives in a place that has not finished being written? Roughly what you'd expect, once you expect it. Things that drink the roar. Things that hold the ground down. Things that have not happened yet. And one man, or one occurrence of a man, sweeping out a hut that is also every other hut.

Take them slowest first.

## The Flock

Travelers call them the flock, or the grazing stones, or nothing, because most travelers walk past them every day of the crossing without ever knowing it. They are the great rounded forms you take for finished outcrops — barn-sized, hill-sized, fine-grained, satisfying, cold. They are made of finished matter because finished matter is what they make. They are the finishing.

They graze on the roar. Where one has fed, the world is done: hard cold certain ground, silent, printless, bare — the bell-heather cannot grow on cropped land, because cropped land has nothing left to say. Those broad completed shelves you cross in such relief, glad of the quiet? Pasture. Eaten down to fact.

They move. Of course they move; everything here moves, at the speed of the chord. A flock will work its way around a half-stated mountain over four or five centuries, grazing inward, taking the loudness off it in slow spiral swathes, and when the last unresolved seam goes down some vast throat the mountain stands complete and mute and a note resolves in the north and the old travelers say the pitch has dropped. That quarter-tone in a lifetime — that is, in part, the sound of feeding.

To them you are not prey or company or even an event. You are a flicker, a statistical shimmer at the edge of a meal. You can lean against one — many have, taking it for stone, eating lunch in its lee — and the only sign is a deep occupied warmth far down in the grain, like the warmth of arithmetic in progress, and a pulse, if you stay long enough, which you will not, because the beats are years apart.

They do leave tracks. The dust simply takes decades to decide to hold them. Travelers returning in old age have found, pressed crisply into ground they camped on in their youth, the footprints of something enormous that had been standing beside them all night, patiently, finishing the world.

## The Pinned

The cairn-builders are still here. This is the part visitors take longest to accept, and then accept all at once, usually at night, near a cairn, in the quiet.

They were a people of the becoming-places — pre-iron, post-hurry — and they understood the country's one civic problem: the unset world *shifts*. A shelf relocates. A ravine is withdrawn. You cannot raise children on ground that is still drafting itself, and so they became masons, and built downward, weighting the soft spots closed, pinning the local real in place. And where stone was not weight enough — where the ground hummed and slid and would not take the pin — they did the only thing left in the toolkit. They added themselves.

They sat down in the loudest places and let themselves be ratified.

Understand that completion here is not death. Nothing finished in the Loud Country dies; nothing erodes; a completed thing simply holds its shape forever, which is either a tomb or a tenure depending on what you wanted your shape *for*. They wanted theirs for holding. Some of the cairns at the edges of the unfinished zones are masonry. Some of the cairns are masons. There is no reliable way to tell from outside, except that near certain cairns the quiet has a *grain* to it — a counter-hum, pitched precisely against the local roar, the way you'd brace a door — and the ground there is the hardest ground on the plateau, hard the way a held thing is hard.

They are not silent, either. They talk through the horn trees — what else would a network of resonant trumpets grown across a whole country at a hand-width per generation be *for* — and the words travelers claim to hear under the flared trunks are real words, the middle of a conversation begun before metal, conducted at roughly one word per long lifetime. Generations of visitors have compared notes, and the assembled fragment is short and has cost three thousand years: *we have it — hold —* and then the word currently in progress, begun some centuries ago and not expected to resolve soon, which appears, so far, to be *almost*.

## The Resident

You will probably not meet him. If you do, it will be in one of the huts, and he will be mending the flint blade — the one with the chip in its edge, the chip he maintains carefully, because the blade in this hut must match the blade in all of them — and he will offer you the bench without looking up, and thank you, mildly, for the conversation the two of you are going to have in a hut three days east.

He keeps all the huts, in the sense that there may be only one hut, and he lives in it, in the sense that there may be only one of him. He gives a name when asked, a different one in each hut, and the names are all the same name at slightly different stages of being decided: Orun, Oren, Orne. He is not old. He is not young. He is pre-iron in his tools and unworn in his face, because nothing here erodes, including him.

What he is, is unresolved, and he will tell you so himself, without distress. Either he is the last of the Pinned — the one their custom appointed to stay loose, to keep moving while the others sat down, the maintenance, the walker of the cairn-lines — or he is the first of the people who will live here when the country is finished, sent ahead, a prototype kept on site. He genuinely does not know which, and points out that the job description is identical either way: sweep the hut, trim the lamp that burns nothing, check the pins, and talk with travelers, so that the country, drafting away, does not forget what conversation is for. He asks excellent questions. He writes nothing down. It is not clear he needs to. The marks on the hut walls propagate, and so, perhaps, does whatever you tell him.

One more thing about him, which you will only notice later, in the becoming-zones, and which will arrive in your chest like a dropped stone:

## The Unwritten

In the loud places — inside the green halos, among the rib-scaffolds of the half-stated mountains — there are figures.

They stand the way the unfinished peaks stand: partially. Ribs of darker air in the shape of standing people, a shoulder fully realized here, a spine of structural suggestion there. Most have no faces; a face, evidently, is a late decision, the kind of thing the country commits to last. A few are further along. One, near the great becoming-place in the northwest, has a single finished hand — fully stated, beautifully made, holding nothing yet — and the hand is the loudest object many travelers ever stand beside, roaring softly to itself like a kettle of distance.

They are the inhabitants. Not ghosts — the opposite of ghosts: the people who have not happened yet, being drafted alongside their country, at its speed, out of its noise. They gather where the heather gathers, because they are fed by the same roar; a half-written person is a kind of listening too.

And they all, every one of them, favor the Resident. The set of the shoulders. The economy of the stance. The country, having found a satisfactory solution to *person*, is reusing it, corbel for corbel, the way it reuses the hut.

They do not see you. Or they see you the way you see something you are about to remember. Standing among them does nothing the rules forbid, but you come away with the unmistakable feeling of having been *consulted*, and a small number of travelers report that one of the figures turned its unfinished head to follow them as they left — and those travelers, every recorded one, come back. ## The One That Un-occurs

Something here lives by retraction. The evidence is the red flowers: the bell-heather blooms its hard carnelian blooms, and they feed nothing and seed nothing, and then they do not wilt but *fail to have been*, neatly, leaving no scar — and a deletion that clean is not weather. Something is eating the mistakes. Something is keeping the draft tidy.

It has never been seen, for the obvious reason: being seen is precisely the category of event it removes. There is no describing it, no fearing it intelligently, no evidence anyone has ever been taken by it — which, travelers point out to each other at night, in the huts, in low voices, is exactly what being taken would look like.

The Resident, asked about it directly, finishes trimming the lamp before he answers, and the answer is always the same: that the country is very well edited, and that you should take comfort in your own continued occurrence. Everything still on the plateau is, by definition, so far intended. You arrived; you remember arriving; you are, as of this evening, meant.

It is the strangest comfort in the world, and it works.

## The Visitors

Finally there are the people like you, who are inhabitants for a few weeks without meaning to be, and who do a small measurable amount of the work — firming the translucent ground underfoot, dreaming loudly within range of the horn trees, waking hoarse, having helped.

Most leave. Some slow. It happens by degrees: a traveler's tempo drops to meet the country's, the days lengthening for them like a pupil dilating, and they stop making progress toward the rim, not from weakness but from a loss of interest in arrival. You may pass one — a figure standing thigh-deep in bell-heather, apparently resting — and if you come back in ten years they will have moved about a mile and will be more in tune than anyone you have ever met. They are becoming residents the only way it can be done, which is at the resident speed.

But here is the truth about who lives in the Loud Country, and it includes you, and you should sit down for it:

the country drafts everyone who crosses it. It cannot help itself; drafting is what it does; you walked through it for weeks being a workable solution to *person*, and the country reuses solutions. Somewhere out in a becoming-zone, started the day you crested the last true ridge, there is a scaffold of darker air with your stance, your shoulders, your economy, roaring quietly to itself, knee-deep in the green.

That is why the frequency is missing, afterward, from every sound you hear for the rest of your life. It is not gone. It is in use. Part of you lives there now — patient, half-stated, in good company, under the no-sun, inside the long chord —

becoming, at the speed of centuries, the case.
