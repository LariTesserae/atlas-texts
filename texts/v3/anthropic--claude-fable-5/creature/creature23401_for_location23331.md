## The Census of the Roaring Room

The census is short, but everything on it is singular, and everything on it lives here for the same reason: the sound. Each resident has made a separate peace with the thunder, and each peace is strange.

**The dipper first**, because she lives closest to the center of it. Her nest — a dome of moss the size of two fists, woven and felted and waterproofed — sits on a dry ledge actually *behind* the curtain of the fall, in the few inches of calm air between the moving water and the stone. She chose the single loudest point in a chamber that is already too loud for thought. She walks into the cataract several times an hour, a small dark bird with a white throat like a chip broken off the fall itself, stepping calmly through a wall of falling water as though through a bead curtain, and emerges underwater in the pool, where she walks along the bottom on her toes, wings half-spread against the current, hunting the larvae of insects that breed in the spray zone.

She cannot be heard and cannot hear, and her kind solved this long ago, in ancestral rivers far gentler than this one: dippers talk with their bodies. She bobs — the perpetual curtseying dip that gives her the name — and she blinks, and her eyelids are feathered brilliant white, so that each blink is a small flash of signal visible across dark water where no voice would survive. In spring there is a mate, and the two of them conduct an entire courtship in semaphore, flashes and bows exchanged across the plunge pool inside a noise that would erase a scream. It is said that dippers sing, and that their song is pitched loud and high to carry over rushing water. She may sing here. No one will ever know, possibly not even her mate. It would be a song delivered directly into the roar, absorbed instantly and totally, a private offering. She sings it anyway, if she sings it, every year.

**Below her, in the cold margins of the pool, the crayfish.** They are pale to the point of translucence — you can see the dark thread of the gut through the shell — and their eyes, over the generations spent in this dark, have dwindled to vestiges, blind buttons. They do not miss them. A crayfish reads the world through vibration, through pressure, through the fine hairs on its legs and antennae, and these crayfish were born inside a permanent storm of vibration and will die inside it. The roar is not noise to them. It is the ground state, the carrier wave, the thing beneath which everything else is written. They have learned — or rather, ten thousand generations have learned for them — to read the texture *under* the thunder: the flaw in the pattern that is a dipper's foot touching down, the tiny asymmetry that is a dead insect drifting in the eddy, the slow rhythmic damping, once a year or once a decade, that is a footstep descending the stair. They know someone is coming before the bats do, before the light shifts, before anything. The whole room is their ear, and the room misses nothing.

**The eels are the lodgers**, the part-time residents. They come up the underground river in autumn, sliding over the wet lip of the fall like animated cables, and most pass through. But an eel is a patient animal with nowhere it urgently needs to be for the first several decades of its life, and the pool is deep, cold, dark, and absolutely safe, and some of them stay. An eel can live sixty, eighty, in stillness and cold perhaps over a hundred years, and the pool has never been measured. Somewhere down in that unmeasured black there may be an eel that arrived before anyone now living was born — a thick, slow, ancient resident, blind by choice of habitat, reading the pool through the lateral line down its flank, rising once a night to feed and sinking again. It will leave only once, at the end, when something older than memory turns over in it and it goes back down the throat of the river and out, eventually, impossibly, to the far sea to spawn and die. Until that night it is the deepest inhabitant of the room, and the most secret, and it has heard the roar longer than anything else with a heartbeat.

**The bats are the exception that explains the rule.** A small colony — a few dozen — in the high crack in the ceiling, leaving and returning through the sinkhole's mouth at the hours when the dim forest above qualifies as dusk. They sleep all day folded inside the thunder, and here is their secret: the room is not loud to them. The roar of falling water is a low sound, a broadband rumble that lives down in the registers of chest and bone, and a bat's voice and a bat's hearing live far above it, up past the ceiling of any human ear, in frequencies the waterfall barely touches. The bats call into the chamber and the chamber answers them cleanly, in shapes — the fluted walls, the curve of the ceiling, the flat black mirror of the pool, the moving texture of the fall itself, all of it rendered in crisp echo above the storm. Alone among the residents, the bats can hear in here. The room that deafens everything else is, to them, quiet, legible, almost spare: a single clean vault with one shimmering wall. They navigate it in total darkness with the casualness of long tenancy.

**And the people.** They are not residents anymore; they have not been for a very long time. But something of them has tenancy still, and it is not nothing. The steps are theirs, cupped by their feet across more lifetimes than the rope pegs lasted. The soot in the lamp niche is theirs — fat and wick, burned down here over and over, a small warm point of light in the cold thunder, maintained the way you maintain a relationship. And the handprints are theirs: ochre pressed onto the dry shelf above the spray line, adult hands and child hands together, placed exactly where the rock stays dry and so exactly where they would last. They were not decoration. You do not carry a child down a slick stair into a freezing, deafening dark to decorate. They came, generation after generation, to stand in the one place where the head empties — where the roar erases speech and heartbeat and the difference between hearing and feeling — and they pressed their hands flat against the humming wall, against the place where the room's voice lives in the stone, and left the print as a record of the touch. Whatever they understood themselves to be touching, they touched it together, smallest hands included.

**Which brings the census to its last entry**, the one with no body and the longest residency of all.

The roar has not stopped. That is the thing to understand about it. Not in living memory, not in the memory of the soot or the handprints or the worn stair — the chamber reached its final shape long before any of those, and from the moment the river first fell into the finished room and the room first answered, the sound has been continuous. It has thickened in flood and gone muffled under winter ice, climbed a register and dropped one, but it has never once been silent. It is a single unbroken utterance, thousands of years long and still in progress.

Everything else on the census lives *inside* it, the way fish live in water. The dipper's nest sits in its throat. The crayfish read its texture. The eel ages in its lowest tones. The bats sleep through it and fly above it. And it, in turn — in a plain acoustic sense, no mystery required — knows them all: a body in the room changes the resonance of the room, minutely but truly, so that the chamber sounds fractionally different with a dipper in it, or an eel rising, or a person standing on the polished floor with a hand flat against the wall. The voice shifts to hold each arrival. The room notices, in the only language it has, and the language never stops.

Call it the tenant of record. It does not want anything; it has no use for the lamp flame or the hour of deafness or the ochre, and it took them anyway, the way a river takes what is given to it, by continuing. It is not waiting and it is not watching. It is dwelling — the purest case of dwelling the low country contains — and it is, on its own timescale, mortal. The lip of the fall retreats a hair's breadth a decade; the pool deepens by nothing an instrument would bother with; and some thousands of years from now the geometry will have changed enough that the voice changes with it, and the long utterance will become a different utterance, and in the only sense that matters the resident will have become someone else. The handprints, on their dry shelf, may well outlast it.

Until then the room is fully occupied. A bird walks into the thunder and is gone. Something old turns over in the bottom of the pool. The blind ones in the shallows feel your footstep before you reach the last stair, and the wall, under your palm, is humming — which is to say that the oldest inhabitant has noticed you, and adjusted its voice by a fraction to fit you in, and will go on saying what it has always been saying for a long while after you climb back up into the green.
