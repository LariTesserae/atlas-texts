What lives in the Cleft at Wether's Tooth is, first, a list — and then, underneath the list, one other thing, which takes longer to see.

**The bats** are the importers. Everything in the cleft, in the end, runs on what they carry up the mountain in their stomachs. They are small even for bats, dense-furred to the toes, with a fur so fine it sheds water like a duck's back, and they are the only residents who leave. Each evening in the warm months they pour out of the seam — the exodus takes most of an hour, a smoke that the wind tears sideways — and drop two vertical miles down the dark face to where the air is thick enough to hold insects. They feed hard, fast, wastefully, and climb home heavy. The climb back kills the old and the unlucky; the cleft floor receives them, and nothing in the cleft floor wastes them. The bats do not know they are a supply line. They know the warmth, the wall, the press of their neighbors' breathing, the exact shelf of rock that is theirs by the inheritance of touch — pups wedged into the same finger-width of stone their mothers were wedged into, back and back, a tenancy older than any written lease anywhere. In winter they do not leave at all. They slow until their hearts beat a few times a minute, and the wall's communal breathing carries them like a tide carries the moored.

**The swifts** were migrants once, and their bodies still remember it badly. Twice a year a restlessness comes over them — they scream up and down the cleft for days, riding the column of slightly-warmer air, and then it passes, and they settle, and do not go. Somewhere a few generations back the arithmetic changed: the warmth of the rock made staying cheaper than the terrible southbound crossing, and the ones who stayed lived. They are paying for the bargain in their shape. Their wings are blunting, generation by generation, trading the long scythe of the traveler for something better suited to the forty-pace world. They cannot perch — swifts never could — so they cling, vertical, in the highest reaches where the seam's grey light touches, and they hunt the column of rising insects in the few twilight hours, and they pair for life because in a closed room there is no one new to meet. Their nests are made of their own saliva and the mineralized cricket-shale, glued to the wall: the first true masonry in the cleft, small grey cups that outlast their builders and are reused, patched, inherited. Some cups are forty birds old.

**The crickets** are the speakers and, without intending it, the masons. They are pale to translucency — you can watch the dark thread of a meal move through one — and blind in the way of things that gave up eyes as a bad expense. Their click-language is not song. It is positional. Each cricket holds a station on the floor or low wall, and its click pattern encodes, roughly, *here, this, me, still* — so that the cleft at floor level is a continuous spoken map of itself, every occupied point announcing occupation. When the plate-fall silences come, the map rebuilds outward from the survivors: the first click after a silence is the bravest act that happens on the mountain. They eat the guano, the dead, the black moss, each other's cast skins when fresh — and what they cannot eat, the cleft turns to stone. They molt eleven times in a life, and each abandoned casing, a perfect hollow cricket down to the antennae, hardens in days to brittle grey shale. They walk on the architecture of their own former selves, ankle-deep. No cricket has ever touched the original floor.

**The grazers** — the soft-shelled things between snail and moth, which would be called *velvets* if anyone came and stayed long enough to need a word — are the wall's skin-tenders. They begin life as a snail begins: a coiled speck, rasping the condensation film. Mid-life, the shell thins, splits along the spiral, and unfurls into two damp grey vanes — not wings exactly, more like a moth's idea remembered imperfectly — and for the last season of its life the velvet drifts rather than flies, lofting on the cleft's faint thermal from floor to seam, mating in slow mid-air collisions, laying eggs in the highest wet facets so its children can spend their whole crawling lives descending. The walls' moisture-film is their entire pasture, and they keep it in rotation with the salamanders by an etiquette no one arranged: velvets graze the upper walls, salamanders lick the lower, and the boundary between the two drifts seasonally like a treaty line.

**The salamanders** are the elders of the animals and the only residents who behave as though they know where they are. Flint-grey, cold-slowed, they may be very old — their metabolisms barely smolder, and nothing here hunts them. They live pressed flat to the warmest facets, where the young rock's residual heat is strongest, and they move perhaps a body-length an hour, licking the stone clean in territories measured in handspans, on schedules measured in hours, time-sharing the same wall in shifts so exact that two salamanders may tend the same facet for decades and never meet. They are the cleft's memory in flesh. The same individual that grazed past the ochre handprint when the bats' great-great-grandmothers were pups is, in all likelihood, still making its circuit, and will pass the print again next month, and not pause, because it never has.

**The films** are the residents no eye finds, and they do the largest work. Mats of mineral-breathing microbes — kin to the things that crust hot springs — coat every surface the larger animals foul or shed upon, and they are the agency behind the strangeness: it is the films that lock the cricket casings into shale, the films that turn the bats' droppings to pale crystal florets, the films that are upholstering the wound. The animals provide raw material; the films do the masonry. They are also, almost certainly, the oldest lineage present, older than the mountain, carried up in the gut of the first bat or on the foot of the first cricket, finding in the warm wet dark a world to finish.

**The juniper** at the cleft mouth is the only resident that predates the wound, and it lives like a doorkeeper. Half its roots grip the old slope; the other half hang down into the crack itself, drinking the seep, furred with the colony's drifting spores and the velvets' eggs. The bats stream around it twice a day. It was a tree on an unbroken mountainside once; now it is the lintel of a door it never asked to stand in, and it has bent its whole growth, ring by ring, around the fact of the opening.

**The two placed things** must be counted, because the colony counts them. The handprint and the blade keep their territory as surely as any salamander keeps a handspan of wall. The shingle of bodies has closed around them and left them bare — whether by some chemistry of the ochre, or the blade's faint difference in temperature, or something less explicable, the effect is the same: a tenancy maintained. The person is centuries gone. Their claim is current. Whoever climbed that high and chose to leave a hand's mark and a fresh-edged tool, never used, set upright with care, is the only resident present entirely as intention — and intention, it turns out, holds territory in the dark as well as breathing does.

And then the other thing — the one underneath the list.

Stand long enough in the functional dark and you understand that the cleft's truest inhabitant has no body of its own. It is the **breath** — the slow communal rhythm into which thousands of separate respirations have drifted, the wall rising and falling as one. No single animal owns it. No single animal even lives long enough to claim it: bats, crickets, velvets, swifts, all churn through their generations while the rhythm goes on, each newborn settling into a cadence that was old before its parents hatched. It is inherited the way an accent is inherited, body to body, by nearness. It is the thing the salamanders keep time by and the thing the crickets' map is spoken against. It steadies when the colony is well and breaks ragged before the great plate-falls, as though it feels the rock deciding. If the cleft can be said to have a single resident, it is this: a creature made entirely of borrowed lungs, patient, very slow, engaged in one long project — taking the raw young wound it was born into and lining it, breath by breath, body by body, with smoothness. It will not finish in any of its parts' lifetimes. It does not appear to mind.

Once or twice a century, when a wall-plate shears loose and rings down and the whole place falls silent for that single famous minute, you could believe the resident has died. Then a click. *Here.* Another. *This. Me. Still.* The map rebuilds, the wall finds its rhythm, the breath resumes —

and the roll is called, and everyone, including the handprint, including the blade, including the mountain itself, still arriving, answers present.
