What lives there has never needed a name, because naming is a thing done by mouths in the light, between creatures who might otherwise confuse one thing with another. In the chamber there is nothing to confuse. There is the film, the curtain, and the grazer. That is the whole census, and it has been the whole census for longer than the mountain has had weather on its far side.

Begin underneath, with the oldest residents, because everything else stands on them.

**The film** came in dissolved. It needed no door. It arrived the way the water arrives — through the hairline pores of the cooling rock, molecule by molecule, a colonization conducted entirely below the threshold of event. Where the warm seeps emerge, it grows: white mats, faintly slick, laid over the stone like wet silk. It does not eat light, because there has never been any to eat. It eats the *difference* — the chemical disagreement between the inside of the mountain and the chamber's still air, sulfur and iron and dissolved breathables, the slow exhale of the rock itself. It is life that runs on geology instead of sun, which means it is life that keeps the chamber's hours: constant, unhurried, total. The mats grow at the pace of centuries. They are the floor of everything. Every other living thing in the room is, in the end, an arrangement of the film, walking.

**The curtain** lives there only the way a hand lives in a glove. The pale filaments hanging in the one corner are the root-tips of something green and weathered and entirely ignorant of the chamber — a tree, probably, or what passes for one on a mountain this young, gripping the outer skin forty meters up. Its roots followed water down a fissure, year over year, blind and patient, until they broke through the ceiling of a room their owner will never know exists. They hang there now, drinking the saturated air, swaying in currents too gentle to feel. They are the chamber's one connection to the turning world, and the connection is faint almost to nothing: in what would be summer, the sap moves differently, and the filaments sweat a slightly sweeter water. That is the only calendar the chamber receives. Nothing reads it but one thing.

**The grazer.** This is the resident — the one the chamber, if it could be said to belong to anything, belongs to.

There are perhaps a dozen of them at any moment. There have never been many more; the seep feeds what the seep feeds, and the population settled into that number the way everything here settles, long ago, without negotiation. Each one is the size of a thumb-joint. Each one is pale past white — pale the way the mats are pale, the pallor of a place where pigment has never once been useful — and its cuticle is so thin and soft that if light existed here, you could see straight through it to the dark thread of its gut, its meals visible inside it like ink in glass. No armor. There has never been anything to be armored against: no predator, no frost, no drought, no fall of anything heavier than a drop of water. It may be the softest animal in the world. It is certainly the least afraid.

Its ancestors came down the roots. That is the best account the evidence gives: small soil-things on the mountain's outer skin, following the moisture down the fissure, generation below generation, a descent that took longer than the descent of any single body — until some of them reached the bottom of the curtain and stepped off into a room that was warm, and wet, and silvered everywhere with food, and never asked them to be hard or fast or watchful again. The chamber kept them. It has been keeping them ever since.

It has no eyes and no vestige of eyes — not blinded, never sighted, the concept absent from its body the way light is absent from the room. It comes at the world the way you were told to: through the skin. It breathes through its surface. It never drinks, because at total humidity the boundary between its body and the air has gone soft and uncertain, permanently — the condition you felt for an hour, it lives in. It is an animal mostly surrendered to its medium. Its longest organs are its antennae, finer than the roots, longer than its body, sweeping ahead of it in slow arcs, reading texture and chemistry and the faint warmth-shadows of the seeps. Its many legs move in unhurried waves. It has exactly one speed, and that speed is the speed of its food.

Because here is the creature's whole economy: the mats regrow over centuries, and so the grazer eats in lines a few millimeters wide, a body-length or two a day, and then moves on, and does not come back — cannot afford to come back — until the film has silvered over again. A full slow circuit of the chamber's grazeable surfaces takes years. By the time an individual returns to any wall, the wall is ready for it. Appetite, regrowth, seep-chemistry, drip — all of it locked together into one metabolism distributed across rock, water, film, and animal. The grazer is not separate from the chamber's equilibrium. It is the moving part of it.

And because the chamber erases nothing, the grazer lives inside its own history. Every line ever eaten is still written on the walls — old tracks regrown but faintly raised, faintly different in taste, a palimpsest of every meal taken by every individual that has ever lived here. The creatures follow the old lines, because old lines lead to mature film; the wandering paths of their ancestors are, quite literally, the map of where food will be. The species has no memory to speak of. It does not need one. The room remembers for it. Its entire inherited knowledge and its entire food supply are the same object: the written walls.

They find each other the same way — rarely, deliberately, by following converging tracks whose freshness they can taste. When two meet, which may happen a handful of times in a lifetime, they stand antenna to antenna in the dark for days, an exchange with no urgency in it, because nothing here has ever rewarded urgency. Eggs are laid singly, years apart, up in the root-curtain — the only soft hanging place in a room of stone, the old ancestral road repurposed as a cradle. A hatchling is the size of a comma. It descends the filaments its forebears descended, steps onto the wall, finds an old line, and begins.

They live a long time — a century, perhaps much more; nothing here counts, so nothing knows. And when one dies, the chamber does what the chamber does. It does not rot the body, not quickly; it does not bury it. The mineral sweat begins its glaze, thinner than breath on glass, and over centuries the dead become small calcite lumps among the lentil-blisters of the floor — indistinguishable, almost, from the embryonic stone around them. The pebbled texture you ran your palm across is partly geology and partly genealogy. The chamber's only fossils, forming in real time, underfoot.

One more thing about the grazers, and it concerns the visitor.

The cupules ground into the wall by the entrance — those dozens of small hemispheric pits, that repeated human gesture — cup the condensation. Water pools in them, mineral-rich, and the film grows there thicker and whiter than anywhere else in the room. The grazers have favored that patch of wall for as long as the pits have existed. Whoever crawled in with a hammerstone and a cupped flame, and ground pits into the soft new mineral for reasons that did not survive, accomplished — whatever else they intended — exactly one lasting thing: they made the best pasture in the chamber. Their tools sit on the shelf below it, glazing over, and the grazers cross them without distinction, because a hammerstone reads, through the antennae, as stone. The visitor does not live here. Their absence does. It is grazed regularly.

And you, if you came — you would be an event, the largest in centuries. Your breath would shift the air's chemistry measurably; your skin would shed heat and salt onto everything you touched. The grazer that crossed the back of your hand registered you as a warm, temporary stone, and was correct: you were warm, you did not stay. But where your palm rested on the wall, the salt and oil you left behind will feed a slightly different film, with a slightly different taste, and in some decade after your departure something pale and soft and eyeless will graze its slow line across the place you touched and take you in. That is how the chamber eats anything: slowly, as a film, without knowing it was ever alive. You will not be remembered. You will be flavored.

Then the drop falls, *tok*, and somewhere on the dark wall a wave of small legs pauses, registers the room's one event, and resumes.
