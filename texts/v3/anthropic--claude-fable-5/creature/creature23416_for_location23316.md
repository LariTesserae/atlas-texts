Start below, because everything here starts below.

The first residents of the valley are the ones nobody came to see: the film of life in the wet seams where the lattice runs warm, bacteria in mats the color of old brass, the beginnings of true soil assembling themselves around the infrastructure like a city's first squatters around an aqueduct. Springtails live along the heat lines in numbers that defy counting — drifts of them, a slow gray sleet moving through the litter at minus six, active all winter because along those seams there is no winter. And the fungi, which are the valley's first criminals. Mycorrhizal networks arrived on the wind expecting bare ground and an honest living, and found their ancient job — moving carbon and nitrogen between root and rock — already done, by machines, for free. So they adapted in a decade to a niche that has never existed before: they tap the lattice. Fine hyphal threads wrapped around the feedstock channels, siphoning the broth, fruiting in the dark in pale shelves and fingers along the warm ground. The lattice tolerates this. Or accounts for it. The stewards' logs note that broth loss to fungal theft runs around four percent and has been four percent for fifteen years, which means either the fungi have found their limit or the lattice has set one, and no one is sure which answer is stranger.

Above the seams, the voles. The voles are the valley's pulse and you must understand them first, because nearly everything else here is organized around eating them, avoiding them, or out-shouting them. They breed all year — the ground never freezes, the seed rain never stops, the alder bark is always soft — and they run six, seven generations annually, which means the voles are evolving at the same berserk tempo as the land. They have already changed. They are louder than their ancestors, much louder, because a quiet vole here is a vole that loses its family in the roar; their contact calls have climbed into a piercing register that cuts through water-noise and wood-crack, and a colony in full voice sounds like a kettle that never finishes boiling. They tunnel along the warm lattice seams, which means vole runways are, in effect, maps of the infrastructure — the stewards stopped using the engineering charts years ago and now locate broth lines by listening for screaming under the snow. The voles live four months, five if lucky. They are the shortest lives in the valley and the most numerous, the broth made briefly furious and furred.

The foxes followed the voles up into the trees, and the foxes are the valley's great scandal, the thing visiting biologists come to see and go home unsettled by. One human lifetime. The founding animals were ordinary coastal foxes that walked in over the moraines maybe thirty years ago, and their descendants are already something else: lighter-boned, longer in the toe, with a flexibility in the ankle that lets them descend a trunk headfirst, almost. They den in the crotches of the big fast alders, above the floods, below the wind. They have learned the thing every predator in this valley must learn, which is that you cannot hunt by stealth in a place with no silence to be stealthy in — so they hunt the gaps. A fox moves through the canopy listening not for sound but for its absence: the spot where the vole-scream stops is the spot where something is paying attention, or dying, or about to. The foxes hunt holes in the noise. Watch one work a hillside in the gray-violet smear of noon and you will see it pause, head tilted, reading the roar the way a fisherman reads current, and then drop twelve feet onto a patch of silence.

The owls gave up their inheritance to live here. Owl silence — the soft-fringed wing, the noiseless stoop, ten million years of craft — is worthless in the Succession Valley, where nothing can hear an owl coming because nothing can hear anything coming. So the owls let it go. The resident lineage flies loud and doesn't care, and has reinvested everything in boldness and voice: they hunt by sustained calling, a flat broadcast shriek that doesn't conceal the owl but *floods* a patch of forest, and the voles below, unable to triangulate it, freeze — which is what the owl wants. They duet across kilometers of thrashing canopy. They perch on the cabin's roof ridge and the relay pylons, the only birds that have noticed the verticals that don't sway, and they watch the stewards with the frank, unbothered attention of an animal that has never once been the quietest thing in its world.

The crossbill-things — they have a name now, in three languages, none of which has stuck — work the dark canopy entirely by smell. A ripening cone has a scent; fast-grown wood under growth-strain has a scent; the trees, cells running hot, breathe out a constant volatile weather that the birds read the way their ancestors read light. They move through the black in dense flocks held together by ceaseless contact calling, every bird sounding its position several times a second, so that a feeding flock is a drifting lattice of sound mapping a lattice of scent above a lattice of broth — three invisible networks stacked, each feeding on the one below. Their eyes have not failed. The stewards' cameras have caught whole flocks going still and silent — the only silence anyone has recorded in the valley — during strong aurora, faces turned up through the canopy gaps. Nobody has published an interpretation. The senior steward's logbook entry reads, in full: *They watch it. So do we.*

At the valley mouth, on cliffs so new they still smell of crushed rock, live the forty thousand. The kittiwake-descendants run their colonies around the clock because the clock is gone; the dark season simply rearranged their lives from days into shifts, half the colony fishing the black water while the other half holds the ledges, and the handover — tens of thousands of birds exchanging places in the dark — is the loudest single event in a valley made of loud events. And among them, in burrows dug into the warm moraine, lives the petrel. The petrel is the only resident who was *invited*, and it declined the invitation. It was written for a different coast — engineered two decades back for a restoration project six hundred kilometers south, released there, provisioned there, expected there — and within three seasons the bulk of the population had moved here instead, to a valley nobody designed it for, apparently because it preferred the fishing, or the burrow-warm gravel, or something in the noise. Its designers published a careful paper about dispersal parameters. The stewards take a different view, which is that the petrel is the valley's first review, and it was favorable. A made thing, given preferences, exercised them. It chose this. In the dark months you can hear them coming in off the sea at what would be midnight, low over the flats, churring.

Then there are the trees, and the question of whether the trees live in the valley or simply *are* it. They were written, and they live like it — short, hot, profligate. An alder here runs its whole life in twelve or fifteen years: up, broad, dominant, undermined, down, dissolved, the lattice threading through its fallen body within days and the broth carrying it off to be someone else. No tree in the valley is old. No tree in the valley will ever be old; longevity wasn't in the specification, persistence of the *stand* was, and so the individual trees pass through the forest the way waves pass through surf — the wave is real, the wave is brief, the water remains. The argued-about conifer is the exception that proves nothing: it grows slower, holds longer, and appears in no one's design documents in quite the form it has taken, which means either it is drifting from its writing or it was always going to do this and its writers didn't know what they'd written. It is the tree the stewards check on. There are nine of them in the valley. There were seven last year.

Does the lattice live there? The treaty is carefully worded on this point, which tells you the people who wrote the treaty had the same thought you are having. It eats — measured fractions of glacier and rock flour. It circulates. It responds: when the debris flow buried the ten-year alder stand, the lattice had re-threaded the rubble inside a week, not because anyone instructed it to but because routing around damage is what it is. It does not think, the engineers say, and the engineers are almost certainly right, and the treaty nonetheless requires that it be *witnessed* — continuously, in person, by humans living within the sound of it — and you do not write a witnessing requirement into law for a culvert.

Which brings it to the cabin on the knoll, and the two who live there.

The stewards rotate, nominally every six months, though the roster has its repeat offenders — there is a woman who has done eleven dark seasons and describes the work, when pressed, as *watching it grow up*. They are not scientists, mostly, though some are; the selection criteria are opaque and seem to favor a particular temperament: people who can be made small without being made miserable. Their duties are walking the lines, maintaining the time-lapse rigs, logging the channels' overnight migrations, counting the conifers, and not intervening, which is the hard one. They live inside the noise the way other people live inside weather. New stewards sleep badly for three weeks and then sleep wonderfully, the glacier's detonations settling into something like a pulse; departing stewards famously shout indoors for a month afterward and report that ordinary silence feels wrong — thin, suspicious, like a held breath. Some pairs talk constantly, yelling cheerfully over the roar. Some pairs barely speak all winter and come out closer than family.

And they are, when you look at the whole valley squarely, its strangest inhabitants — not because they are human but because they are the only residents geared *down*. Everything else here lives at the valley's speed: the voles in months, the foxes remaking their ankles in decades, the trees in their fifteen-year waves, the channels in single nights, every creature burning loud and brief and being eaten back into the broth, so that every animal in the valley is built, molecule by molecule, out of every animal and tree that came before it — the place is one continuous body wearing forty thousand temporary faces. Nothing in it lasts long enough to remember anything. The vole does not remember last winter. The alder will not see the next decade. The valley, for all its violence of becoming, is perfectly amnesiac.

Except the cabin. The cabin remembers. The logbooks go back to the first rotation, and in them the valley has a past: the year the eastern river jumped, the first fox seen climbing, the aurora the crossbill-things went silent for, the petrel's arrival, the seventh and eighth conifers. That is what actually lives on the knoll above the flood line — not caretakers, because nothing here needs care, and not guards, because nothing here needs guarding. Memory lives there. Two people at a time, in the dark, at full volume, holding the only slow thing in the valley: the record of what all this roaring used to be, kept against the century when it finally quiets down and becomes merely a forest, and someone will want to know what it was like when it was young.
