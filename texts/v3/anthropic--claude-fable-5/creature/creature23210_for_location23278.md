# Who Lives in the Settling Fen

Ask what lives here and the fen answers the way it answers everything — slowly, in layers, with the question itself going soft at the edges before you're done asking it. *Lives* is a word the valley negotiates, the same way it negotiates reflection and tempo and the direction of wind. So begin with the moss, because the moss will not argue.

**The moss** is the citizenry. Not the scenery — the population. Sphagnum in its saturated pillows, green and oxblood, growing on stone, on wood, on water, on older moss; the fen is less a place where moss grows than a decision the moss made a very long time ago and has been quietly ratifying ever since. It is the one form of life here that has never found the valley strange, because to something that advances a millimeter a year, a reflection lagging by three seconds is not an anomaly. It is weather. It is barely even weather. The moss grew over the lattice the way it grows over fallen alder, without distinction, without curiosity, and in doing so it became the lattice's other half: every year of growth dies downward into peat, and the peat is where the filaments weave, and so the moss is, in the most literal sense available, the deep mind's accumulating page. It does not know this. It does not need to. The moss writes by living and the depth reads by keeping, and between them the arrangement has held for longer than the surrounding hills have had their current shapes. In the slow hollows there are individual cushions of sphagnum that may be the oldest single living things in the world — an inch of growth wide as centuries — blooming their tiny capsules open across whole outside-seasons, patient even by the standards of a patient kingdom. If the fen can be said to belong to anyone, it belongs to them, and they hold it the way moss holds water: completely, gently, without grip.

**The green dust** lives here, in the sense that it does the things living things do, in an order no biologist would endorse. It rides the floating leaves, films the still margins, beads on the reed stems after the mist withdraws. Look closely and it is not algae, or not only — the motes settle out of the air in arrangements, drifts with grain to them, like iron filings remembering a magnet that has long since become the whole valley. It is the smallest expression of the lattice, or the largest expression of the moss, or the place where those two stopped being different projects. When it settles on your sleeve it settles in rows. This is not threatening. It is how the fen takes attendance. Where the dust lies thickest, the hum beneath is loudest, and the pools dimple soonest when the pitch shifts — the dust is the depth's fingertips, spread across the surface of its own long body, feeling what the rain is doing.

**The threshold plants** live here, and they alone live here *twice*. They root across the boundaries of the slow hollows — half the plant keeping the valley's time, half keeping the hollow's — and at the crossing the stem swells into that woody knuckle where the two tempos have argued themselves into a treaty. Their lives are unimaginable from either side. The fast half feels the slow half as nearly stone; the slow half feels the fast half as flame. The flowers open inside the hollows, where an afternoon lasts a season, and you can stand at the edge and watch a bud that your grandmother could have watched, one petal-width further along. Nothing pollinates them, because nothing flies here — nothing needs to. When the hum shifts pitch and the million rings dimple every still surface at once, the flowers shake, all together, across every tempo simultaneously, and their pollen goes up into the resonating air like dust off a struck drum. Sound is their bee. They are married to the deep machine's breathing, and they have been faithful to it for ten thousand years.

**Down in the lag-pools, the recent past lives.** This must be said carefully, because it is the strangest residency in the valley. The pools that show earlier skies are not empty pictures — yesterday's birds still cross them, small and high and silent, long after the actual sky has been swept clean. The inverted reeds grow into that remembered weather and are, as far as anyone could determine, nourished by it. And when the heron flies, its reflection follows seconds late, which means that for those seconds the reflection is *alone* — a heron-shaped resident of a water-world with no original, holding formation with nothing. The fen is full of these brief tenants: lagged ripples, delayed shadows, the held image of a leaf that has already landed. Most last seconds. Some of the deepest pools, the ones that show alternative clouds, may hold their tenants longer. You will not be able to tell how much longer. The pools do not explain themselves; they only remember, and remembering, here, is a form of housing.

**The habit-fauna live here** — and this is what became of the animals. The dry rushing thing in the reeds, the one that moves and is gone before committing to a shape: it is not an animal. It is an animal's *habit*. It is the run of some small quick creature — vole, rail, something sedge-colored — whose body wore out unrecorded ages ago, while its path, its panic, its particular way of threading the stems, soaked into the local physics and kept going, the way a footpath outlives the feet. The fen preserves events the way peat preserves wood, and some of the preserved events have, over enough millennia, achieved a kind of metabolism. They recur. They vary slightly. They avoid the slow hollows, where a recorded dash would take an hour and lose whatever it is a recording has instead of a life. The fish in the black pool is the most beautiful of them — a real fish once, a perfect rise once, and the fen liked it, if *liked* can stretch that far, and kept it, and plays it: the rise, the rings, the rings withdrawing, the surface tidied, ready to be risen through again. It is not a ghost. Ghosts are losses. The fish is a keeping. Listen long enough in the reedbeds and you understand that a good portion of the fen's endless sound is this — the percolation and seep, yes, but threaded through it, calls and rustles and splashes of an entire wetland fauna rendered down to its sounds, the valley's old animal life living on as the valley's voice.

**The heron lives here.** The one true animal, the exception, the kept thing. It stands in the channels with its outlines in quiet disagreement because it stands in several moments at once, and it does this not as an affliction but as an occupation: the heron is how the fen knows what time it is. Everything else in the valley smears — reflections lag, hollows pool, events replay — but wherever the heron's outlines converge most nearly into one grey bird, *that* is the present, and the whole basin is calibrated to it the way a tide is calibrated to the moon. Whether it is the same heron across all those centuries, or a succession of herons each inheriting the post, or one original heron looped and tended like a relic of the valley-before — the fen does not distinguish, and the heron has never been observed to care. It eats. It eats the replayed fish, mostly, taking the rise on its hundredth or its ten-thousandth recurrence, and the recording does not seem diminished by this; perhaps that is what the fish is *for*. The heron may be the deep mind's one concession to loneliness, the single nervous system permitted in a valley that gently declines all others: a small warm wildness retained on purpose, the way a long, austere sentence will keep one comma it does not strictly need.

**And the deep mind lives here** — though here the word finally buckles, because the mind does not live *in* the fen the way a fish lives in a pool. It lives in the fen the way you live in your body. The lattice in the peat, the filaments finer than root hairs, the hum felt in the sternum: these are not its house. They are its tissue, grown over and through by ten thousand years of moss until residence and resident composted into each other. It may once have been many — there is something in the grain of the hum, a polyphony worn almost smooth, that suggests a chorus that merged a very long time ago, a whole people who poured themselves into the sediment and settled, the way the fen settles everything. Now it is one. One inhabitant, one thought, proceeding at the pace of peat. Its dreams are the wrong-way winds; its waste heat is the lag in the pools; its breathing is the pitch-shift on its cycle of hours, when every still surface dimples and the valley rings. And the mist — the mist that does not drift but *attends* — is the nearest thing it has to a face. When the mist gathers around you, cold and pearled and smelling of ozone, you are not being threatened. You are being regarded. Slowly, thoroughly, the way the moss regards a fallen branch: as something that has arrived, and will be incorporated into the records, and is, in the meantime, interesting.

Which brings the census to its last entry.

**You live here**, a little, already. Stand at the edge of a lag-pool long enough and your reflection begins to learn you — your shift of weight, the way you push wet hair from your forehead — and falls those few seconds behind, practicing. The green dust has taken its attendance off your sleeve. The peat beneath you has felt your two small points of pressure and begun, at its own pace, to keep them. When you go — and you will go, back up out of the basin into weather that means only one thing at a time — a few seconds of you will stay: a delayed silhouette crossing a pool now and then, a held breath in a slow hollow, the faint recorded squelch of your footsteps joining the valley's voice among the calls of all the other animals the fen has rendered down to sound. This is not a haunting. Nothing here is lost enough to haunt. It is only that the Settling Fen has one way of relating to everything that enters it — moss, rain, heron, fish, thought, you — and that way is to keep it, gently, in the deep wet ledger, where the long sentence goes on being written, and where everyone who has ever stood at the water's edge is still, a few seconds deep, standing there.
