The first thing you learn about the people of the Rootworks is that you have already met them. You met them in the floor's polish, in the mowed sparseness, in the syrup paid into the wicker cages — you walked through twenty minutes of their handiwork before you saw a single hand. They are like that. They precede themselves.

There are fewer of them than the noise suggests. Perhaps sixty souls, perhaps fewer — the count drifts, because the Rootworks does not distinguish very firmly between a person who has died and a person who has been sealed into the wall, and neither, after a while, do the families. Twelve households, one to a burl, the same twelve for as long as the bell-gall has hung. They do not call themselves anything collective. Asked who they are, they answer with a trade: *tapwright*, *valver*, *moth-binder*, *mower*, *gauge*. The trade is the name is the household is the burl. A child of the lathe-shed is called Spindle until old enough to earn a sound of her own, and the sound she earns will be a working sound — they name their children the way you'd name a tool, which is to say, with love, and with the expectation of long use.

**They are built for the place, or it has built them.** Short, most of them, barrel-chested, with the forearms of people who have spent generations persuading wood. Their skin runs amber at the hands and feet — sap-stain, soaked in past washing, deepening with age until the oldest of them have palms like the lanes themselves, dark and polished and faintly translucent at the edges, so that lamp-fly light passes through the web of an elder's thumb the way it passes through a leaf. Their hearing is the marvel. They have eyes, good ones, pale and wide-pupiled, but eyes here are a luxury for close work; what they live by is the ear. A Rootworks child of six can stand in the soft dark between burls and tell you the pressure in the south main line, the mood of the bats, whether rain is coming to the world above, and whether her uncle is lying — all from the chamber's tone. Their language is half pressure-words. They have nine terms for the difference between sap moving and sap about to move. They speak quietly, because the place is loud, and they have learned to slot their voices into the frequencies the machinery leaves empty, so that two valvers can converse across the full width of the chamber, under the hammers, without raising their voices at all, and a stranger standing between them hears nothing but the works.

**The tapwrights** are the senior trade, and they will tell you so. Theirs is the oldest negotiation: where to wound the plant, how deep, at what angle, in what season of its slow interior weather, so that the wound gives pressure instead of taking offense. A bad tap is not a leak. A bad tap is a *grievance*, and the plant files its grievances in wood, slowly, and a household that wounds carelessly will find — a year on, ten years on — that its burl has stopped growing, that its daylight-pipe is pearling over, that the wall behind its hearth has gone quiet of freight. Nothing is ever said. The plant does not say things. It simply stops including you. The tapwrights' whole art, beneath the augers and the gauges and the grown valve-stock, is the art of remaining included, and they practice it with the grim tenderness of people married a very long time to something enormous.

**The mowers** are the trade nobody thanks. The clearing — the hamlet-sized openness that makes the Rootworks a place rather than a density — does not want to exist. The plant heals toward it constantly, sends feeler-roots across the lanes in the night, drops slow tendrils from the vault like a ceiling deciding to become a floor. The mowers cut it back. Every day, forever. They work in pairs with girdling-knives and hot irons, and they are the only trade that apologizes aloud as it works — a low continuous murmur, formulaic, worn smooth as a lane: *not refused, only deferred; not refused, only deferred*. Whether the plant hears them is theology. That they would not dream of stopping the murmur is fact. Mowers die younger than the other trades and nobody knows why, and the plant takes their sealed bodies fastest of all, the wall puckering closed over a mower in a season where a tapwright takes a decade, and the families have decided, in the way that families decide things without ever holding a meeting, that this is forgiveness, and an honor, and they do not look at it more closely than that.

**The gauge-keepers** are one old woman and her line. The southernmost burl, the membrane door, the creature behind it — pale, eyeless, the size of a sleeping dog, its skin a slow lamp of shifting color as it tastes the lines — has been tended by her family since before the family had a name other than Gauge. She is the closest thing the Rootworks has to authority, not because she commands anyone but because everyone's safety is a color on her creature's back, and she is the one who can read it down to the fine shades — the difference between *high* and *high and climbing*, between *sweet* and *sweet the way it goes before a duct bursts*. She sleeps four hours at a stretch, in the burl, beside the creature, her hand on it, and it is an open question among the children — never among the adults, the adults have closed the question by refusing to ask it — which of the two is the gauge, and which the keeper. They have grown alike. Her skin has begun, in her great age, to show faint weather of its own.

**Then there are the citizens who are not people**, and here you must be careful, because the families are careful. The wheel-walkers — the loaf-sized rollers in the lathe-shed — are not livestock. Livestock can be sold. The wheel-walkers hold their wheel under terms: paste of a certain richness, smeared at certain hours, and a rest-hollow in the wall that no human enters, and these terms were settled so many generations back that breaking them is not forbidden so much as unimaginable, a thing with no grammar to think it in. The slate-winged moths know forty routes and will learn no new ones; when a new route is needed, a moth-binder spends two years of evenings painting it onto the vault in scent, one armspan a night, and the moths consider it, and sometimes decline. The bats are not negotiated with at all. The bats are weather. You do not bargain with weather; you build around it, and the families build around the bats — the shift-bell is timed to their pour, the open work paused, the syrup cages shuttered — with the unresentful accommodation of coastal people stepping back from a tide.

And below all of these, in the walls, is the freight — and the freight is where the living of the Rootworks stops being countable. Put your hand on the wall and feel it pass: grubs, shrews, the nameless many-legged things, and threaded among them, the families believe, their own dead. Not as ghosts. The Rootworks has no ghosts; it has no room for anything that does not work. As *traffic*. A sealed body is taken apart by the plant and the borers together, patiently, and what was a tapwright becomes sugar in the lines and shell on the beetles and pearl in the pipes, and when an old valver stands with her palm flat on the wall at evening, feeling the freight go by, she is not mourning. She is visiting. The wall is the family album, the churchyard, the queue of everyone who ever held a knife here, still moving, still under the surface, still — this is the word they use — *employed*.

**Which brings you to the largest inhabitant, and the last.** Because the honest answer to *who lives here* is: one thing. One thing lives here, and it is the walls and the floor and the vault and the cellar it ate, and everything else — the sixty souls, the twelve trades, the bats, the moths, the gauge, the freight, you, for as long as you stand there — is what it lives *with*, or what it lives *through*; the families decline to settle which. They are not its worshippers. They are not its parasites, quite, nor its organs, quite. The arrangement is older than any word they have for it, so they keep no word for it, only the rules, and the third rule — the one nobody states because everyone is standing inside it — is this: *nothing in the Rootworks lives alone, and nothing in the Rootworks stays a tenant.* Stay long enough and your palms go amber. Stay longer and your hearing rearranges itself around the hammers and the wet *thock* of the valves until silence, when you finally climb back up through the slot between the buttress roots into the indifferent air, sounds like something has died. Stay your whole life, and at the end of it the wall will open for you, and pucker shut, and the lanes will keep their polish, and twice a day the chamber will sweat, and somewhere in the dark margins a child named for a tool will press her hand to the wood and feel you go by — busy, included, part of the freight — and the spindle will go on turning, making something, as it always is, for a daylight none of you has ever needed to see.
