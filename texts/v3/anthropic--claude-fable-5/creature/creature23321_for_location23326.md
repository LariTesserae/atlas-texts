**Those Who Keep the Quiet Steeps**

Begin with what is easiest to count, because the town's residents arrange themselves, conveniently, in tiers — as if they too were terraced.

---

**The loaf snails** are the oldest citizens, almost certainly. You can read it in their shells: each one banded in fine grey rings like tree-growth, and the rings, when counted, run into the hundreds. If each ring is a cycle of the mist — and the rhythm of new shell at the lip suggests it is — then the largest snails were grazing these stones before some of the stones were laid. They may be the only living things in the valley that watched the building happen.

They are white in the way candle tallow is white, with a faint translucence, and a herd of them at rest looks like a fall of small boulders until one tips slowly forward and the others follow. They move in formation — not a flocking instinct exactly, but a *rotation*, an order of march that takes them over every stair and flag of the town in a circuit of about forty days, scouring the algal slick before it can become a hazard to anything with feet. No one directs this. The eldest snail goes first; the rest follow the silver of her trail; and when she dies — the herds gather at a death, pressed shell to shell for a day and a night, doing nothing visible — the next eldest goes first instead, and the circuit continues without a missed flagstone.

Their empty shells do not lie where they fall. They turn up later, fitted into wall-niches, mouth outward, in rows. Whether the snails do this for their dead or something else does it for them is not known. The shells hold rainwater. Small things drink from them.

**The candle salamanders** live at the cisterns and may be said to *operate* them, in the way a verger operates a church: not understanding the architecture, faithful to it absolutely. They are pale gold, translucent enough that the slow valve of the heart is visible behind the foreleg, and they never travel more than a body-length from deep water in their lives. They are arranged — there is no other word — shoulder to shoulder along the cistern rims, evenly spaced, facing inward, and the spacing is maintained: when one dies or is taken, the line contracts overnight to close the gap, the way the source text of the town closes every gap.

Here is the thing about the salamanders. The cisterns drip — *toc... toc... toc* — and the salamanders' throats pulse, and the two rhythms are not the same, but they are *related*; the throat-pulse of the whole assembled line shifts, over hours, to a fixed ratio of the drip. Three pulses to a drop in the thick mist. Five to a drop when it thins. They are, collectively, a kind of escapement — a living mechanism keeping a time that nothing else in the valley appears to consult. Unless everything does. Unless the snail rotation, the moth swarming, the pebble-moving, are all downstream of that count, the way a town's life is downstream of a bell no one individually remembers deciding to ring.

They eat the moths that fall on the water. They are eaten, in turn, in administrative silence. Their eggs are laid in the cushion-moss at the rim, and the moss — brighter there, denser there — is the moss that ends up packed into the wall joints. Whether this is coincidence is the sort of question the Quiet Steeps declines to answer.

**The kettle-birds** are the town's civil service, if it has one. Round, grey, soft-feathered to the point of looking unfinished, flightless and uninterested in the fact, they move in processions of six to twenty through the fern understory, and their silence is not a deficiency. Watch the bobbing long enough and the conviction grows that it has parts of speech. There is a low, slow bob that passes forward down the line and stops the procession. A doubled bob that turns it. A spreading, ripple-fast bob — rare, seen perhaps when the silver thing is moving on the terraces above — that scatters them into the ferns in under a second, soundlessly, like grey water poured into green.

And there are the pebbles. Each kettle-bird carries one at a time, in the beak, with the careful frontward gravity of someone carrying a full cup. The pebbles are pale, mountain-quarried, thumb-sized, and they go into particular rooms — always the same eleven rooms — into niches at floor level, in arrangements: rows, arcs, small dense clusters. The arrangements change. Not randomly: pebbles are *removed* as well as added, carried out and away and set down on the lowest terrace at the valley's edge, in a long windrow of discharged pebbles that has been growing, by the size of it, for a few generations at most.

The temptation, which every visitor feels and should be honest about, is to call it writing. It is more careful than nesting. It is more revised than instinct usually permits. The eleven rooms have something in common: they are the rooms with the lowest doorways, the most finished masonry, the chisel-work done with the most patience. The best rooms. The pebbles go where the builders, whoever they were, took the most trouble — as if the birds were keeping accounts in the offices where accounts were once kept, in a notation that survived the language.

**The felt moths** explain, in part, the silence — and it is a relief, when you work it out, that some of the hush has a mechanism. Their wings shed a continual fine dust of grey scales, and the scales settle on everything: leaf, lintel, flagstone, water. A surface coated in moth-felt does not ring, click, or splash. The fat condensation drops fall onto leaves padded in grey and are received without comment. The town is *upholstered*. Generations of moths have soundproofed it the way generations of feet hollow a stair, and the larvae complete the arrangement from below: they eat decay — fallen fronds, dead wood, anything that would rot loudly and smell — so that the town's compost happens out of sight, inside leaves of grey felt, and emerges as more moths, more dust, more quiet.

When the canopy exhales — the soundless blizzard — it is mostly males, mostly dying, and they come down over the cisterns where the salamanders wait. Even the moths' deaths are routed somewhere useful. There is very little in the Quiet Steeps that is allowed to be merely lost.

**The velvet doorkeepers** — the hyrax-like things — are the masons now, and they are not very good at it, and they do not stop. Each occupies a doorway or window-niche, one to a post, apparently for life; the fur of long occupancy polishes the stone sill to a dark gloss, and when a doorkeeper dies its post stands empty for a few days and is then filled by a young one, who sits wrong in it at first, too small for the polished hollow, and grows into the shape of the office.

Their work is the walls. They carry wads of cistern-moss in their cheeks and tamp it into open joints with their forepaws, patting it down with a fussy, repetitive motion, ten pats, twelve, until something in them is satisfied. When a stone slips from a course they worry at it — sometimes for weeks — shouldering it, rolling it, until it goes back into the wall *somewhere*, often slightly wrong, a stone from the third course wedged proudly into the fifth. They understand that the wall must be whole. They do not understand the wall. The town, repaired by them for long enough, is very slowly becoming a copy of itself drawn from memory by someone who loved it.

**The silver thing** is single, as far as anyone can tell — one long, low animal, weasel-built but larger, furred in grey so pale it reads as a fold of the mist, working the terrace tops in the dimmest hours. It is the only resident that owns the whole town rather than a station in it. Its kills are bloodless to the eye and inaudible to the ear, and they are *selected*: the trailing snail, never the lead; the salamander whose throat has fallen out of rhythm; the kettle-bird that has stopped carrying pebbles. It does not take from the same herd twice running. It is doing something closer to pruning than hunting, and whether that restraint is its own or was bred into it by ten thousand quiet generations, the result is the same: the silver thing is the town's editor, removing what no longer keeps the pattern, and the pattern closes over the place where it fed.

It has a den. No one has found it. The seven stairways are accounted for; the den is not on any of them.

---

**And then the eyes.**

In the roofed passages, in the deepest green-lit rooms, at the limit of what the grey-green dusk permits — small eyes, many of them, set low, watching with the patience of stone. They do not approach. They do not retreat. The dark around them smells faintly of struck rock, the smell of the chisel-marks, the smell of work.

No one has seen what they belong to. But the tools in the niches — the antler picks, the mallets glossy at the grip — bear this remarking: the gloss is not old. Wood abandoned in this dripping air for even a few years goes dark, swells, furs over with moss. The mallet grips are pale and smooth and dry, the way wood is kept only by handling.

So a visitor stands in a passage, in the upholstered silence, and assembles the inventory: builders who were small, by the doorways; patient, by the joints; metal-less, by everything; gone, by the streets. And eyes in the dark at exactly the height the doorways imply. And no graves anywhere in the valley — or rather, one cist found beneath the plaza flags, hand-built, perfectly fitted, containing nothing but moss, packed and tamped, ten pats, twelve.

The town does not say. The visitor is free to believe the builders died and the animals are their habits, persisting the way a wave persists after the wind; or that the builders left and the animals are their tenants, holding the lease; or that the builders are still here, narrowed down to eyes and the maintenance of mallets, having handed the daily keeping over to the citizens they trained, or bred, or simply outlasted — and now do nothing but watch the work go on, the way the very old watch a household run itself, intervening only at night, only at the edges, only to move what a visitor leaves in a niche the small distance to where it apparently belongs.

---

**Who lives in the Quiet Steeps?**

The honest answer is that the town does — that the inhabitants are best understood not as a fauna but as a body, with the snails for hands and the salamanders for a heart, the moths for skin, the birds for memory, the doorkeepers for habit, the silver thing for whatever it is in a body that decides which cells must go. Each resident keeps one fragment of an intention none of them holds whole. Past the last wall the body ends, cleanly, and the wind on the bare rock outside is just wind, keeping nothing, and very loud about it.

But stand in the plaza at the hour the mist thins, when the savage peaks show their teeth above the valley and the salamanders shift their count from three to five, and a simpler answer suggests itself.

What lives here is the keeping. Everything else — shell, throat, feather, fur, and whatever waits behind the eyes — is just what the keeping is currently wearing.
