Begin with the obvious inhabitants, because they are only obvious for a moment before they become strange again.

The butterflies are small — smaller than you'd guess from the mass of them, each one hardly bigger than a postage stamp. Folded, they are the color of the shadowed wall: grey-brown, faintly scaled with rust, a camouflage so complete that the roost reads as texture rather than population. The fire is on the inside. The upper faces of the wings, hidden when they cling, are orange and amber shading to something near ember-red at the body, and this is why the storms burn the way they do: every butterfly in flight is showing the room what it keeps concealed the rest of the time. A creature that is a wall when it is still and a spark when it moves.

No individual butterfly has ever seen this place twice. That is the first thing to understand about them. They hatch far below, in the last warm weeks of summer, in meadows two vertical miles down where their caterpillars chew some tough, unglamorous alpine plant that nothing else wants. They pupate in the frost-shadow of stones. They emerge, harden their wings, feed for a handful of days — and then something turns over in them, all at once, and they fly *up*. Not dispersing, not wandering: up, into thinner and colder air, against every apparent interest of a cold-blooded animal, following a route that none of them has flown before and that none of them will ever fly again. They funnel along the ridge past the drowned cairns, find the gap under the diverged trunks the way water finds a drain, and pour inside, and stop.

Then they do almost nothing for the better part of a year.

This is the second thing to understand: the roost is not a gathering, it is a suspension. Inside the chamber they drop into a torpor just shy of death — heartbeat slowed to a rumor, wings closed, metabolism banked down to embers to match the ember-light they're hiding. They do not eat. There is nothing here to eat. They live on the fat they carried up the mountain and on the water the room gives them: when a drop of condensation completes its long fall and lands in the moss, butterflies converge on the dark spot slowly, like a crowd around an accident, and unroll their tongues into it. The room drinks the air and the butterflies drink the room. It is the whole economy of the place, conducted one drop at a time, in silence.

The storms are the exception, and nobody — no observer, if there were observers, and possibly not the butterflies either — knows what they are for. Perhaps they shiver the condensation off their wings before it can seed mold. Perhaps the mass must periodically redistribute itself, moving the ones on the cold outer margins into the warm interior of the clusters, the way penguins rotate through a huddle. Perhaps it is simpler and worse than that: perhaps the signal to fly is always latent in each of them, held down by a hundred thousand neighbors also holding it down, and every so often the suppression fails somewhere — one butterfly's nerve gives out — and the failure cascades. A panic with no predator. A rumor with no content. The roost erupts, exhausts the impulse, and settles, having communicated nothing except that it can still, at any moment, become the other thing it is.

In spring the survivors mate — here, in the cold light, a brief graceless flurry that looks like a smaller storm — and then they leave the way they came, downhill this time, gliding on the fat they have left, and they lay their eggs in the meadows and die within days. Their children will never meet them. Their children will nonetheless fly up the mountain, along the ridge, past the cairns, through the gap, and cling to the same vault, sometimes to the same square foot of moss. The route is not remembered. The route is inherited, printed somewhere below memory, so that the true resident of the chamber is not any butterfly but the *habit* of butterflies — an unbroken thread of arrival that has been re-tied every year for longer than the trees have been fused.

Not all of them leave. Every winter takes a tithe. The ones that fail simply let go — a closed grey wing detaching from the mass, falling without struggle through the beam, landing in the moss with the same soundlessness as the water. And the moss takes them. Within a season there is nothing to find: the room digests its dead, quietly and completely, and the ochre stain beneath the great roosts is not only wing-dust but generations, whole invisible strata of them, feeding down through moss and lichen into the roots. The trees are, in a strict nutritional sense, made partly of butterflies. The vault the living ones cling to is grown from the ones that stopped. They roost on their own ancestors and do not know it, and the room does not tell them, and this is the arrangement — shelter paid for in bodies, so slowly and so gently that it looks like nothing at all.

Which brings up the second inhabitant, the one that is easy to mistake for architecture: the room itself is alive. Not metaphorically. The fused crowns, the braided trunks, the roots crossing the floor — this is a community of trees so old and so interpenetrated that it may by now be effectively one organism, sharing water and sugar through grafted wood, keeping the oculus thinned in the one place where thinning lets in light without letting in wind. It grows perhaps a millimeter a year. On its timescale the butterflies are weather — an annual precipitation of wings that arrives, shivers, feeds it, and evaporates. It has been drinking this weather for centuries. If it has anything like preference, it prefers the arrangement to continue.

And below the scale of butterflies there is a third population, the one you will never see without lying face-down in the moss with a lens: springtails, oribatid mites round and armored as poppy seeds, tardigrades fat with stored water, small pale things that spend entire lifetimes inside a single handful of green. Some of the mites ride the butterflies — up the mountain and down again, generations commuting on wings they will never understand — so that the roost is also a port, and every eruption is also, at some minuscule scale, a shipwreck and a landfall. Notably absent: spiders. There are no webs anywhere in the chamber, and there never will be, because twice an hour the air becomes a blizzard of ten thousand tumbling bodies and no web survives that. The storms are, among whatever else they are, housekeeping. The panic keeps the room safe for the panicking.

The people are gone, but it would be careless to say nothing lives here of them. They came, the traces suggest, in ones and twos, seasonally, carrying almost nothing — a lamp or a bowl for the flat stone, patience for the bench. They took nothing either, which means they were not here for the butterflies as material: not for dye or food or medicine. They were here to sit in the beam and wait for the walls to let go. Whatever they called that — worship, divination, or only the deep human need to be inside a large event and survive it — the practice ended, the path went unrenewed, and all that persists is a rectangle of rot held in shape by roots that grew through the bench while it still had a shape to learn. The trees are, in the most literal way, remembering the furniture. That is where the people live now: as a habit in the wood, the way the route lives as a habit in the butterflies.

Which leaves the last inhabitant, the one with no body at all. Something in this room decides. Something moves through a hundred thousand separate nervous systems in under two seconds and turns a wall into a storm, and it is not any single butterfly, and it is not in the air, and it leaves no trace except a new arrangement of the roost. It has lived here longer than the oldest tree. It is what the people climbed two miles to sit near. Call it the signal, the letting-go, the one thought the colony has — it spends its whole existence waiting in a hundred thousand small cold bodies at once, and every so often it stands up.

If you hold still, it will treat you as terrain. That is the closest it comes to acknowledgment: butterflies on your shoulders and hands, wings breathing open and closed, cool as coins, and you standing in the beam like the bench, like the stone, like one more thing the room might slowly learn — waiting with all of them for the instant when it says *now*.
