**Census**

The first thing to understand about the inhabitants of the Works is that they cannot be counted, and that this is not because they are hidden. They are in plain sight, in the bright sourceless light, on the housings and in the aisles and at the bolt-pools. They cannot be counted because counting assumes that one thing is one thing, and on the deck this assumption fails gently, the way the geometry fails — not all at once, but when you compare notes.

So the census of the Works is not a number. It is a list of *kinds*, and the kinds are these: the iterated, the crews, the provisional, and — depending on which theory of the place you hold — the Works itself, which may be the only inhabitant there has ever been, with everything else a symptom of it.

What unites them is the work they do, whether or not they know they are doing it. The deck is being written from one side and weathered from the other, and everything that lives here lives on the weathering side. To inhabit the Works is to soften it. A handrail gripped ten thousand times learns smoothness. A path walked daily stops ratcheting. The inhabitants are not guests of the place. They are its finishing process — the running-in of a machine too large to be run in any other way.

**The Iterated**

Start with the pigeons, because the pigeons make the principle visible.

There appear to be thousands of them — silent, grey, roosting in the gantry lattice in such density that whole crane structures seem feathered. But watch one long enough and you will notice a particular bird: a missing toe on the left foot, a white fleck above the cere. Then walk a kilometer and look up, and notice the same bird. And again. The deckfolk, who have had time to study this, hold that there is exactly one pigeon. It is everywhere it needs to be. The flock is not a population; it is an *emphasis* — one small original, stated as many times as the place requires, the way a draftsman hatches a shadow with repeated strokes.

The goats are the same, but the goats know it. There may be five of them, or nine; the count drifts seasonally, if the deck can be said to have seasons. They stand on the high housings and watch you with the horizontal patience of their pupils, and they are unbothered by their own multiplicity in a way that suggests either deep stupidity or a relationship with location that you simply do not share. The deckfolk milk them, and occasionally — carefully, with a kind of liturgical apology — slaughter one. The meat is real. The fat renders. The hide tans. And within a day or two the count restores itself, the same chipped horn reappearing on the same far housing, chewing the same wiry grass, holding no apparent grudge. The deckfolk eat this meat but call it *borrowed*, and there is an old prohibition, its origin lost, against ever slaughtering all the goats you can see at once. Nobody tests it.

The small grey animals — the residents call them **greys**, with the flat practicality of people who name what they live with — den in the cable trays and drink at the bolt-pools in shifts. They are the most weathered things on the deck: their coats are exactly the color of swarf, their droppings seed the moss, their endless small traffic has worn the first true patina anywhere in the region, faint dark trails polished into the plating along their runs. If the deck is ever to look old, it will look old there first, at ankle height, where the greys have been insisting on time.

The swifts and the insects belong to each other and to the heat. The insects rise in slow columns off the warm machinery, the swifts cut through the columns, and neither makes a sound that arrives when it should. Sometimes, standing in an aisle at evening, you will hear the screaming of swifts from a hunt that happened that morning, delivered to you now like a letter.

**The Crews**

The people of the Works number somewhere between two hundred and four hundred, and they will tell you frankly that the figure is soft, and they will not explain why, and after a while you will understand why on your own.

They did not found this place. Nobody founds a place that finishes itself. They came up through the cloud over a few generations — surveyors who stopped sending reports down, salvors who found nothing loose enough to salvage, a handful of pilgrims following a rumor of a country with no dirt in it, sick of dirt for reasons of their own. They stayed for the same reason anyone stays anywhere: the place had a use for them before they had a name for the use.

The name they eventually found is **the crews**, and the use is *finishing*.

They are deburrers, polishers, breakers of edges. They wrap raw handrails in goat-hide and cloth and walk them with gloved hands, season after season, until the steel concedes and goes smooth — and it does concede; the deck appears to accept use as a kind of instruction, hardening into whatever shape habit insists on. This is the central fact of crew life and the root of all their customs: *what is done repeatedly becomes true here.* So they do things repeatedly, and they are extremely careful about what.

Every morning, crews **walk the lines** — fixed routes through the machine-forest, the same turns, the same ducks under the same trunk-thick pipes, recited with the body rather than the voice. A line walked daily holds still. The four-way junction stays four-way along a walked line. Distances stop ratcheting on them; a crew route out to the deck's edge is the same length as the route back, which makes the walked lines the only honest roads in the region, and the keeping of them a civic duty as serious as a well. Let a line lapse for a season and the geometry begins to negotiate again, machinery sidling, aisles re-deciding, and the line must be *re-argued*, walked twice daily by relays until the deck gives in.

They live inside the housings — those hills that were never hills — in chambers warmed by the deep labor of the machinery, their walls thrumming at the edge of feeling. They harvest the afternoon cloud: every dwelling flies a rig of cold plates and channels that beads over when the haze settles, and the runnels feed cisterns, and the water is pure and mineral-flat and tastes, residents say, *of nothing at all, which takes getting used to.* They eat borrowed goat, silent squab, the boiled wire-grass (bitter, then suddenly not), moss broths, and whatever they can trade for on their rare descents below the cloud line — salt, mostly, and grain, and news, which goes stale slower than grain but stales all the same.

They do not talk much in open air, because the air cannot be trusted with a voice. Speak in an aisle and your words may be delivered an hour later, three districts away, to whoever happens to be standing there — and so an unguarded sentence is considered a kind of littering, and overheard far-off voices are received with the etiquette of mail: heard out fully, never answered. Instead the crews speak through the metal, which is faithful. The pipe runs are their telephone exchange. Every dwelling has its tap-bar, every crew its knock-name, and at any hour the deep continuous steel of the deck carries a low traffic of conversation, felt in the palm or the teeth, rhythmic, patient, ringing from district to district through tree-trunk pipe — the only communication on the deck that arrives on time, every time. A crew child learns the knock-language before the spoken one. Some elders have nearly stopped speaking aloud altogether and converse by resting their knuckles on a stanchion, and these elders are considered, not jokingly, to be *partway home*.

You can read tenure on a resident's shadow. Newcomers cast one, dark and singular, and the crews can spot a one-shadowed stranger across a whole district. Stay a few years and a second shadow buds off the first, pale, at a slight angle. The oldest crews cast three or four, overlapping like a misregistered print, and walk inside their little fan of selves without ever looking down at it.

**The Provisional**

Stay long enough, and the deck begins to take an interest in you the way it took an interest in the goats.

It starts small. A crew elder is seen tending a line in the eastern trenches at the same hour she is verifiably asleep in her housing. The deck has *drafted* her — sketched a confident second instance, doing the work she would be doing, doing it correctly. The instances do not meet; the deck seems to manage them with the same tact it uses on the goats. The drafted elder remembers the trench-work afterward, dimly, the way you remember a task you did while thinking about something else. Among the crews this is not feared. It is, in fact, the closest thing they have to an honor: it means the deck has decided your habits are worth stating more than once. It means you have become *load-bearing*.

And when a load-bearing resident dies, the deck is reluctant to lose the habit. For a season or two after a death, the dead one's line continues to be walked. You can see them at it — fully real for ten steps, so to speak, and then continuing as a confident sketch of themselves, edges luminous and undecided, casting plural pale shadows, making the turns, ducking the pipes, tending the route. The crews walk beside these sketches in silence, matching pace, and consider it the proper form of mourning: you keep the dead company while the deck finishes forgetting them, or — the elders' version — while the deck finishes *learning* them, taking the route into itself, hardening the habit into plating the way the half-real stairways harden into steel. Either way, when the sketch stops appearing, the line is found to hold by itself thereafter, without walking. No one has ever been comfortable saying what that means. Everyone is comforted by it anyway.

Children born on the deck cast two shadows from the first day, and their cries arrive in the room a few seconds after their faces show the crying. Their mothers learn to watch instead of listen. These children are the first true natives of the Works, and they move through the machine-forest with an ease that unsettles even their parents — turning down aisles before the aisles have fully decided to be there, knocking greetings into pipework toward people who haven't arrived yet, drinking at the bolt-pools shoulder to shoulder with the greys, who do not run from them.

**The Principal Resident**

There remains the question of the Millwright.

The crews maintain almost nothing, because almost nothing needs it — but *almost* is the word that keeps the theory alive. Now and then a belt is found retensioned that nobody tensioned. A bearing is greased an hour before the crew assigned to grease it arrives, by no one, with the right grease, in the right amount. The skeptics say this is just the deck's mis-delivery running in reverse — that sound arrives late here and work arrives early, that the greased bearing is simply your own round, posted ahead of you. The believers say: then who posted the *first* round? And they speak of the Millwright — one true maintainer somewhere in the dense interior, never seen, walking lines no one else knows, the original of which all the crews are perhaps the iterations, the one pigeon of the human kind.

The elders hold a quieter view than either. They say the Works is the inhabitant, the only one; that the deck is an organism still mid-birth, manufacturing itself a few cubic meters a day; and that everything living on it — moss, wire-grass, the one pigeon, the borrowed goats, the greys, the crews, the children with their plural shadows — is the deck's own immune system against permanence-without-character, its hired weather, invented or invited because a thing cannot become *real* by fabrication alone. Something has to grip the handrails. Something has to wear the trails. Something has to die on it, and be sketched, and be absorbed. By this account the inhabitants are not living *on* the Works at all. They are how the Works lives.

The elders knock this theory into the pipes sometimes, late, as a kind of prayer. The deck, of course, does not answer.

But the routes hold.

**Evening Round**

So: a crew woman, three-shadowed, walks her line as the cloud settles and the whole region beads over and goes jeweled. She wears goat-hide gloves gone glassy with use. Her knuckles rest on a waist-height pipe as she walks, reading the evening traffic — her daughter's knock-name from two districts north, a long slow message from the trench crews below the grating, where the brightness is exactly equal to the brightness above and someone is always climbing. A grey crosses her path on its polished trail and does not hurry. On the compressor housing overhead, a goat with a chipped horn watches her pass, and four minutes later, at the end of the aisle, watches her arrive.

Behind her, faithfully, her words from this morning are being delivered to an empty junction. Ahead of her, at the line's far turn, a pale luminous figure makes the turn first — old Sef, dead since the spring, still tending the route, a little more finished than he was last month, a little more the deck's than hers.

She matches his pace for the length of the trench, as is proper. Then he is not there, and the line holds anyway, and she walks home through the dense silent forest of machines to a warm chamber inside a hill that was never a hill, where the walls labor and thrum, and the water tastes of nothing at all, and the household speaks in knocks, and everyone she loves casts more than one shadow — which is, on the Works at Altitude, simply what it looks like to belong.
