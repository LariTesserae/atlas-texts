**The people of the shoulder**

Start with the body, because the place wrote the body first.

They are short and dense, the people who live here, built close to their own centers of gravity the way their houses are. Barrel chests — the lungs inside them are half again the size of a lowlander's, and the blood is thick with what it needs to pull a living out of thin air. Their hands are the most worked part of them: broad, dry, seamed, the nails kept short and the pads callused into something between skin and horn, because every day of a life here involves stone, and stone does not negotiate. A child of six already has a mason's hands in miniature. An elder's hands look quarried.

They move slowly and they are not slow. This is the first thing a traveler misreads. At this elevation, hurry is a debt the body collects immediately, so the people have evolved a gait — everyone has it, herders and weavers and children carrying water — that looks like strolling and covers ground like weather. They can hold that pace up a thousand feet of switchback and arrive breathing through their noses. They find running funny. There is a whole genre of jokes about lowland traders running, and the punchline is always the same: *and then he had to sit down.*

Their faces are wind-finished. Dark, in the way of people who live under hard light with no green to soften it, with a permanent flush across the cheekbones where the small blood vessels have given up retreating from the cold. The eyes are creased nearly shut by reflex and habit both; you learn to read mood here from the mouth and the hands, because the eyes are always squinting at something a valley away. They go gray early and live long, and the very old are a category of their own — tiny, folded, almost mineral, sitting in the warm angles of courtyards like something the masonry produced.

**How they sound**

Quietly, and then not with the voice at all.

Spoken language exists, of course, and is beautiful in a dry way — a tongue with eleven words for kinds of stone fracture and one word that means both "water" and "permission." But the voice is for indoors, for the lamplit honeycombs and the shared courtyards, for distances of an arm's reach. Past that, the whistle takes over. Every person carries a second vocabulary made of two-note phrases, pitched and bent, that ride the thin air the way shouting can't. There are whistles for *coming up*, *coming down*, *the herd has turned*, *stone moving above you*, *a death*, *a birth*, *strangers on the high path, three of them, slow.* A mother can call one specific child out of forty on a slope. Lovers have private phrases. The whistled language has dialects, drifting from city to city along the benches, and a traveler can tell how far they've walked by how the death-whistle has changed.

The deepest fluency is in silence, though. They are not a taciturn people — they gossip, they argue water rights with real venom, their courtyard evenings are dense with low talk — but they treat silence as a substance rather than an absence, something the mountain makes and they live inside. Nobody talks over the *tock* of the settling rock. There is a half-beat pause after it, every time, unconscious, the way other peoples pause for thunder. Ask them why and they look at you. The mountain said something. You let it finish.

**How they are arranged**

By water, first and always. The seep-pockets — those wet green lungs where moss and the blue grass grow — are owned the way nothing else here is owned, in lineages, with the inheritance reckoned through whoever has tended them. A family is, at bottom, the set of people with a claim on a particular seep. Marriage is partly hydrology. The feuds the land breeds are about water and the law that has grown up to contain them is the most elaborate thing the cities have: arbiters who memorize generations of seep-claims in knotted cord, hearings held at the seep itself with both families standing in sight of the disputed moss, judgments delivered in a formal register of the whistle-language so that the whole slope hears the ruling at once and there can be no later argument about what was said.

Below the water-law, the work sorts people loosely. Masons, who are closer to priests than to builders — anyone can stack stone, but a mason is someone certified by other masons as able to read grain and settle, someone permitted to lay a course on a wall that will outlive them, and the certification takes twenty years because the proof of the work takes twenty years. Followers, who walk the seasonal circuits with the half-wild herds and are half-wild themselves by city standards, smelling of lanolin and absence, returning each year with wool, news, and the particular long-distance gaze of people who navigate by ranks of peaks. Weavers and ropemakers, whose braided spans across the gorges are the only infrastructure here that is ever replaced rather than added to, and who therefore carry a faint glamour of mortality — rope people understand that things end. Cordkeepers, who maintain the knotted records, and whose training is mostly memory and whose vice is mostly pedantry. And the lamp-quarter people, the families of the oldest honeycombed districts, who live by tallow-light at noon in the buried original streets and are considered — gently, by everyone above them — a little strange, a little inward, the city's marrow.

There are no kings. The land won't carry one. Authority here is the authority of the senior mason, the senior cordkeeper, the water-arbiters — competence with a long fuse — and decisions of weight are made the way walls are made: proposed in one season, left to settle through a winter of courtyard talk, taken up again. A traveler asking *who is in charge* gets the answer *of what?*, and it is a real question.

**How they begin and end**

Children are communal by architecture. Six families to a courtyard means a child belongs to the courtyard first, and the language reflects it — the word a child uses for the adults of their courtyard is one word, undifferentiated, something like *the older warmth*. A child's first real possession is a stone: at around seven they are taken to the talus by whichever courtyard elder is the certifying sort and told to choose a slab, and the choosing is watched closely, because how a child reads grain at seven is held to predict everything. The stone is laid into the family wall. The child then knows, permanently, in a way no lowland child knows anything, exactly where they are in the structure of the world: third course above the door, left of the smoke-gap.

The dead go up. There is no soil to take them and no wood to burn them, so they are carried — slowly, at the strolling pace, in a procession that whistles its single repeated phrase ahead of itself so the slope falls quiet as it passes — to the platform ledges above each city, and given to the scavenger-birds that ride the thermals all day without a wingbeat. The people do not find this grim and are puzzled by visitors who do. The birds are known individually; the great ones, the ones that have attended a city for forty years, have names, and a death attended by a named bird is a good death. The bones come back. They are cleaned by the birds and the wind and brought down a year later, and worked — bone is one of the four materials of life here — into tools that stay in the family. A grandmother's awl. A grandfather's loom-comb. The dead keep working. Nobody here would say *gone.* They say *settled*, the same word the masons use for a wall that has taken its weight.

**Their fellow residents**

The cities are not human-only and the people don't pretend otherwise. The soot-colored wall-sparrows that nest in every drystone gap are considered part of the house, almost part of the family — a house that goes silent of birds is a house with something wrong in its walls, dampness or shifting, and the birds' departure is taken as the building's own report. Killing one is not illegal because it would not occur to anyone. The whistling rodents of the talus are food, fur, weather-prophets, and comedy; the children's stories are full of them, brave and foolish in equal measure. The herds are something between livestock and weather: owned in the loose sense that a seep is owned, followed rather than driven, their circuits older than any city record, the cities themselves arguably built where they are because the herds already passed there.

And the pale cat. It is the one animal the people will not whistle about, on the theory — never quite stated as belief, never quite dismissed as habit — that it understands the whistle-language and finds being discussed rude. It takes from the herd margins at dusk and the followers count this as a tithe rather than a loss; a circuit year in which the cat takes nothing is held to be a bad sign, evidence of something amiss above. Most people see it once or twice in a life. The seeing is private. You do not announce it. You let it settle, like everything else, and years later, in some lamplit evening, you might say *I was on the north bench, the year of the rockfall,* and the courtyard goes quiet, and that is the whole story, and it is enough.

**What they believe**

That the world is young and they are early. This is the root of everything else. They live on rock so new it still announces its own rising, and they have drawn the obvious conclusion: the mountains are not finished, therefore nothing is, therefore the proper relation to existence is the relation of a mason to a wall mid-course. Not despair, not urgency — *tenure*. You are here during the work. You will not see the work done. The work does not require you to see it done; it requires you to lay your course true and leave it to settle.

They have no creation story to speak of, which startles lowlanders, but they have an enormous, intricate, lovingly maintained *continuation* story — the cord-records, the genealogies of seeps, the named birds, the wall where every family can point to the child-stones of six generations. Their cosmology is, in effect, the same as their masonry. The mountain rises a finger-width a year. The water beads out of the rock each morning, the day's allowance, neither pleaded for nor thanked for, simply caught. Somewhere above the highest city the ice does whatever the ice does on its rhythm longer than memory, and the people's word for that rhythm and their word for the divine are, perhaps not coincidentally, built on the same root, which is also the root of their word for *patience*, which is also — and this they will tell you with the nearest thing they have to a smile, the mouth and the hands both opening slightly — the root of their word for themselves.

They have no other name for themselves. There has never been anyone else to be distinguished from. Asked who they are, they answer with the word, and the word means, more or less, *those who wait well.* Asked what they are waiting for, they make the small gesture — palm tilted, fingers loose — that means the same thing here as a shrug means anywhere, and they go back to whatever they were doing, which is, if you watch long enough, always some version of the same thing: laying a course, and leaving it to settle, and not expecting to be the one who lays the next.
