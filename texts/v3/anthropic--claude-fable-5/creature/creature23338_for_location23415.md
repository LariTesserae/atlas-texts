**Who Lives in the Penstock Vaults**

Begin with the part of the population you will never see whole, because it is the floor.

The white threadwork stitching the moss-felt together is the vaults' majority resident — a fungal commons that has been knitting itself outward since the year the rift opened, and which has long since stopped being plural. It runs from column to column under the felt, climbs the root-bark in pale veining, threads the grafts where spruce has fused to spruce, and through those grafts it almost certainly continues upward, out of the vault entirely, into the living forest overhead. The surveyors have stopped trying to map where it ends. The honest answer may be that the largest inhabitant of the Penstock Vaults is a single organism wearing the entire rift as a glove — floor, columns, ceiling, and some unknowable acreage of ordinary daylit woodland above — and that everything else that lives here lives *on* it, or *in* it, the way mites live in a pelt. It fruits rarely and shyly: in late autumn, under the drip-lines, brittle white bodies stand up out of the felt for a week or two, release whatever they release into the cold mist near the machine works, and lie down again. The crews step around them without being told to. Nobody made that a rule. It became one anyway.

In the felt itself: springtails, pale and numberless, and the slow white beetles that work the root-bark. The beetles deserve a moment, because they are the vault's truest natives — deaf as stone, eyeless or nearly, long-lived for their kind, and entirely organized around vibration. They do not hear the roar; they *stand in it*, the way fish stand in current. Their larvae chew galleries in the dead outer bark of the columns, and the adults navigate by reading the timbre of the wood underfoot — which means they read the river, secondhand, through three meters of grafted spruce. When a relief valve opens somewhere and the whole vault changes pitch, the beetles swarm. Briefly, urgently, all at once, across every column in the chamber. Then the valve closes, the pitch settles, and they stop. The pitch-change is their weather. It may be their seasons. It may, for all anyone knows, be their language, and the river the only thing that ever speaks it.

In the black seeps that cross the moss, where the standing water never quite freezes, live blind amphipods — small, colorless, deliberate, swimming with the unhurried confidence of things that have never been seen by anything. Best guess is that they came down with the river itself, in the years before it was piped, and were stranded here when the water was taken into the dark of the penstocks; their descendants now inhabit the leaks and condensation-pools of the very system that orphaned them. They are, in a sense, the river's children, living in its absence, fed on what filters down through the felt. The maintenance crews have a sign for them: two fingers wriggling, then a fist closing slowly. *The ones the water left.*

The bats are the commuters. A colony of perhaps two hundred holds the upper fissures of the second chamber, sleeping in the root-weave by day, pouring out through the gaps at dusk to hunt the ordinary insect-rich air of the surface, returning before dawn to the one place on the plateau where absolutely nothing will ever find them asleep. Inside the vaults their echolocation is useless — the roar erases it — so they fly the chambers by sight and by memory, threading remembered corridors between the columns, crossing the gray light-shafts in quick stitching arcs. Watch one navigate two hundred meters of booming darkness on pure recollection and you understand something about what the vaults ask of their residents: not toughness, exactly. Memorization. A willingness to know the place so thoroughly that the senses become optional.

The fox is a vixen — the third, the crews think, or possibly the fourth, of an unbroken line that has held the route through the third chamber since the lamps went in. Her sign-name is two fingers drawn flat back along the skull: *Ears-Down*, for the way she travels, ears pinned against the roar, trotting the cable runs with the brisk offended dignity of a commuter on a familiar and disgraceful train. She hunts topside, by ear, at the fissure mouths — voles, mostly — but she dens below and travels below, navigating by foot-feel and scent through country where her hearing is worthless, and she has learned things no fox should need to know: which catwalk grating her paws fit, the warm-air margin around the transformer fence where she sleeps in the deepest cold, the schedule of the crews, who leave the route clear and occasionally, deniably, leave other things as well. The arrangement is old enough now that it is hereditary on both sides. Crews train their replacements in it the same week they teach them the lamp-codes.

Once a season, the owl. Always alone, always motionless, always on the same gantry rail above the turbine gallery, enduring the worst of the sound for an afternoon or a day and then gone. There are practical theories — it hunts the bats at the fissures; it is molting and wants somewhere nothing will trouble it — but the crews hold a different one, and after enough shifts down here it stops sounding whimsical. An owl is an instrument for listening. It spends its whole life inside its own ears. The crews believe it comes down to the one place in the country where listening is impossible — comes down, in other words, to *rest* — and their sign for it is a flat hand laid gently over the ear. They do not approach it. You would not interrupt anyone else's silence either.

And then the crews themselves, who would object to being listed among the wildlife and who belong on the list anyway.

Three crews, six or seven to a crew, week on and week off, bunked in a side chamber behind a timber-and-felt baffle where the roar drops from weather to rumor. They are, technically, hearing people. Functionally they are something else. The vault dialect runs to four hundred signs and grows every year — signs for pitch-change, for *the column is weeping* (a sudden sheen of condensation on the grafted wood, which means a pressure shift, which means someone walks the penstock line), for fox and owl and topside and the seam, for *I felt it in my teeth, did you* — and the older hands have stopped speaking aloud even in the bunkhouse, even on the surface, even at home, where their families have learned the core vocabulary in self-defense. Their hearing tests fine. Their attention does not. A twenty-year veteran can stand on the moss-floor, jaw slightly loose, and tell you from the texture in his back teeth which turbine has a bearing going, how many weeks it has, and whether the surveyors will find the fourth chamber wider this year. Topside, the same man cannot follow a conversation in a quiet kitchen, because he is listening *under* it, for the structure, for the bass, for the thing the sound is resting on, and a kitchen rests on nothing. Retirement goes hard here. The silence above ground keeps them awake. More than one has been found, on the company's books under the soft heading of *long-term monitoring*, simply staying.

Which brings us to the keeper.

There is one permanent human resident of the Penstock Vaults, quartered past the last lamp-string in the seventh chamber, in a felt-lagged cabin with a stove vented up a natural fissure, and the crews will tell you his history in sign with a fluency that suggests the history matters less than the office. A turbine-wright, originally. Retired, technically. He has not slept a full night on the surface in over a decade, and when he speaks aloud — rarely, perhaps twice a year — his own voice visibly startles him. His work, insofar as it is work, is this: he knows the columns individually. He claims he can tell, from the change in a single column's creak, which tree overhead — which one tree, out of a standing forest he has not walked under in years — is failing, leaning, dying, dropping its share of the load onto its grafted neighbors. The surveyors used to humor him. They have since quietly stopped reporting how often he is right. The crews leave supplies at the sixth-chamber lamp and he leaves the empties, and whether the current keeper is the original or the second or simply the next, no one presses, because the position has the look of something that refills itself. The vaults appear to require one person who has gone all the way over — who lives inside the roar without rotation or relief — and the vaults appear, by some means nobody examines closely, to always have one.

Last, largest, and least separable from the architecture: the forest itself. It is conventional to say the roots *line* the vaults, as if they were wallpaper. The truth the surveyors' instruments keep insisting on is stranger. Through the grafts, through the fused columns, through the white threadwork binding root to root and the vault-floor to all of it, some unmeasured fraction of the plateau forest has become physically continuous — one circulatory commons, one body — and that body has moved its underside indoors. The trees stand in daylight; their other half stands down here in the frost and the roar, thickening a few rings a year, leaning its patient millimeters into the maintenance shed, holding the world up. The moss-floor that gives underfoot like the back of something sleeping gives that way because it is the back of something sleeping. The trees that hum faintly on still nights are not haunted. They are dreaming the river — the river that was taken from under them, that now runs through their held-open hands in the dark of its pipes, shaking them from the inside on its way to somewhere it is allowed to be seen.

So: thread and beetle, amphipod and bat, a dynasty of foxes, a resting owl, a few dozen people who speak with their hands and listen with their jaws, one keeper, and a single buried half of a forest. All of them tenants of the same landlord. The roar is the oldest resident of the Penstock Vaults and the only one that everything else has shaped itself around — deafened toward, signed beneath, swarmed to, slept inside. Rent is paid in patience, and the lease has a term, though no tenant will see its end. Someday the machines will stop, the pipes will fail, the river will come back into its bed in the dark, and the hand that has been closing all this time will finally close — on a sealed black country where water runs loud and unheard, and the only resident left is the one that was always going to inherit: the forest, holding everything, slowly, in the grip it never once relaxed.
