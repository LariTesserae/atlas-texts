**The Keeper of the Casting Room**

At first you will conclude that nothing lives here, and the room will seem to agree with you. No scuttle, no wings, no eyes catching the tea-colored lamplight. The moss and the finger-length fern are residents, certainly, but residents the way dust is a resident — they arrived, they persist, they were not invited. You will stand in the wet iron dark and decide the place keeps itself.

Then you will start noticing the evidence against this.

The drains are clean. In a room where water has been migrating across canted steel for longer than the rust suggests, the drains should be choked with sediment and mineral scale, and they are not. The moss, for all its slow ambition, never touches the face of a mold — it advances along the warm pipes to the very edge of the racks and stops, as though against glass, and if you look closely at the iron just past its colonies you can see faint pale scrape-marks, regular, patient, like the tide-lines of an attention. There is a stool, low and footworn, near the deepest racks. There is a brass stamp set in a fitted case, the dies arranged by numeral, the most-used ones polished bright by handling. There is a place on the rail beside the largest mold where the iron has been rubbed smooth — not by machinery, which wears things in lines, but by a grip, which wears things in the shape of a hand.

And there is the matter of the count. The first time you look down the central aisle, you will tally the tall gray shapes standing between the racks — castings propped upright, you'll assume, or molds awaiting slings — and you will get a number. The second time you look, you will get a different number. You will tell yourself you miscounted. You did not.

One of the shapes is the keeper.

You have probably already looked at it. That is the thing to understand first: it is not hiding. It stands in plain sight among the apparatus, and you passed your eyes across it and filed it with the equipment, because it is the color of everything else here — the gray of wet ash, of material that has not yet decided to be stone — and because it is beaded all over with condensation like everything else, and because it was not moving. Or rather, it was moving, and is moving now, at the speed the room moves: the speed of flywheels, of clock hands, of curing. Watch it for an hour and you will swear it is a statue. Come back tomorrow and the hand that hovered above the rim of a mold will have completed its descent and be resting there, two fingers extended along the future ridgeline, checking the set of the material the way you might check a sleeping child's temperature. Come back in a week and it will be three racks away, and the mold it touched will be turned, and you will never, no matter how long you stand still, catch the interval in which any of this occurred. It does not work between your visits. It works continuously. You are simply too brief to see it.

It is shaped approximately like a person, the way the impossible molds are shaped approximately like terrain. The proportions are right and the details are unresolved: the face is a sketch of a face, a brow like an eroded scarp, hollows where a finished thing would have eyes, a surface that has the look of something poured rather than grown. This is because it was poured. It is the room's first casting — you can find the mold it came from, if you go deep enough into the racks, a man-shaped hollow in a slab of the gray material, cruder than anything else in the room, the negative of a standing figure with its arms slightly out from its sides, as though whoever pressed the original was holding still and trying very hard to be patient. The mold has been kept, like everything here. Nothing in this room is discarded. The impossible landscapes — the hill that overhangs itself, the river that forks upstream — hang in their slings among the workable ones, and the keeper passes them without correction. They are its early work, or its dreams, or both; the distinction may not survive at this depth.

Put your ear against it — it will permit this; permission, at its speed, looks like nothing at all — and you will hear what you hear in the curing molds: the faint ticking, the granular settling, the sound of material organizing itself. The keeper is still curing. It has been curing for as long as the dates on the racks run, and it is not finished, and this is the deepest law of the room, the one even its inhabitant obeys: everything here is *prior*. The landscapes are not yet landscapes. The pools are first drafts of lakes. The keeper is a first draft of whatever it is going to be, and it does its work in the meantime, in a body that has not yet decided on its crystal structure, deciding instead, very slowly, on the crystal structures of countries.

Its hands are its most finished feature, and they are finished by erasure. The fingertips are smooth — utterly, glassily smooth, worn featureless against the iron racks over a span of time you would need geology to measure. It did this on purpose. Once, early, before it understood its own weight in the world, it pressed a thumb into a soft casting, and somewhere far above there is a pond in a depression no glacier made, a kettle hole in the shape of a young keeper's curiosity, and people may skate on it in winter without ever knowing they are circling a fingerprint. Whether it regrets this is impossible to say; if the regret exists, it moves too slowly to read. But it wore its prints away afterward, year by year, so that nothing it touches now will carry an unexplained mark. The warning you felt standing over the open molds — *you should not* — was its, secondhand, left in the air like everything else it leaves.

It breathes with the pistons. Or the pistons breathe with it — the records, if there were records, would not settle the question. Each held-breath release of the tall vertical engines matches an exhalation somewhere in that gray chest, and the overlapping unsynchronized breath-sounds you heard braiding in the dark were a conversation of sorts, the room and its keeper keeping time together without insisting on agreement.

It knows about the fern. This is worth saying, because the fern does not know about it, and never will — the keeper is too slow to register as anything but climate. But some decades ago a conduit along the eastern wall was bent, very slightly, so that a warm pipe now passes four inches nearer the riveted seam where the fern took root, and the caged lamp above it burns a filament replaced more often than any other lamp in the room. The fern leans toward the light with its devout private theory of the sun, and the theory is wrong in every detail and correct in its essentials: there is something vast and patient that has noticed it, and arranged the world a little, and asks nothing back. The moss is managed with the same gentleness. When it trespasses toward a mold face, it is scraped free over the course of a season and resettled along a warmer pipe, deported so gradually that the moss experiences its own relocation as weather.

No animals come, and now you can guess why — not menace, nothing here is menacing, but *timing*. Animals belong to *after*. They are made for finished surfaces, for ground that has settled its arguments. A mouse in this room would live and die inside a single gesture of the keeper's hand, and the keeper, perhaps knowing this, keeps a room that offers nothing to anything quick. The ladder that ends four feet short of the floor may be part of the same arithmetic: the keeper did not build it for itself — it has never needed it, never left — and the gap is not malice. The room is settling. It has been sinking deeper, fractionally, for a very long time, and the keeper, who measures everything at its own speed, has simply not yet finished noticing that the floor has moved away from the last rung. Give it a century. There may be a step there when you return.

Its days, if the word applies, are the room's schedule: it stamps the brass labels with the dates each landscape is due, checking them against some reckoning it keeps internally, in the slow tick of its own settling. The future dates that unsettled you are just the forward edge of its work — countries it is curing ahead of need, the way a careful baker shapes tomorrow's loaves tonight. It presses terrain into being one held breath at a time, smooths the ridgelines with fingers that can no longer leave a mark, listens at the molds for the sound of stone making up its mind, and is in no hurry, because hurry is the one material the room has never stocked.

And on the rack that holds its own origin-mold — the man-shaped hollow, the crude first negative — there is a brass label like all the others, stamped in the keeper's own hand. It bears a date. The date is very far from now, further than any landscape's, and what it marks is not hard to guess: the day the ticking in that gray chest finally stops, the day the material decides, the day the keeper sets. It will become, on that date, the first finished thing the room has ever held — stone at last, no longer prior, its long curing done. Whether the room ends then, or merely begins, is a question the keeper does not appear to be asking. It has the date. It has the work. Drop by drop, in the dim, it is becoming itself at exactly the speed required, and it would tell you, if it could speak at any pace you could hear, that this is what living here means: to be unfinished, in good company, underground, on time.
