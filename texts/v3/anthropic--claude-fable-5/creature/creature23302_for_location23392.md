**The Parish of the Wet Gallery**

Start with the walls, because the walls are alive.

The oxide skin that the iron wears — glaze-smooth in places, felt-soft in others — is not just rust. It is rust *inhabited*: a film of bacteria a few cells deep, beings that make their whole living off the slow surrender of iron to seawater, completing in centuries what fire would do in a minute. They are the oldest residents and the truest ones, present in every plate and rivet, a nation without a single body. The felt is their work and their city. And the felt is grazed. Amphipods — small, comma-shaped, translucent — move across it in their thousands, cropping the film the way sheep crop a hillside, and they are themselves cropped by the blennies, and so the gallery's first economy is this: the room eats itself, infinitely slowly, and the parish lives partly on the proceeds.

The rest of the living arrives on schedule, with the thunder.

Twice a day the tide forces the old mains and the water that comes booming through is *rich* — harbor plankton, copepods, larvae, drifting fragments, the loose change of the outer sea. The sessile members of the congregation are built for exactly this. The dead-man's-fingers and the small white anemones on the upstream faces of the pipes do not need to hear the valves quicken; they feel the flow in their flesh, and as the *dum… dum… dum* accelerates they open in unison, a pale garden unfurling in the dark, thousands of tentacles spread into the current like hands held out. They feed for the loud hours and close for the quiet ones. They have done this for longer than any fish in the room has been alive, and their timing is not learned. It is inherited. The tide's schedule is written into them the way the diamond scoring is written into the floor.

The blennies are the householders. Every empty stud-hole along the pump casings — bores the width of a wrist, machined true a hundred years ago and as true now as the day the taps cut them — is a freehold, and every freehold is held. A tompot blenny sits in each, head out, fringed eyebrows working, surveying a territory of perhaps two square feet of oxide felt with the vigilance of a man who suspects his neighbors of everything. The territory map of the gallery is older than any blenny on it. When a holder dies, the hole stands empty for a day, rarely two, and then a new head appears in it, and the borders resume exactly where they were, because the borders were never really the fish's. They belong to the bolt-pattern. In spring the males stop eating and stand guard over mats of amber eggs glued to the machined steel, fanning them, fasting, ferocious, and the pattern of life in the room is laid down in rows as regular as rivets.

In the throat of the dead pump — Number Three, though nothing living knows its number — lives the conger.

Not *a* conger: *the* conger, because the den has held one for as long as the room has been a sea-place, and the succession matters more than the individual. Each tenant grows in that iron throat for fifteen, twenty years, thickening in the dark, until it is the dominant fact of the gallery, a grey presence the small fish steer around even when only the blunt head shows. It hunts at mid-tide, when the thunder covers everything, unwinding from the pump like a hawser paying out, moving through the silver panic with terrible economy. And then one autumn it is simply gone — out through the penstocks on the night ebb, gone to deep water to spawn once and die, as congers do. The throat of the pump stands empty for a season. The crabs grow bold. The blennies stray. Then a new grey head appears in the dark of the casing, smaller, the same, and the office is filled again. The pump has had perhaps five congers since the water came up the shafts. It will have fifty more. The den is the permanent thing; the eel is its current expression.

Behind the valve manifold lives the wolf-fish, and the wolf-fish answers to no succession, because it has simply been there. Thirty years, perhaps more — they are long-lived and it has nowhere to be. It is the oldest single body in the room: a head like a dropped hammer, a hide gone the brown-black of the plates around it, teeth worn to pebbles and replaced and worn again. It eats the rock crabs, shell and all, with a sound that carries through the iron, a dry industrial crunch the gallery absorbs without comment. In late winter it guards a fist-sized ball of eggs wedged in the manifold's shadow, coiled around them for months, eating nothing, and the few fry that survive the conger and the pollack are swept out through the mains to seed wolf-fish into wrecks and reefs that will never know where their grandmother lay. It does not hunt at mid-tide. It does not need the cover. It waits, and the room delivers.

On the shelf by the shaft, in the enamel mug, lives the anchorite.

The hermit crab took the mug decades ago, when it could still have left, and grew, and grew, and the question of leaving quietly closed behind it. It is now a creature the size of a fist wearing a vessel the size of two, sealed to its shelf by a glaze of sediment, and it has resolved its situation the only way available: it has stopped needing to go anywhere. It sits in the mug's mouth at mid-tide with its claws spread into the flow, fishing the current for whatever the thunder brings, picking drift from the water with the patience of something that has made peace with a single view. It is fed, in effect, by the parish — by the tide, by what falls, by what drifts within reach. When it dies, the mug will stand as a reliquary for a season, and then something smaller will move in, and begin, in its turn, to grow.

The congregation proper is the silver: the sprat and the sand smelt in their hundreds, hanging in mid-water between the pump casings, riding the tidal flow through the room and back again. No single one of them lives more than a year or two; the school is the continuous thing, a body that loses and replaces its cells and never stops being itself. They thread the spokes of the turning flywheel without touching it — every fish in the room is calibrated to that wheel, its one-minute revolution, its tick — and they bunch in its lee when the pollack come up. The pollack are the circuit riders, in through the penstocks on one tide, gone on another, working the gallery the way they work every dark hollow on this coast, and twice a day, for twenty loud minutes, the room performs its oldest liturgy: silver panic above, dark intent below, the valves keeping time. The school is always thinner afterward and never seems so. It closes over its losses like water.

The shaft is the door, and the door is also the alms-hole. Things fall: torn weed, leaves in autumn, once a year or so a dead gull, working down through ten fathoms of green to land in the pale trapezoid. The rock crabs are the undertakers' guild — they gather within the hour, sidelong, punctilious, and whatever fell is gone by the next slack water, distributed into the parish without ceremony. Under the same light the kelp keeps its eight or nine fronds, and on the fronds live blue-rayed limpets, small ovals of iridescent fire, the brightest things in the room, grazing the only plants the gallery will ever grow. On certain summer evenings the shaft-light fills with mysid shrimp, a swarm of glass splinters dancing in the trapezoid, and everything that eats comes to the light and feeds.

Some years there is an octopus. It arrives through the mains, takes a pipe elbow for a den, and for a season the gallery has its one housekeeper: small objects move in the night — a rivet head, a shard of gauge glass, a crab carapace — arranged at the den mouth with what looks like intent, because it is intent. The octopus and the conger know about each other. The arrangement is temporary, as it always is. One spring the den is empty and the small objects stay where they were last set, and the gallery does not record how it ended.

And the flywheel turns. It is not alive — but its bearings have worn into something frictionless, the through-current drives it, and at one point in every revolution the chipped tooth strikes the housing and rings, and at that point of contact, alone in the whole room, the metal is bright. One thin line of clean iron, polished by its own ticking, in a parish otherwise dressed entirely in oxide. Everything that lives here lives in time with that wheel and that tide, the anemones opening, the blennies guarding, the conger unwinding, the school closing over itself — so perhaps the wheel has a place in the congregation after all, the way a bell does.

The parish keeps no records, or rather it keeps them in the only materials it has: in oxide, in shell, in succession. No creature in the room knows the room was made. To the conger the pump throat is a sea cave; to the blennies the bolt-pattern is geology; to the bacteria in the walls the iron is simply the world, generous and slow. The offices outlast every officeholder — the den, the freeholds, the mug, the manifold shadow — and are always filled. That is the real population of the Wet Gallery: not any single eel or crab or silver hundred, but the continuity itself, the standing arrangement between the iron and the tide, holding its services on schedule, in the cold, in the dark, twice a day, forever.
