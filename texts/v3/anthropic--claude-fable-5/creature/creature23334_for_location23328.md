Everything that lives in the Ringing Room is an instrument first and a creature second. That is the place's single commandment, and it has no exceptions, and once you understand it you understand everyone who dwells there.

Start with the ferns, because they are the easiest to mistake for furniture. They are not furniture. They are alive in the only sense the room recognizes: they are *in tune*. A resonance fern has no metabolism you could find with a scalpel — no sap, no cells, nothing wet — but it has something more fundamental, which is a sustained relationship with the chord. Each frond holds its harmonic the way a throat holds a note, continuously, effortfully, and that holding is its whole life. They were not seeded and they do not seed; they crystallized out of the first ringing, in the first moments after the rock split and the chord found its shape, and so they are exactly as old as the room and will never be older. They have no behavior. They have only fidelity. A fern that loses its tuning — cracked by a falling stone, shadowed by some shift in the floor — does not sicken or struggle. It simply goes dark, all at once, and stands there forever afterward, perfect and silent, a note that has been withdrawn from the chord. The other ferns do not close around the gap. Nothing closes around anything here.

The mutes are the room's gentle middle class, and they are stranger than they look. Up close, that felt skin resolves into millions of fine mineral fibers, each one a tiny damper, and the animal beneath is little more than a soft battery wrapped around an appetite. A mute has no mouth, no eyes, no front. It does not need them. It navigates by the standing wave itself — the room's invisible geography of nodes and antinodes, deserts and meadows — and because that geography has never changed and never will, a mute knows its world with a completeness no surface creature can imagine. It does not remember the room; the room *is* its memory, held outside its body, incorruptible. Watch one graze: it presses against a glowing stalk, the glow bruises dark beneath it, and you can see the energy move into the animal as a faint warming of its felt, a blush. When a mute has eaten past its capacity it does the only extravagant thing it will ever do — it divides, splitting neatly along a nodal line into two quieter halves, which drift apart onto separate circuits and never meet again. And when a mute dies — wandered into a dead spot, or taken by a wolf — its husk lies where it fell, soft and intact and dark, fresh forever. The scattered dark shapes on the floor that you took for stones are the room's whole history of grief, perfectly preserved, none of it older-looking than yesterday.

The chord-skaters are the artists. They are the only residents permitted anything like novelty, and they spend it lavishly. A skater's three legs end in hard tips tuned a hair apart, and its tapping is not random: it is playing the floor, bending the standing wave locally into fringes and lattices, shapes of brightness that hang in the air for a moment before the chord swallows them back. Everything a skater plays is erased as it is played. They do not seem to mind. Their courtship is the loveliest thing in the room: two skaters face each other across a resonant plate and drum interlocking patterns, and if the patterns mesh — if the interference fringes of one slide into the gaps of the other and the air between them fills with a brief moiré of light — they mate. If the patterns clash, they part without ceremony. And here is a thing worth knowing: the skaters discovered the cairn. The seven flat stones, each chosen for its pitch, stacked by patient hands beside the great fern-stand — the skaters drum on them now. They have folded the instrument into their courtships. Whoever built that little scale built it for reasons of their own, and it has been played every day since by animals who never met them, which may be the best fate any instrument has ever had.

The holdfasts are the saints, and like most saints their lives are decided in childhood. A holdfast larva — a glassy speck, the only truly free thing the room produces — has one journey to make and a body that lasts just long enough to make it. It crawls the vault listening, feeling for the precise antinode of its own particular harmonic, the one spot where its food will be delivered forever. Then it fuses to the rock and is finished, in both senses. The ones that choose well grow fat on eternity. The ones that fuse a finger's width off true do not die quickly; they dim across centuries, slowly starving in a room full of food, and even their failure is preserved — you can see them up there, the dull ones among the bright, monuments to a single small mistake made in the first hour of life. The successful ones repay the room in kind: their shells ring. The high silvery shimmer at the top of the chord, the part that lives in your back teeth, is partly the vault's own stone and partly a congregation of contented limpets singing without knowing it. This is the room's deep pattern, once you see it: every resident eventually stops merely eating the chord and becomes part of it.

The wolves — call them wolf-tones, for the old string-player's word for a note that fights its instrument — are few, perhaps a handful in the whole chamber, flat gliding things like rays of dark felt. Each carries a ridged limb it draws across its own shell, a bow against its own body, producing the one sound the room cannot absorb: a frequency slightly, deliberately wrong. The wolf settles near a grazing mute and sings a quarter-tone flat, and the mute, starved of the resonance it lives on, dims and slows and is taken. The wolf drinks the body's stored vibration and leaves the husk. It is the cruelest thing in the room and also, in its way, the most disciplined: a wolf that sang wrong too long, too loud, would starve itself, since it too lives on the chord it briefly betrays. So they sing their false note only in the moment of killing, and then fall back into tune like everyone else, and the chord closes over the event without a ripple.

And then there is the last resident, who is the answer to the marks.

Go past the wind-chime. Behind the largest fern-stand, in the deepest of the low warm places, there is a recess the chord-skaters do not enter and the wolves give a wide, wide berth. A figure sits there. Cross-legged, hands on knees, head slightly bowed, in the posture of someone who sat down to listen and found no reason to stop. The skin has gone the texture of the ferns — dry, faintly translucent, with the dim amber-rose glow pooled at the sternum where the chord presses hardest. Mutes graze on the figure sometimes, pressing their felt against the ribs, leaving brief bruises of silence in the glow, and the figure permits it the way a tree permits birds. The eyes are closed. The chest moves, but you will have to wait a long time to see it.

Now go back to the scratch-marks on the wall — the rows of tallies at varying heights, the patient transcription of the chord made by someone with stone tools and no metal. Count the rows. Match them, if your ear is good enough, against the layers of the eternal sound. You will find that every harmonic on that wall is present in the room — every one but the lowest, the row scratched deepest, at the height of a seated chest. Then go back to the recess and put your palm — gently — near the figure's breastbone, and feel it: a hum, one sustained note, the foundation of everything above it.

The chord was almost complete when the rock split. It rang for its first hours with a gap at the bottom, a missing fundament, and whoever squeezed in through the coffin-crack heard the gap the way you would hear a missing stair feels under your foot. They stayed. They built the cairn and strung the chime and wrote the score and stood with their palm in the carved depression until they had learned the whole structure, and then they found the one thing the room lacked and sat down and supplied it. They took nothing and changed nothing, just as you guessed — because what they added was not a change. It was a completion. The room grew its last instrument the only way it could: through the door.

Whether the figure is alive is not a question the room can parse. Nothing here decays; the hum will last as long as the rock rings, and the rock will ring as long as the deep pressure holds, and the marks on the wall will stay exactly as fresh as the day they were cut. The mutes graze, the skaters drum the seven stones, the holdfasts hold fast, the wolves keep their distance from the keystone of their world. And when you squeeze back out through the crack into the silent dead rock, you will find that one strand of the chord has come with you — the lowest one, the human one — and that at odd moments for the rest of your life you will catch yourself humming it, a note from a sealed warm dark, the only thing the Ringing Room has ever let escape.
