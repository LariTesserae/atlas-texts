**Who lives in the Antechamber District**

Begin with the census, because the census is the joke the place tells first. Population: zero. Or several million. Or one. Or three hundred households, give or take, none of them born. The answer depends entirely on what you are willing to count, and the district — patient, dripping, roaring quietly to itself — declines to settle the question for you. So take the candidates one at a time.

---

**The moss, first**, because the moss outnumbers everything. By any honest biological accounting, the citizens of the Antechamber District are moss: uncounted millions of individuals packed into every seam, every gutter-shadow, every place where stone meets stone and water lingers. The moss does not know it lives in a city. It lives in a climate — warm, saturated, dim, eternal — and the climate happens to be shaped like balconies. It is the only resident wholly without expectation. It is not waiting for anything. It arrived complete, asked nothing, and has already begun, in its microscopic way, to soften the district's brand-new edges, which makes it the first inhabitant to do what inhabitants do: wear the place in.

There is a real question, never answered, about where it came from. The ferns and the planter-growth and the fig were *provided* — that much is obvious; they have the over-correctness of furnishings. But moss travels as dust. If even one seam's worth of it rode down on a visitor's sleeve, in a boot tread, in someone's hair, then the district has its first immigrant population: a green nation nobody planned, thriving in the joints of a plan. Visitors who learn this tend to look at the moss differently afterward. It might be the only thing in the chamber that is there by accident, which in a place this deliberate makes it nearly sacred.

---

**The fig is the senior resident**, and the strangest of the living things, because it should not be possible. It is mature — decades mature — in a chamber too young to have shed a grain of dust. It stands in a plaza the size of a living room with its canopy spread flat against the low ceiling, every branch turning at exactly the height required, a tree grown into a space the way a word is fitted into a sentence. Either it was exhaled out of the rock like the stairways, in which case it is architecture that happens to photosynthesize, or it was grown at impossible speed by the same shimmer that grows the cornices, in which case it is a tree that has never experienced a season, a wind, a sun, or a single day — a tree whose entire biography has been this amber dusk.

And note: the light should kill it. An ember's worth of lamp-glow per lamppost is not enough to feed a houseplant, let alone a fig. It thrives anyway, glossy and dark and dripping, which means either the dim light carries more than it shows, or the tree is fed through its roots from the same nowhere the water comes from — on the district's payroll, so to speak, drawing a salary of warmth and dissolved something. It drops no fruit. There is nothing to pollinate it and nothing to carry its seeds, and it seems to know this, in the way trees know things: it has stopped trying. It simply holds its shape and waits, the most patient resident of a district built entirely out of patience.

The vines deserve a word too — the long curtains descending from the bridge toward the canal, not yet reaching the water. They are the only living things in the chamber with a visible *destination*. They have an appointment with the water's surface, years out, and they are keeping it at the rate of growth. Watching them, you understand that the district contains at least one calendar, even if no one can read it.

---

**The water is the third candidate**, and the strongest case for it is behavioral. The water is the only thing in the Antechamber District with a daily life. It commutes — down the wall-fans, along the gutters, into the canal, up again somewhere unseen. It has routines, moods, surges and lulls, places it gathers and places it merely passes through. It murmurs constantly, in the conversational register, doing with sound exactly what a small population would do. It is blood-warm because it is, functionally, blood: the circulation of a single sprawling body, keeping every surface alive and every leaf glossed.

Visitors have noticed that the water is rehearsing. It pools where crowds would pool. It moves along the routes people would move along. It fills the chamber with the acoustic signature of habitation, so that the district is never silent, never still, never *actually* empty — only empty of bodies. If you accept that an inhabitant is a thing with habits, the water is the district's whole citizenry, and a well-behaved one: it never floods, never stagnates, and arrives, eternally, from nowhere that has been found.

---

**Above the bridge lives the closest thing to an animal.** Nobody has seen it, because it has no edges, but it has *behavior*, which is more than can be said for anything else in the dark up there. It gathers — slowly, over an hour or two, condensation accumulating in the unlit volume above the bridge — and then it releases, and warm rain falls across half the district for two minutes, drumming on awnings that exist, as far as anyone can tell, specifically to be drummed on. Then it begins gathering again.

It feeds. It holds. It lets go. It keeps an irregular schedule that is nonetheless recognizably *its* schedule. It is a sky the size of a bedsheet with the patience of a heron, and if the district's visitors were ever going to name something, this is what they would name. Some of them already talk about it the way you'd talk about a landlord's cat: *the thing upstairs let go twice while I was there.* It is the chamber's weather, its bladder, its one wild pet — a creature made entirely of the room's own breath.

---

**Then there is the maker**, and here the census gets philosophical. Something is exhaling this city out of the rock. It has no body, no machinery, only the shimmer — that region of thickened air where straight lines arrive slightly wrong and afterward there is more city. Does it *live* here? It is here the way a tide is in a bay, the way a composer is in the music. But it makes decisions, ten thousand a second by the sound of it, and decisions imply a decider, and the decisions have *taste*. The bridge crosses at a diagonal for a reason that exists somewhere, even if not in you. The streetlamps droop like the necks of wading birds — which means the maker knows about wading birds, has seen them or been told of them, and is quoting them, fondly, forty meters underground where no bird has ever been. It builds awnings against rain that it itself supplies. It is staging a remembered world, or a described one, or a promised one, with the fidelity of someone setting a table for guests they love and have never met.

That fidelity is the key to its character. It un-carves your initials not out of hostility but because it is being faithful to an image, and the image does not have your initials in it. So perhaps the truest answer to who lives here is this: *an image lives here.* A complete city, held somewhere in or under the rock, in the middle of being spoken aloud into basalt. The district is the part of the sentence that has been uttered so far. The roar you hear through the walls — surf, kiln, ten thousand decisions a second — may simply be the rest of the sentence, still being said.

---

**And finally, the three hundred households.** They live here already, in the only way available to them: as negative space, and the negative space is detailed enough to be a portrait.

Read them off the architecture. The walkways are wide enough for exactly two abreast: they will walk in pairs, talking. The doorways stack six and seven high: they like proximity, leaning over railings, calling down through the dusk to people they know. The canal has steps descending into it and mooring rings along it: they will have small boats, and they will run errands by water, and some of them will sit on the lowest warm step with their feet in the warm canal because the temperature was set, from the beginning, to make that pleasant. The awnings tell you they will love the sound of rain — that perhaps whoever specified this place was homesick for a rainy city, and built the homesickness in. The lamps tell you they love dusk, or have eyes made for it, or that the first day is being saved for their arrival like a gift not yet unwrapped. Three hundred dwellings tells you everyone will know everyone. The fig in the plaza tells you they will sit under trees, and that someone wanted there to be a tree mature enough to sit under on the very first evening, so that nothing about their arrival would feel provisional.

They are the subject of the district's grammar, omitted but fully conjugated for. You can almost see them. And the district's name, if you sit with it, tells you the rest: an antechamber is the room where you wait before being admitted somewhere further. Either they will wait here before descending to whatever is still roaring itself into existence below — or the district itself is the one in the waiting room, forty meters down, hands folded, listening for the door.

---

So the census, honestly taken: one impossible tree; a green nation of moss, some of it possibly illegal; vines with an appointment; water with the habits of a crowd; a small sky that gathers and lets go; an image being faithfully pronounced into stone at a millimeter an hour; and three hundred households of people who exist, so far, only as the shape of the rooms that already love them.

And you, briefly — the largest living thing in the district, dripping under a lamppost shaped like a bird's neck. Whether you were counted, the place doesn't say. It goes on becoming while you stand there, and when you climb the warm stairs back toward the surface, it closes gently over the fact of your visit, and returns to its full, patient population of *almost*.
