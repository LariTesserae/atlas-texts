**The Tenants of the Cistern**

Begin with the rule of the place, because everything that lives here has been shaped by it: the stone does not change, and the water never stops. Whatever made its home in the cistern had to make peace with both — had to learn to live inside a permanent storm held in a permanent cup — and the result, after ten thousand years of cold and turbulence and mineral hunger, is a fauna unlike any other on the world below. Nothing here grows. Everything here *endures*. The cistern's tenants are not abundant, not bright, not quick to anger or to breed. They are patient the way the stone is patient, and restless the way the water is restless, and every one of them is grey or white or silver, because in a sea without gardens there is nothing worth being any other color for.

Start where the light still reaches.

**The flowers that are not flowers.** In the lee of every fallen lintel, in the dead water behind the great toppled column-drums, the grey flowers grow — though *grow* is the wrong word for something that has not measurably changed in living memory of anything. They are filter-feeders the size of barrel lids, soft radial animals anchored to the stone by a foot that has, over centuries, etched itself a shallow socket in the pavement, so that the oldest of them sit in the rock like jewels in settings, and could not be removed without breaking either the flower or the floor. They open when a sediment plume rolls over them, a slow unclenching of fifty grey petals, each petal furred with combs fine enough to strain rock-flour from cold water, and they clap shut when the plume passes, and that is the whole of their visible life. But watch a colony long enough — and a colony can paper a sunken gallery wall, ten thousand strong, all leaning the same way into the through-current like a congregation facing an altar — and you begin to see that they are not merely passive. They lean *together*. When the current shifts, the lean travels down the gallery in a wave, flower tilting after flower, faster than the water itself moves, as though the news of the change runs through them by some signal in the stone or in their crowded feet. They are the cistern's weathervanes. The eels watch them. The fish read them. Everything in the basin that wants to know what the storm will do next looks first at which way the congregations are leaning.

They reproduce — they must — but no one has ever seen it happen, and the young, when they appear, appear in the worst places: out on the open storm-country pavement, alone, a grey coin no bigger than a thumbnail clamped shut against the scouring currents. Most are ground away. A few, by luck of fallen stone or the slow wandering of a vortex that deposits a sheltering ridge of silt, survive long enough to begin their socket. The colonies in the galleries are not crowds that gathered. They are the residue of ten thousand years of failure, the few that held.

**The seam-eels.** The joints between the great hexagonal flags run for miles, dead straight, intersecting at perfect angles softened by age into rounded grooves — and the eels live in the grooves, and have become, over uncounted generations, creatures of the straight line. A seam-eel is as long as a man and no thicker than his wrist, colorless to the point of translucence, with a blunt patient head and eyes reduced to dark seeds. It owns a length of seam — a territory measured not in area but in *distance*, a furlong of groove, a half-mile, more for the old ones — and it knows its length the way a blind man knows his own house. It knows every chip in the joint-walls, every place where the groove deepens enough to turn around in, every intersection where its seam crosses another eel's seam and the two of them have, by long mutual arrangement, worked out which one withdraws.

They are ambush feeders, after a fashion, though what they ambush is mostly the rain — the crabs and the stunned small fish that fall out of collapsing vortices onto the plain. An eel feels the funnel coming through the stone, the deep grinding hum of a mile-wide spiral wandering its way, and rises in its seam to wait with its mouth at floor level, a pale ring flush with the pavement. When the funnel dies and the crab-rain comes down, the seams of that whole district open like a field of mouths.

And they speak, or something like it. Sound runs along the joints of the old stone with uncanny fidelity, and the eels drum — a soft knocking of the blunt head against the groove wall, patterned, answered, passed along. A disturbance at one end of a three-mile seam is known at the other end before anything swimming could have carried the news. When the great *tonk* comes — the round enormous voice of a foundation block shifting a finger's width somewhere in the dark below — every eel in the basin goes still at once, and stays still, and the seams hold their silence sometimes for a day. They are listening to something. Whether they understand it, or are merely afraid of it, or are paying it some attention older than either, the eels do not say.

**The funnel-riders.** The blind white crabs are the cistern's only travelers, the only animals that cross the open country by choice, and they do it by surrendering to the storm. A funnel-rider is a flattened, smooth-shelled thing, its carapace worn — or grown — into the same soft-edged polish as the ancient stone, so that a crab sitting still on the pavement is simply a bump in the floor. When it feels a vortex coming it does not flee. It loosens its grip, angles its shell, and lets the spinning water peel it off the flags and carry it up — up into the shear layers a hundred feet above the plain where the storm grinds its richest food, the suspended rock-flour and the falling dust of the surface — and there it rides, eating, sometimes for days, a passenger in a glassy turning tower a mile wide, until the tower dies and drops it.

The drop is the thing. A collapsing funnel rains crabs by the hundreds across the plain, and they strike the pavement, and right themselves, and walk away in every direction — and the directions are not random. The riders are blind, but they carry the turn of the water in their bodies; they count the spirals, somehow, hold the tally of their own revolutions, and walk off the impact in a line that corrects for every circle they were carried through. A crab can ride three funnels across a province of open stone and arrive within a few flags of the gallery it left a season before. Their whole geography is remembered rotation. They navigate a sea they have never seen by the arithmetic of having been spun.

They molt in the protected vaults, in crowds, the soft new-shelled ones guarded in the center of a ring of hard old ones — the only thing the funnel-riders do that looks like care — and the cast shells, pale and perfect, drift out into the currents and tumble across the plains for years, so that a traveler in the storm-country is forever meeting empty crabs walking nowhere on the wind of the water.

**The grey readers.** The small silver fish are the only things in the cistern that could be called quick, and even their quickness is a kind of stillness. A school of greys does not swim, in the ordinary sense; it *positions*. The fish hang motionless in upwellings, treading the rising water at exactly the speed of its rise, fixed in place like notes held on a stave, feeding on what the column carries up to them. They are readers of water. Each fish carries down its flanks a lateral line so elaborated, so flowered with sensory pits, that it amounts to an organ of weather: a grey feels the cold front coming down a buried stairway three plazas off, feels the precise moment a funnel a mile away begins to starve and wobble, and the school redistributes itself — vanishing sideways, all at once, in a single shared thought — into the next good water before the change arrives.

Here is the strange thing about them: in still water, greys ignore each other. Pen two of them in a calm vault and they will hang at opposite walls, indifferent, solitary. The school does not exist in calm. It exists only in moving water, assembles itself out of the turbulence, each fish holding its station in the flow and finding that the stations, held truly, make a formation. The school is not a society of fish. It is a property of the storm, drawn in silver. When the water turns, the school turns, because the school *is* the turn, made visible.

**The lamp-bearers and the deep tenants.** Below a hundred fathoms, where the light is memory, the fauna changes register. Here are the fish that carry the blue points on their brows — slow, large-eyed, soft-boned things, each with a single cold star above its face, by which it does not hunt and does not lure but seems merely to *announce*, drifting through the flooded arcades like lamplighters who have forgotten the rest of their duties. They are rare and long-lived and apparently unkillable by anything that lives here, and they pass each other in the dark colonnades without greeting, each in its small sphere of blue.

Here too, in the dead water of the vaults-below-vaults, live the still things: white worms a fathom long that lie in the foundation joints and may move a body-length in a year; soft pale urchin-shapes that sit in the corners of drowned rooms grinding mineral crust from the stone with mouths like masons' chisels, the only animals in the cistern that eat the building itself, and so slowly that the building has not noticed. The deep rooms are very quiet. The storm is a ceiling-sound down here, a far-off hum through hundreds of feet of masonry. The deep tenants live not on the turbulence but on what settles out of it — the final, finest snow, the dust of dust — and their lives are correspondingly final and fine: slow hearts, century bodies, a metabolism a degree or two above death.

And below them, in the warm water, lives the last tenant.

**The warm one.** It was said of the rising plumes — the columns of gentler water that climb the open shafts and feed half the basin's circulation — that they are warmed by nothing anyone has found, perhaps only by the friction of the sea against itself. Follow one down, though. Follow a plume down its shaft, through the gallery levels, through the vault levels, down past the last lamp-bearer and the last white worm, into the foundation country where the rooms are the size of valleys and the dark is also made — and the water grows not colder, as it should, but soft, and faintly, unmistakably warm, and it carries a slow rhythm in it, a swell and slack, a swell and slack, with hours between.

Something is breathing down there.

It is not the maker. That should be said at once, because the deep rooms make you want to believe otherwise, and the belief would be wrong: the warm one builds nothing, knows nothing of stone, has no hands and no works. It is an animal — the oldest animal, almost certainly, the founding tenant or close to it — and it is vast, and it is soft, and over a span of time that has to be reckoned in thousands of years it has flowed itself into the foundation chambers until its body and the architecture share a boundary. It fills rooms. It is molded into doorways. Its pale flank, where a plume-shaft opens onto it, is dished and smoothed like the flagstones above, worn by its own slow water into the same final shapes the stone wears, so that the first travelers down the shafts read it as masonry — warm masonry, masonry with a pulse — and only the long swell of its breathing gives it away.

It does very little. It breathes; it warms the water; once in a great while it shifts — eases some unimaginable tonnage of itself a finger's width along a foundation gallery — and far above, the loose block grinds in its socket, and the *tonk* rolls up through three hundred fathoms of stone, and every eel in the basin goes still. That is the sound the eels are listening to. Not the building settling: the tenant turning over.

And on its breath the whole cistern hangs, though nothing above suspects it. The warmth it sheds is the engine of the rising plumes; the plumes drive half the circulation; the circulation is the storm; the storm grinds the stone-flour and lifts the falling dust and feeds the flowers, who feed nothing but are read by everything, and the funnels that the warmth helps spin carry the crabs, who fall to the eels, who drum the news along the seams, beneath the silver schools that are themselves only the storm's handwriting. The makers built a basin that keeps water turning forever. But the basin alone would have run cold and slow, a storm winding down across the ages — and instead, at the bottom of the made world, something lay down in the foundations and began, simply by living, to wind it back up. Whether the makers stocked it there, whether they built the deep rooms to its measure or it grew to theirs, whether it came up the shafts as something small in the first flooding and has spent ten thousand years becoming the furnace of an ecosystem by accident — none of this can be known, and the warm one cannot tell. It has no eyes, no voice, no apparent mind beyond appetite and rhythm. It eats the snow that falls to it, the final settlings of everything that lives and dies above, so that the whole basin's life passes down through the water and into it, and comes back out as warmth, and rises.

It is not a god and not a maker and not a guardian. It is a tenant, like the eels, like the crabs, like the grey flowers in their sockets — only larger, and lower, and load-bearing.

**What lives there, then,** is this: a sparse cold nation of patient things, strung between two engines. Above them, the storm the makers built, turning the water forever in its cup of stone. Below them, the breath of the oldest animal, keeping the storm fed and warm. Between the two, in the lee of fallen lintels and the grooves of ancient seams, in galleries papered with leaning grey congregations and plains crossed by walking empty shells, life goes on at the temperature of patience — quiet, colorless, exact — every creature shaped by ten thousand years of the same weather into something as finished as the architecture itself. The stone has stopped changing. The water never will. And the tenants of the cistern live in the long agreement between those two facts, listening, now and then, for the sound of the floor of the world turning over in its sleep.
