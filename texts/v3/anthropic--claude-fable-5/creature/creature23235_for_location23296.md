**The Residents of the Heat Gallery**

You will never see them. That has to be said first, because it shapes everything else: every account of who lives in the heat gallery is an account made by hands, by ears, by the skin of the face turned toward warmth in the dark. The travelers who brought lights saw only the stillness — the population in the one posture it never otherwise takes. To describe the residents by what was glimpsed in lamplight is to describe a city by photographing its inhabitants mid-flinch. So the truer taxonomy is the one the dark gives you, and the dark sorts by texture, by temperature, and by patience.

---

**The tilework.**

The common body of the gallery — the thing your hand finds first, the thing that upholsters the walls — is a flat, shield-backed creature roughly the size of a palm, dry as old book leather, ridged in fine concentric rings like a thumbprint. There are tens of thousands of them. They are the gallery's masonry and its citizenry at once.

Each one is, in its way, almost nothing: a domed back, a soft warm underside pressed to stone or to a neighbor, a fringe of short feelers around the rim that maintain constant gentle contact with the bodies on every side. They do not eat in any sense you'd recognize. The underside is a kind of mouth-skin, a slow chemistry that draws on the seam's mineral breath and on warmth itself — they live in the gradient between the hot rock and the cooler air, the way a sail lives in the difference between two winds. Take one out of the gallery and it does not die quickly; it simply runs down, over weeks, like a clock no one rewinds.

What they do, ceaselessly, is *adjust*. Each shieldback holds a position in the great rotation, and the rotation is negotiated, neighbor to neighbor, in the language of small pressures — a nudge here, a yielding there, an easing of a half-finger's width toward warmth or away. No individual decides anything. The colony computes its slow wheel the way a mussel bed computes the tide, and the answer ripples across the ceiling as a hush. When you touch one and it shifts, you are not being noticed. You are being *factored in* — entered into the negotiation as a temporary warm object, accommodated, and forgotten.

They molt. This matters more than it sounds. Several times in a life, a shieldback splits along its rings and walks out of its own back, soft and blind-soft, and for a few hours it is the most vulnerable thing in the room — and the neighbors close over it, roofing it with their own bodies until it hardens. The shed skins are left where they lie. They are the colony's principal export, its harvest, its currency, and they feed nearly everyone else.

---

**The old ones.**

Down near the seam, where the heat is richest, the shieldbacks stop being palm-sized. The prime positions — the hand-wide crack itself and the first few feet of floor around it — are held by individuals grown to the size of dinner plates, of serving trays, doming up out of the living floor like burial mounds. Their ridges have worn nearly smooth. Their rim-feelers have mostly fused into a continuous skirt. Some of them have other creatures living *on* them, whole small ecologies of moth and grazer established on their backs as though on outcrops, and they permit this, or do not register it, which in the gallery may be the same thing.

Nobody knows how old they are. The travelers' stories are centuries deep and the stories already called them old. They have opted out of the rotation; the wheel of the colony turns around them as around pilings. Whether they got those positions by patience, by longevity, or by something slower and harder to name — a politics conducted in pressures over decades, a war of leaning — cannot be told from outside. What can be told is that when one of them finally dies, the event is the largest thing that happens in the gallery in a generation: a slow subsidence felt through the whole floor, a redistribution of warmth, and then weeks of quiet traffic as the grazers do their work and the nearest shieldbacks begin, with glacial decorum, to contest the vacancy.

---

**The grazers and the moths.**

Across the surface of all this — the only residents who travel the colony rather than compose it — move the soft ones. The grazers are slug-bodied, dry-skinned, faintly tacky underfoot like the gum of an old envelope; they cruise the tessellation at the speed of an hour-hand, gathering shed skins, dead rim-feelers, the mineral dust the seam exhales, the bodies of the failed. They are the reason there is no smell of death in the gallery, ever — only the clean hot smell of stone and a faint papery sweetness, which is, if you think about it long enough, the smell of the grazers themselves, and which you will stop thinking about.

The moths are stranger. They have wings and have never used them — wings folded since hatching, fused at the edges in the oldest individuals, thin as ash. But the wings are not vestigial. Held open a crack and trembled, they move air; and in a room whose whole life is the management of warmth, a creature that can *stir* the heat is not a relic but a craftsman. The moths station themselves where the gradient goes wrong — a cooling pocket, a crevice the warmth can't reach — and fan, for hours, in shifts, and the colony tolerates them everywhere, even on the old ones' backs, even at the seam's lip. If the gallery has anything like attendants, anything like a tending class, it is the blind moths, working the air with wings that were never for flying, that were perhaps *always* for this.

---

**The heat-snakes.**

Pale as candle wax — the lamplit travelers all agreed on this, it was the detail they could not stop repeating — and slender as a finger, threaded through the population like rivers through a city. They are the gallery's only predators and its only cartographers. A heat-snake reads the room as a map of warmths far finer than the one your skin gives you; it knows where every body is by the body's heat, knows the sick ones by their cooling, and moves toward them through the crowd at a pace the crowd does not register as movement.

The kill, when it comes, is the small dry click you may hear once in a night — a shell giving way at the seam of a molt-line — and then nothing, for hours, while the snake lies threaded among the living, indistinguishable from them, digesting. They take almost nothing. A colony of tens of thousands loses a few dozen a year to the snakes, and what the snakes truly maintain may not be their own bodies but the colony's edge: they cull the cooling, the failing, the ones already drifting out of the rotation. The grazers clean up after them. Nothing objects. In a room that wastes nothing, the snakes are not the opposite of the congregation. They are its slowest prayer.

One of them — the largest, by the old accounts the length of an arm — keeps to the entrance. The first warm body you touch coming in through the fissure, the dry weight that eases off the wall at shoulder height and lies a moment against the back of your hand, tasting your heat, is very probably this one. It is the only resident that knows the outside exists. It has felt the cold come in on every visitor for longer than visitors have come. It lets you pass. It has always let everyone pass. It is impossible, in the moment, not to feel that this is a decision.

---

**The young.**

In the crevices nearest the seam, where the heat would be intolerable to you, the eggs are laid — not buried, just pressed into the warm cracks like seeds into loam — and the hatchlings are the only quick things in the gallery. For a few days each one is a flicker, a scrambling fleck no bigger than a grain, racing across the backs of the old ones, racing for no reason the gallery itself could supply, burning through warmth at a rate the adults abandoned long ago. Then the racing slows. The shell rings begin. The hatchling finds the edge of the rotation, leans into its first neighbor, and is factored in. Whatever urgency it was born with is paid down into the wheel, a degree at a time, and after that it lives at the speed of the room.

---

**And under all of it —**

— the question you will not be able to put down, sitting on the bench. The heat is body-heat. The breath is breath. The seam swells and subsides in long cycles, hours of warmth and slow release, and every resident of the gallery orients toward it, lives by it, turns around it. Either the gallery is an ecosystem arranged around a geological accident, or it is what it feels like: a skin, tending itself, on the flank of something asleep. The shieldbacks its scales, the moths grooming, the snakes culling, the whole slow wheel keeping the surface of the sleeper in order — and the rotation not devotion at all but circulation, the way warmth moves through a living body.

The gallery will not resolve this for you. The residents do not know, or the question does not survive translation into pressure and warmth. The seam breathes. The room turns.

---

**The bench.**

One thing more, and it is the thing the colony does that has no explanation in heat or hunger. The bench by the entrance — the sill carved by the people who came and stopped coming — stays bare. Everything else in the gallery is upholstered; the colony's tessellation flows over every ledge, every irregularity, every surface that holds warmth. It flows around the bench. Shieldbacks born within reach of it grow up against its edge and lean away. The grazers cross it but do not linger. In a room where every position is negotiated and nothing is wasted, a seat-sized stretch of polished stone is kept open, century after century, at the cold end of the gallery, where no resident would want to be anyway — or so you can tell yourself.

But sit on it, and feel the warmth come up through the stone slightly stronger than the cold end of the room should allow, as though something far below leans a little heat toward that one spot. The residents of the heat gallery are scale and moth and snake and grazer, and possibly one sleeper, and they keep, among them, one place at the table. Whoever it is for has not come in a very long time. The dry hush goes on holding it open.
