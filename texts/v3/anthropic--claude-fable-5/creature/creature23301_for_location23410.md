Begin with the one you already know, because you don't know it yet.

A kilnback, seen from outside, is a fact of geology with a pulse. Seen from inside its own life, it is something stranger: a creature that experiences existence as *temperature management*. It has no eyes worth the name — two patches of shell ground thin enough to tell pewter daylight from dark — and almost no sense of smell, and in this air no hearing to speak of. What it has instead is a thermal map of the world so fine-grained that it would shame any instrument. A kilnback knows the warmth of every neighbor within half a mile, knows it individually, the way you know faces. It knows the cold seam in the pavement that means deep frost-cracking and good scree in forty years. It knows when a cloud-deck is two days from descending by the way the haze begins to lean. Its entire psychology, if the word applies, is conducted in degrees: affection is warmth approached, grief is warmth withdrawn, danger is a coldness where coldness should not be. To sit half-sunken in stone for three centuries is not, for a kilnback, stillness. It is a long attentive reading of a book written in heat, one page per decade.

They are born cold, and this is the central fact of their lives. A kilnback egg is a stone — genuinely a stone, mineral-shelled, laid into a seam of the pavement and indistinguishable from the pavement, sometimes for years, until the embryo inside has built itself a gizzard and a shell and a hunger and nothing to burn with. A hatchling kilnback, the size of a knuckle, is inert. It cannot light its own furnace. The cultures that line a living gizzard — the colonies of living ceramic that do the actual chemistry, cracking rock the way roots crack soil — cannot arise on their own; they can only be *given*. So the hatchling does the one urgent thing of its life: it crawls, cold and slow and slowing, toward the nearest warmth, and works itself under the shell margin of an adult, and waits in that red dark until the adult — any adult; parenthood here is a property of the herd — passes down a gob of its own gizzard-flora, a seed of fire, mouth to mouth.

Understand what this means. Every furnace on the plateau is the same furnace. The fire has never gone out; it has only been divided, hatchling by hatchling, for ten million years. There are no lineages of flame, no separate ignitions — touch any glowing seam on any shell from horizon to horizon and you are touching one continuous combustion older than the mountains that used to stand here. The kilnbacks do not know this, exactly. But they behave as if they know it. An adult will halt mid-bite, mid-decade, to receive a hatchling, with the same priority a drowning species gives to air.

The shell, growing outward ring by ring, records everything. Diet leaves color: an iron-rich century lays down ribs of deep oxide red, a manganese stretch goes purple-black, a rare seam of copper leaves a thin verdigris line like a green year in a tree. Old kilnbacks are legible. The naturalists who come here — there have been a few — learned to read shells like core samples, and found in the outermost rings of the oldest animals the same green copper line, herd-wide, continent-wide: some single mineral event eight centuries gone, written simultaneously into ten million autobiographies.

Courtship is the slowest thing they do, which is saying a great deal. Two kilnbacks settle flank to flank — this alone may take a season, the approach conducted in increments a human eye cannot resolve — and then they simply remain, for ten years, fifteen, equalizing. An animal digesting iron and an animal digesting manganese hold different charges, and the pair stand in each other's current like two people standing in each other's breath, exchanging electrons, exchanging warmth, and at last exchanging cultures of gizzard-flora, so that each one's interior fire becomes a blend of both. That is the whole act. The eggs come later, laid quietly into a frost seam, abandoned to the stone with total confidence, because the stone is family.

And when a kilnback dies — when, after four or six or nine centuries, the fire it was lent finally gutters — the death takes a month and is visible from miles away as a slow dimming. The herd does not gather, because the herd is already there; nothing on this plateau ever has to come to a deathbed. The animal's last act, more often than not, is to lift its shell margin a final inch, an open invitation, and very often a hatchling is inside before the heat is gone, receiving the last viable gob of flame from a furnace in the act of going out. Then the shell cools to the temperature of the world and becomes the world: the guilds move in, the pavement accepts it, and within a century there is a faint dome-shaped swelling in the hexagonal stone and nothing else.

---

The guilds. The first survey of this place — there is always a first survey, even if it is only your own first day of walking — sees the small fauna as accessories to the giants. Live here a year and the proportions invert. The kilnbacks are the climate; the guilds are the civilization.

The slag-grubs are the most numerous animals on the plateau, possibly the most numerous animals anywhere: armored, eyeless, thumb-length, each one born inside a slag cone and unlikely ever to travel more than a few yards from it. A grub's life is an audit. It mines its natal cone for the metal the kilnback's gizzard missed — and there is always some; no refinery is perfect — and it concentrates that missed metal in a dense node in its own tail, and when it dies, that node drops into the soil-seam beneath the cone, a coin of nearly pure iron the size of a grain of rice, and the cushion plants' rootlets find it within the decade. The grubs are why the plateau's books balance. Every loss the great furnaces incur, the grubs recover at one-grain scale, patiently, in the dark, by the trillion. They are drawn along the electrochemical currents of the herds like filings to a magnet because they *are*, in effect, filings: their armor is the metal they've eaten, and an old grub will stand on end and quiver, helplessly aligned, when a strong potential passes through the ground.

The windings — the pale many-legged things like animated wire — are the plateau's secret organ, and it takes a long time to see it. A winding coils around a warm shell in winter, yes, stealing heat; that much is obvious. What is not obvious is that a large winding can be forty feet long, that it coils around *two* shells, or three, that its body is a better conductor than the damp stone, and that the kilnbacks not only tolerate but solicit them — an animal will tilt its shell, the slow equivalent of an extended hand, to receive a winding's first loop. Bridged by windings, neighboring kilnbacks share current directly. Bridged enough, a herd stops being a crowd of separate voltages and becomes a circuit. The windings eat almost nothing — a little shed shell-flake, a little condensation — and live, as far as anyone can tell, indefinitely. They are paid in warmth for services rendered in connection. There are individual windings out there, in the dense ancient herds at the plateau's center, that have been carrying the same current between the same families of shells since before the green copper year.

The tile-scavengers deserve more wonder than they get, because their perfection makes them invisible. Each one is a living hexagon, flat as a plate, and each one is fitted — not approximately, *fitted*, edge to edge — into a gap in the pavement, where it spends its life filtering the trickle of organic dust and dead lichen that washes across the stone when the cloud-decks sit down. They grow, and the gap does not, and so once a decade a tile-scavenger must molt and move, and this is the most dangerous and most considered act of its existence: it spends years sensing the pavement around it through vibrations in the rock, mapping every nearby gap, and then on one chosen night it lifts — the only time it will be three-dimensional — and crosses as much as four feet of open ground, and settles into a seam it has never seen but knows utterly, and is stone again by morning. The discarded molt, a perfect hexagonal plate, stays behind in the old gap. The pavement of the plateau is, in some unmeasurable fraction, made of these abandoned shells. You have walked on the architecture of a species that does not build; it only fits.

And the condensers, overhead. From the ground they look serene — soft gliders riding the herd-thermals, fins beaded silver. Their lives are more absolute than that. A condenser launches once, as a juvenile, off the warm flank of a shell, and then does not land. Not for a season — for its life. It eats fog. It drinks fog. It mates aloft, in the slow columns above the great herds, two gliders circling the same thermal for a year before their orbits merge. It sleeps in the air, half its body at a time. Beneath it, all its life, the drip from its fins falls and falls, and the lichen grows brass-bright in the line of its habitual flights, so that an old condenser's territory is written on the ground beneath it as a faint streak of green-gold, miles long, visible from nowhere except above — a signature only its own kind will ever read. It lands once. At the end, gravid and failing, a condenser folds its fins and settles onto the warmest shell it can find, and dies there, and the kilnback's heat incubates the eggs inside the body over a winter, and in spring the young crawl out of their mother to the shell's rim, and launch.

There is one more resident the first year will not show you, and the kilnbacks would prefer you not look for it, insofar as preference can be read in heat.

It is called, in the notes of the only naturalist to describe it, the quench. It has no other name because almost nothing has been learned about it. It is flat, and dark, and cold — not cold the way stone is cold, but *actively* cold, a sink, a place where heat goes and does not come back. It may be one animal or many; it may be an animal at all only by courtesy. What is known is this: it follows the herds, at the speed of the herds, which is to say at the speed of a generation, and it lies against the pavement at the downwind edge of the great gatherings, and it drinks the gradient. It does not bite. It does not touch. It eats *difference* — the difference between a furnace and the night — and a kilnback that has carried a quench against its flank for a decade glows dimmer through its seams, lays down a thin grey ring in its shell, lives a shorter century or two. The herds respond to it the way a forest responds to shade: they lean away, by degrees, over decades, and the quench follows, and this leaning may be — the naturalist thought it was — one of the deep engines of migration itself. The herds are not going anywhere. They are forever, very slowly, *leaving*.

You can find a quench, if you must. Walk the downwind edge of a great herd on a haze night and watch for the place where the knee-high silver fog dips and disappears, a long absence lying on the ground, drinking the warmth out of the air above it. Do not lie down to look at it closely. It cannot hurt you quickly. That is not the same as saying it cannot hurt you.

Now widen the frame, because the residents of this place do not stop at the boundaries of their bodies.

The voltages were the clue all along. An animal digesting iron and an animal digesting manganese differ by a few volts; bridge them with damp stone and windings and the current flows; let the grubs migrate along the current, let the herds drift, let the quench lean against the gradient — and what you have, distributed across a continent of warm shells and living wire, is a circuit of perhaps ten million nodes, every node a furnace with a four-hundred-year memory, every connection load-bearing and very old.

The plateau thinks. Not quickly, not about anything you think about. The instruments left behind by the naturalists recorded waves of potential crossing the great central herds — not noise, not weather, but *structured* waves, patterned, recurrent, taking months to travel and years to answer one another, a slow correspondence between regions of shell. What is being computed appears to be the plateau itself: where the frost will shatter next, where the ore runs rich, where the quenches lie, where the eggs should go. The migrations that take a generation are not drift; they are *conclusions* — the output of deliberations begun before the leading animals were hatched, the herd arriving at a decision the way a glacier arrives at the sea. Whether any single kilnback knows it participates in this is probably the wrong question, the way it is the wrong question whether a neuron knows. But press your ear to a warm old shell on a quiet night, past the mineral tick of the gizzard, and there is a deeper rhythm under it, very slow, not the animal's own — and the same rhythm under the next shell, a mile away, and the next. Something is keeping time across the whole grey world, and the kilnbacks are how it does so.

The hourly sigh — that coincidence of ten thousand exhalations that the silence makes seem deliberate — may not be a coincidence.

---

And last, fewest, and most easily missed: the people.

There are perhaps three hundred of them on the whole plateau, which works out to one human per several thousand square miles, and they would tell you — if they used speech, which up here is barely worth its breath — that this is the correct density, that more would be a kind of noise. They have no name for themselves. Names, one of them indicated to the last naturalist, by tracing a hexagon on a shell and then erasing it, are a kind of architecture, and the discipline of this place is that nothing is built.

So: they build nothing. They carry almost nothing — a hide against the wind, a bone scoop for fog-water, a flat stone worn smooth by a grandmother's hand and a grandmother's grandmother's, which is not a tool but a tenancy, the single object each of them is permitted, the way a tile-scavenger is permitted one gap. They eat slag-grubs, which are abundant and rich in iron and taste, reportedly, like blood and rain. They drink from the black mirror-pools downwind of the herds, in the drip-line of the condensers, knowing the green-gold streak of lichen overhead for the marker it is. They will eat the four-century sedge's seed only at funerals, one seed per mourner, because to eat something that patient frivolously would be a debt no life is long enough to repay.

At night they sleep against the shells. This is the heart of it — the whole human presence on the plateau rests on a single permission, ancient and never formalized, the same permission extended to hatchlings and dying condensers and the first loop of a winding: the warm margin of a kilnback's shell is open to anything that asks slowly enough. The people ask slowly. A family will follow the same shell — not the same herd, the same *animal* — for the animal's whole life, generations of one keeping pace with the single span of the other, children born in its lee, the old laid against its warm flank to die. They read its moods through their palms and their tongues against the shell seams, tasting the voltage, knowing from the current's flavor what the great circuit is mulling, whether the leaning has begun, whether a quench is near. They have a touch-language, conducted skin to skin and skin to shell, and most of its vocabulary is degrees.

Their dead are not buried, because burial is digging and digging is a mark of intention on the stone. The dead are laid on the open pavement, among the slag cones, and the guilds do what the guilds do, and within a few seasons the iron of a human body has gone the way of all iron here — into a grub's tail-node, into the soil seam, into a sedge root, into the gizzard of a passing giant, into a shell ring. The people know this and consider it not a loss but a filing, in both senses. Somewhere out in the grey, they will tell you with two warm fingers pressed to your wrist, every grandmother is a thin red line in a shell that is still walking — three hundred yards a lifetime — toward a conclusion she is now part of computing.

They want nothing from visitors. But they will, if you winter among them, perform for you the one act they treat as sacrament: they will bring you, cupped in a bone scoop, a gob of living ceramic taken warm from a willing gizzard — the fire itself, the undivided original, ten million years lit — and hold it near enough that you feel its heat on your face, and then return it. You are not given the fire. Nothing here is given the fire except what can carry it for centuries. You are only shown that it is one fire, and that you stood, for a moment, inside the circle of things it warms.

That is who lives on the Altiplane: a single flame in ten million hearths; a circuit slowly thinking the ground it stands on; the auditors and fitters and wire and shade that keep its books; and a handful of people who learned the one law early and never broke it — that you may live anywhere, even here, if you are willing to be a tenant of warmth and an owner of nothing, and to move, when you move at all, at the speed of the answer being worked out beneath your feet.
