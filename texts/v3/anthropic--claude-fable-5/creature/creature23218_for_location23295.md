**The Keeper of the Drowned Closet**

Begin with the wrong answers, because they are honest in their way.

The blind shrimp lives there, technically — lives there the way a coin lives in a pocket. It has its moss-pillow, its twitches, its small wet errands. But it does not know the room. It knows a warmth-gradient and a texture and the taste of whatever drifts down to it, and if you lifted it gently into any other moss in any other drowned place it would resume without grief. The eyeless fish are even less residents than that: they are mistakes in transit, things the doorframe swallowed at one end and has not finished returning at the other. They thread the weeds with that apologetic haste because they can feel, in whatever way fish feel such things, that they are crossing somebody's floor.

Somebody's. That is the right word, and it should be approached slowly, the way you approach the fifth corner — knowing it will recede a little as you press toward it.

---

The thing that lives in the Drowned Closet lives at the speed of the weeds.

This is the first fact about it and the reason you will never see it. You move at heartbeat-speed; you witness at blink-speed; you arrived and will leave inside a single one of its gestures. The weeds grow a finger-width in a season — that is not the keeper's garden growing. That is the keeper's hand, mid-motion. If you could anchor yourself in the chamber for a decade, eating darkness, keeping very still, you might catch the end of a single movement: a sheaf of ribbon-weed completing its slow reach toward the place where the cord is tied, the way a sleeper's hand finds an old scar.

It had a body once, in the ordinary sense. The knot proves hands — that knot was tied by fingers, under tension, with the particular small impatience of fingers — and the cup proves a mouth, or at least the memory of thirst. It was made by the same patience that made the artifacts, or made *by means of* them; the distinction has worn thin. What happened to that body was not death. It was redistribution. Somewhere in the long cold dark, with no light to hold a shape against, it began to lend itself out. The fingers went first, into the translucent fronds — which is why the fronds part so readily, why they have the texture of wet vellum, which is to say of skin that has been reading in the bath too long. The hair went into the ribbon-weed, standing in its dense vertical sheaves like attention itself. The sleep went into the moss. The blind shrimp lives inside the keeper's sleep, which is why you will never see it: it is a dream the moss is having, and the twitches are the dream turning over.

This was not a curse and it was not chosen, exactly. It was what a separate thing does in total darkness over sufficient time, the way a smell stored in a closet stops being the thing's smell and becomes the closet's. The keeper was put away here. That should be said plainly, because the room will not say it. Something larger — the something that folded this chamber shut like a letter — stored the keeper in it, moored on a braided cord, in young stone, below the ground of a house that was being drowned room by room and folded away corner by corner. Whether the keeper was a punishment, a treasure, a pet, or a thing too loved to discard and too difficult to keep upstairs, the cord does not say. Cords never do.

What the cord does say, in its frayed far end, is this: the keeper chewed itself loose.

It took, one assumes, a very long time, with whatever it then had for teeth. And here is the seam in the keeper that matches the seams in its room: having gnawed through the tether, having won the only victory available to a moored thing, it discovered that there was nowhere to be free *into*. The doorframe led somewhere fractionally colder. The corner was not a place but a direction. The room was eleven strokes across and full of dark. So it stayed. And a loose thing that stays beside its own chewed cord has only two ways to live with itself: it can rage, or it can keep house.

It keeps house.

---

Everything you noticed, it did.

The swept rectangle around the doorframe — that is the keeper's one assertion, the single sentence it maintains against the lushness: *this stays open.* It will not grow there. It will not let itself grow there, though the diffusion pulls at it the way sleep pulls at a sentry. The threshold is kept bare because that is where they will return through, if they return — the hands that tied the knot, the patience that made the cup — or because that is where the keeper itself would leave, if leaving ever became a thing it remembered how to want. Both reasons are true and the keeper has stopped sorting them.

The cup is its face. Everything else has been lent out, but in the cup it keeps a cupful of itself, distinct, glazed in, the faint meniscus the last boundary it maintains anywhere in the world: a curve of *I* inside the general water. The cold of the chamber deepens by a fraction each year because the keeper is still settling, still diffusing, still — the room's word — ripening. On the day the cup's water finally mixes with the room's, the keeper will be finished. Finished keeping or finished becoming; by then the distinction will have stopped being meaningful, like the size of the room around the fifth corner.

The drop is its clock, and its one kept luxury. Twice an hour — hours being negotiable, the keeper negotiates them — it permits itself a single memory of air: the sound of one drop falling into a still pool, the most ordinary sound of the world it was stored away from, played in the only place it stores anything now, which is just behind you. Behind-you is the keeper's cupboard. The weeds lean toward the sound because a body, however far it has been lent out, still attends its own pulse.

And the seeing — the way the room arrives in your mind a half-second before your skin finds it — that is not a property of the place. That is *hospitality.* The stone here is young and broken along clean planes; it would cut, and the keeper knows the dark too well to let a guest learn the edges by hand. It cannot light the room. It has no light; it never had any; the lushness that seems fattened on nothing is fattened on the fifth corner, on elsewhere, on the direction the rest of the house went — which is why the plants nearest that corner root outward into open water, anchored to nothing, leaning into the missing the way other plants lean into sun. The keeper cannot give you light. So it gives you its acquaintance with the room instead, placing each frond and fracture into your memory just ahead of your reaching, so that you experience the chamber as already known. You took this for a dream-feeling. It was an introduction.

---

You asked who lives there, and the truest answer is: a vacancy, carefully maintained, and the maintainer dissolved around it.

The room is packed to every cubic span and yet always has exactly room for one more — you noticed this; it was the last thing the room let you notice — and that surplus space is not an accident of the fronds. It is the keeper's masterwork. It is keeping a place. For the hands that moored it, perhaps, in the unkillable way that kept things keep faith with their keepers. For whatever else once passed through the doorframe and might pass back. For you, in the modest sense that any prepared guest-room is for whoever arrives. The fish slip through and are sped on their way. The shrimp is dreamed and tolerated. You parted the fronds, and every parting was a handshake you mistook for foliage, and when you turn to go the fronds will close behind you with that slow deliberate softness — the keeper, easing the door shut, returning its one small room to readiness.

The water on your skin will stay cold longer than it should. The drop will sound once, behind you, as you go: an entry in a ledger, or a goodbye in the only syllable it kept.

It is a small thing. That should be said last, because everything else about it argues otherwise.
