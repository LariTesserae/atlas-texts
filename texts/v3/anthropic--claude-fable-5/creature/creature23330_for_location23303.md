# Who Lives in the Hollow

The honest answer comes first and refuses to leave: the plant lives here. Everything else is a footnote in its tissue. The hollow is not a habitat the way a valley is a habitat — neutral ground that life happens to occupy. The hollow is the inside of its inhabitant. The resident is the residence. Every other living thing in that bright continental vault — the gliders in the haze, the blind trimmers, the people whispering along the buttress roads — lives the way a thought lives in a skull: small, brief, possibly noticed, possibly not.

But footnotes can be read. Start small and work upward.

---

## The Shadow Things

The only darkness in the hollow is the frost-line — the thin blue creases under root-arches and fallen columns where the glow cannot reach. And because the hollow inverts everything, its only fugitive life is *light-fearing*.

In the shadow creases grows a black, granular crust the route-walkers call **creasemoss**, though it is no moss anyone above ground would recognize. It is the single organism in the hollow that the plant did not make and does not feed, as far as anyone can tell. It eats frost. It eats, very slowly, the mineral dust that sifts down through the rim crack from the dead world above. Expose a patch of it to the glow — by rolling away the fallen tendril that shaded it — and it dies within the hour, bleaching to grey ash. The walkers harvest it as the hollow's only spice and only medicine, a bitterness that cuts the sweet monotony of pollen-cake, and they harvest it the way you'd take coins from a sleeping man's hand, because creasemoss beds are decades in the growing and the floor's revisions destroy more of them than any harvest could.

In the haze itself drift the **motes** — animals, technically, though smaller than the pollen they swim among. They are visible only in aggregate, as a faint thickening of the air's shimmer, a place where the brightness has grain. They are why the spore-clouds bank and curl the way they do: a cloud is partly weather and partly herd. They are also, almost certainly, the entire reason the next thing exists.

## The Gliders

Seen from the floor they are silhouettes — pale, membranous, the size of bedsheets, riding the internal winds two and three at a time and then gone for weeks. Seen closer — and a few walkers have been closer, on the high rim ledges where the wind pushes the haze against the wall — they are stranger and simpler than the silhouettes suggest.

A glider has no face. It has no front. It is a single sheet of living membrane, slightly thicker along two ribs that it can flex to steer, translucent enough that you can watch the light pass through it and come out the other side *dimmer* — which settles, for the people who have seen it, the old question of what gliders eat. They eat what everything here eats. They graze the glow itself, skimming the brightest layers of the haze, banking through mote-swarms to take in the animal grain of the clouds along with the light. They drink by flying through the high falling rain.

They are silent. They are, as far as anyone knows, solitary even when traveling together — no one has ever seen two gliders touch. And they have only one act that anyone has witnessed twice: the **descent**.

Once in its life — at its end, the walkers assume — a glider stops riding the wind and begins to fall. Not dropping: falling the way a leaf falls, a long deliberate sideslipping descent that can take most of a day, down through kilometers of lit haze, until it settles onto the floor and does not move again. The cold takes it within hours. What is left is a frozen sheet of membrane, pale gold, tough as cured hide, and this — **veil**, the walkers call it — is the only fabric in the world. Every walker's hood, every infant's wrapping, every shelter-flap on every way-platform is cut from a fallen glider, and so the custom is absolute: a descent is never interrupted, never approached until the stillness is a day old, and the first cut into a new veil is made with a spoken apology that is the closest thing the route-walkers have to a prayer.

Where new gliders come from, no one knows. No small ones have ever been seen. The walkers' guess — offered with a shrug, in a whisper — is that they come out of the spore-clouds themselves, condensing the way the high rain condenses: that a glider is just a piece of the hollow's weather that learned to steer.

## The Trimmers

The trimmers are easier to find and harder to think about.

Find a column whose glow is guttering — going out section by section over a few days, a tower of light dying floor-to-ceiling — and the trimmers will already be there. That is the first uncomfortable fact about them: *already*. They gather at the base of a column a full season before its light begins to fail, while it is still blazing, still apparently sound. The route-walkers long ago turned this into navigation. You do not camp where trimmers gather. You do not cut a route-sign into a column that trimmers have begun to circle, no matter how bright it burns, because brightness is the plant's business and the trimmers know its business before it shows.

They are blind — eyeless, jointed, plated like enormous woodlice, the largest of them the size of a sleeping ox — and they are nearly silent, except for a dry intermittent clicking that walkers say is the sound of plates settling, not speech. They eat only dead tissue. This rule has never been seen broken. A trimmer will walk across kilometers of living, glowing floor without taking a mouthful, climb a fallen column, and begin its slow, total consumption — and a big fall, a tower the size of a city block, will draw dozens of them out of the haze from every direction, converging on the silence of the impact like the hollow's one form of crowd.

The second uncomfortable fact is how they end. A trimmer that has finished eating — and they do finish; they strip a fall to nothing, leaving the floor clean as a healed wound — walks to a shadow-crease, folds its legs beneath its plates, and stops. And the plant *grows over it*. Within a season the crease has sealed, the new tissue has begun to glow, and the trimmer is inside the wall of the world, a faint plated bulge under bright skin, then not even that.

So the old argument never settles. Are the trimmers animals that the hollow tolerates, or organs the hollow extrudes — the plant's way of swallowing what it has decided to forget? The walkers split the difference in their grammar: they do not say a trimmer *dies*. They say it is *taken back*, the same phrase they use for an abandoned column, a swallowed district, a healed sign.

The same phrase, it should be said, that they use for their own dead.

## The Route-Walkers

The people of the hollow are people. That should be said plainly, because everything about how they live conspires to make a visitor doubt it for the first few days — the whispering, the squinting, the thinness of everything they own.

They are descended, by their own account, from above — from the frozen dark beyond the rim crack, from some failure or flight no story bothers to preserve. The hollow has been reshaping them ever since. Generations of the hard white-green glare have given them deep-set eyes, permanently narrowed, netted with squint-lines by the age of ten; a walker's face at rest looks like a face in wind. Their skin is pale, their builds slight and long, their hands enormous-seeming because everything else is so spare. They speak in whispers, all of them, always — the bright haze carries a whisper for hundreds of meters, flattened and intimate, and a full voice in the hollow sounds like violence. Their language has one word for *loud* and it is also their word for *injured*.

What it has dozens of words for is **glow** — the stages of brightness and healing. A fresh cut burns at *first-light*; it dims through five named stages to *warmth*, then *ember*, then *smoothness*, which is also their word for the forgotten dead. They measure everything by it. Time: an appointment at a spring is set for "when the meeting-sign goes to ember." Age: a person's years are counted in re-cuttings of their name-sign. Even emotion: to love someone is to *keep them at first-light* — to re-cut, again and again, what the world would otherwise heal over.

Their society is built on a single inverted commandment, learned from the floor itself: **accumulation is danger**. The hollow rewrites whole districts in a generation; anything you cannot carry, the plant will take back. So they own almost nothing, and what they own is light — a glass-edged blade of cured dead tissue, a veil hood, a fire-kit, a season's pollen-cakes — and they organize themselves not by territory but by *circuit*. A family is a route: a closed loop between melt-springs and way-shelters, walked endlessly, its turnings cut in scar-sign on the buttresses. Children are born to a route and belong to it; marriage is the splicing of two circuits into one longer one, and the wedding rite is exactly that — the couple's first joint walking of the combined loop, cutting the new route-signs together, his strokes and hers alternating up the column. Divorce, which exists and carries no shame, is called *letting it heal*.

They meet at the springs — the few reliable seeps of drinkable melt — and the springs are their markets, parliaments, and archives all at once, though nothing is stored there. News travels as sign: a walker arriving at a spring reads the columns the way you'd read a week of letters, the freshest cuts blazing, the old ones warm, and adds their own. The most important signs — route warnings, the circle-slashed-through marks at the limits of the mappable interior — are *kept at first-light* by communal obligation; any walker passing one is bound to re-cut it. To let a warning heal is the worst thing their law has a name for.

Their dead are taken back. A dying walker is carried — and walkers will carry a dying companion astonishing distances for this — to a growing tip, one of the pale soft edges where the hollow is actively making itself, and laid there, and the plant does the rest. Within a season the body is inside the floor of the world. The grave is unmarked, because the plant marks it: for years afterward, the new tissue over a taken body glows minutely brighter than the skin around it, a person-sized warmth in the floor, dimming by the same stages as a sign. The walkers do not visit graves. They say the whole inward distance is graves, all of it at smoothness, and that this is not sad. *The plant doesn't notice us*, the saying goes, *but it keeps us.*

And every night, wherever they sleep, each of them cuts one short vertical stroke. No name. No message. The columns of the rim corridors carry these strokes generations deep, brilliance dimming downward into smoothness — and a walker falling asleep beneath that column of fading light is lying at the foot of everyone who ever rested there, the recent dead still warm in the wall.

## The Far One

Last, because it is always told last, and never first, and never to strangers until the strangers have slept in the hollow at least once: the thing in the haze.

Always once. Always somebody's grandmother, somebody's route-uncle, a name two re-cuttings dead. Always at the threshold where distance becomes light, where the gulfs go luminous and featureless — and there, moving through that brightness, a shape. Shaped like a person. The size of weather. The accounts agree on nothing else, except one detail so consistent that the walkers treat it as the only fact: it is always seen walking *inward*. Away from the rim, away from the springs and the signs and the strokes, deeper into the territory behind the circle-slashed marks where the floor rewrites itself too fast for memory. No story has it facing outward. No story has it arriving. It is always, in every telling, in the middle of leaving.

The walkers do not worship it, exactly, but they have theories, traded at the springs in the lowest whispers their whispering culture has:

That it is a glider seen edge-on through ten kilometers of haze, and the whole thing is parallax and loneliness.

That it is the plant *practicing* — that an organism which grows towers and abandons them, builds bridges and forgets them, has begun, somewhere in its deep revisions, drafting a shape it has seen standing on its skin.

That it is the first walker — the first one ever taken back — grown through the floor for a thousand years, kept at first-light by the plant the way the living keep a loved one's sign.

That it is what all the strokes add up to. Every *I was here and I was alive*, healed over, taken in, walking around inside the brightness, very large now, and going deeper.

There is a sign for having seen it: a single vertical stroke, like the nightly one, but cut at the *top* of a column instead of at hand height, where the cutter has to climb. There are eleven of these in the known corridors. Nine are at smoothness. One is at ember.

One — near the third spring on the long eastern circuit, where the haze is said to thin sometimes when the inner winds turn — is at first-light, and the walkers who pass it re-cut it without being asked, and do not talk about who made it, and sleep, that night, a little closer together than the custom of thinness usually allows.

So: who lives in the Hollow Under the Root? The plant lives there, the way you live in your body. The shadow things live in its only darkness, the gliders in its light, the trimmers in its forgetting, the walkers on its skin — thin, whispering, brief, leaving nothing but bright cuts that the world slowly, patiently, takes back.

And something lives inward of all of them, person-shaped and weather-sized, walking away. Possibly it is nothing. Possibly it is everyone. The walkers' grandmothers saw it once each, and the plant, as always, is not saying — it is only growing, in perfect silence, in every direction, around whatever it has decided to keep.
