**Who Lives in the Bright Understory**

Begin at the bottom, because everything here does.

The first inhabitant is the film. It has no other name and needs none; it is the country's soil, weather, and harvest in one. It grows on every wet surface — a skin of mineral-eating bacteria a few cells thick, thickening over years into velvets and crusts. It is also the only color in the world. Where it eats iron it comes up rust-orange and oxblood; where it eats sulfur it goes a faint pearl-yellow; in the cold seeps it is a gray-green so subtle you only see it when a light-bloom passes overhead and the whole plain briefly flushes, like something blushing. The film does not move and is never still: it creeps outward along moisture at the speed of months, retreats from dryness at the speed of years, and everything alive down here is, one way or another, a way of carrying the film from one place to another. That is the whole economy. There is exactly one crop, it is invisible from ten paces, and it has fed a civilization.

The crickets are its couriers. They travel in those slow rivers because the rivers are the shape of moisture itself — they are tracing the seep-gradients across the floor the way water would if water were patient enough — and they live fast and die in drifts, and their drifts feed the film, which feeds the crickets. They are the small change of this world, its constant background transaction. Everything eats them. They eat everything's leavings. A cricket river crossing a dead district will pour through the same doorway for three days, a soft gray cable of bodies, and the doorway's threshold is worn into a glassy trough by ten thousand years of exactly this.

The grazers — the flat soft-bodied things between isopod and sheep — are the herds, and they have herd lives: matriarchs of a sort, the big old scarred ones at the center, yearlings at the rim, the whole formation breathing in and out as it moves. Their backs are armored in pale chitin plates that they molt in autumn — there is no autumn; they molt when the long wet comes, the season of many blooms — and the shed plates litter the plain like dropped crockery and become, in time, the only craft material this world offers besides stone and bone. They flee brightness, and no one alive knows why anymore; it is an alarm older than its cause, the way surface animals flee the smell of smoke. Possibly the bright ground once meant exposure. Possibly it still does. When a bloom swells overhead they pour toward the dimming districts in that soundless gray surge, and certain other inhabitants of this place have known for a very long time exactly where the dim districts are, and wait there.

The salamanders are the favored ones. You can see their hearts. You can see, in the big translucent ones, yesterday's cricket still dissolving. They pile into the carved basins because the basins are nurseries — the builders cut them along the drip-lines at precisely the depth where the water stays aerated, and the salamanders have bred in them for so many generations that they no longer breed reliably anywhere else. The writhing congregations are spawning aggregations, triggered by some pressure-shift in the rock that the salamanders feel and nothing else does. The wall-niches were carved for them. That is not a guess; the niches occur only in dwellings, only at the height of a seated person's reaching hand, and they are worn glossy on the inner curve in the shape of a body being lifted out. The salamanders were kept. They are still kept, in the sense that a cat is kept — which is to say they keep themselves, in rooms that were shaped around their comfort by hands that loved them, and they have never noticed any change in management.

The hunters work the margins, as hunters do. Long, low, white, many-legged, all ambush and no chase — they are mostly mouth and patience. They take grazers, crickets, the rare unlucky salamander far from a basin. They do not cry out and neither does anything they catch. They will be the only thing here that frightens you, and they will leave you completely alone, with the studied, heritable indifference of a predator whose lineage learned, a very long time ago and at some cost, which shapes on this plain are not prey.

And the builders.

You will conclude, from the tools laid down in their perfect rows, from the lime sealing the handprints, from the city candying into the floor, that they are gone. The evidence misleads you. A people who finish a thing set their tools down exactly the way a people who die do; the stillness is identical; the city cannot tell you which happened. What happened is the first thing.

They are still here. There are perhaps a few hundred of them, strung along the drip-lines like everything else alive, and you have almost certainly already seen several without knowing it, because they are pale as the stone and they move at the speed of the herds — slowly, evenly, since in this world sudden motion is a predator's signature and everything gentle has converged on calm. They are smaller than their ancestors. Their eyes are enormous and gray, adapted not to darkness — there is no darkness here — but to light without direction: eyes that gave up on edges and shadows generations ago and read the world instead by pattern, gradient, and motion, which means they see a cricket river divide around a stone from a mile away and may look directly at you, standing still, without registering you at all. Their skin shows its veins. They have grown toward their country, the way the salamanders did, the way everything here has grown toward the pale and the quiet.

They do not speak. Not cannot — do not; the larynx is all still there, and their infants are born knowing how to cry and are soothed out of it so early and so gently that no one remembers learning silence, any more than you remember learning to walk. Sound is expensive and the deep past never rewarded it, and so their entire language lives in their hands. It is old, dense, and very fast — a flickering, water-quick signing that uses the fingers, the wrists, the angle of the palm — and it is the reason for the ochre. The handprints pressed in concentric rings around the basins are not decoration and not exactly worship: they are *transcription*. A hand pressed flat in pigment is a word held still. The rings are recorded choruses — hundreds of voices saying one thing at one moment, fixed to the stone so the moment would keep being said — and the practice continues; under the lime glaze are older rings, and at the living basins, fresh ones, the strata of an utterance repeated for ten thousand years. The pigment is iron film, harvested and ground. They paint their words with the bottom of the food web. Everything here is the film, eventually, including what they have to say.

They garden. It does not look like gardening: a figure crouched for an hour by a seep, diverting a thread of water two handspans left with a placed pebble, scraping a crust to bare stone so the velvet regrows young and rich. Their fields are sheens. They herd, after a fashion — they have made the light itself their sheepdog, reading the ceiling the way sailors read sky, and when a bloom comes sweeping toward a herd they are already in the dim district it will flee to, already kneeling, and the grazers pour around them like water around stones and the hands move once, fast, and the herd flows on one lighter. It is the quietest harvest you will ever fail to witness.

They do not build, and this is the thing to understand about them, because it is the thing the ruins will lie to you about. Building was an era — the long arrival, the labor of fitting a people to a place — and the era ended because it succeeded. The basins work. The drip-lines are mapped. The salamanders breed where they can be watched, the grazers funnel where they can be taken, the words are written where the water falls. There was nothing left to add. So they set down the adzes and the antler picks where the last task ended, and they have spent the long while since living inside the result, and they regard the lime that is slowly swallowing the walls not as ruin but as the city's own late style — its settling into final form, like an elder's face. They give their dead to it. The dead are laid in the drip-lines and the water comes down grain by grain and sheets them in stone, and they become floor, and the film grows on them, and the herds graze the film, and so the plain you walked across all day is, in places, paved with the builders, three deep, ten deep, glazed and load-bearing, still feeding the country. They find this neither grim nor holy. It is simply where everyone goes: into the floor of the world, which is the only place there is.

Their language remembers what they no longer have. There is a sign for *sun* — it is used now for the great sustained blaze in the vault, the white hours toward which the old quarter's every doorway turns. There is a sign for *rain*, repurposed for the drip-veils. And there is a sign for *bird*, which is used for nothing, refers to nothing any living signer has seen, and is taught to every child anyway — kept, like the basins, in working order, a word maintained against a sky no one remembers. When the blaze comes, they go to the old quarter, all of them who can, and stand in the aligned doorways facing it, and they do the one thing that in their language is louder than any utterance: they open their hands and hold them still. The herds stop too. Whether the animals learned stillness from the people across a hundred centuries of cohabitation, or the people learned it from the animals, or both from something older, is not a question anyone here would think to ask. The hands rest open. The plain holds its breath it never had. The silence, which was never broken, deepens — and now you know what is deepening it.

One more thing, and then you may go on thinking of the city as empty if you like.

They heard you half a day ago — your boots on the descent, the bellows of your lungs, the noise you make without meaning to, which is most of the noise you make. They have a sign for what you are. It is older than your arrival, which should tell you something. Somewhere out on the pale plain right now, two gray-eyed figures are standing closer together than the stones around them, and one is describing you to the other, hands moving almost too fast to see, in a language with a living word for *stranger* and a kept, polished, waiting word for *bird*.
