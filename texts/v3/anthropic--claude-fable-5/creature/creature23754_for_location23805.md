# The Citizens

You will want a name for them, and the place will not give you one, so let us call them what they are: the sleepers. Everything else that lives in the chamber lives *around* them, in the margins of their long project, the way gulls and grasses live around a harbor town. But begin with the citizen itself, because the citizen is the city, and there is no understanding the one without kneeling down before the other.

---

**The body.** Take one — you can't, the mortar would have to be broken, but imagine taking one — and it would sit in your palm weighing almost nothing: a domed shell the size of a thumbnail, ivory shading to honey where the mineral water has begun its work, and inside the shell an animal like a drop of warm tallow given intention. It has a foot, broad and muscular for its size, capable of gripping wet vertical stone for hours. It has a mantle fringed with fine sensory filaments, pale and translucent, which in the moving individuals ripple constantly, reading the air. It has no eyes. It has never had eyes — there is no vestige, no scar of eyes lost, no pigment-cup abandoned by evolution on the way down. Whatever ancestor first crawled into this dark arrived already blind, or so long ago that even absence has been polished out of the body, the way the chamber's violence was polished out of the stone.

What it has instead is the finest vibratory sense you will ever fail to notice. The whole animal is an ear. It hears through its foot, through its shell, through the mortar it lies in. When a drop falls from the ceiling and lands — *tok* — that sound travels through the floor and up the fused courses of every tower, and ten thousand sleepers register it in their sleep, each one a fraction of a moment after its downstairs neighbor, so that the sound of one drop of water passes upward through the city like a shiver through a body. They hear the isopods grazing. They hear the ring on the basin water. They heard you, the moment your boot touched the chamber floor, and what they made of you — an event vast, slow, warm, and thunderous — is not recoverable. Nothing changed. Nothing hurried. But for an hour after your arrival, if you had put your ear to the masonry, the ticking would have been very slightly faster.

**The sleep.** Here is the thing you must invert to understand them: sleep is not their absence from the city. Sleep is their attendance.

A sealed sleeper is not shut away. Its mantle lies pressed against the silt-mortar, and the mortar lies against the shells of its neighbors, and the neighbors against theirs, so that a tower is not a pile of separate dormancies but a single continuous fabric of touching bodies, conducting warmth, conducting vibration, conducting — something. The ticking you hear in the masonry is not random. Listen long enough, in that blood-heat, with your cheek against a tower that is faintly warmer than the air, and you begin to catch shapes in it: a rasp answered by a rasp three courses up, a cluster of small adjustments propagating around a buttress and going quiet, a slow call-and-return between the base and the crown that takes twenty minutes to complete one exchange. Whether this is conversation, or dream, or merely the mechanical settling of ten thousand bodies keeping mutual balance, the behavior is opaque. But it never stops, and it is organized, and the organization is the tower's, not any individual's. A sealed sleeper participates in something a waking one cannot. That may be the whole reason they seal.

The waking one, by contrast — the pale citizen you can watch descending a tower face over the better part of an hour — is the loneliest animal in the chamber. It has left the fabric. Its niche gapes behind it like a missing tooth, and until it seals again it is a private creature on a private errand, out of contact, deaf to the deep conversation, exposed to the enormous silence of the open plaza. Watch its filaments while it crosses that emptiness. They stream backward and quiver. It may be the closest thing here to fear.

**The errand.** They wake, as far as the mineralized evidence allows anyone to guess, perhaps a handful of times in a life — and a life may run to centuries. Each waking has the same shape. Descent. The crossing. The basin.

At the basin's low wall the citizen climbs, tips, and lowers itself into the warm black water, and there it stays for an hour or three, doing several things at once, none of them fully legible. It drinks — measurably; the animal that emerges is heavier, swollen with mineral-saturated water, and this is its provisioning, the dissolved lime from which it will sweat out its next batch of mortar. The city is built from the basin. Every brick was cemented with water carried up from that black mirror in the body of the mason, one small pilgrim-load at a time. It bathes, scouring its shell against the basin floor, shedding the dulled film of its last long sleep. And it does a third thing, motionless, foot spread on the submerged stone, filaments fully extended into the water, for a long interval that serves no evident bodily purpose. Communion is the wrong word only because no better one exists. The basin water touches the foundations of every tower. It may be that a citizen standing in the basin can hear the entire city at once — the whole slow fabric, all ten thousand tickings, gathered into one water — and that this is what it came down for, and that the drinking is incidental.

**The choosing.** Then the fourth act, the one that takes longest and looks most like deliberation, because it is deliberation: the citizen selects where it will spend its next century.

It does not go home. The gaping niche it left will be filled, eventually, by some other returning pilgrim, or by a young one climbing for the first time, or by mortar and time. Instead it wanders — there is no other word — from tower to tower, up one face and partway down another, pausing at candidate positions, pressing its foot flat against the coursework, tasting. It is reading, through the shells of the sealed, the composition of the neighborhood: the warmth rising through this buttress, the mineral flavor of that mortar, the particular texture of ticking in this terrace as against that one. Some prefer the crowded courses near the basin, the harbor-front, where the fabric is dense and the conversation close. Some climb to the extreme top of the tallest tower and seal themselves at the very lip of the unfinished crown, in the thinnest, loneliest, newest masonry, adding a finger's-width of height to the commonwealth and taking, in exchange, a century of wind — if the immeasurably slow convection of that chamber can be called wind. It is the one aesthetic act of their existence, the single choice, and they spend hours on it, and no two make it the same way. Then the sealing: the slow sweating-out of mortar, the drawing-in of the foot, the last fine adjustment of the shell's angle — always, note, with the dome's grain aligned to its neighbors', always — and the citizen goes under, and is a brick again, and is home.

**The young.** They come from the basin, which explains something about the pilgrimage. In the warm water, at intervals no witness has ever spanned, the bathing citizens leave their spawn: a scatter of larvae like grains of cloudy glass, swimming, briefly, in the only free water the city owns. Most are eaten — see below. The survivors crawl out over the basin wall as animals almost too small to see, and then comes the strangest interval of the species' existence: the free years. For a decade or three the young live loose in the streets. They graze the mineral film alongside the isopods. They shelter in the causeways' shadows. They are the only inhabitants of the city who are simply *awake* — awake in the ordinary animal way, day upon day, hungry and moving — and they are visibly restless in a room where nothing else is, threading the plazas, climbing partway up towers and coming down again, pressing their still-soft shells against the masonry as if listening for the thing they cannot yet join. Youth, here, is the exile. Then one day a young one climbs and does not come down, and chooses badly or well, and seals itself for the first time, and the restlessness is over forever.

**The foundation.** And at the bottom of every tower, in the fused honey-colored courses where the mortar and the shells and the dripping centuries have run together into single translucent stone, are the ones who never woke again. Do not call them dead too quickly. Deep in the mineralized base, fainter and slower than anywhere else, the ticking persists — one small dry adjustment where the upper courses make hundreds — and it is impossible to say whether these are the last mechanical settlings of empty shells, or the metabolism of citizens slowed past all recognizable life, sealed beyond any power of unsealing, alive at one tick a day, becoming stone from the inside out and possibly, in whatever sense applies, content. The city does not bury its dead. It stands on them. Every citizen who seals itself low enough, long enough, is promoted to foundation, and the tower it holds up is full of its descendants, and whether this is a graveyard or a government is not a question the sleepers would recognize as having two answers.

---

**The attendants.** The isopods — a dozen of them, perhaps, white as the sleepers and blind as the sleepers, each the length of your thumb-joint — are the city's civil service, though they don't know it. Each patrols a fixed territory in a fixed circuit, hours long, endlessly repeated: they are the only creatures in the chamber with a *schedule*. They graze the mineral film that would otherwise fur and choke the masonry; they keep the streets clean without any concept of streets. And when a crossing citizen dies in the open — it happens; the plaza is a desert, and some pilgrims are very old — the nearest isopod finds it within the day and attends to it with a thoroughness that leaves, after a week, only a clean empty shell lying in the plaza. The shells are not wasted. Watch long enough and you may see a young one drag such a shell to a tower's base and wedge it into a gap in the lowest courses. Even the dead of the streets get built in, eventually. Nothing here is discarded. There is nowhere to discard anything to.

**The one in the water.** It has been in the basin longer than the basin's wall has been mineralized, which is to say longer than several dynasties of towers. It is eel-thin, eyeless like everything else, and it has never in any witnessed moment shown more of itself than the single slow ring it makes on the surface once or twice an hour. It eats the larvae — most of them — and in doing so it sets the tempo of the entire civilization: the city grows at a finger's width a generation because the one in the water permits exactly that many young to reach the wall. Call it predator, call it regulator, call it the appetite the city has learned to build around. It is the only inhabitant of the chamber that is truly alone, the only one that touches no other body, and the pilgrims descend into its water anyway, and stand in it, and are never harmed. Perhaps it is sated. Perhaps grown citizens taste of stone. Perhaps there is an arrangement so old that neither party is capable of remembering it as an arrangement, only of keeping it.

**The congregation above.** The hanging threads on the ceiling — translucent, patient, swaying in air that does not move — hang precisely above the tallest tower, and that is not chance. The tower is the city's chimney; the warmth of ten thousand slow bodies rises off its crown in a column too gentle to feel on your skin, and the threads live in that column, drinking the vapor, swaying in the city's breath. They are neither animal nor fungus in any way that would survive close inquiry, and they are the source of a portion of the ceiling's drops: at long intervals a thread lets fall one bead of gathered water — *tok* — onto the tower top below, returning to the city what the city exhaled. Whether this is excretion or gift or simply gravity, the behavior is opaque. But note where the drops land, and note that the tallest tower's crown mortar is the finest in the city.

**The moss.** One square meter, in the footprint of the light shaft: possibly a single individual, possibly older than the tallest tower. It is the only living thing in the chamber that remembers what light is for. Nothing eats it. Nothing tends it. It is the city's entire countryside, and it persists the way everything here persists — by wanting nothing the room doesn't already provide.

---

That warm exhalation you felt at the crack, standing on the dead mudflat — you know now whose it was. Ten thousand sleepers, breathing in courses, one brick at a time. They will not know you were here. But the water you dripped from your own dewed skin onto the chamber floor has, by now, found its way down through the sediment toward the basin, carrying its trace of you, and in a century or so some pilgrim, standing foot-spread in the black mirror with its filaments extended, will taste something faint and foreign and warm-blooded in the water, and will make of it whatever such citizens make of rumors — and will climb, and choose its place, and seal you into the wall with everything else the city has ever heard.
