Whoever first told you about the Caldera Sea will have said *people live in it*, and then hesitated, the way you hesitate before describing a relative you have never met. The lowlanders, in their wet valleys under the haze, call them **the cousins above the cloud** — and they are cousins, literally, descended from lowland families who climbed up within the last dozen generations and never came back down. Nobody below remembers exactly why the first ones went. The cousins themselves tell it differently in every dome: some say their ancestors were quarriers following the new soft stone, some say refugees from a war the valleys have since forgotten, some say simply that there was a cold year, and the mountain was warm. All the versions end the same way. *They felt the heat through the water, and they stayed.*

They have no name for themselves. When pressed, they say they are **tenants**. The mountain is the landlord, the sea is the house, and the rent — they will tell you this with a perfectly straight face — is the wall.

---

**The body of a tenant**

They are human. That should be said first, because at the rim, in air, in daylight, you might wonder. Twelve generations of warm-water living have not made them another kind of thing, but they have begun, gently, to edit them — the way the mountain has not yet been edited, its people have.

They are smooth-skinned and rounded, carrying a soft even layer of flesh that the warm sea makes cheap to keep; sharp-boned people are considered unlucky, possibly ill. Their skin has a faint greenish pallor, light-starved and tea-stained, and it is their finest instrument. A tenant *sees with skin*. The dive into the brown dusk below thirty feet is a dive into a geography of temperature — the rising columns off the vents, the cool threads sinking from the rim, the body-warmth of a neighbor working two terraces over — and their word for *knowing a place* is the same as their word for *feeling its warmth*. To call someone a stranger, you say their warmth is unread.

Their eyes are wide-pupiled and dark-adapted, very good in the olive gloom, nearly useless at the bright rim; tenants who climb up to the basalt edge squint and weep and wear woven blade-fiber visors slit like snow goggles. Their hearts are famous. A working elder's heart, three minutes into a dive, beats slower than a sleeping child's, and their breath economy is the pride of the culture the way horsemanship or seamanship is the pride of others. Children compete at it. Grandmothers humiliate them. Breath is counted not in time but in heartbeats, and the polite answer to *how long can you stay down* is not a number but a shrug: *long enough to finish the course I'm laying.*

They are born in air, in the breath-domes, into the smell of warm stone and shell-mortar — and they learn water before they learn walking. An infant is carried down through the hanging forests at one month old, eyes open, held against the chest where it can feel the carrier's slow heart, and brought back up. This is not a baptism. It is an introduction. The child is being shown to the house.

---

**How they speak**

In the domes, in air, they speak a softened descendant of the lowland tongue — slow, low, full of long pauses that drive their valley cousins to distraction. In the water, they speak two other ways.

The first is **knock-speech**: a percussive language tapped out on stone with the knuckle, the palm, the butt of a maul. Young stone is glassy and rings; the city is one connected instrument; a phrase knocked on a terrace wall can be read by the hand of anyone touching masonry within a hundred yards. The tenants say, again with a straight face, that the mountain taught them to talk — that knock-speech began as imitation of the ticking and groaning of the settling rock, and that the oldest words in it are the mountain's own. The word for *patience* in knock-speech is a single slow strike followed by nothing, held until the listener gives up waiting. That is the whole word. The silence is part of the spelling.

The second is barely a language at all: single exhaled words, shaped in a bubble and released, rising past the listener's face. These are spent breath and therefore expensive, and therefore honest. A tenant who tells you something in spent breath is telling you the truth, or at least paying full price for the lie. Marriage vows are exhaled. So are accusations.

---

**What a life looks like**

A tenant's life is organized vertically, and it runs downhill.

The young live high — in the newest terraces near the bright warm surface, where the swimming is long and the breath-runs are easy, where the basket-relays start and the pruning of the street-corridors is done. Adults in their strength live in the middle city, where the real masonry happens. And the old move *down*. As the body slows and the climbs to air grow expensive, an elder takes a chamber one terrace lower, then another, settling into the deepening warmth, tended by visitors, doing the close patient work that needs no breath-runs — mortar-mixing, fiber-weaving, the carving of the glowing posts. Aging, in the Caldera Sea, is a descent. It is not spoken of sadly. The deep is warm, and the warmth reads as company.

When a tenant dies, the body is laid in their lowest chamber with their tools, and the doorway is closed with one final course of masonry — laid by the grandchildren, who are thereby taught to lay a course. Then nothing else is done. Nothing else needs to be. The mountain rises a hand's width a year, the city climbs to follow, and the sealed chamber is simply *left behind* — carried down, relatively speaking, into the hot dark with all the other abandoned foundations. The tenants do not say *we bury our dead*. They say: **we are lifted from them.** The drowned floor of the caldera's center, that sunken architecture of generations, is the necropolis, and no one has built it; the mountain assembles it by ascending.

They do not visit it. The deep center belongs to someone else.

---

**The other tenants**

The translucent grazers — dog-sized, slow, lit from within by the dim green of whatever they have eaten — are called, in air-speech, **the readers**. They drift through the gardens and along the city walls mouthing the films and filaments off the stone, and in doing so they keep the carved knock-posts and boundary marks legible, so the tenants regard them as something between livestock, librarians, and weather. They are never harmed; harming one is the worst thing a person can do, worse than murder in the telling if not in the law, because a reader's judgment is trusted over a mason's. Where the readers linger and graze, the wall is sound. Where they drift past without pausing, where they will not put their soft mouths — the masons go back, that day, and check the mortar. A reader avoiding a wall has emptied whole chambers before. It has always, the tenants say, been right.

When a reader grows old it stops rising to the gardens. It drifts down, dimming, past the lowest inhabited terrace, into the abandoned city, and does not come back. The tenants find this proper. Everyone goes down to the old foundations eventually. The readers just go under their own power.

And below the readers, below the sealed chambers, in the hot dark over the vent fields where the water shimmers like air over a fire, lives the one the tenants call the **Under-Mason** — though never loudly, and never in spent breath. No tenant living has seen it. They know the long smooth furrows it leaves in the deep sediment, furrows that run along the lines of the drowned old walls, following the architecture, tracing it. They know the note — once, twice a year, that single resonant bass tone that stands every blade in every forest briefly on end and passes through the chest like a verdict. The lowlanders, when they hear this story, assume a god. The tenants are mildly offended by the suggestion. The Under-Mason is not worshipped. It is *deferred to*, professionally, the way you defer to the senior colleague who was on the site before you arrived. They believe it is sounding the foundations — striking the deep city the way a mason strikes a course to hear if it is true — and that the note is its report. They cannot read the report. They pause their work when it comes, out of courtesy, the whole city going still in the green dusk, hundreds of hands flat against the ringing stone, listening.

Then the note ends, and the blades go slack, and they pick up their mauls. Whatever the Under-Mason found in the foundations, the answer above is the same. *Until then, the wall.*

---

**What they want**

Ask a tenant what they do and they will not understand the question. Ask instead what they are **finishing** — that is the question they ask each other, the question that means *who are you*. A maul comes down through five generations with five generations of grips worn into the haft, and the holder is expected to know which course of which wall the great-great-grandmother was laying when she set the tool down. The unit of ambition is not the lifetime. A tenant who finishes anything significant within their own life is suspected of having chosen too small a thing.

Every few years a relay of the young goes down the mountain through the hours of grey, blinking and visored, carrying salt, luminous paste, blades of black glass, and coils of woven fiber stronger than any lowland rope. They come back with timber, with fired wood for wedges, and usually with one or two lowlanders — new spouses, new blood, valley cousins who felt the heat of the story through the telling and could not stay below. The lowlanders find their new family unnerving at first: the slowness, the long pauses, the way they knock softly on the table when moved beyond speech. Then a wet season passes, and another, and the newcomer is found one afternoon holding a palm flat against warm stone, waiting through a silence, learning to hear the spelling of patience.

They settle accordingly. Everyone here does. The sea is rising through the sky a hand's width a year, carrying the whole city upward on the back of newborn rock, toward an altitude where the water must someday boil or freeze, and the tenants know this, and have decided it is not their course to lay. They are the people of an ascending ocean, lifted daily from their dead, neighbors to something that sounds their foundations from below — and what they are, finally, what lives in the Caldera Sea, is this: a people who have looked at a house that cannot last forever, on a mountain that has not decided how high it means to go, and concluded that the correct response is masonry, done properly, at the speed of grandmothers.
