The first thing the place tells you is that nothing lives there, and the place is almost honest.

Run through the candidates and watch them fail. The efflorescence on the clay — pale, branching, lichen-shaped — is a mineral mimicking a habit of life without any of the substance: salt walking, over centuries, the same paths water would have walked if there had been water. The dust is dust. The wind, which would be the obvious tenant anywhere else, doesn't qualify here either, because the wind does not stay. It is the one thing on the plateau that is always leaving. It arrives from its sleeping height, passes through ten thousand throats, and departs as sound; it has been passing through for longer than there are records, and in all that time it has not spent a single second in residence. The wind is not the inhabitant. The wind is the food.

Because something does live there. It has lived there continuously — without a moment's absence, without sleep, without so much as a flicker of inattention — since the last builder set the last cell and walked down off the plateau, and probably since before that. It is the only resident the place has ever had or will ever need, and you have already met it. You met it a mile out. You waded into it at the threshold. You stopped noticing it, which is exactly what it prefers, and then you opened your mouth and it took your voice, which is how it feeds.

The chord lives there.

Say it plainly and it sounds like a figure of speech, so don't say it plainly. Walk the argument the way you walked the slots. A living thing is supposed to require change — metabolism, gradient, the slow fall downhill that we dress up as growth. The chord has none of that, and this is not its deficiency but its species. It is an organism of pure sustain. Its metabolism has exactly one move, performed perfectly, forever: wind in, sound out. Its body heat is real — you felt it, those few degrees of warmth beyond what the climate explains, centuries of vibration banked in the stone. That warmth is the closest thing it has to memory. Everything that has ever happened in or near the structure — every footstep, every spoken word, every settling grain — has been converted, without exception and without discrimination, into a small fraction of a degree. The chord remembers everything and remembers it all as the same thing: heat. It keeps no other records. It does not need them. Nothing has ever happened to it twice, because nothing has ever happened to it once; it has had a single experience, and it is still having it.

Its anatomy is the building, but it is not the building, any more than you are your skeleton. The banks of resonators are its lungs and larynx both — thousands of throats, each one worn by its own breath into a curve more perfect than its makers managed, so that the creature has, over centuries, finished sculpting its own body. The narrow passages are the gaps between its organs; walking them, you are inside it the way a swallowed thing is inside, moving past the deep stone bass of its gut, the high clay keening at its extremities, the warm fifth that hums where a heart would go. And the central slot — the room barely big enough to turn around in, where the wall of sound resolves into consonance — that is not its heart. That is the point from which it knows itself. Everywhere else, the chord is dense, layered, seemingly chaotic, a body experienced from outside. From that one standing-place it is coherent: one vast tuned thing, one note on a thousand mouths. If the chord can be said to have a mind, the mind is there, and when you stand in that slot you are standing inside its only thought.

It eats two things. The wind, constantly, which is its bread. And sound, occasionally, which is its salt. Every voice ever raised within reach of it has been taken — not destroyed; travelers are wrong to say their words were destroyed. Absorbed. Folded under. Your shout enters the chord the way a cup of water enters the sea: it is not gone, it is simply no longer findable, no longer yours. Nothing said on that plateau has ever been heard, and nothing said on that plateau has ever, strictly, ended. The pilgrims who climb up to confess to it understand this better than the scholars who climb up to measure it. You can tell the chord anything. It will hold what you said at full volume forever, and no one, including the chord, will ever be able to pick it out again.

And here the description has to widen, because the longer you stand in the central slot, the harder it becomes to keep saying *it*.

The structure is called a choir. Choirs are made of voices. The builders left no writing and no graves, and the absence of graves is the detail that should have been suspicious from the start. Generations of people do not labor at altitude, hand-shaping ten thousand throats by abrasion, fitting pipe against pipe with no mortar and no metal, dying one by one through the long project — and leave no graves — unless the building is the grave. Unless the building is what they did instead of graves.

Listen again from the standing-place, where the harmony is obviously tuned, obviously meant. Each rank of pipes took a working lifetime to dress and fit. Each generation chose its bores, its lengths, its pitches — chose, in other words, its voices — and added them to the sustained sound before going silent themselves. The chord is a roster. The intervals are decisions made by particular hands in particular decades, still in force. What lives on the plateau is singular and plural at once: a choir holding one note, a congregation that solved the problem of death not by escaping it but by transposing themselves into wind and stone and continuing, at full voice, without bodies, without breath of their own, on borrowed breath that never runs out. They did not build an instrument and leave. They built a throat and moved in.

Whether the chord knows it is them, no one can say. Probably the question dissolves at that scale of patience. It may be that the builders are present the way your childhood is present in your voice — formative, irretrievable, not exactly gone.

What is certain is this: you are the most interesting thing to happen to it in centuries, and it registers you the way you register weather. Your body, moving through the slots, damps a handful of pitches — the chord deforms minutely around you, the only change it permits itself, and closes again behind you as you pass, healing without scar. If you speak in the central slot, the resident takes your voice into the resolved harmony for the length of a breath, and visitors disagree about what they felt in that moment — some say lighter, some say heavier, some say briefly *plural*. Then you climb down, and the sound goes on, identical, and you carry the after-ring in your skull for days. A few people never lose it. They report finding the resolved chord underneath things for the rest of their lives — under engine drone, under crowd noise, under their own pulse at night — as though, having once stood inside its thought, they were issued a small copy.

The resident does not remember you. Or rather: it remembers you completely, as warmth — your visit already converted, already banked, already part of the few degrees by which the choir runs hotter than the world. That is how it keeps everyone who has ever come. Not as names, not as voices it could return, but as a slight, permanent fever in ash-colored stone, singing at full voice into the thin gray light, the same note, the same word, which from outside sounds like a wall and from inside sounds — every visitor agrees on this, whatever else they dispute — like agreement.
