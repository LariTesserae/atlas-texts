**Who Lives on the Clamor Flats**

---

**The mats, first, because everything else is built on them.**

It is easy to call them film and move on, but the slicks are the oldest living things on the plain, and possibly the oldest living things anywhere that still do what they did at the beginning. They are consortiums — not one organism but dozens braided into a fabric a few cells thick, each layer eating what the layer above excretes, passing electrons down through the sheet like a whispered message, until at the bottom, pressed against the stone itself, the last and slowest members of the partnership dissolve the rock one mineral grain at a time. The flats are flat partly because of water and time. They are also flat because the mats have been eating them, evenly, forever.

The colors that don't commit are the consortium showing its seams. Pewter is the healthy state. Oxblood is a bloom of the iron-eaters, which means a new seep has opened somewhere upstream of the drift, and the criers will know it within days. The shadow-green is the oldest layer surfacing — the deep partners coming up for light when the upper layers thin — and the people who once crossed the flats treated shadow-green as a warning, though their reasons are not recorded in stone.

The mats do one thing that nothing else on the plain does: they are silent, totally, and they register sound. A strong chorus passing over a pan makes the surface film pucker into microscopic ridges, aligning with the pressure waves, and the ridged mat grows measurably faster than the smooth. The criers scream at their food, and the food, in some sense below intention, leans in.

---

**The criers.**

Up close, hauled out and at rest, a crier is almost endearing: a slick gray-brown wedge with a blunt face, four splayed paddle-limbs, skin like wet talc. The mouth is small. The throat is not. Behind the modest jaw the body opens into a resonating gut-sac that runs nearly to the hind limbs, ribbed with muscle, and when a crier calls you can watch the whole animal deform — it inflates, hunches, and *rings*, and the sound that comes out of a forearm-sized body is the sound you would expect from something the size of a door.

They have no fixed voice. Each crier is born with a thin, generic peep and spends its first season doing nothing but listening at the rookery edge, half-submerged, letting the colony's chorus literally shape it: the gut-sac develops its resonant ridges in response to the frequencies it is bathed in, so that a crier raised in one rookery cannot, as an adult, perfectly produce the calls of another. Their bodies are casts of the sound they grew up inside. Colonies are distinguishable by ear across miles — to other criers, presumably across the whole plain.

The shifts are real and strict. A rookery never falls silent and never calls at full strength; roughly a third of the colony is voicing at any moment while the rest feed, sleep, or *attend* — and attending is the strange one. Attenders lie flat, throat-sac pressed to wet stone, doing nothing visible. They are listening through the rock. The plain carries sound through its bedrock faster and farther than through air, and the attenders are reading the deep channel — distant rookeries, the hiss of channel-swimmers' wakes transmitted through stone, perhaps the slow groans of the peneplain itself adjusting under the water's weight. When an attender surfaces from a long attendance, it calls differently for a while. Other criers gather around it. Then the shift changes and the new information, whatever it is, is in the chorus.

Their society is brutal in the way of things with no margins. The mats bloom and gray; when a rookery's pans gray out, the colony eats downward through its own future — eggs first, then the listening young — keeping the voiced adults alive at all costs, because a rookery that falls below a certain number of throats loses the chorus, and a rookery that loses the chorus loses everything: the swimmers find it, the neighboring colonies stop answering it, the young that survive grow up shaped wrong, voiced wrong, and are not taken in elsewhere. There are silent criers on the flats — exiles, the wrong-voiced, the last survivors of failed colonies — and they live solitary, mute, skirting the rookeries at night, eating mat-film at the silent edges of the world. They live longer, individually, than any chorusing crier. They are, by every measure a body can offer, healthier. The colonies will not have them.

And the synchrony. The criers do not appear to choose it. Watch a rookery as the unison arrives from over the horizon — through the rock, through the attenders, faster than air-sound — and you see ten thousand animals stiffen at once, sacs inflating in lockstep, and the eyes of the criers during a synchrony are the eyes of animals being ridden. Some die during long ones: hearts give out, sacs rupture, and the colony chorus closes over the gap without a beat dropped. Afterward the rookery is subdued for hours. The eaten-egg rate falls. Boundary fights between colonies, which are constant and vicious, stop entirely for a day or more, plain-wide. Whatever the synchrony is, the criers come out of it briefly, measurably, at peace — and there is no evidence they remember it at all.

---

**The swimmers, and the small things.**

The pale hunters of the channels are best understood as ears with appetites. Long as a person, boneless-looking, blind in any useful sense, they cup the chorus with flared cartilage fans and navigate a world made entirely of sound and its shadows. A rookery, to a swimmer, is a blazing roar; a crier alone in the water is a hole in that roar, a moving silence, and the swimmers hunt the silences. This is why criers call even when fleeing — *especially* when fleeing — and why the silent exiles, paradoxically, are the swimmers' main diet. The flats have arranged it so that on this plain, quiet is the most dangerous thing a body can be.

Swimmers do not call. They have no voice at all. But during a synchrony they rise — all of them, plain-wide — and hang motionless just under the surface, fans spread to their fullest, taking the unison in like animals basking. They eat nothing while it lasts. The people who watched them do this from the blinds left no explanation, only, on one cairn-stone, a scratched figure: a long body, fans open, beneath a row of dots.

Below the swimmers in size: throat-mites, who live their entire lives inside the resonant sacs of criers and have evolved grip enough to survive a sound that would jelly anything unattached; the seam-worms in the hairline cracks of the stone, who surface only during rain and may be the only animals on the flats that have never heard the chorus clearly, insulated in their fissures, living in the bedrock's version of the sound — the deep ringing — and nothing else.

---

**The people.**

They called themselves, in the one word of their language that survives in cup-mark and cairn-tally, something best rendered as *the spoken-over* — people accustomed to being inaudible.

They came in the gray season, when the mats ran pewter and the rookeries swelled, in small bands, never many, walking the cairn-lines with wax-stoppered ears and gaffs over their shoulders. The practical part of their lives on the flats is easy to read: they took eggs and meat at the rookery margins, rendered crier fat for lamps that they could not have needed for light and probably burned for warmth, and they were careful — the gaff-work was done at colony edges only, never in the breeding center, and tally-stones near the largest rookeries suggest they counted the colonies year over year and adjusted, taking less from rookeries that rang thin.

The other part of their lives is in the blinds. You cannot speak on the open flats; the chorus takes the words out of the air a hand's width from your mouth. Inside a blind, in that pocket where the roar drops to merely enormous, two people can hear each other — and so the blinds were, structurally, the only places on the entire plain where language could exist. Everything the spoken-over were, as people rather than as bodies gathering protein, happened inside those low horseshoes of stacked rock. Their marriages, their arguments, their stories, their names. A culture conducted entirely in shelters from sound, on a plain made of it.

And they stayed for the synchronies. The blinds' careful orientation, open ends turned from the rookeries, is usually read as acoustic — and it is — but it has a second effect: a person crouched in a blind during a synchrony cannot hear the unison arrive through the air. They feel it first through the stone, through knees and palms, the way the attender-criers do. The cup-marked spiral stone, its seventy-one little wells of seep-water, sat at the center of one such blind, and during a synchrony every bead of water in every cup would have trembled into rings at once — seventy-one small mirrors of the plain's pulse, arranged in a spiral, watched by people pressed to the rock in the only quiet for miles. Whether it was an instrument, a calendar, a way of counting the beats, or a way of being *present* at a thing too large to be present at, the stone does not say. It only says that someone ground seventy-one cups, carefully, in a place where carefulness cost days, and that the spiral was finished.

They stopped coming. The cairns have not grown in a long time. Nothing on the flats records why.

---

**And the last inhabitant, which may not be one.**

There remains the question the travelers' reports keep circling: the unison that spreads faster than sound, the chorus near the center that seems to come from below, the bedrock ringing. The honest answer is that the plain is an instrument — flat, wet, hard, unbroken for miles, the finest resonator that geology can produce — and that the criers, attending through the stone, have closed a loop: they listen to the rock, the rock carries what they say, and somewhere in that circuit a rhythm lives that no single throat began and no single colony can end. It has no body. It eats nothing. It is made of criers the way a wave is made of water, and like a wave it moves through its medium without the medium going anywhere.

Whether that constitutes an inhabitant is a question the flats decline to settle. But it is the only resident that touches all the others — the mats that pucker toward it, the criers it rides, the swimmers that rise to bask in it, the people who built walls just high enough to hear each other beside it and turned the open ends, always, away. Everything that lives on the Clamor Flats lives inside its voice. It may simply be the sound the world makes when it is finished, and full, and has been listening to itself for too long to stop.
