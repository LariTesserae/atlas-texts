Begin at the bottom, because everything here does.

The films are the oldest living things in the Hollow, and the least alive by any measure a visitor would bring. They are sheets of chemistry that learned to repeat themselves — lacquers of cells thinner than a fingernail, spread across the warm basalt in continuous fields that run for hundreds of kilometers without a break. They eat the disagreement between deep stone and trapped air: sulfur on one side, heat on the other, and in the narrow seam between, a living margin. They have no fronts and no backs, no individuals, no edges except where the rock itself breaks. A film does not die so much as thin out. It does not grow so much as continue. If the Hollow has a flesh, this is it, and everything else in the bubble is something the flesh supports — or something the flesh, over enough time, dreamed up to keep itself company.

On the films move the grazers, and the grazers are best understood as furniture that decided to participate. The largest are three meters across and shaped like worn flagstones, domed slightly, skirted at the edge with a fringe of gripping feet so numerous and so small that the animal seems to pour rather than walk. The underside is the whole of the creature's purpose: a broad rasping plate, ridged like a file, that strips the film from the rock in a band exactly as wide as the body. They are never still and never fast. A grazer crosses a gallery the way a year crosses a calendar. Behind each one runs a clean stripe of bare glittering stone, and ahead of each one the film grows back at precisely the speed of the animal's hunger, so that a grazer is, in effect, chasing its own meal in a circle the size of a parish, forever, and catching it, forever. They navigate by taste — the film carries chemical gradients the way surface ground carries scent — and they remember their territories in their tongues. Their digestion is audible. In the deep quiet you can stand in a gallery and hear, all around you, the long mineral churning of stomachs working on stone-jelly, a sound like distant gravel being turned over very slowly, and this is the closest thing the Hollow has to birdsong.

Above the grazers, the waiters. The chandelier-things hang from ceiling spurs and crystal columns, anchored by a single gripping root, their bodies a loose bell of tissue from which dangle filaments — dozens, hundreds, in the great old ones thousands — each filament finer than hair and sticky along its whole length. In air that never moves, filter-feeding is not a strategy; it is a vow. What drifts to them is almost nothing: a shed cell, a spore, a mote of film-dust dislodged by a grazer's rasp, descending through the dead air over hours. The chandeliers have answered this scarcity by approaching stillness from the inside. Their metabolisms burn so low that a touch reveals them as barely warmer than the rock — and in the Hollow, where everything is the same temperature, *barely warmer* is a signature, the faintest of confessions. They may be the oldest individuals in the bubble. Some of the great ones, the ones whose filaments have fused into curtains, are thought by the builders to have hung in their places since the world was soft. The only motion they ever make is the flinch: a filament touched draws upward with terrible slow purpose, carrying whatever it caught to the bell. To be brushed by a chandelier in the dark and feel it begin, very slowly, to keep you, is the nearest thing the Hollow offers to a nightmare.

The chandeliers cluster thickest over the smolder-patches — the places where the floor glows its sullen coal-red — because the smolders are the one exception to the stillness. Over a patch of thin hot crust, the air does the only thing air ever does here: it rises, a column of warmth climbing at the pace of smoke in a sealed room, lifting drift up to the waiting filaments. The builders have a click-phrase for the smolders that translates, roughly, as *where the world breathes*. It is the only place the phrase would mean anything.

And between and around and through all of this run the quick things, the Hollow's single concession to speed. They are small — palm-sized, most of them, soft-bodied, legged like brushes — and they are the reason the stillness of the air matters more than a visitor would guess. In air that never moves, every motion leaves a wake, and the wake *persists*. A grazer's passage stands in the atmosphere behind it for hours, a corridor of disturbed pressure as legible as a footprint. The quick things read these. They are blind in every conventional sense, but their bodies are covered in pressure-hairs so fine that they perceive the recent past of the air as a landscape: here something large went by, there something small struggled, here the air still carries the collapsing ghost of a flinch. They hunt the wakes. They scavenge the dead, harry the small, thread under the skirts of grazers to steal from the rasp-line. They are the Hollow's nervous system, its gossip, its weather — the only inhabitants for whom anything is ever *urgent* — and the larger fauna tolerate them the way a sleeper tolerates a dream.

Then there are the builders.

They are seldom encountered, and the reason is discipline. In a world that perceives by sound and pressure and warmth, to hide is not to crouch behind something; it is to *quiet the body*. The builders have made this their central art. They can slow the heart until it taps once in the time a grazer takes a step. They can still the gut, hush the breath, let the surface of the skin cool toward the temperature of everything. They cultivate the rock-films on their own backs and shoulders — deliberately, tending them like gardens — so that to every sense the Hollow possesses, a motionless builder reads as floor. They are not hiding from predators; nothing here hunts them. They are quiet because quiet is what their world is made of, and they have decided to be made of the same thing.

In form they are low and broad, built for ground that is everywhere a blade. They move on four limbs and rise onto two to work. The hands — and they are hands, six-fingered, dry-palmed, callused to the hardness of horn — are where all their wealth is kept. The skin elsewhere is thick, pebbled, the gray of basalt, and a builder's age can be read in the lacquer of film grown into its back, ring on ring, like bark. They have no eyes in any working sense. What they have instead is a single patch of photosensitive skin, high on the brow, an organ that does exactly one thing: it feels the blue flicker. It cannot form images. It cannot find the smolder-glow, which is too dim and too red. It exists for the piezoelectric discharge alone — a sense kept, at real metabolic cost, for an event that a given builder may experience a few hundred times in a long life. They are creatures who maintain an entire sense for the sake of a rumor. This tells you most of what you need to know about them.

They speak in percussion. The dense, dead air of the Hollow carries sound with uncanny fidelity, and the builders' language is struck rather than spoken: blade tapped on blade, knuckle on stone, the glassy vocabulary of obsidian rung at different thicknesses. Each builder carries a speaking-set — a small cache of knapped pieces, tuned by shape — and a conversation between two of them sounds like very slow, very deliberate rain, the only rain the Hollow will ever hear. For intimacy there is a second language: palm pressed to stone, and a low hum sent through the rock itself, body to floor to body, words that travel through the bones of the world and arrive in the sternum. The dead are mourned this way, in hums too low for air.

Their grammar has no future tense. This is not a poverty; it is an accuracy. The language has an *is* of great precision — dozens of forms of the present, graded by duration, by nearness, by whether a thing is present-and-touched or present-and-heard or present-and-merely-known. It has a past tense used mainly for the dead and for the world's making. For what has not happened, it has only a ceremonial mood, used in exactly two contexts: the moment before striking a core of obsidian, and the vigil at a flicker-seam. Both are the same word. It translates, as nearly as anything can, to *the edge that is not yet*.

Because knapping is the one thing they do that the world does not. The Hollow is finished — the rock will not weather, the films will not spread, the census will not change — and into this completed stillness the builders introduce the only genuinely new objects it will ever contain: edges. To strike a blade from a core is to make something that did not exist a heartbeat before, and the act is performed with the gravity of sacrament. Their craft reached perfection generations beyond counting ago — obsidian rewards the first technology so completely that there has never been a second — and so each new blade is identical to the oldest blades, and this is the point. They are not improving. They are *repeating*, the way the films repeat, the way the grazers circle, and the repetition is devotion. A builder's blades pass at death to the one born in their place, for the population keeps its balance the old way: one out, one in, the dead one's water shared drop by ceremonial drop, the dead one's name-click struck once over the body and then never struck again.

The dulled edges of the cairns are their ethics made visible. The world is sharp everywhere; it was born sharp and will stay sharp; a careless hand opens on it like fruit. So wherever the builders pass and pause, they grind. They take the edges off the stones they stack, off the seams beside their paths, off the lips of the crevices where they cache their blades — hours of patient abrasion to make a fist-sized region of the world unable to cut. They will never dull more than the smallest fraction of an infinite sharpness, and they know it, and they do it anyway. Asked why — if one could ask — they would strike the phrase that serves them for *kindness*, which is also their phrase for *a worked surface*: the world, made slightly less itself, where someone might put a hand.

And the shrines. At the seams where the crystal is stressed and the blue flicker runs strongest, they sit. Sometimes one, sometimes several, backs filmed, hearts slowed, palms flat on the stone to feel the strain accumulating in it, brow-patches bared to the dark. The settling of the crust cannot be hurried and cannot be predicted; a seam may flicker twice in a vigil or not once in a year of them. The builders keep their tallies — scratches in the wall-film, one per flicker witnessed — and a builder's standing among its kind is counted in this currency and no other: not blades, not walls, not water, but *instances of light*. The eldest of them carry tallies in the hundreds, and are touched on the brow in greeting.

What the flicker is to them is hard to render in surface terms. It is not illumination — it shows them nothing, falls on nothing, reveals nothing. It is the one perception they have that arrives through nothing: not through air, not through stone, not through touch. Everything else in their lives is *contact*. The flicker alone crosses the gap. Their word for it is the same as their word for the ceremonial mood, the edge that is not yet — because the flicker is the only thing in the Hollow that is always about to happen, the world's single unkept appointment, and to sit at a seam is to live, briefly, in the tense their language otherwise refuses.

A visitor who waited long enough — and waiting is the local fluency — might at last make out a builder at its vigil: a low gray shape indistinguishable from the floor, heart tapping its slow once, palm to the crystal, feeling the stress of a continent gathering in a seam thinner than a wrist. The strain peaks. The discharge runs — a pale blue pulse, gone before the eye is sure of it — and against the dark the brow-patch fires, and through the whole still body goes the one event.

The builder does not move. There is nothing to do with it. The light is not for anything. It is simply the only thing that happens, and they are the only ones in the Hollow who have arranged their entire lives so as to be present when it does — the held breath's one held thought, kept, and kept, and kept.
