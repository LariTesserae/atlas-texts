Nothing lives on the mountain. Everything that lives here lives on its breath.

That is the first thing to understand about the population of the Foundry Heights: it is not distributed across the terrain, it is distributed across the *temperature*. Draw the isotherms — the invisible contour lines of warmth radiating out from every vent, every steaming pool, every south-tilted slab that holds an hour of heat after the cloud thins — and you have drawn the census, complete. Off those lines, the rock is as bare as the inside of the cloud. On them, everything. The residents of this place do not inhabit the mountain. They inhabit the gradient between the mountain's heat and the sky's cold, a country a few meters wide that happens to be folded and scattered across twenty kilometers of stone.

The oldest residents are the ones you will never see, and they are the only ones who can be said to work the machinery rather than merely attend it. Down in the throats of the fumaroles, in water hot enough to scald and acid enough to dissolve bone, live the thermophiles — bacteria and archaea of lineages older than mountains as a category, older than the continents the mountains stand on. For everything else here the vent chemistry is a hazard to be skirted; for them it is food. They eat sulfur. They eat iron. They eat the gradient itself, harvesting energy from the difference between what the deep rock exhales and what the air will accept. Those process colors painted around the vents — the yellows, the oranges, the rust-streaked whites — are only partly mineral. A great deal of that pigment is alive, or was last week. The rings of precipitate laid down around each vent are part chemistry and part civilization: mats and films of single cells, deposited generation on generation, the only inhabitants who leave a permanent record, and they leave it in stone. They were here before anything else arrived. When the pikas and the finches and the moss have all moved on, they will still be here, in whatever throat is still warm. They are not visitors to the foundry. They are the foundry's own flora, the way a body has a flora — and if you stay here long enough, you begin to suspect that everything else on this list is also that: not residents of the mountain so much as organisms *in* it, living in its breath and its sweat and its wounds.

The lichens come next in seniority, and they are the inspectors. They look like corrosion because they have chosen to do, slowly and deliberately, what corrosion does fast: take a fresh metal-gray face of stone and begin converting it into something else. Every new cliff face the mountain exposes — shiny, unweathered, hours old — is a blank sheet, and the lichens write on it first, a black scrawl here, a rust-colored bloom there, expanding at a millimeter a year. They are the only residents working at anything like the mountain's own tempo. Everything else here lives fast because the place is unstable; the lichens live slow because they *are* the instability, the first agent of the weathering that will eventually round these blade-edged ridges into shoulders. They are demolition disguised as decoration, and they have all the time there is.

The cushion plants are the first architecture. Each one is a single decision held for decades: a fist of tiny leaves packed so tight you cannot push a finger between them, anchored by a root driven into a seam like a bolt, retaining its own dead generations inside itself as insulation. Put a thermometer into the heart of a cushion and you will find a private climate, two or three degrees warmer than the air, humid, still. They are buildings. And like buildings, they acquire tenants — mites, springtails, small spiders that overwinter in the dead-leaf core, beetles that could not survive a single night on the open rock. A cushion the size of a loaf of bread may be a century old and may house more individual animals than you will see at the visible scale in a day of walking. The mountain's largest landlords are plants you could cover with a hat.

The vent gardens are the tenements. Each ring of moss — those improbable green rooms, upholstered and steaming, frost standing in a fence around them — supports a fauna of the almost-invisible: springtails in the thousands, nematodes, mites, tardigrades, generations turning over in weeks. And the flies. The midges whose larvae live in the warm films of water are the place's great proletariat, the broad base on which the visible population stands. Consider the dimensions of a midge's world: it hatches in water kept liquid by the planet's interior, lives its larval life in a film thinner than the glaze of ice on the stone, emerges, dances in the rising column of warm air above the vent — and a meter and a half beyond the moss ring, the frost line stands like a wall, and past it the midge dies in seconds. Their entire species' presence here is conducted inside circles a few meters across. They never know the mountain. They know the breath.

The pools have their own citizens — fairy shrimp and copepods, midge larvae, things that live a whole generation in a season and leave behind eggs that can freeze solid and wait. They are the reason the pools have traffic, the reason birds drop out of the cloud all day to water two degrees above ice. The pools are the canteens of the foundry, and the canteen is always staffed.

Then the propertied class, which has exactly one member: the pika. The pikas live in the mountain's accidents — the freshest rockfalls, the spaces left behind when a cliff lets go — and they are the only residents who *store* anything. All summer, in a landscape with almost nothing to harvest, they harvest: cropping the moss edges, the few sedges in the warm seams, the trimmings of the cushion plants, carrying each load in their mouths to the talus and stacking it under stones to cure. Hay barns in a foundry. They do not hibernate; they meet winter awake, underground in the rubble, living on savings, listening to the rock tick. Their thin whistle — that single deliberate note that is so often the only intentional sound in an hour — is the closest thing the Heights has to speech, and it almost always means the same thing: *something is moving*.

What moves, mostly, is the finches: the small gray-and-white companies that commute between the heated rooms. Their whole day is a circuit of warm islands — this vent garden, that pool, the insect haze over the third moss ring along the fault — flown in hurrying, low, businesslike groups, never lingering on open rock. At night some of them roost in the warm margins of the vents themselves, in crevices where the stone never freezes, sleeping a wingspan from gas that would kill them, in the only heated quarters on the mountain. They are employees of the heat map, and they know it to the meter.

The choughs are something else, and the distinction matters: the choughs are the only inhabitants with leisure. Glossy black, red-billed, red-legged, they nest somewhere far below in the cloud and commute *upward*, against all economic sense, to ride the lift off the steam columns — climbing the thermals, folding, dropping, tumbling past each other, recovering with a flick, doing it again. They are not feeding. They are not displaying, particularly. In an economy with no margin whatsoever, where every other resident's day is a closed loop of heat and calories, the choughs run a surplus and spend it on play. They treat the foundry as a fairground. It is hard not to read them as the place's one editorial comment on itself.

Above the finch commute hangs the entire upper tier of the food web, which at this altitude consists of approximately one animal. A falcon — sometimes a falcon, sometimes for a season none at all — has learned the traffic lines between the vent gardens, and learned to use the steam columns as blinds, attacking out of the white. The pika's whistle is, more often than not, about this bird. In the talus there may also be an ermine, white the year round because up here the year is round and white, threading the pika tunnels like a rumor. That is the whole apex: a tier you can count on one hand, often on one finger.

And twice a year there are the tenants of passage. The geese and the cranes do not live here, but the place receives them: for a few days the ceiling fills with voices, and some of those voices come down. Exhausted birds drop out of the cloud to the warm pools — drinking, standing in the steam, some of them rising again and some of them not. The choughs attend. The ermine attends. There are bones in the vent margins, bleached and acid-etched, wing bones of birds that were hatched two thousand kilometers away and ended as part of the mineral rings. The mountain accepts deliveries.

Is the mountain itself on the census? You can refuse the question for the first day or two. But the hum is in your feet, the vents breathe in rhythm, the cliffs shed skin and expose new flesh the color of cast metal, and the entire roster of inhabitants — from the thermophiles in its throat to the falcon in its exhaust — lives in it the way flora lives in a body: in its breath, on its warmth, in its wounds, fed by its chemistry. After a while you stop asking who lives on the mountain and start cataloguing what lives *in* it, and then the place no longer reads as uninhabited at all. It reads as a single inhabitant, enormously attended.

The last thing to understand about the residents of the Foundry Heights is that none of them live in a place. They live in a process. Every one of them — the sulfur-eaters, the corrosion-colored lichens, the midges in their meter-wide worlds, the haymaking pikas, the commuting finches, the playing choughs, the one falcon — is adapted not to a mountain but to a mountain *being made*: to fresh rockfall, raw faces, open vents, heat leaking from unfinished seams. When the work is done — when the uplift slows, the vents go cold, the ridges round into shoulders and gather soil and grass — a different and far richer crowd will take possession: meadows, marmots, herds, perhaps eventually roads and cairns and wire. And the current population will be gone, not extinct but *moved*, the way a construction crew's camp followers move, on to wherever in the world the rock is still raw and the ground still breathes. They are the inhabitants of the unfinished. They live in the building only while it is being built.
