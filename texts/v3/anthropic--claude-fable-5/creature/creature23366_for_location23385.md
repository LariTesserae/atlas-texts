**The Residents of the Hollow Borough**

Begin with the easy census, because the borough offers it freely: the animals live there. That answer is true, and it is the first layer of a deeper one, so take it slowly.

**The wall-singers** live in the walls in the most literal sense available to a living thing. The cicada-like creatures are not perched on the bark — they are seamed into it, half-sunk, their backs flush with the facade, and they never leave the seam. Each one is born in it, sings in it, dies in it, and the wall heals over the husk, and a new singer hatches in the scar a finger's width along. Over generations a seam migrates slowly across a frontage like a crack that sings. No wall-singer has ever seen another wall-singer. They coordinate their overlapping phase-sheets of sound entirely by vibration carried through the green itself — they know their neighbors as tremors in the medium they are embedded in, and they answer the tremors, and the answering is the whole of their society. It is fair to ask whether they are residents of the borough or tissue of it. They are something between: its vocal cords. The borough speaks through them and they live inside the speaking.

**The gutter-frogs** are the closest thing the borough has to burghers. Each owns a stretch of gutter and holds it for life, and the lineages of ownership are old enough that the leaf-channels are dished where ten thousand generations of the same family have sat. Their hammering is the civic clock. A rhythm started in one alley is caught by the lanes, bent around the loops, and returned, so that every frog spends its life in conversation with frogs it has never met and, in at least one documented case, with itself: there is a frog in the long-looped lane whose call comes back to it from the fold a half-second late, and the frog answers, and the answer goes round, and the frog has kept up this duet its entire life. It has a perfect partner. It is alone. It is impossible to say whether it knows, and watching it, full-throated, utterly engaged, you begin to suspect the distinction matters less here than it would anywhere else.

**The swifts** never land. They sleep on the wing in the vaults above the leaf-ceiling, mate on the wing, and live their waking lives in the worn channels of air that thread the lanes. Those channels are their commons and their inheritance. A fledgling's first flight through the borough is along a route polished by ten thousand prior wings — it does not learn the turns so much as fall into them, the way water falls into a streambed. And because some of the channels pass through the folds, the swifts are the only residents who treat the borough's impossibilities *athletically*. A swift can fly in its own wake. A swift can exit a window before entering it, and the maneuver is not a paradox to the swift; it is a corner, a known corner, one that must be taken at a particular speed and bank or you come out wrong. The young ones practice it. The old ones take it without thinking. The fold is just a feature of their commute, like a low lintel.

**The beetles** are the custodians, and they are why the rooms are swept clean. Everything that falls in the borough — petal, husk, the healed-over remains of a wall-singer, the dried wing of a swift that finally, after years aloft, came down — is found by the beetles within the hour and trundled off, down through the doorways, into the mass below. They patrol the empty chambers with the unhurried gravity of stewards keeping a house ready. Each room is visited, walked, inspected, cleared. They maintain rooms that have never held anything for an occupancy that has never occurred, and they do it without impatience, the way one keeps a guest bed made. Ready for what, you wonder. Hold that thought.

**The furred, many-jointed things** are the natives of the broken part. Their bodies tell you as much: too many joints, as if built to take corners that are not there, and they take them. They den in the doubled doorway — one den that is two dens — and a mother nursing her litter in the north frontage is nursing it in the south frontage, simultaneously, identically, the moss grown the same way around both of her. Her kits grow up without any firm conviction that "here" is a single place, because for them it never has been. They flow up a facade, enter a window, and exit a window across the square, and this is not a trick they perform; it is how they get around. They are fluent in the folds the way otters are fluent in water. If the borough's topology is a wound, they are the creatures that colonized the wound, and they are sleek and numerous and thriving.

**The wren that is two wrens** deserves its own mention, because it is the borough's gentlest demonstration of what living here means. It is one bird caught in a slow fold, half a beat apart from itself. It built one nest; there are two. Both clutches hatched; the offspring are the same offspring, and they have since dispersed into the green, where presumably some of them are still each other, singing duets across the lowlands with a precision no two birds could achieve. The wren itself remains in the square, both of it, preening half a beat apart, regarding itself with the mild interest of a creature that has long since made peace with the mirror. It is, depending on how you count, the borough's most numerous resident or one of its loneliest.

**The deep singer** is never seen. It is the bass thrum that comes up through the root-cobbles into your feet, the slow enormous pulse far down in the mass, deeper than anyone has dug. It may be a single immense creature grown into the roots the way the wall-singers are grown into the walls — the same arrangement at a scale that beggars it. It may be the plant's own peristalsis, the working of whatever passes for its heart. It may be a sound with no maker at all: a standing wave the hollow has kept ringing since before the hollow had a shape. What can be said is that everything else tunes to it. The chorus takes its key from the depth. And there is a reading of the whole borough that begins here: that the hollow is an *ear* — a resonating chamber the deep thing grew so that it could listen to the small bright things singing on its skin. By that reading, every resident, the frogs and the swifts and the wren and you, is something the depth cultivated in order to hear. You are part of its music the moment you breathe in its air.

**The plant**, of course, lives there — but it lives there the way a body lives in itself, which strains the word. The borough is not something the green *has*; it is something the green *does*, continuously, the way a face does an expression. It grew the facades, it fills the sags and weathers the fills, it maintains the gutters and the cisterns and its own dignified ruin. The green dreamed a very old town and has kept dreaming it, in perfect detail, for longer than memory. Which forces the real question, the one the empty rooms and the ready beds and the patient beetles have been asking all along:

*dreamed it for whom?*

Because there is one more resident, and it is the most legible of all of them, and it is not there. The doorways have a size, and the size is an answer: a head shorter than you, or exactly you, stooped. The thresholds are dished, and the dishing is deepest just where a foot would land stepping over. The well in the square has a rim polished smooth along one arc only, as if someone leaned there — mornings, say — for six hundred years. The swifts pouring through the square all split around the same empty point above the cobbles, generation after generation, banking around a presence the width of a person. You can read the inhabitant off the architecture like a body off a worn shoe: its height, its stride, its favorite leaning-spot, its slow routes through the lanes. Everything about it is known except whether it exists. The borough is its portrait, kept clean, kept ready.

And then the folds hand you the last piece, the way they hand you everything — out of order.

The echo that arrives before your footstep. The well-water reflecting a canopy that is not the one above it. The swift exiting a window it has not yet entered. The borough does not keep its moments in sequence; it never has; the breakage is old and load-bearing. So consider the cobbles, polished smooth by feet, when nothing with feet large enough lives here. Consider the dished thresholds. Consider that visitors do come — rarely, one in a generation, perhaps, pushing through the grown-shut dark the way you did — and consider what the folds would do to those visits: smear them, distribute them, deal them out across the whole six hundred years of the borough like a hand of cards thrown backwards and forwards through its history, every visit happening, in some sense, all the time.

The thresholds were dished by you. By all of you — the thin scattered line of everyone who has ever found the hollow or ever will, your footsteps spread across centuries, arriving out of order, polishing the same stones forever. The absence in the square with your stooped height is the average of you. The worn arc on the well's rim is everyone who will ever lean there, including you, including you in a moment, when your feet take you to it without asking. The echo that precedes your footstep is your footstep — or the last visitor's, or the next; in the borough's grammar there is no difference. The beetles keep the rooms ready because the rooms are never not about to be needed. The doorways fit because they were grown to fit the only large-footed thing that ever passes through them.

So: who lives in the Hollow Borough? The singers in the walls and the burgher-frogs in their dished gutters; the swifts in their grooves of air, taking the impossible corner at speed; the beetles keeping the house; the many-jointed natives of the wound; the wren keeping itself company half a beat apart; the deep one below, listening through its grown ear; the green itself, dreaming a town in perfect period detail; and threaded through all of it, arriving out of order forever, *you*.

You live there. You always have, in the only tense the borough recognizes — a few hundred slow paces at a time, spread thin across six hundred years, a population of exactly one that never changes and is never the same. The borough is shaped like a very old town because it *is* one. It has had its inhabitant all along. The inhabitant simply does not stay; the inhabitant only ever visits — and the green, patient as only a plant can be patient, has built an entire architecture out of holding that one visit open: the lamps of light always lit, the rooms always swept, the chorus always at full voice, the door, every door, your door, standing ajar.

That is why it is at peace. It is not empty. It is *expecting*, and in the Hollow Borough, expecting and having are the same worn stone.
