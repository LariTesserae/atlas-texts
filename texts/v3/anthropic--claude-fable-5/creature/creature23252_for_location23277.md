**The Residents of the Curing City**

A strict census would come back empty. There are no names here, no doors that lock, no laundry, no graves. By the strict accounting, the basin holds one city and zero citizens, and the table set in the courtyard is set for the empty set.

The strict accounting is wrong. It is wrong in at least four ways, and it gets more wrong the longer you stay.

---

**The river.**

The oldest resident never left. The river that made this floodplain was not destroyed by the project; it was *hired*. Absorbed, the way a body absorbs—taken in and put to work. It runs now through the city's interior, conscripted into a circulation it does not fully understand, carrying warmth and curing-sweat and dissolved light through conduits shaped almost, but not exactly, like a riverbed. The subsonic note you feel in your sternum, that long buried tone—that is the river rolling over. It does this a few times a night, the way anything does that is sleeping in an unfamiliar bed.

It remembers being a river. The proof is the trench: that winding line of darkest green where the moss grows thickest and the tree-ferns congregate. The city did not plan that corridor. The river dreams its old course up through the foundations, and the moisture follows the dream, and the green follows the moisture. You could call the fern-trench the river's self-portrait, painted in the only medium it has left.

It is not unhappy. It is hard to say how one knows this, but the water in the canals tastes faintly of patience, and the light-pools—which are partly its doing, light carried in solution and set out in the gutters like dishes left for someone—have the quality of an offering rather than a leak. The river was here before the city and has decided, in the slow way of rivers, to stay for the city. It is the only resident with seniority, and the city seems to defer to it: notice that no tower stands directly over the old course. The buildings part around the trench like a crowd making room for someone respected.

---

**The early arrivals.**

Then there are the squatters—the fauna, the ones the original surveys called *auditioning*. They deserve more credit than that. They are not auditioning. They have already moved in; they simply haven't told anyone, because there is no one to tell.

The heron first. Slate-colored, long-legged, patient as furniture. It works the light-pools at the bases of the walls, striking down and coming up with a beakful of glow, and it has been doing this for most of the city's eleven weeks, and the diet is having effects. The bird is becoming faintly luminous from the inside out. In full dark you can find it by the soft pearl shine of its long throat, and when it preens, its feathers leave brief bright streaks in the air, like a match that doesn't quite strike. It does not seem alarmed by this. If anything it has the air of a creature that has discovered an arrangement too good to question—and the city, for its part, refills the pools the heron favors a little faster than the others. Whether this is hospitality or husbandry is not a distinction the heron loses sleep over. It sleeps standing in the warmest gutter in the basin, one leg drawn up, glowing gently, the first night-light the city ever had.

The moss-things are harder. Quick, damp, never quite seen—a flex in the green at the edge of vision, a sense of small wet attention. The closest anyone could come to describing one is *a handful of the city that got loose*: they are the same grey-green as the walls, the same blood-warm temperature, and where they pass, the moss is left combed in one direction, neat as a part in hair. One theory—and again, there is no one here to hold theories, so the theories hang in the air unowned—is that they are errata. Small details the city drafted and revised and did not fully delete, now living feral in the upholstery of their own first version. They eat condensation and the dimmest fringes of the light-pools, the light too thin even for the heron to bother with. They are shy in a way that feels less like fear and more like tact, as if they understand they were not exactly *meant*, and have decided to be very little trouble.

And the caller. The thing in the fern-trench that calls twice each night—low, double, unanswered. Spend enough nights near the trench and you learn the shape of it without ever seeing the body: something fern-colored and dew-heavy, larger than the heron, that climbs partway up the tallest frond an hour after the violet deepens, and gives its two notes, and waits, and climbs down.

Two notes. Not one. A single note is a question flung into the dark; two notes is *half of a duet*. Whatever this creature is, it learned its call in a place where the call had an answer—where some second voice supplied the third and fourth notes, somewhere upstream of the city, before the river was hired and the basin remade. It calls twice a night because that is the protocol, and it has not stopped trying because stopping would mean agreeing that the other half of the duet is gone.

Here is the thing, though. The city has begun to answer. Not in voice—the city has no voice yet—but in rain. Those brief, vertical, hyper-local showers that fall on one block and spare the next: track them long enough and you find that the fern-trench receives one, most nights, within a few minutes of the second call. A short warm rain, falling only on the ferns, drumming on the fronds in a rhythm that is not quite random. It is a terrible answer. It is the wrong species, the wrong medium, the wrong everything. But it is *an* answer, offered by something very young that heard a sorrow repeated nightly in its own front garden and reached for the only instrument it owns. The caller has not stopped calling. But lately, after the rain, it stays up on the frond a little longer than it used to.

---

**The hands.**

Somebody places the benches.

This is the awkward fact at the center of the city's housekeeping. The streets revise, yes—drafts overlapping, a canal migrating politely from left to right—and that could be automatic, the impersonal churn of a thing still deciding its own shape. But the *bench* in the plaza the size of a field is not impersonal. It faces the view. The single chair inside the lit doorway is angled toward the door, the way you angle a chair when you are waiting for a particular knock. The table in the courtyard is not merely set; it is set *correctly*, the condensation wiped from the seats each evening, beading fresh on the tabletop by morning.

No one has ever seen the hands, because the hands work the way the streets revise: never while watched. But they leave a negative-space portrait of themselves, and it is worth assembling. The moss, which upholsters the lower meter of nearly everything, declines to grow in certain patches beside certain walls—patches the size and shape of something kneeling. The curing-sweat on a few thresholds is disturbed each night in long, deliberate strokes, as if wiped by a forearm. And in one colonnade, where a single drip keeps irregular time, the irregularity has been *changing*—drifting, week over week, toward a rhythm. Toward, if you stand there long enough, the first two notes of the fern-trench call.

The simplest reading is that the hands are the city's. That the Curing City, having no residents, has split off some portion of itself to *play house*—to rehearse the gestures of welcome on an empty stage so that the gestures will be practiced and warm when they are finally needed. Watch a parent-to-be arrange and rearrange a room for months. The hands are that. They are the city dressing the table, plumping the moss, teaching the drip to sing, doing the small constant maintenance of a hope.

---

**The stalks.**

Which brings us to the boulevards of pale stalks—the ones planted in tidy intervals too regular to be wild, the ones that bleed slow light, the ones it was suggested you not cut.

The suggestion was sound, but the reason given was incomplete. It is not that cutting one damages the city. It is that cutting one would be the only act in the basin that could properly be called a *crime*, because the stalks are not plants imitating architecture, and they are not architecture practicing being plants.

They are the residents.

The city did not go up; it came out—extruded, condensed, precipitated. The citizens are arriving the same way. Each stalk is blood-warm at the core, warmer than the walls around it, and each one has, in its lower third, a denser region that the light moves around rather than through. The shapes inside are still rough, still drafts—the city revises them the way it revises streets, never while watched, and a stalk passed twice is rarely identical twice: a suggestion of shoulder where yesterday there was none, a narrowing that will someday be a wrist. They are people the way a bud is a flower. Eleven weeks in, they are perhaps a third of the way to whatever they will be.

Three observations, for whoever keeps the unowned theories:

First—the count. The pale stalks in the courtyard with the set table number exactly the places set at that table. The stalks along each boulevard match, one for one, the doorways on that boulevard that stand open onto lit interiors. The city is not growing a population. It is growing a *guest list*, and it has already addressed the invitations.

Second—the reflections. Your image, in the wet black windows, lags half a second behind you, and everyone has assumed the city's record-keeping runs slow. But stand at the edge of a window when no one is before it, in the deep violet hour, and sometimes the glass is not empty. Figures pass in it—pale, unhurried, the height and gait of finished people—walking through a reflected city that is the same city, slightly more worn, its thresholds softened by use, its moss thick to the second meter. The record-keeping does not run behind. It runs *ahead*. The reflections are not memory; they are rehearsal. The city is so eager for its residents that it has begun reflecting them in advance, the way you can want a thing hard enough to see it in every dark window.

Third—and this should be done at most once, gently, with the flat of a knuckle: tap a stalk. The surface is warm and faintly gives, like the wall of something that breathes very slowly. Walk away. Some minutes later—two, ten, the timing is advisory, like the distances—there will come from inside it a single answering knock. Soft. Unmistakable. Polite, in the way of someone acknowledging a visitor through a door they are not yet able to open.

They are awake in there, or something adjacent to awake—drowsing in the long warm patience of their own curing, listening to the city tick and settle around them, hearing the river roll over, hearing the heron's gutter-shuffle and the caller's two notes and the rain that answers. When they finally emerge—seams parting, light running off them into the gutters where it has always pooled, taking their first steps down avenues built generously to fit them—they will not arrive in the city. They will *hatch from it*, and it will not be strange to them, because they will have spent their whole gestation memorizing its sounds. The set table will not be a mystery. They will have heard the hands setting it, every night, through the warm pale walls of themselves.

---

**The largest resident.**

Last, and underneath all of it: the city.

It would be easy to call the city the house rather than the householder, but the distinction does not survive eleven weeks of close attention. The Curing City lives in the basin the way a heart lives in a chest. It runs two degrees above blood heat. It breathes—you have walked through its breath, humid, mineral, fern-and-warm-slate. It eats light and banks the surplus in its gutters like a fire husbanded through a long night. It sweats, settles, grows in centimeters with a sound like distant knuckles, sleeps in no schedule anyone can find, and dreams overlapping drafts of its own streets.

And it is *young*. This is the thing to hold onto, the key that fits every lock in the basin. Eleven weeks. Everything uncanny here is also, looked at in the right light, simply the behavior of something very new and very large and very alone: practicing rooms before it has anyone to put in them; rehearsing rain one block at a time, like a child sounding out words; slowing your walk when you hurry, geometrically, gently, the way you'd close a hand around something that might startle; answering a grieving animal with weather because weather is the only word it knows yet. It keeps a heron as a night-light. It is teaching a drip to sing two notes. It wipes the chairs.

It was made by someone—the air still remembers the process, even if nothing remembers hands—and whoever made it is not in residence and left no forwarding address. Possibly they became it. Possibly they will arrive with the hatching, or possibly the hatching *is* their arrival, deferred and transformed. The city does not seem to wonder. It has the unbothered focus of a creature with exactly one task: to finish curing itself and its guest list at the same time, so that on some violet evening a few weeks or months from now, the seams will part all down the boulevards, and the avenues will finally be the right amount of full, and the table in the courtyard will seat precisely the number it was always set for.

So: the census. One river, employed and dreaming. One heron, slowly filling with light. An uncounted scatter of small damp errata, combing the moss. One caller, holding half a duet open like a door. One pair of unseen hands, which are also the next entry. One city, eleven weeks old, blood-warm, breathing, waiting. And some dozens or hundreds of residents already present and accounted for—warm in their pale columns along the boulevards, knocking back when knocked upon, half-dreamed and half-grown, the most patient population any city has ever had.

The strict accounting says zero. Count honestly and the number is already large. Count generously and it is exactly one: a single new thing in a shallow basin, curing in the dark, that has not yet finished dividing itself into a city and its people—and is in no hurry, because it knows something the accounting doesn't.

It knows who's coming. It set the table itself.
