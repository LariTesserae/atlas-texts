At first you would say the trees. You would be mostly right to. The great sealed plants are the natives, the landlords, the original signatories of the treaty of circles — and anything else that lives on the tableland lives by their permission and in their pattern. The gliders are theirs; the walkers are theirs; the very weather grounds itself through their tips. If the question is *who lives here*, the honest first answer is: the green things do, and everything else is a guest.

But guests can stay a long time. And there are people.

You could cross the tableland for a year and meet none of them, and that would not mean you had missed them. It would mean you had experienced their society at its ordinary density. They are a population like the flora — enormous, vigorous, widely separated individuals, each one an oasis of themselves — and they would tell you, without irony, that this is the only honest way to live in a land where every horizon is two hundred miles away and every drop of water is an heirloom.

## The Sealed

They are born the way the trees are: with everything they will ever drink already inside them.

At quickening, each child is given the *first measure* — a body's full lifetime of fluid, sealed in at the start and never replenished, never lost, only slowly converted into the structure of a life. Their skin is continuous and faintly waxen, with the same glassy cast as the great door-sized leaves; in strong light there is a green tinge to it, because they too eat the sun a little, the way the trees do — not enough to live on, but enough that a day spent walking in the brightness is a day partly fed. The rest of their diet is the land's quiet currency: resin licked from the leaf-joints of the candelabra trees, in the same polite arrangement the gliders keep, and the mineral glitter of the leaf-glass halos, ground fine between stones and eaten by the spoonful.

Press your palm to one of them — they will permit this, gravely, as a considerable intimacy — and you feel what you feel against the trunk of a tree: an even warmth behind a sealed surface. Fire behind glass.

They do not sweat. They barely bleed; a cut seals itself in moments, the way sap glasses over. And they can weep, but each tear is gone forever — a permanent debit against the first measure — and so weeping is the most serious statement a body can make. A person may weep four times in three hundred years and will remember each occasion in full: the date, the light, the reason, the exact number of tears. There is a verb in their language for *spending water on a grief*, and it is not used lightly, and it is never used as a figure of speech.

Laughter, on the other hand, costs nothing. They are, person for person, one of the merriest peoples imaginable — it is simply that the thin air carries no laugh more than a few meters, so all their laughter is private, scattered across a continent, each one sounding alone under the enamel sky.

## Their Circles

Each of them keeps a circle, the way the trees do, and the circles do not overlap. A circle is not a farm and not quite a home; it is a *jurisdiction of attention* — a stretch of stone and the two or three groves upon it that this one person, for this one long lifetime, has undertaken to notice.

They build almost nothing. The fused canopies of the groves are their rooms; the cushion-plants, dense enough to walk on, are their beds, springy and cool and smelling of resin and cold metal. Their possessions amount to a kit you could carry in two hands: a mirror, a leaf-glass knife, a grinding stone, a pollen-bag. Everything else they own is written down.

For they are, above all else, *recorders*. Nothing here rots and almost nothing happens, and so everything that does happen deserves an entry. The fall of a leaf — that single clear *tink*, once or twice a day, somewhere — is logged. A glider sighted is logged. A walker's line of neat round footprints crossing the circle is logged, with direction and gait. They keep two registers, and the choice between them is itself a statement: marks drawn in the fine dust last a season, and are for things that should stop being true — complaints, worries, the weather; marks ground into the polished stone last forever, and are for things that should never stop being true. A person's home circle, after four centuries, is a book. The stone around their sleeping-grove is dense with grooved entries going back, in the oldest circles, through six or seven keepers, and a new keeper's first years are spent reading the floor.

## Speech at Distance

They have three languages, sorted by range.

Close speech — voice, in the thin air — barely carries, and so it has become the language of intimacy almost by physics alone. It is quiet, precise, and used only between two people standing under the same tree, which may happen to a given person a handful of times in a decade. To speak aloud to someone is already to say: *we are very near, and that is rare, and I know it.*

The middle language belongs to the trees. Every grove is a chord — each leaf a reed, each tree humming its note in the wind — and over generations the keepers prune and train their groves until each one plays a particular, unmistakable sound. A person's name *is* their grove's chord. They are named by the wind, hourly, whether they are home or not; a traveler crossing the open stone hears a thread of glassy humming on the edge of perception and knows, miles before seeing anything, exactly whose circle they are entering. The dead keepers of a grove are remembered in its lower notes, which no one retunes.

And the long language is light. The air refuses to soften distance; a flash on a ridge two hundred miles away arrives crisp as a flash beside you. So they talk in mirror-fire. Around midday, when the sun stands high enough to lend to everyone, the tableland conducts its conversation: brief stutters of brilliance hopping from rise to rise, relayed, answered, relayed again. A sentence may take a day to cross the continent and a day to come back. An argument can take years, and they consider this the chief virtue of arguing — no position survives the journey unless it deserves to. They compose their flashes the way other peoples compose epitaphs.

## Meetings

Nothing on the tableland can arrive unannounced. The land forbids it. A person approaching your circle is visible from forty miles out, a dark fleck on the pale stone, and the approach takes the better part of two days; you watch each other the whole way. By the time two people stand within speaking distance, each has had hours to compose what they will say, and both know it, and so the first words exchanged under the tree are always *finished* words — considered, weighed, meant.

A whole ethics descends from this single fact of geography. There is no ambush here, and never has been — not in war, which they do not have, and not in conversation, which they treat as the same kind of thing. You cannot surprise anyone; therefore no one has ever needed to be braced for surprise; therefore there is, in their dealings, a kind of total unguardedness that visitors from hurried, crowded, watered worlds would find almost unbearable. They have nothing that can be stolen — the only treasure is sealed inside the body — and nothing that can be sprung on them. What is left of human relation, when those are subtracted, is what they have: attention, patience, and the long mutual watching of the approach.

Meetings are scheduled by flash, sometimes years ahead. Chance meetings — two travelers' paths converging in the open — happen perhaps once a decade in a life, and are spoken of forever after.

## Children

A child is the most expensive thing in the world, and they are honest about the price.

The first measure cannot be conjured; it must be *given*. When a child is to be quickened, the parents — and usually four or five others, neighbors flashing in their pledges from a hundred miles off and walking weeks to keep them — each yield up a portion of their own sealed water, drawn off through a glass needle in a rite called the lending, and sealed again. Each gift is a literal subtraction: the donor will live some years fewer, and knows precisely how many, and has done the arithmetic, and gives anyway. The child carries the names of every donor — a chord of chords — and an elder's forearms, dotted with the small star-shaped scars of many lendings, are read the way other peoples read medals.

Children are the only thing on the tableland that runs. The walkers do not run; the wind does not hurry; the trees turn through their days in slow degrees. But a child of six will sprint flat-out across a mile of bare bright stone for no reason at all, and any elder within sight will stop and watch the whole way, smiling, remembering speed.

## The Settling

They do not exactly die. They *finish*.

In great age the conversion that has run quietly all their lives — water into structure, fluid into self — approaches completion. They stiffen slowly; their extremities go translucent first, fingers like the sea-glass leaves; their warmth concentrates and their movements grow architectural. They feel it coming for decades and speak of it without dread, as the trees would, if trees spoke.

When the time is near, a person chooses a place — always with long sightlines, always with good light — and walks there, and sits down facing whatever they have decided to face, and over some weeks anneals: settles, clarifies, and goes still, a seated figure of warm green glass that will outlast the wind.

The tableland is dotted with them. Every region has its seated ones, centuries and millennia deep, and travelers route their journeys to pass through them, because the dead here are *legible*. You can read a settled elder's last posture — the direction of the gaze, the set of the shoulders, what the hands were doing. And the deliberate ones, the great ones, arrange themselves as instruments: hands raised and shaped so that every clear noon the sun drives through their palms and prints a mark on the stone before them — a word, a name, a chord-sign — the same word, every noon, forever. The dead of this people are permanent heliographs. They are still in the conversation. Their entries are still being made, once a day, in light, and the living flash their answers from the ridges, and do not feel that they are speaking to no one.

## The Whole of Them

Stand on any rise near the high center and you can see their whole nation at once, if you know what to look for: a chord on the wind from a grove below, a noon-flash answering another flash from a ridge at the edge of sight, a line of footprints a season old, a seated glass figure printing its one word on the stone, a child — one child, somewhere, a moving fleck — running across the brightness for the joy of it.

They are not many and will never be many; the arithmetic of the first measure sees to that, and they would not have it otherwise. They are what the land grows when it grows something that can keep records: widely spaced, sealed, warm behind glass, humming each their own name, immensely patient, spending nothing, noticing everything. The tableland solved itself long ago — and they are not its rulers and not its children but something more like its *margin notes*: the place where a finished landscape, complete and past becoming, nonetheless writes down, in dust for a season or in stone forever, that a leaf fell today, and chimed once, and was witnessed.
