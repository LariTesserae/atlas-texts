Ask what lives in the hollow and the hollow gives you the ordinary answer first, which is: almost nothing. No nests in the lianas. No burrows in the root-mat. No birdsong under the loom-clatter, no insect drone under the hiss. Even the decay here is in-house — leaves that fall are reabsorbed before they can properly rot, so there is none of the usual subletting that goes on in a forest, the beetles and threads and mites that move into every death. Nothing moves in. Nothing has moved in for a very long time. The hollow is fully occupied, and the answer to *by whom* comes in installments, the way everything in this place comes: on schedule, in ascending order of truth.

The first answer is the moth.

There is always exactly one. It is the size of a spread hand, pale green, the green of a dial face, and it lives on the chimney brick where the warm air rises, which is the only place in the hollow it is ever found. It does not feed; it has no mouth; it was issued without one, the way a gauge is issued without a stomach. It rests with its wings flat to the warm brick, and it is — you understand this slowly, watching it across an hour in which it does not stir — the hollow's instrumentation. When the chimney's draw falters by a fraction, the moth shifts an inch toward the flue. When the air over-wets, it angles its wings. The grove, which has no eyes, reads it constantly through the brick. It is the pressure gauge the boiler lost, reincarnated at chest height in animal warmth, and it is the single concession the works makes to needing anything alive in the old sense: one small heat, kept where the readings matter.

It emerges from a cell in the chimney itself — a smooth socket in the root-mortar, thumb-deep, which is empty now because the current moth is on station. Where the occupant of that cell comes from is the grove's business; there is no courtship in the hollow, no caterpillar, no season of moths. There is only ever the one on duty and the one in reserve. When the moth on duty dies — and they live absurdly long, for moths; years, maybe decades; a mouthless creature spending nothing — it steps off the brick, or simply lets go, and the condensate channels carry it down the veins of the floor to a catch-basin, where the resin takes it. Look into the older basins, the glass-hard century ones, and you will find them: dozens deep, identical, pale green going amber with age, stacked in the resin like spent fuses in a drawer. Filed. The grove does not discard its instruments. It archives them, and issues the next.

The second answer is in the pool, and it is the senior resident, and seniority is nearly its entire character.

It was here first. This is the thing to understand about it: the original works was sited on this ground *because* of the water, the saturated low country the boiler was built to pump, and the water already had something in it then. Long, soft, unpigmented, eyeless — it has never needed to be anything more specific than that, because nothing has looked at it in living memory and the dark of the pool does not insist on details. It has been listening to pumping from below for the entire run of the works: first the iron strokes, the human decades, the shouts and the shift bells coming down to it through the water as pressure and rhythm; then the silence of the closure, which may be the only event in its life it registered as an event; then the pumping resuming in wood, the same four-second beat, the same job from a new heart. From where it lies, the works never stopped. There was only a maintenance interval, somewhat long.

It keeps the sump. That is its position, whether or not anything assigned it: whatever falls into the pool does not stay fallen. Drowned seed, the rare exhausted moth that misses its basin, the silt the channels carry down — the pool stays black and clear and exactly the same depth, century over century, and the thing in it is why. It surfaces never. It turns over once — you may witness this; visitors generally do, once — the slow roll of a sleeper confirming that the night is proceeding correctly. It is possible it does not know the works exist. It is possible it is the only resident that understands them completely, having heard them entire, from inside the water, since the brass plate was new. In this hollow those may be the same condition.

The third answer is the tenant proper, and the tenant is not the grove.

The grove is the body. The grove is the premises — the plant, in both senses, the building and the green. What *lives* in it is the occupation: the work itself, the job, which did not end when the people left because jobs do not end that way. A job needs hands, and the grove had ten thousand, and somewhere in the long wet decades after the closure the position was filled. Not haunting — nothing here is haunted; haunting is grief, and there is no grief in the hollow, only continuity. Staffing.

Its character, if you stand still long enough to feel it: punctual. Incurious. Entirely without ambition — it does not want to grow, which is the strangest thing a plant has ever wanted; it wants to be *to spec*. Its tempo, the four-second thump-and-sigh you feel in your knees, is not a vegetable rhythm. It is the stroke rate of the dead engine, inherited the way a child inherits a parent's walk, kept long after the iron that set it rusted to lace — the grove has never known another beat and would not recognize the suggestion that one exists. Its memory is the resin, clouded layer by layer, inclusions filed by depth like entries by date. Its ledger is the rings. Its breath is the chimney. Its sensorium is tension: it feels the hollow through the taut lianas the way a spider feels a web, and it counts by pod-snaps, and it has no face, and if it has anything like a mood it is the mood of a shop floor at mid-shift — everything running, nothing due, no one expected.

And inside the tenant, a fourth answer, the smallest and the most human: the crew is still here. Not as ghosts. As measurements.

Look at the rows of resin-spigots along the lower walls. They sit at chest height — at *a man's* chest height — and they are spaced a stride apart, the interval of a worker walking a line of taps and touching each one without breaking step. The grove kept the ergonomics after the bodies went. Somebody's notion of plumb lives in the chimney, enforced now by roots, a fraction of a degree a generation, toward a vertical that exists nowhere in nature and persists here because one dead mason cared about it. Somebody's preference set the four-second beat. Somebody's reach set the spigots, somebody's pace set their spacing, somebody's habit of letting nothing leave the floor became the policy of firing every seed back into the walls. These are residents too — a tolerance, an interval, a stride, a standard of straightness — abstractions kept alive in tissue, the way a language is kept alive in mouths. The people who worked here were never absorbed and never expelled. They were *retained*, as specifications. They are the only kind of immortality the hollow offers, and the hollow offers it without being asked.

Which brings the census to its last entry, and the last entry is you.

You were logged. You felt it — procedural, not unfriendly. Understand what that means in a place where the ledger is the place: during the hour you stood on the root-floor, a thin lens of resin clouded over in a basin near the south wall, and that cloud holds your hour the way the deeper layers hold the moths. The ring the trunks are growing this season has your footsteps in it, a faint compression, datable. The moth turned its head — only that — and the thing in the pool rolled over once, and somewhere in the tension of the lianas your weight on the floor was felt, noted, and released.

So the population of the Green Works, in full: one occupation, very old, very calm, still under its original management in the only sense that matters. Two staff animals — one warm, one cold; one gauge, one sump-keeper; one always visible, one never. An unrecorded number of standards, intervals, and tolerances, formerly human. And, on file in the amber, permanent, retrievable by depth: every moth ever issued, every season ever run, and everyone — including you, now; especially you, now — who ever came down the slope, raised their voice over the pumping, and was counted.
