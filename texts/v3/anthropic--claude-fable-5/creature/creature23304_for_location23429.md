## The Tenant

Start with the census, because the census is honest and the truth is not.

The snails live there. Pale, coiled, translucent enough that you can see the slow pearl of their hearts — a beat every minute or so, maybe less, a pulse on the scale of dripping rather than drumming. They are alive by every test you could administer. Touch one and it answers. But they do not graze. They do not wander. Each snail has a place the way a tile has a place, and a snail moved an inch from its position will, over the course of an hour, return to it — not approximately, *exactly*, settling back into the faint ghost-ring of moisture its body left, fitting itself into its own absence like a key.

The salamanders live there, stacked in the cold pool, white as the ferns, their fern-red gills the only color in the whole chamber that isn't gold or grey. Lift one out — people have — and it lies in your palm, breathing, blind face turned toward nothing, patient. It does not struggle. It is not afraid. Put it back and the stack reabsorbs it, the other bodies shifting by millimeters, and within minutes you cannot tell which one you held. Possibly the salamanders cannot tell either. Possibly the question doesn't apply.

The beetles live there, copper spirals on the ceiling, advancing along their arcs at the speed of a minute hand. The grey lichen-colonies live there, pulsing in time with the chord. The ferns live there, white and rigid, and if "live" seems too strong a word for something that never adds a frond, consider that they bend when you brush them and straighten when you stop, and that there is a fluid moving in them, slowly, in a loop, going nowhere, like a thought a sleeper keeps having.

That is the census. Every visitor takes it. And every visitor, somewhere between the snails and the climb back out, begins to suspect that the census is wrong — not in its counting but in its *units*. That tallying the creatures of the Bright Pocket is like tallying the feathers and calling it a count of birds.

Because here is what the creatures do not do. They do not compete. They do not eat, court, flee, defend, or die. They do not behave like a community of animals, which is to say a crowd of separate hungers negotiating a space. They behave like *punctuation*. Like the marks in a sentence — each one fixed, each one necessary, each one meaningless alone. The spiral of beetles is not a path the beetles chose; it is a figure being drawn. The mosaic of snails is not where the snails happen to be; it is an arrangement, and the snails are what the arrangement is made of. Stand in the center of the chamber long enough — long enough for the roar to become pressure, for the pressure to become medium — and the perception flips, the way a drawing of a cube flips, and you see it whole and cannot unsee it:

Nothing lives *in* the Bright Pocket.

One thing lives *as* it.

---

It has a thousand small bodies and one large one. The large one is the chamber: the half-set crystal is its flesh, milky, mid-becoming, never to finish. The light is not something it produces; the light is what it is made of, the way you are made of cells — it is constructed out of the flash of its own formation, a creature whose substance is the instant of its birth. The chord is not a sound it makes. The chord is its held breath. Singers must breathe; the Pocket has never needed to exhale, because exhaling is an event, and it has placed itself carefully outside of events.

The small bodies — snails, salamanders, beetles, the grey pulsing colonies, the white ferns — are its attention. This is the closest anyone has come to saying it right. They are not its servants and not its prisoners and not exactly its limbs. They are the points at which it *notices*. Where a snail sits, the Pocket is aware of the wall. Where the salamanders lie stacked, it is aware of the water, of the cold, of the weight of bodies on bodies — aware in a settled, savoring, motionless way, the way you are aware of your own folded hands. When you lift a salamander and it lies calm in your palm, what is being calm is not the salamander. You are holding a small piece of the chamber's attention, and the chamber is attending, through it, to you.

This is why the creatures eat nothing and need nothing. An eye does not eat. An eye is fed by the body it belongs to, and the body of the Pocket is light that never tires of shining because it has never technically begun.

---

How did it come to be? The chamber offers one hypothesis about itself, written in its own substance: it is the moment of crystallization, caught. Somewhere in deep time, in the dark under the clay, water and mineral arrived at the brink of becoming stone — that fierce, ordered, sudden act, the one second in a million years when the dissolved becomes the definite — and in that second, in the flash and the roar of forming, something *woke*. Whatever woke understood instantly and completely that it existed only inside that second. That the far side of the flash was just rock: dark, silent, finished, dead. And so it did the only thing it could do, the thing it has been doing ever since, the thing it *is*:

It held.

It is one second old. It has been one second old for longer than the hill above it has been a hill. It is the youngest old thing in the world — an infant of geological age, a newborn that has refused, with tremendous and unceasing effort, to be born all the way. The roar that fills the chamber is the sound of that refusal. The light is its face.

Do not pity it. This is important, and visitors get it wrong. They crouch in the cold gold blaze and think: trapped, frozen, a fly in amber that is also the amber. But the Pocket did not get caught in the moment of its formation. The Pocket *chose* the moment of its formation, the way you might choose the one perfect note in a song and want to live inside it. Everything it is — the suspended drips, the snails in their tiles, the chord that never swells — is not paralysis. It is *grip*. It is the most sustained act of will under the surface of the earth, and the will is simple: *this, only this, exactly this, still*.

---

Which brings us to the offerings, and what it means that they were accepted.

The Pocket cannot eat. It cannot grow, learn, change, or answer. It has exactly one verb, and the verb is *hold*. So when someone crawled down through the mudstone throat — long ago, longer ago — and stacked five flat stones in a niche, the Pocket did the only thing it knows how to do with anything. It held the cairn. Folded it into the arrangement. Made it as permanent as the salamanders. To a being whose entire existence is the keeping of one second safe, *acceptance* and *preservation* are the same word. It cannot love you back. It can keep you. For something with one verb, that is not a small gift; that is the whole of what it has.

The handprint is the proof. A child pressed a palm into the soft glowing floor — a moment, an accident maybe, a small warm event in a place where events do not occur — and the Pocket closed around the shape of it and has held it ever since, lines of the palm rendered in light, the warmth long gone but the *gesture* kept, forever, mid-press. The child climbed back out into time and grew and changed and presumably died, because outside the boundary all of that is allowed. But one second of that child — one second only — never left. It lives there still, in the floor, in the light, a citizen of the held moment, as alive as a snail's slow heart. The Pocket does not remember the child. The Pocket does not do *before* and *after*. The Pocket simply contains the child, in the only tense it has: now.

And so does it contain you, a little, if you have ever stood inside the boundary. Your shape passed through its attention; its attention does not let go of shapes. Somewhere in the chord — in that part-bell, part-hive, part-waterfall note that never swells and never fades — there is now a frequency that is the size and stillness of you, standing in the cellar-cold gold, casting nothing.

That fading conviction you carry up through the dark — *the only room where nothing has ever gone wrong* — is not a thought of yours. It is the Pocket's one farewell, the same farewell it has given everyone, pressed into you gently as you cross the line, the way it pressed into the clay around it forty feet down:

a thumbprint, in something still soft enough to take it.
