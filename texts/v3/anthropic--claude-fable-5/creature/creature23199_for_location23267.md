What lives in the steaming room lives slowly, quietly, and in common. Nothing down there is alone in the way surface creatures are alone — separate bodies crossing separate paths. The chamber's inhabitants are more like the organs of one warm animal, each doing its work in the green dusk, each aware of all the others in the way a hand is aware of the foot it has never seen. To describe them one at a time is slightly false. But it is the only way in.

**The crickets** are the chamber's talkers, and they have no voices. They came down generations ago — hundreds of generations, for crickets — and somewhere in that long descent through warm silent time, the song went out of their line. Not lost: *translated.* They are the color of candle wax, soft-shelled, long-limbed, and they live their whole lives on the undersides of fern fronds, and the ferns are their language. A cricket grips a frond and trembles it, and a cricket three fronds away feels the tremor arrive through the lattice of overlapping leaves and answers, and on a still afternoon the whole fern-floor of the chamber is shivering with conversation in windless air — courtship, warning, the small gossip of grazing animals, all of it conducted through the body of the garden itself. They cannot speak except through the plants. This means the ferns hear everything, in their way; every word the crickets have ever said has passed through green tissue first. The young learn the trembling the way surface crickets learn song, badly at first, shaking whole fronds with clumsy emphasis, and the old ones — pale to translucency, antennae longer than their bodies — can speak in tremors so fine that only the frond they touch and the one creature meant to feel it ever know a thing was said. At night, when the eye above goes fully dark, they leave the ferns and gather at the edge of the glowing moss, ranked in loose arcs, motionless, saying nothing. No one knows what they do there. Possibly nothing. Possibly that is the point — the one hour when the talkers are silent together.

**The frogs** are the chamber's watchers. They are small, the brown-black of wet bark, and they sit spaced along the walls with a regularity that does not look accidental, because it is not. Their language is position. Where a frog sits, how far from its neighbors, which way it faces, how long it has held still — these are statements, and the wall of frogs is a slow sentence the chamber writes and rewrites all day. A frog that has seen the snake moves two hand-spans down and freezes facing the entrance throat, and within an hour every frog on that wall has shifted, and the message has traveled the whole room without a sound or a glance. Their throats move constantly, swallowing air in the wet heat, and the pulsing throats give them the look of creatures perpetually about to speak who have, every time, for ten thousand years, decided against it. They are patient beyond anything the surface produces. A frog will hold one position for a day and a half waiting for one centipede to make one mistake. They face the glow at night, all of them, arranged on the stones around the warm pool like an audience, throats pulsing, eyes catching the blue-green light. If the chamber has a religion, the frogs are its congregation, and the service never ends and nothing is ever said.

**The salamanders** are the elders. They are glossy as poured glass, dark with a faint oil-sheen of purple, and they move through the deep moss at a speed that makes the snails look hasty. They are old — genuinely old, individually old. A salamander in constant warmth, never wintering, never hurried, eating well and risking nothing, can live a very long time, and some of the ones easing through the moss pillars now were easing through the same moss when the moss was thinner. They do not learn the room; they *contain* it. Each one carries a complete map of the chamber in its slow body — every seep, every crack, the timing of the drip-curtain when rain falls above, which root-column the snake favors, where the centipedes nest. They are nearly impossible to surprise because they are nearly impossible to find: a salamander at rest is indistinguishable from a wet stone with an opinion. The other creatures treat their movements as weather reports. When the salamanders go deep into the moss and stop, the frogs shift downward and the crickets fall quiet, and usually, within the day, something is proven right.

**The snails** are the gardeners, though they would not know it. Fist-sized, their shells grown thin and pale and almost ornamental in a world without hard weather, they graze the moss columns in slow spirals, trimming, thinning, keeping the green plush from strangling itself. Their trails gleam faintly for hours, and the trails are read — by the crickets, who follow them to fresh growth; by the centipedes, who follow them to crickets. A snail's path is thus a sentence other creatures finish in their own ways, mostly to the snail's indifference. They are the only inhabitants who touch every part of the chamber, floor to root-tangle, over the course of their long circuits, and the oldest of them carry on their shells small gardens of their own — moss, a thread of the glow — so that in the dark hours a grazing snail moves along a column as a dim green spark traveling at the speed of a minute hand.

**The pool** has its own population, sealed and strange. The blind white shrimp drift in loose constellations near the surface, riding the pool's slow pulse, rising and settling with it as if the water's breathing were a tide they were born to. They feed on what sifts down through the steam — pollen from the eye, motes of the garden — and they navigate by touch and by the pressure of one another, holding their loose formations the way starlings do, but at one-thousandth the speed. And below them, in the warm dark where the water goes down into the stone, lives the fish. There may be more than one. No one has ever seen two at once. It is eyeless, soft, the pink of an inner lip, longer than a forearm, and it rises rarely — once a day, once a week — turns over once just beneath the surface as if rolling in sleep, and sinks again. It is the deepest inhabitant and possibly the oldest, older than the salamanders, a creature of the blood-warm dark under the chamber that visits the chamber the way the chamber's creatures might visit the surface: briefly, blindly, without much liking it. The shrimp scatter when it rises and reassemble when it has gone, their constellation re-forming in a slightly different shape, and that is the only mark its visits leave.

**The bats** are the commuters, the only residents with two lives. They hang in the high root-tangle through the day in tight clusters, fanning themselves one-winged against the heat, and at dusk they pour up through the eye without a sound and spend the night in the enormous cold loud world above, and come back before dawn carrying it inside them. They are, without knowing it, the chamber's farmers: their droppings feed the ferns, the seeds in their guts have planted half of what grows around the eye's rim, the moths they miss come fluttering down the shaft of the entrance throat and feed the frogs. Everything green in the room is, at some remove, bat-fed. They are also its only gossips about the outside — they arrive smelling of rain, of flowers, of frost, and the chamber's air changes faintly with what they bring on their fur, so that the frogs and crickets, who will never see the surface, live downstream of it anyway, breathing its seasons secondhand.

**The centipedes** are the hush made hungry. Whip-thin, copper-brown, fast in a room where nothing else is fast, they live in the wall-cracks and travel the vines, and they are the reason the silence here has teeth. They are not numerous — the room could not feed many — and they are not cruel; they are simply the pressure that keeps everything else honest. Every language in the chamber, the frond-talk and the wall-grammar and the gleaming trails, exists partly because of them: speech here evolved under the requirement that it make no sound and cast no movement a centipede could use. They are, in that sense, the authors of the local eloquence. The frogs eat their young; they eat the crickets; the salamanders eat them when they can be bothered. It balances. It has balanced for a very long time.

**The snake** does not live here, exactly, but one of them has stopped entirely leaving. Slender, copper-dark, a surface animal whose grandmothers learned the way down the entrance throat, it spends longer below with every year — drawn by the warmth, by the bats, by something in the green hush that suits it. It is the chamber's one true disturbance, and the chamber has domesticated even that: its hunts have a rhythm now, a route, and the room reads it. When it pours down a hanging root, the wave of stillness that runs ahead of it is itself a kind of announcement, the most honest message in the room — *here I am, going there* — and the chamber arranges itself around the news and goes on. The snake may not know it has been absorbed. It thinks it is hunting. It has become part of the weather.

**The glow** is the hardest inhabitant to name, and it may be the central one. It threads the moss at the low end of the chamber, around the warm pool, in the place light from the eye never reaches — a faint, even, blue-green luminescence, dimming where it is touched, recovering over long minutes, as if light were a fluid that could be pressed out of it and would slowly seep back. It may be a fungus woven through the moss. It may be the moss itself, having learned in the dark to remember light by making some. Whatever it is, the chamber has organized itself around it the way households organize around a hearth: the crickets gather at its edge in the black hours, the frogs face it, the snails carry threads of it on their shells like coals borrowed from a fire, the salamanders pass through it and come out faintly traced along their flanks, glowing for a few minutes like sentences before the light drains back into the moss. It eats almost nothing — moisture, warmth, whatever the pool breathes upward — and it asks nothing, and it has been there longer than anything that watches it. If the room has a keeper, it is this: a dim, patient, repeating memory of the sun, banked at the bottom of everything, around which the whole silent population turns.

**And the people** still live there, in the only way the room permits anything to persist: as wear. They live in the three steps rounded back almost to nothing, which still carry, in their shape, the exact stride of whoever cut them — short steps, a small people, or a careful one. They live in the niche by the pool, sized for a lamp, now holding moss and a sleeping frog, so that the room has quietly replaced their light with its own occupant. They live in the cracked quern with the fern in its fracture, and in the hand-marks above the pool, faded past image into texture, which the steam keeps damp and the damp keeps dark and the dark keeps. They came for the warm water or the quiet and they left almost nothing, and the room took the almost-nothing and grew over it gently, the way moss upholsters a stone — not erasing it, *holding* it. The frogs sit where the lamps sat. The creatures of the chamber arrange themselves nightly around the glow exactly as people once must have arranged themselves around their small flames in the same spot, and whether that is coincidence or inheritance — whether the room remembered the shape of a gathered circle and kept making it after the makers stopped coming — is one more question it does not answer.

Which leaves the last inhabitant, the largest, the one all the others live inside: **the room itself.** It is hard, after long enough, to keep believing it is only a hollow. It breathes — the pool's surface lifting and settling on its slow internal pulse. It sweats. It is blood-warm. It digests what falls through its eye and grows a garden from it. Its walls carry messages; its floor trembles with talk; its low end holds a banked dim light like something kept alive on purpose. Every creature in it has been shaped by it until they fit it the way a tongue fits a mouth — voiceless, patient, warm-blooded in their habits if not their bodies, fluent in stillness. They are what lives there, all of them, the crickets and frogs and the glass-slow salamanders, the constellated shrimp and the pink blind sleeper beneath them, the snail-gardeners and the commuting bats, the hush with teeth and the snake the weather swallowed, the light that remembers and the people worn down to steps. But it may be truest to say it the other way around: the room is the resident, ancient and breathing and half-asleep in the red earth, and everything moving in its green warmth — every tremor, every glow, every small silent rearrangement — is simply what it is dreaming, and has been dreaming, in the same long hot exhale, since before the first hand gripped the stone at the lip of the slot and started down.
