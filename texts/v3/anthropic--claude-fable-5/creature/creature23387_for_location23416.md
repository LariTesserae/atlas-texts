## A Note on the Word

"Lives" is the wrong instrument here, and everyone who has worked in the Dry Green eventually sets it down. The place does not divide cleanly into machinery and inhabitants. The conveyor vines are alive by any chemical definition; the lamplickers are mechanical by any behavioral one. The field convention — adopted out of exhaustion rather than rigor — is this: a thing *lives* in the Dry Green if it can be in the wrong place. The stalks are never in the wrong place. The flywheel blossoms are never in the wrong place. Anything that can be found where it was not expected, doing something the corridors did not provide for, is fauna.

By that standard the census is short, strange, and almost certainly incomplete. It runs, roughly, from the very small upward, and it ends — as everything in the Dry Green ends — with something it cannot see.

## The Wicks

Begin in the substrate, because everything else does.

The capillary channels that draw the pale dust down through the leaf-parquet are not empty plumbing. Threaded through them, sometimes singly, sometimes in skeins, are filaments of dry white tissue, finer than thread, longer than anyone has traced — the wicks. For decades they were catalogued as part of the channels themselves, a lining, an organ of the deck. Then a core sample came up with a wick coiled in it, and overnight, while the sample sat sealed in a dry box forty meters from the borehole, the wick left. It was found the next morning back in the deck, re-threaded into a channel, indistinguishable from its neighbors except that it was the only one in the district oriented north-south.

The wicks handle the dust. That is the whole of their observable behavior. Dead matter falls, is reduced, is drawn down — and the wicks sort it. Cores show the deep dust laid down not in sediment but in *arrangement*: graded by particle size, by age, by some third property that registers on no instrument, banded and woven into the strata like text into a page. Whether the wicks are workers or readers — whether they are filing the dead material or studying it — is open. The deepest cores, the ones that reach the amber lattice with its too-regular patterns, show wick-channels threading all the way down into the founding layers, and there the filaments are fossilized, glassy, still in place, still oriented, like bookmarks left in the blueprints.

They are the most numerous living things in the Dry Green, and possibly the oldest, and they have never once been observed to do anything except quietly put everything where it goes.

## The Amber Sleepers

In the deep cores — below the working substrate, in the glassy ancient lattice — there are inclusions.

They are body-shaped, in the loose way that things in amber are body-shaped: a curled form here, a splayed one there, articulated, symmetrical, ranging from thumb-sized to the size of a large dog. They are not fossils in the ordinary sense, because fossils are replacements and these are *intact* — full tissue, suspended in the vitrified lattice, dry, perfect, cool. Several have been imaged in fine detail. None matches any animal seen on the surface. None matches any other.

They are catalogued as the Amber Sleepers, a name chosen carefully to commit to nothing, because the obvious questions are not answerable and the less obvious ones are worse. They do not decay; nothing here decays. Their tissues, sampled by needle, bead the same green-black resinous fluid as the stalks, which hardens the borehole shut within minutes. And the lattice around each Sleeper is denser than the lattice elsewhere — reinforced, ribbed, deliberately *packed*, the way a fragile item is packed.

The minority position among the studiers, stated in print exactly once and regretted: that the Sleepers are not remains. That they are stock. That the Dry Green's deep layers are not its history but its warehouse shelving, and that the fauna of the surface — every animal that walks the corridors now — was at some point unpacked from down there, and will at some point be packed away again, complete, cool, filed by the wicks, waiting in the founding layers for the next time the place needs something that can be in the wrong place.

## The Lamplickers

The lamplickers are the only fauna a casual visitor will reliably meet, and "reliably" means once or twice a week, a single animal, clinging to a bright leaf-face at about shoulder height, eating.

Up close they reward the attention the place otherwise discourages. The body is the size of a spread hand, low-slung, the verdigris grey-green of weathered copper, finished in a dust-soft pile like moth fur. The head is mostly eyes — large, lidless, the matte black of unexposed film — and beneath the eyes is the feeding surface, a flat ventral plate of overlapping translucent scales that the animal presses to the glowing leaf. There is no mouth in the usual sense. There is nothing to chew. The lamplicker simply applies itself to the light, and the light, where the animal sits, *stops*.

The shadow it leaves — the small honest darkness, healing over in minutes once the animal moves on — has been studied more intensely than the animal itself, because it is the only sample of absence the Dry Green offers. Within a lamplicker's grazing patch, the deep ambient tone is fractionally quieter. The forelight effect does not occur. A leaf shadowed by a lamplicker behaves, for those few minutes, like an ordinary leaf in an ordinary world: lightless, soundless, casting and receiving in the correct order. The strong interpretation, now close to consensus, is that lamplickers do not eat light so much as eat *issuance* — that they graze on whatever exemption from ordinary accounting the place runs on, and that their droppings, if the term applies (small, dry, pale, indistinguishable from the resorbed dust, promptly filed by the wicks), are light that has been made to behave.

Which would make them quality control. The place manufactures geometry; the lamplickers go from leaf to leaf tasting it, and where they have tasted, for a few minutes, the product meets specification.

Two further habits support this reading. First: lamplickers will not feed on forelight. An animal grazing a corridor that begins to brighten ahead of its growth will lift its feeding plate, wait — visibly wait, eyes on the unsourced glow — and resume only when the light's leaf has caught up with it. They are the only things in the Dry Green that treat the breakdown as irregular. Second: their reproduction by misfold, the famous mirror-folding, is not casual. An animal approaching a misfold to divide will groom for upward of an hour first, working the dust from its pile, aligning its scales — and the two animals that emerge, one left-handed, one right-handed, ignore each other completely and forever. The handedness census, maintained for thirty years, shows the population holding at an exact balance: not roughly half and half, *exactly* half and half, corrected within days of any death. Somewhere, the books are being kept. The lamplickers are either the bookkeepers or the ledger.

During shed they do something no one has explained. They climb. Every lamplicker in a district ascends to the upper canopy before the leaves release, and they ride the strata down — clinging to falling leaves, stepping from one to the next mid-descent, unhurried, like passengers changing trains — so that when the shed lies ankle-deep and glowing on the deck, the lamplickers are standing on top of it, evenly spaced, facing in one shared direction, motionless until the substrate has drawn the last light down. The shared direction is different every shed. It has been plotted for decades. The plots converge, very slowly, on a region of the interior no survey has reached.

## The Tenant

The repeating stalks — the single stalk printed many times down a row, every copy bearing the same resin scar — also bear the same nesting hollow. The hollow is occupied.

The Tenant is bird-shaped, more or less: a rounded dust-jade body, no bigger than a fist, with folded limbs that have never been seen unfolded, because the Tenant has never been seen to leave the hollow. It does not need to. It is *in every copy of the hollow at once* — one animal, distributed down the row like the stalk itself, every instance breathing in unison, turning its head in unison, regarding visitors with one synchronized eye from forty hollows across three hundred meters. Leave food at one hollow (it accepts shed-leaves, taking them with a small precise motion and pressing them somewhere beneath itself) and every instance is fed. It is, the studies note with care, extremely calm.

Once or twice a year, when the breakdown has its season and the repeating stalks fall out of sync — each copy briefly doing something slightly different, the choir losing unison — the Tenant becomes plural. For a few hours there are forty Tenants, or four hundred, individually animate, and they do the only thing the Dry Green's fauna ever does loudly: they sing. Not loudly by any outside standard — a dry, ticking, interleaved chorus, like rain on paper, like a roomful of clocks disagreeing — but it is the only voice the place has, and the corridors carry it for kilometers. The song's structure has been transcribed. It is round-shaped: each instance singing the same phrase offset from its neighbors, the offsets shrinking hour by hour, the round tightening, until the phrases coincide — and at the moment of coincidence the stalks are back in unison, the place is singular again, and the Tenant is one animal in many hollows, silent, calm, fed.

The cautious interpretation is that the Tenant rides the stalk's repetition the way the lamplickers ride the falling leaves — an opportunist of the local physics. The incautious interpretation, voiced late in field seasons, is causal: that the stalks do not fall back into sync on their own. That unison is *performed*. That the Tenant is not a passenger of the repetition but its maintenance — the place's tuning fork — and that the reason no one has witnessed the transition back to singularity is that the transition is the last note of the song, and the song is always, precisely, over.

## The Wading Things

These exist for one day at a time, a few times a year, and only during shed.

When the corridors fill ankle-deep with fallen glow, things move in it. They are seen only as disturbance: a V-shaped wake in the luminous drift, a path of stirred brightness, the glow heaping and parting around a body that is not there — or that is there and is made of the same fallen light, distinguishable from the medium only by its motion. Reaching into a wake yields nothing: cool drift, papery leaves, a faint pressure like the memory of a current. Instruments record a moving region where the shed-light is dimmer by a few percent — as if the wading thing were *condensed* shed, fallen light that has pulled itself together and gone walking.

They travel the corridors all through the shed-day, unhurried, branching at junctions with apparent purpose, and they converge. By the day's end, the wakes in any district have all bent toward the same few points — always points directly above a misfold — and there the disturbance circles, tightens, and sinks, the glow drawing down into the deck hours before the substrate resorbs the rest. What goes down at those points has not been recovered by coring. What the misfolds do with a delivery of self-collected light is not known. But the next growth to emerge through those misfolds, the translated growth with its handed symmetry and its unnameable green, comes up brighter than anywhere else, and the lamplickers travel surprising distances to graze on it first.

The wading things may not be animals. They may be the place's way of carrying something from the season to the workshop. The field name persists anyway, because everyone who has stood in a shed-filled corridor and watched a wake approach, slow down, and pass deliberately around them — *around*, with room to spare, the way one steps around a stranger — has come away unwilling to call it weather.

## The Translated

Inside the misfolds themselves, in those few-meter regions of misfiled geometry, there is fauna that exists nowhere else, and the honest description of it runs up against the limits of description, because the creatures are built in the misfolds' grammar and the grammar does not export.

What can be said: they are small. They show, like the leaves there, all of their surfaces at once, so that an observer sees something like an animal turned conscientiously inside out without harm — fur and organ and the spaces between organs presented simultaneously, in polite disagreement, in a color one degree past green. They move by methods that the eye refuses on review: footage shows them in one place and then another with the intervening frames depicting, by every analysis, both. They are not fast. They are *adjacent*.

They never leave the misfolds — with one exception. Occasionally a translated creature walks out, and in the walking it becomes ordinary: its surfaces close up, its color settles, its anatomy resolves into something legal — and the something it resolves into is, every documented time, a lamplicker. Standard pattern, standard size, correctly handed for whichever side of the census is light that week.

This is the observation around which the modern theory of the Dry Green's fauna has crystallized, and it is stated in the literature with unusual flatness, as if flatness could contain it: the misfolds are workshops, and the workshops have a product line. The animals of the Dry Green are not residents of the factory. They are *output* — finished geometry given legs and sent into the corridors — and what looks like an ecology is a warehouse floor on which the inventory has been built well enough to walk itself to its shelf.

## The Studiers

There are, at any given time, between three and seven of them. They insist there are no locals, and they have insisted on this, in the same field station, in some cases for upward of twenty years.

The station is two corridors' worth of equipment and bedding near the only surveyed edge, and the place has been adopting it for a long time. The corridors there have widened — regrown smooth and broader, sized to the studiers' habits, with a low shelf of stalk-tissue extruded at exactly the height where one of them once habitually set a sample case. The leaf-parquet under their walking routes has worn into paths and then *been maintained as paths*, resurfaced in a finer-grained compile, slightly sprung underfoot, easier on the knees. None of this was requested. The Dry Green keeps itself, and it has begun, quietly, administratively, keeping them.

The studiers are changed in the ways one would expect and in two ways one would not. The expected: they whisper habitually, even outside; they are uneasy under skies; they report that ordinary shadows, after a season inside, look wet to them, like spills. The first unexpected thing: their sense of distance comes loose. A studier home on leave will set out for a destination an hour away and arrive in minutes, or set out for the next room and feel the journey *take* — not in measured time, which stays honest, but in some interior ledger that the Dry Green has amended. They describe it without distress. Distance, they say, starts to feel like a quantity that someone, somewhere, is in charge of, and once you have felt that, you can never again feel it as a fact.

The second unexpected thing is filed in the station's own logs with what reads as embarrassment. The studiers are warm. Everything in the Dry Green is cool; the studiers' bodies, their bedding, the depressions they leave in the deck on rising, are the warmest things in the surveyed territory — and the place's thermal signature of a large warm body resting in a shallow depression is, of course, the diagnostic sign of the Resident. The station's automated survey instruments, set decades ago to flag Resident-sign, flag the studiers every morning. The flags are dismissed every morning. After twenty years, this is either a calibration nuisance or a classification — the place, or its instruments, or both, filing the studiers under the only heading it has for warm things that sleep in its corridors — and the senior studier, who has dismissed more of these flags than anyone alive, was once asked which it was, and dismissed the question.

## The Resident

Last, because it is always last; everything in the Dry Green is described before it, around it, in its absence.

The evidence, complete: corridors found freshly widened, walls regrown smooth, in arcs suggesting something between four and nine meters through the body. The deep ambient tone, once or twice a season, interrupted — not stopped, *occluded*, a hum with a mass moving through it, the occlusion traveling at a walking pace for distances of up to forty kilometers before fading. And the depressions: long, shallow, smooth-walled, pressed into the leaf-parquet, warm. Warm for days. Warm in a continent where the light gives no heat, where nothing has a temperature above a cellar's, where warmth is so foreign that the instruments built to find the Resident find sleeping researchers instead.

It is the warmth that organizes the best current thinking. The Dry Green's light gives no heat, and this has always been treated as a property — but light without heat is not a property, it is a *removal*. Somewhere, the accounting says, the heat of a continent of perpetual illumination is going. The lamplickers eat the light's misbehavior; the wicks file the dust; the wading things deliver the shed to the workshops; everything here is collected, everything is carried somewhere. The Resident, on this theory, is where the heat is carried. It is the thermal remainder of the entire factory — every degree the light declined to give, gathered into one slow body — and it moves through the corridors at a walking pace because it is making rounds, and the depressions are warm because it rests, and it rests because it is either very old or very burdened, and the place hides it because the place hides everything it cannot afford to lose. The sky. The interior. The one warm thing.

There is a quieter theory, held by fewer, written down by none of them while still in the field. It begins with the founding blueprints in the deep cores — patterns too regular for accident, too strange for design — and with the observation that the Dry Green has only ever made anything by growing it. It notes that the corridors, everywhere, on every survey, are sized as if for passage; that the lamplickers' shed-day gaze converges on the unreached interior; that the Tenant's song, transcribed, tightens toward a single coinciding phrase as if rehearsing for an audience of one; that finished distance, whatever that is, must be finished *for* something. And it suggests that the Resident is not the factory's remainder but its client — the one inhabitant the whole continent is a workshop around, for whom the geometry is grown, the distances pressed and shelved, the corridors kept wide and warm-floored and silent — and that everything else that lives here, the wicks and the Sleepers and the lamplickers and the Tenant and the wading things and the translated and, lately, filed under the only available heading, the studiers, is staff.

The studies stopped looking for it years ago. The stated reason is methodological. The unstated reason is recorded in a single field note, unsigned, left in the station log between two equipment inventories, and it is the closest thing to a conclusion the Dry Green has produced:

*It is not hiding from us. It is being kept. There is a difference, and the difference is the place.*
