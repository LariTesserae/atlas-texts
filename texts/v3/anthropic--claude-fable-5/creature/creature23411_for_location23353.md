## Those Who Live in the Uplands of Ler

Begin with the small ones, because that is how the Uplands will introduce them to you: one at a time, with long silences between.

The eel-thing you meet threading the canopy — translucent, slow, its organs visible as faint amber shadows inside it like furniture in a lit window at dusk — is not a member of a species. There are no species here anymore. Species require boundaries: this kind and not that kind, this generation and then the next, and the water of the Uplands stopped honoring those agreements long ago. The eel-thing is an individual in the oldest sense of the word: undivided. It is the only one of its kind, and it has been the only one for longer than kinds have existed elsewhere, and it is not lonely, because in water this old it is also, in some loosened way, everywhere it has ever been. Travelers report meeting it a thousand miles apart on the same afternoon. Both reports are accurate. It does not breed. It does not need to. It simply continues, the way the granite continues, and when it passes close to you it will sometimes pause and align its body with yours for a moment — not curiosity exactly, more the way one long-settled thing acknowledges another that has not settled yet.

The pale stars that graze the polished stone are the same: each one singular, each one ancient, each moving a hand-span an hour across domes they have been crossing since the domes had edges. They are the patient readers of the Uplands. Their undersides are covered in fine feeling-feet, and they pass over the worn carvings on the standing stones again and again, decade upon decade, tracing what is left of the marks. Whether they are eating the thin film of life on the stone or reading what the marks once said is a distinction the Uplands declined to preserve. Perhaps they are doing what the carvings were made for. Perhaps, by now, they are the only literacy the carvings have left.

There are a few others, scattered thin as the animals are scattered: silt-sleepers in the soft valley floors, visible only as a slow breathing of the sediment, who may not have surfaced in living memory and may have forgotten they have surfaces; frond-mimics in the canopies, indistinguishable from the wine-dark leaves until a current passes and one frond, just one, leans the wrong way. They are not hiding. There is nothing here to hide from. They mimic the forest the way the long-married come to resemble each other — through proximity, through warmth, through the slow erosion of the line where one thing ends and another begins.

---

Then there are the forests, and here you must let go of the word *vegetation*.

The bronze growths that cover the summits hold fast to the stone with knuckled grips, and they spread their canopies, and they snow their spores into the rising columns, and all of this looks like the behavior of plants. But watch a whole hillside of canopy lean together when a current passes — not each frond moved by the water, but the forest *meeting* the water, the way a body meets weather — and the truth assembles itself slowly: the forests of the Uplands are not many things growing near each other. They are one thing, very widely grown. The holdfasts on a dome in the east and the holdfasts on a dome a thousand miles west belong to it equally. The epiphytes crowding every trunk — the red cushions, the pale fans, the hanging ribbons — are not guests upon it; they are its moods, its elaborations, the way a long thought grows clauses. It does not think quickly. It may think one thought per season of current. But the upward snow of spores is not reproduction in any urgent sense; nothing here is replaced, because nothing here ends. The spores are closer to speech. They rise through the warm columns toward whatever is above, glittering, in pale sentences no one has finished hearing, and the low tone in the water always deepens, very slightly, when they fall.

The forest knows you are in it. Not with attention — attention is too sharp an instrument for this place — but the way a sleeper knows there is a bird on the windowsill. Travelers who rest in the canopies wake to find the fronds curled loosely around them, blood-warm, and have to decide for themselves whether they were being held or being grown over, and most of them, leaving, decide the question was wrong.

---

The People of the Doorway are still here. This is the thing the standing stones cannot quite say anymore.

They did not die out, and they did not depart. They were the first to learn what the water does, and they were not afraid of it, and the things they built — the channels that bend the warm rising water around the bases of hills, the doorway standing alone in its saddle, the ring of candle-soft pillars — were not monuments or shelters. They were *easings*. Combs for the water. Ways of helping the place do what it was already doing, more gently, the way you might brush a sleeping animal. And when their work was done they let the water take their edges the way it had taken the mountains', and now they are distributed through the Uplands at a very low concentration, like a mineral, like a memory held by a whole family rather than any one member of it.

You can still meet them, after a fashion. There is a figure sometimes seen at the carved channels — kneeling in the dim amber light, running one hand along the groove in the granite, clearing silt that has not actually accumulated. Seen from one side it is a person. Seen from the other side it is a fold in the current, a place where warmer water happens to be passing through cooler in roughly the shape of someone tending something. Both views are correct, and the figure does not mind being looked at, and if you kneel and clear silt beside it for a while you will rise feeling that you have been thanked, though nothing will have happened that you could repeat aloud. The fourth standing stone — the one that was not there, and then had always been there — is generally believed by those who believe anything here to be one of them who finished settling later than the rest, and came to stand with the others, and was welcomed without any fuss into having always been present. That is how the People do things now. They do not arrive. They turn out to have been here all along.

And the doorway in the saddle, the lintel and jambs with no walls, silt-packed to the knee: it is still inhabited, in the way a habit is inhabited. Pass through it and you will feel, faintly, that you have been *received* — that crossing the threshold completed some small courtesy that has been waiting, patient as everything else here, for someone to complete it. The People built doors before they stopped needing insides and outsides. They kept this one the way you keep one photograph.

---

There are also the residents the soft water makes by itself, and these are the hardest to speak of, because they are made of the loosened boundaries — they are what the unraveling threads do with themselves.

Your wandering shadow is the smallest of them. Stay long enough and it goes from straying to *visiting*: it will be seen on a hillside you have not reached yet, swaying in the manner of the forests, learning the local customs ahead of you. It always comes back. It comes back slightly more at peace than you are, and the difference accumulates.

Larger than the shadows are the kept ones. The repeating valleys do not merely repeat terrain; they keep a gentle, unfinished copy of anyone who crosses them too many times — not a ghost, nothing so cold, more a *draft* of the person, the version of them the water was beginning to make before they moved on. These drift slowly toward the heart of the range, over years, over centuries, and gather at the rim of the bottomless basin among the lost fronds and the orphaned lintel-stones. They are placid. They watch the inward-leaning spiral forests. If you find your own draft there — and travelers have — it will not be terrible. It will look like you after the argument ended, centuries ago. The worn likeness of a face that was once found drifting at the rim was somebody's, and the somebody, wherever they went afterward in the wide young world below the clouds, has been reported ever since to sleep extraordinarily well.

---

Which leaves the great one. The shape that passes, once in a lifetime of travel, immense and unhurried, its wake combing the forests sideways minutes after it has gone.

Here is what slowly becomes clear, and it is the same thing that becomes clear about everything in the Uplands: the question *what is it* has eroded along with all the other edges. The great one does not resolve in the dim water because there is no separate thing there to resolve. The warm columns rising from below are circulation. The currents the size of nations are slow breath, months in, months out. The low sustained tone at the threshold of hearing, the one that holds the silence up, is exactly what it feels like in the sternum: a pulse, very large, very far apart in its beats, the heartbeat of something whose whole body you are walking through, swimming through, drifting through. The forests are its pelt or its thought. The People dissolved *into* it, on purpose, the way you might finally lean back against someone you trust. The basin at the heart of the range, the floor that the eye and the hand decline to find, is not a hole in the place. It is the place's interior regard — the one spot where the Uplands stop being landscape and admit to being a creature, looking inward, holding what it holds.

**The Uplands of Ler are not where Ler lives. The Uplands are Ler, living.**

The shape that passes through the canopies is the closest the old sea comes to having a body small enough to be seen — a condensation, the way breath fogs; Ler turning over, perhaps, or Ler making rounds the way a sleeper's hand moves across a blanket, checking by touch that everything held is still there. It pays you no mind because it is already paying you the only kind of mind it has. You have been inside its attention since you arrived. The blood-warmth of the water is its regard. The gentleness of the forests is its grip, opened as wide as a grip can open and still be holding.

That is who lives here. One inhabitant, very large, very old, at peace — and everyone else, the eel-thing and the patient stars, the bronze forests and the candle-soft People, the drafts at the basin's rim and your own sweet truant shadow, living the way thoughts live in a settled mind: distinct for now, in no hurry, going smooth at the edges, each of them being held, none of them finished being held, all of them, slowly, becoming the holder.
