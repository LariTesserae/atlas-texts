**Those Who Live on the Pavement**

The first thing to say is that the question of who lives there turns out to be a question about the word *lives*, and the plain answers it three or four different ways depending on how long you are willing to stand still.

A visitor counting on their fingers will find the census short. The tufts. The pacers. The walkers. And then — depending on doctrine — the sleepers, or the plain itself, and at that point the fingers stop being useful.

Begin with the smallest.

---

**The tufts** — the silver-green hermits in the seams — are the only residents that everyone agrees are alive, and they are alive in the most begrudging way imaginable. Each one grows alone. This is not an accident of scattering; it is apparently a rule. Two tufts have never been found in the same groove, and where dust has drifted thick enough to support a dozen, you will find one, central, with a margin of bare dust around it like a parish. They grow with terrible slowness — a tuft the size of a closed fist may be older than any nation you have heard of — and they grow, as far as anyone can determine, only over flaws. A seam that has cracked too deep, a panel gone porous, a place where the slow self-digestion of the substrate has stalled: that is where the dust gathers, and that is where, within a decade or a century, a tuft appears. They do not seem to *cause* the healing beneath them so much as preside over it. When the flaw is closed, the tuft dies — quietly, going from silver-green to seam-gold over a season, until it is a small stiff brush of the same color as the ground, and then a soft place, and then nothing, taken down into the surface it served. The walkers say a tuft is a held breath. They say the plain notices its own small pains the way a sleeper does, distantly, and grows a tuft the way a sleeping body shifts its weight off a numb arm.

The flowers are the mystery. A tuft blooms perhaps once in its long life — one waxy, ground-colored flower, scentless to a human nose, open for a few days. There are no insects on the plain. There is no wind reliable enough for pollen. And yet the blooms are visited. Which brings us to the pacers.

---

**The pacers** are the bone-colored creatures, the long-legged things between hare and heron, and the more carefully they are watched, the less like animals they become — and then, past a certain point of watching, the more like animals again, which is its own kind of unsettling. They walk in straight lines for hours. They stand still for hours. They are the same warm ivory as the pavement, with the same faint translucence at the thin parts — the legs, the long ears or plumes (no one has settled which they are) — and through that translucence, in good light, which is to say always, you can see that there is no red in them anywhere. Whatever moves them is not blood.

What they do, as far as it can be reconstructed from generations of patient observation, is *attend*. A pacer's straight line, plotted on a map, almost always ends at something: a flowering tuft, where it will stand over the bloom for a day with its head lowered, not touching, after which the flower closes, apparently satisfied; or a soft region where the surface has gone milky, where it will stand listening to the ticking below with its head cocked; or, very rarely, a walker. There are accounts — consistent across centuries of the walkers' shallow recorded history — of a pacer arriving at a camp and standing six or eight hours over a sleeping child, or a sick elder, or once, famously, over a woman who was not sick at all and who died, without distress, within the year. The pacers do not threaten and cannot be threatened; they cannot be touched, not because they flee but because they are never, somehow, quite where your hand arrives. The walkers do not worship them but make a small accounting gesture when one passes — a touch of two fingers to the ground — as one might nod to a doctor on his rounds.

No pacer has ever been seen to eat, mate, or die. But they are born, if that is the word. In the soft regions, the digesting fields, observers have seen the surface gather itself over a span of weeks into a standing curd, a pale upright clot, which thins and clarifies and resolves legs, and one day steps out of the ground wet-looking and translucent as raw horn, and stands drying in the light for a day, and then walks away in a straight line. And at the other end: pacers grown old — they do go a deeper gold, over centuries, the way the buried strata do — are seen, at last, to stop walking, fold their legs, and lower themselves to the surface, and the surface takes them, over a year or ten years, the body sinking without displacing anything, the way a thought moves through a brain. The walkers say that some of the newer shapes visible in the uppermost stratum, the small folded ones, are pacers; that the plain reabsorbs its instruments and reads them, the way you might take the thermometer out of the roast.

So: sense organs, the travelers' theory goes. The plain's remaining nerves, pacing its own skin, taking its pulse, sitting with its flowers and its sick. This may be true. It does not stop the walkers' children from naming them.

---

**The walkers** are people. It is worth saying plainly, because everything else about them bends toward the plain's strangeness: they are people, a few thousand of them at most, scattered across a surface the size of a planet's face, and they are, by every ordinary measure, the principal inhabitants — and by the plain's measure, possibly weather.

No one knows where they came from, and this is not a romantic mystery but a structural one: their history has a horizon, and the horizon is the afterglow. The plain remembers weight for centuries — a path stays legible, at a slant, for perhaps three or four hundred years — and the walkers, who keep no other records, can therefore know their past exactly that far back and no further. Their oldest ancestors are the dimmest visible threads, traceries seen only in the slant hours when a brightening tide passes through and rakes the surface with contrast. Beyond those threads: nothing. They came from somewhere; the ground has finished forgetting it. They are a culture with a fixed depth of memory, like a tide pool with a fixed depth of water, and they have made their peace with it, mostly, and built their entire moral world on what the ground does keep.

Because here is the thing about living on a surface that records you: **you cannot lie about where you have been.** A walker's biography is laid out behind them in light. Everyone's is. A stranger arriving at your camp can be read — not their thoughts, but their path, which the walkers consider the more honest document. Courtship among them is largely a matter of walking each other's paths: you go back along the line of the person you might love, sometimes for weeks, seeing where they lingered (warm pools where someone sat a long time, bright as ponds), where they hurried, where they circled something and could not approach it, where their path braids with old grief. To marry is to braid paths deliberately and publicly, two threads wound for a year's walking, visible from a swell's crest, undeniable. To wrong someone and flee is nearly meaningless; you trail the evidence behind you like a wick.

And grief, among them, has a precise and terrible physics. When a walker dies, their path begins to fade — predictably, evenly, the far end first, the way the plain forgets everything, oldest first. A mourner can keep a beloved path bright by re-walking it. Many try. All eventually learn what the elders tell them at the start: that a re-walked path glows with *your* warmth, not the dead one's; that you cannot keep the dead bright without slowly replacing them; that there comes a step where the thread underfoot stops being the person you lost and starts being your portrait of them, and the plain, which keeps an honest ledger, records the substitution without comment. Most mourners stop. Some walk a dead path exactly once, the whole length of a life, and call it the second funeral, and never again.

They live lightly because the ground asks nothing else. They drink the exudate — the slow non-aqueous resin the seams give up, harvested by laying woven cloths in the grooves through a sleep and wringing them after. They eat the curd-fields, the milky digesting regions, which are sweetish and faintly waxen and entirely sustaining, a fact that troubles the walkers not at all and troubles visitors a great deal, since it means the people of the plain are nourished directly by whatever the plain is metabolizing, which is to say by the reliquary, which is to say — but the walkers wave this off. *Everything here is the same stuff,* they say. *You included, after a while.*

They sleep, in a place that has never known night, by lying down in their own shadow: the bruise-dark penumbra that pools beneath every standing figure pools deeper beneath a prone one, and — this is the tender part — the ground dims further beneath a sleeper than physics requires, a slight extra hush of light, as if the plain lowers its voice. The only darkness on the whole bright face of the world is made by the sleeping, each person sealed in a thin personal dusk exactly their own size. Walker children are taught that this is the plain's one courtesy, and that it proves the ground knows the difference between a thing standing on it and a thing trusting it.

They navigate by the sleepers below. Paths fade; the embedded forms do not — or rather, they drift so slowly, millennia to the meter, that they serve as fixed marks within any human life. The walkers have named the great visible shapes the way other peoples named stars: the under-constellations. The Coiled One. The Six Vanes. The Long Mother, a whale-sized rounded mass four strata down in the gold, visible across a hundred-kilometer region as a deeper warmth in the ground, by which whole lineages of walkers have steered for their entire recorded history. The names are navigational, but they are not *only* navigational. Children lie face-down over the clearer panels and look into the depths and ask the question children ask. The adults' formula, old as the oldest visible path, is exact and refuses both available errors: *They are not dead. They are not waiting. They are kept.*

---

Which brings the census to **the sleepers**, and to the argument that never ends.

The forms in the depths do not move. But the plain moves them — the slow turning-over, the millennial peristalsis, carries them, clarifies them, sometimes raises one a stratum nearer the light over the course of generations, sometimes takes one down into the deep gold where the shapes stop being shapes. Whether anything in them experiences this is the plain's central theological question, and the evidence is thin and strange. The ticking underfoot is densest above the largest forms. The brightening tides — the light-weather — have been tracked, by walkers with long patience and longer string, and they do not move randomly; they pass from region to region along routes that recur over decades, the same way a sleeper's eyes move under the lids in the same seasons of the night. A few walkers, the ones who sleep directly over the great under-constellations on purpose, report dreams with a particular texture — vast, calm, architectural, nothing happening in them and the nothing happening *magnificently* — and they report waking from these dreams reluctant, and rested in a way ordinary sleep does not achieve, and a little changed.

The cautious conclusion is that the distinction between alive and dead was not one the builders kept, and that the sleepers are exactly what the formula says: kept — in a state for which the surface peoples have no word because their lives are too fast to need one.

---

And then there is the last entry in the census, the one most travelers come to only after months, usually in the middle of a sleep, sitting up in their small personal dusk with the thought already finished in their head:

that the plain is the inhabitant. The only one. That everything counted above is anatomy. The tufts its slow attention to its own small wounds; the pacers its proprioception, walking its skin so that it knows where its skin is; the exudate its sweat, the tone its breath, the ticking its pulse, the curd-fields its digestion, the brightening tides the movement of its sleeping eyes. One organism, finished, the size of a world's face, lying in the light with its trillion contents held inside it like the contents of a long life held inside an old mind — not thinking anymore, exactly, but not done either. A thing that reached its final form and is now living that form out, at a pace of millennia per heartbeat, beneath creatures so quick that to it we are weather.

In which case the walkers are not residents. They are the dreaming.

The walkers themselves have heard this theory many times from many visitors and they have an answer for it, which they give patiently, the way you give an answer you have given to every generation's clever newcomer. *It remembers weight,* they say. *It dims itself under our sleeping children. It keeps our dead bright for three hundred years before it lets them go, and it lets them go oldest-first, gently, the way you let go of anything. A thing that wanted nothing would keep nothing.* The plain, they say, is finished — but finished the way a story is finished, not the way a stone is. It needs to be walked on. Not for repair; the pacers and the tufts handle repair. It needs the footsteps the way a sleeper, even in the deepest stage of the night, keeps one thin thread of hearing tuned to the house — to the small sounds of the people moving in it — because that is how the sleeper knows, without waking, that everything is still all right.

So when a walker grows old enough, they do the thing the visitor will eventually see if they stay long enough, and which no visitor forgets. The old one walks a final figure — every family has its own, a spiral, a long ellipse, a slow braid around an under-constellation — and at the end of it lies down, in their own dusk, on the panel of their choosing. And the ground beneath them does not dim, this one time. It brightens. Slowly, over hours, a warmth spreads up around the lying figure like shallow water, and the watchers stand back at the customary distance, and the path the old one walked closes its loop behind them, glowing whole for the last time, and the plain — patient, bright, full, nearly silent — adds one more small shape to the uppermost stratum of its keeping.

*Not dead. Not waiting. Kept.*

That is who lives there. One of those four answers, or all of them, or — the walkers' own quiet position — the distinction was never one worth keeping.
