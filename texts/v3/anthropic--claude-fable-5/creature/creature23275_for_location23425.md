**The Gardener**

Everything in the garden lives there, which is the easy answer and not a small one. The crickets of folded bark live there; the felt birds live there in their patient, relocating way; the clay mice live under the largest root and have, over the years anyone has watched, raised four litters of smaller clay mice, each generation a little better made than the last, the ears thinner, the whiskers — still real, still unexplained — set with more confidence. The breathing thing in the far corner lives there most of all, if living can be measured in continuity. But this is taking the question at its shyest. Something else lives in the Wax Garden, and the garden has been telling you about him the whole time, the way a workshop tells you about its owner: by the worn places.

He is the reason the unresolved edges have always *just* finished themselves when you turn. Not because he moves quickly — nothing in the garden moves quickly, and he least of all — but because he is wherever the finishing is happening. That is closer to a location than to a habit. The sound underneath the other sounds, the knife drawn slowly through something soft, coming from no direction: that is the sound of him, and it comes from no direction because he is not in a direction. He is in the work. If you want to find him, do not look. Hold still until your stillness has a texture, the way the quiet does, and he will be there, a little to one side, having been there for some time, pressing a shadow flat with two fingers.

He is made. Of course he is made. There is no seam you cannot find if you look, and this is true of him too — he would be embarrassed by anything else; seamlessness would be a kind of lying. His wrists are coiled rope under a skin of fine kid-paper, smoothed with slip. His knuckles are wax, and the fingerprints on his fingertips were pressed in by hand, individually, with great care — whose hand is the same question as the whiskers, and is answered the same way, which is to say by his face, which is kept very smooth. His hair is flax dyed with the same lichen-juice as the leaves, so that in the honey light he is slightly green at the temples, like something coming into season. He has ten fingers, or nine, or ten. He breathes like the animals breathe, on warmth alone. He has never drunk anything. He has never been thirsty. When he works, he holds his tools the way the tools have been held for so long that the holding shaped them: the awl is worn to exactly his grip, which proves either that the awl was made for him or that he was made for the awl, and the garden, characteristically, declines to choose.

His eyes are the one place where you cannot find the seam. They may be the last real part of him. They may simply be where the workmanship is finished. These are not the same thing, but in the honey light it is hard to say which would be the more remarkable.

There are two stories about how he came to live here, and the garden keeps both, the way it keeps two doors.

In the first story he is the maker — the one whose paddle-marks are still in the soil, who built the eleven or nine or eleven trees up from rope and clay with his palms. In this story he stayed too long with his own technique. The garden learned to grow by being made of making, and he, being in the garden, was made of it too; and as parts of him wore out, he replaced them in the only materials to hand, which were the materials of his craft, until the replacing was complete and nothing original remained except possibly the eyes, and possibly not even those. In this story the work continued *without him* in the most literal sense — there stopped being a him for it to be with — and yet nothing was interrupted, the way a song re-sung is still the song. He gave his whiskers to the mice somewhere in the middle of this. They were the last of his beard. He does not appear to miss them. The mice take very good care of them.

In the second story he is a visitor. He came through the door — the larger draft of it, or the smaller — and stayed an afternoon, and his footprints grew tidier than his feet, and the creases of his clothes folded themselves along their veins, and at some point the tools fit his hands so exactly that not picking them up began to feel like rudeness. In this story the felted thing in the corner, dog-sized, warm-colored, breathing and breathing, is the visitor before him: one who lay down to rest and was crafted past the point of getting up, not unkindly, the garden working on him as it works on everything, leaf by leaf, until rest became his species. The Gardener tends it like a hearth. He turns it, very gently, once a season. He has never said which story is his, and the suspicion — this is the part that is harder to say — is that he holds both the way the northeast corner holds two directions: incompatibly, healthily, bearing fruit on each.

His days are the garden's days. In the morning he resolves edges — he goes to the periphery of what anyone has recently looked at and decides the foliage's species, creases the uncreased folds, primes the paleness behind any shadow that has curled. At noon, when the wax creaks, he walks among the fruit with his thumb-burnished plum mold and his roll of needles, not using them, mostly, just letting the fruit know the standard it is ripening toward. In the afternoon he makes one thing. A leaf a week, a fruit a month, but every afternoon some portion of one thing: a vein, a stem, the bloom of dust laid on with a brush of three hairs. He never finishes anything. This seems to be deliberate, and it may be the most important fact about him. Finishing is the one technique he has refused to learn, or refused to teach the garden, and it is why the place is safe in the way it is safe: nothing here can be completed, including you. He will tidy your footprints. He will stitch the edge of your shadow — he has already done it; it was while you were watching the birds; it is his neatest work, and he does the edges first. But he cannot finish you, and he would not, for the same reason a potter does not fire the pot she is still in love with throwing.

Through the south door — either of it — the workbench is still visible, and the figure at it is still folding something green, even while the Gardener stands beside you in the garden with his hands at rest. He does not look at the door. If you ask him about it, he answers in his voice, which sounds like paper relaxing, and what he says is that the garden keeps a few drafts of everything and hasn't yet discarded the rejects, and that he tries not to take a position on which of them he is.

He does not eat. He does not sleep, exactly, but in the deepest part of the warm afternoon he sits down against the largest tree and goes still in a way that has grain to it, like wood, and the mice come out and sit on him, and the garden's sound rises a little, the tick of copper, the taut thread, the page turning in another room, as though the work were being passed briefly to everything else. Then he stands, and presses the seat of the soil flat again with the wooden paddle, fresh as a thumbprint in dough, and takes up the awl that fits him, or that he fits, and goes back to the edges, where there is always something not yet decided, patiently, with no water at all, a leaf a week, forever, or until the next visitor stays an afternoon too long — at which point, in one of the two stories, he will finally be allowed to lie down in the far corner, warm-colored, and breathe.
