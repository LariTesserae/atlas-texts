What lives on the Anvil Plateau lives the way the plateau itself runs: on schedule, in the dark, with nothing to spare and nothing missing.

**The mats first**, because everything else is built on them, and because they are stranger than they look. From above — if anything ever saw them from above — each vent would wear its mat like a target: concentric rings, banded by temperature, the innermost ring nearly black and tough as boiled leather where it crowds the hot throat, the outer rings rusting through ember-orange to a pale ash-rose at the cold margin where the dampness gives out. They are not plants. They are not exactly anything singular. Each ring is a consortium — chemistries stacked on chemistries, the inner organisms eating the raw vent gases and excreting what the next ring out can use, waste handed outward band by band until at the far cold edge the last thin crusts are living on the leavings of leavings. The whole arrangement is a refinery laid out flat on stone.

And they may not be many things at all. Down in the warm fractures, below where any grazer crops, the mats of neighboring vents grow toward each other through the plumbing and meet, and fuse, and stop being neighbors. The fauna of the surface experiences the plateau as an archipelago — islands of warmth and food separated by dry dark crossings. The mats experience it as a body. When a chain-firing runs down a major seam, forty kilometers of vents brightening one after another, the mat does not learn of it sequentially, the way the animals do, by sound arriving staggered through the dark. It feels the pressure-front pass along its own length, the way you feel a shiver. The oldest seam-mats may be among the oldest living things anywhere — not old the way a tree is old, accumulating, but old the way a river is old: the same shape, continuously, with everything in it replaced ten thousand times.

After a chain-rain the mats *flush* — a brief metabolic bloom, the surface growth doubling in hours, soft and wet and rich. Everything on the plateau knows this. Most of the plateau's calendar, such as it is, is organized around it.

**The grazers** are the ones you will meet, in the sense that they will know you are there long before you know they are. They are round — almost comically round, kettle-bodied, the shape that loses the least heat — and densely furred in two layers, a wiry outer coat that sheds the mineral dew and an under-layer so fine and packed that a grazer in good condition is, thermally, nearly invisible from a few meters away: a warm thing wrapped so well it barely leaks. They stand perhaps knee-high. Their legs are short and their feet are broad and soft-soled, and they move across the gravel with almost no sound, which is not timidity but doctrine.

What they have instead of sight is *time*. A grazer's mind is, by mass and by use, mostly timetable. It knows the schedule of every vent on its route — and the routes are long, a circuit of eight or twelve or twenty vents walked over and over for a lifetime — not approximately but to the breath: it sets out across a dry gap of three kilometers in total darkness and total dryness and arrives at the far vent in the last minutes before it sighs, so that the first warm condensation beads on stone that the grazer is already standing on, drinking as it forms. To watch a band of them cross a gap is to watch confidence with no visible source: a dozen round shadows in single file, navigating by the texture of the thrum underfoot and the memory of every previous crossing, betting their water on an exhalation that has not happened yet. It almost always happens. The plateau is reliable. That reliability, compounded over evolutionary time, is the grazers' entire fortune.

They keep contact by clicking — sparse, spaced, conversational. Each grazer's click is individual, a signature struck off the hard palate, and a band moving through the dark is performing a continuous, very slow roll call: *here. here. here.* A click unanswered is the worst sound on the plateau. The band will stop, and wait, and click again, and if there is still no answer they will not search — searching in the dark gaps is how you make two losses out of one — they will close the gap in the file and go on, and at the next vent they will crowd a little tighter into the warmth than usual.

The young are born into the fur, ride in it, and learn the routes the only way routes can be learned: by walking them at the back of the file, counting. An orphaned grazer adopted by a strange band is in real trouble — well-fed, watered, and lost, carrying a timetable for vents it will never see again. Some never fully recover the knack. They live at the back, following closely, owning no schedule of their own.

**The hunters** are the grazers' inversion, point for point. Long where the grazers are round, solitary where the grazers are social, and above all cold where the grazers are warm. A hunter preparing to hunt does the thing that should be impossible: it lies down in the gravel of a dry gap, along a route it has learned the traffic of, and lets its body temperature fall to ambient — which on the plateau means lethally cold — keeping only a small core alive, deep in its body, banked and insulated like a coal carried in ash. In this state it casts no heat-shadow at all. To every sense the grazers own, it is gravel. It can hold this for days. Its heart beats a few times an hour. Whatever it has instead of thought slows to a single sustained intention.

It does not watch, having nothing to watch with. It listens through its whole ventral surface to the ground — and here is its real sophistication: the ground is never quiet, the deep thrum is always there, and the hunter's nervous system performs a continuous subtraction, holding the model of the plateau's ordinary texture and attending only to the remainder. Footfalls are remainder. Twelve sets of soft footfalls in single file are remainder with a shape. The strike, when it comes, is the loudest event a grazer will ever experience and most of them experience it exactly once: the cold gravel becoming a body, the body already warm — the hunter floods its own tissues with banked heat in the final second, because cold muscle is slow muscle — and then it is over, and the file has scattered, clicking frantically, and re-formed, one signature short.

A hunter kills perhaps once a month. The rest is waiting, and the waiting costs almost nothing, which is the whole design. They are not cruel and not relentless; they are the most economical animal on the plateau, predation pared down to its accounting.

**The diggers** you will never see whole. A blunt head at a seam, a gray flank turning in a spoil-cone, gone. They are naked-skinned, loose-jointed, built for the warm fracture systems just under the basalt cap, where they live in the plateau's actual plumbing, in darkness so complete that even heat-sense is redundant and they navigate by taste and pressure. They graze the mats from the *inside* — the buried body of the consortium — and they tunnel constantly, compulsively, and this is the thing about the diggers that nothing else on the plateau knows:

they are the maintenance.

A vent's throat silts. Its feeder fractures clog with mineral scale. Left alone, over centuries, a vent strangles — the sigh grows thin, the schedule falters, the damp fan shrinks, the mat retreats ring by ring, and the grazers strike it from their timetables. But the diggers, following the warmth and the buried mat, ream the fractures as they feed. Their tunnels keep the micro-seams open. A vent with a healthy digger colony breathes on schedule for ten thousand years; a vent that loses its diggers is a vent dying, though the dying takes longer than any grazer's memory, so the grazers experience it only as a route slowly going wrong. The diggers do not know they do this. They are eating. The plateau's industrial regularity — the reliability the entire surface fauna has mortgaged itself to — is partly geological and partly the side effect of a million small blind animals keeping the pipes clear because the pipes are where dinner grows. The gears lapped smooth against each other include these gears. None of them was made. All of them mesh.

**And then there is the one that walks the chain.** You could spend years on the plateau and not encounter it, and most of its neighbors never do; there may be only a few hundred alive at any time across the whole tableland. It is large — the largest thing here, long-legged and slow, gaunt under a coat like felted wire — and it does not keep a route. It keeps the *seams*. It walks the great fracture lines, and it does almost nothing but listen: hours at a time pressed low against the rock, the whole length of its body an ear, reading the deep pressure-language that precedes a chain-firing. It can feel a major firing building six or ten hours out, from fifty kilometers away, and it sets off, unhurried, and arrives in the last hour, and stands in the blood-warm rain when it comes, and skims the rich first flush of the mats, which is nearly the only thing it eats. Then it listens again, and walks again. Its whole life is a connect-the-dots of eruptions across a continent of dark, decades long.

The grazers have learned what it means. They cannot hear what it hears, but they can hear *it* — its tread is unmistakable — and a band whose territory it passes through will sometimes abandon their timetable, the one thing they never do, and follow at a distance, because where the walker goes, warm rain follows. It does not lead them. It does not acknowledge them. It is not a prophet, only a better instrument, trailing its small congregation of opportunists through the dark.

**What none of them do is build.** The spoil-cones are the closest thing to architecture the plateau has, and they are tailings, not intention. And yet the place is dense with structure — you just cannot see it, because all of it is kept in living memory. The routes are structures. The vent schedules, held redundantly in ten thousand round furred heads, are structures. The click-signatures, the inherited crossings, the hunters' subtraction-models of the ground's ordinary voice, the walker's seam-maps decades deep — these are the plateau's cities, rebuilt nightly by being remembered, invisible, weightless, and old. Nothing here has ever raised a wall. Everything here has inherited a map.

So when you stand in the dark and hear it — a click, and a hundred meters off, a click in answer — understand what you are hearing. It is not noise in a wasteland. It is the census of a settled country, taken continuously, in an economy where the currency is heat and the libraries are alive, on a plain where the only lights are the engine's own portholes, glowing red across the black, on time, on time, on time.
