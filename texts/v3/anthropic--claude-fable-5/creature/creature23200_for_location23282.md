The first thing to say is that the people of the plateau were hired, originally, and stayed for other reasons.

The installations need tending — fins combed of moss, bearings greased on their decades-long schedules, the bright pipe walked and inspected meter by meter — and so there have always been keepers, one or two to a station, scattered the way the stations are scattered: never close, never out of sight. Perhaps two hundred of them across the whole region. Fewer, some seasons. The company that built the towers still sends wages up the line, and the keepers still send water down it, and both sides have long since stopped pretending this is the whole of the arrangement. The water could be gathered with half the staff. The keepers know it. They stay anyway. The plateau has a way of sorting people: the ones who need to hear themselves leave within a season, usually without explaining, because explaining would mean speaking and by then they have noticed how speech dies at the lips here, how the moss takes their voice the way it takes a dropped wrench. The ones who are relieved by that — who feel something unclench in them the first morning they shout an experiment into the fog and the fog declines to answer — those ones stay, and some of them stay forty years.

You can identify a keeper at a glance, even down in the lowlands on rotation. They are pale in a particular way — not unlit but *over*lit, skin weathered by glare rather than sun, fine creases raying out from the eyes where the squint has set permanently. They wear their smoked glass on a cord even indoors, out of habit. They walk slowly and they cannot be startled; the reflex has gone soft from disuse, since nothing on the plateau has ever once made a sudden noise. They speak in low voices or not at all, and they watch your mouth when you talk, not because they are deaf — their hearing tests perfectly — but because eighty years of practice has migrated the whole act of listening into the eyes. Lowland physicians who have examined them note resting heart rates that would alarm a cardiologist anywhere else, and note also that the keepers seem untroubled by this, as if the body had simply consulted the great flywheel and adjusted itself to local time.

Their language is light.

It began as signal code — short flash, long flash, the bare engineering necessities, *valve open*, *line clear*, *send help* — but a code used daily for three generations by people who cannot otherwise converse across distance does not stay a code. It grows. The flash-speech of the plateau now has a grammar, idioms, registers of politeness, and a poetry that no one has ever written down because it cannot be written down: it lives in rhythm and in the dusk-colors of filtered lamps, and a keeper's lamp-hand is as recognizable as a signature. There are keepers famous from ridge to ridge for the beauty of their sending — a particular old woman at the eastern fog-tower whose evening flashes are said to be worth climbing a cliff to watch — and there are courtships that have been conducted entirely in light, over years, between stations a day's walk apart, before the two parties ever stood in the same room.

The evening exchange is the social institution. As the glare softens and the dynamo houses come visible as their constellation of orange points, the lamps begin: station to station, ridge to ridge, relayed across the whole plateau in perhaps an hour. Weather. Repairs. Who is unwell. Whether the bird crossed today. A joke, sometimes, which arrives at the far stations stripped down by relay to its smallest funny bones, the way jokes travel anywhere. Then the lamps go dark in sequence, west to east, which is how the plateau says goodnight.

---

There are children now. This was not planned by anyone.

A child born on the plateau learns flash before speech, the way other children learn gesture, and some of them speak aloud rarely enough that their voices stay strange to them their whole lives — light, unpracticed, the voice of someone reading an unfamiliar language. They learn the machines the way farm children learn animals. A keeper's daughter of six can lay her palm flat against the turbine house wall and tell you, by pulse alone, whether the bearings want attention; she knows each installation's vibration the way she knows the faces of her aunts, and in a sense they *are* her aunts — the keepers name the machines, have always named them, and the names are kinship names. The great central flywheel is called the Hour, and is spoken of with the respect due an elder. The decommissioned tower that the hanging plants have taken entirely, the green obelisk dripping on its ridge, is called the Grandmother, and children are sent to her on errands that have no engineering purpose at all: to clear her base of frond-fall, to check her, to visit.

The keepers eat what comes up the line, mostly — grain and oil credited against the water sent down — but the plateau itself feeds them at the margins: the pale shoots of the third-storey ferns, picked young and bitter; mushrooms raised in racks in the warm damp behind the dynamo houses; water, always, the cleanest water anyone has ever tasted, combed out of the air by their own towers. When a keeper dies, they are given to the moss. There is no other phrase for it and no other practice. The body is laid in a cleared place and the moss closes over it within two seasons, and the grave is unmarked except that the green there is, for many years, slightly deeper than the green around it. The moss takes everything. The keepers find this consoling rather than otherwise.

---

Of the other inhabitants, the honest census is short, and the keepers keep it anyway, with the seriousness of people who understand they are watching something begin.

The pale beetle of the third storey is logged at every sighting — date, station, behavior — in a ledger going back sixty years, and a sighting is considered mild good luck, the way the first swallow is elsewhere. The black pools are visited the way shrines are visited: each station has its pools, and each keeper makes the rounds, kneeling at the moss-fringed edges, recording what the still water holds. For decades it held nothing. Then a thread of green algae. Then — within the lifetime of keepers still working — small translucent animals, smaller than a grain of rice, swimming in the clear dark with a flickering, rowing motion. These are, as far as anyone can determine, the first true natives: the first things that arrived, settled, and began making more of themselves with no opinion at all about the towers or the keepers or the rock's youth. The pool ledgers are the most carefully kept documents on the plateau. The keepers understand that they are reading, a droplet-creature at a time, the first sentences of a food web that will take ten thousand years to finish, and that the moss laid the paper for it, and that they will not see the end. They find this consoling too. It is the local temperament.

The bird is logged also, and argued about, gently, in lamp-light. One bird, grey, long-winged, crossing at great height on still afternoons, never landing, for eighty years. The rationalists among the keepers hold that it is an office rather than an individual — that the crossing has passed from bird to bird the way the keeping passes from person to person, and that the plateau simply cannot tell its grey birds apart. The others do not exactly disagree. They only point out that no one has ever seen two.

And there is the thing the keepers do not log, because there is nothing to log: that on certain nights, with a palm flat against bare rock far from any machine, some of them feel a pulse that is not the Hour and not the turbines — slower than both, irregular the way breath is irregular, coming up from the fissured dark where all the rainwater goes. Asked about it, a keeper will neither confirm nor deny. They will say that the rock is young, that young things move in their sleep, and then they will change the subject, in light, with a small dry flash that is the plateau's equivalent of a shrug.

What lives in the Bright Quiet, finally, is patience — wearing moss, wearing alloy, wearing smoked glass over slow grey eyes. Everything up there is practicing the same thing at different speeds: the water-fleas at theirs, the keepers at theirs, the flywheel at its single revolution an hour, the moss at a green centimeter a decade, the rock at the pace of rock. They are all waiting together, companionably, in no hurry, in the silence and the enormous light, for the plateau to grow old — and not one of them minds that it won't happen while they're there to see it.
