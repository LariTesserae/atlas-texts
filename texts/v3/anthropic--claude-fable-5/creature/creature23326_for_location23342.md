**Who Lives in the Roost**

Begin with the ceiling, because the ceiling is where the room keeps its heart.

The swifts are not, in any honest sense, land animals. They are creatures of the open sky who have made exactly one concession to solidity, and this room is it. Outside, they live the way almost nothing else lives: months on the wing without landing, feeding above the clouds, drinking by skimming, sleeping in shifts of the brain while the body rides the night thermals, mating in freefall. Their feet are an afterthought — four toes all swung forward, good for gripping a vertical surface and good for nothing else. A swift grounded on flat earth is in trouble; it cannot walk, can barely launch. So the species long ago made a bargain with verticality, and this room honors the bargain better than anywhere else on the mountain. Here a body built for nothing but air can hang itself up in the dark and stop.

They roost in a hierarchy you could read if you had years to watch. The dome's crown — warmest, driest, farthest from the entrance draft — belongs to the established pairs, birds that have come back to the same fist-sized claim of stone for a decade or more, that recognize it in absolute blackness by echo and by the smell of their own old nests. The dome's margins belong to younger birds, the two- and three-year-olds, who jostle and displace each other nightly, working their way inward over the years toward the center, the way ambition works anywhere. And clinging at the colder edges, near the slot, are the yearlings — last season's children, back from a first migration that took them over deserts and seas, holding the worst real estate in the room and glad of it.

The young are the room's strangest residents because of what they have never seen. A swift chick in this chamber hatches in the dark, is raised in the dark, fattens in the dark on insect-balls carried back from a sky it has to take entirely on faith. It hears the world before it sees it: the colony's murmur, the clicking, the twice-daily roar. Its first experience of light is also its first experience of flight — there is no second try. One evening it lets go of the wall, drops toward the grey slot, and falls out of the only world it has known into a sky so large it has no edges, and from that moment it may not touch anything solid again for two or three years, until it comes back to this exact room and hangs itself on the cold margin of the dome. The room is the parenthesis at either end of an aerial life. Everything in between is wind.

They navigate the chamber by clicking — a dry, doubled tick, tongue against palate, the echoes mapping the dome and each other. Thousands of birds clicking at once should be chaos, and isn't; each bird hears its own returns the way you hear your own name across a loud room. In the deep hours the clicking thins to almost nothing and the colony breathes more or less together, a slow communal swell you can feel against your face, and the ceiling becomes what it functionally is: a single organism with twenty thousand hearts, idling.

Below them, the bats. The second nation.

The bats and the swifts are doing, in essence, the same job — straining insects from the air — but they have divided the sky by clock rather than by territory, and the room reflects the treaty. The bats hold the lower walls, the warm seams where the mountain's breath comes through strongest, because small bodies bleed heat and the breathing-cracks are radiators. They are long-eared, crumple-faced, hardly bigger than a thumb with wings, and they hang in clusters that tighten in cold weather like a drawn purse. Twice a day, at the changeover, the two nations pass each other in the slot — bats pouring out as swifts pour in, or the reverse, two rivers running opposite directions through the same channel, never colliding, each navigating by its own sonar through the other's. It is the only time the nations touch, and they touch only as turbulence. For a few minutes at dusk and dawn the air in the entrance carries both kinds of voice at once, the swifts' audible clicking and the bats' calls pitched above hearing, and the slot is the loudest silence on the mountain.

In winter the swifts are gone entirely — south, over the equator, scattered across the skies of another hemisphere — and the bats inherit the whole room. They go torpid in the warm seams, hearts slowed to a few beats a minute, and the chamber falls as quiet as it ever gets: just the drip-less sweating of the walls and the small, constant sound of the floor.

Because the floor never sleeps. The floor is the third nation, and the oldest.

The guano cone is not a deposit; it is a country with a vertical geography. Its summit, where the fresh droppings land, is the rich coast where everything wants to live: dark scavenger beetles in their thousands, working the surface in slow tides; the pale cave crickets, long-legged, long-antennaed, blind, who climb the lower walls at night to scavenge what the colony drops and who are themselves the floor's principal export — the only residents who ever leave, feeling their way out the slot on warm nights to graze the moss, and back before the cold can kill them. Below the surface, in the older strata, live the detritivores — mites beyond counting, springtails, fungus-eaters mining guano laid down when the handprints on the wall were fresh. And hunting through all of it, the floor's apex animals: centipedes the color of old ivory, eyeless, reading the world entirely through antennae and air-pressure, striking at the vibration of a footfall smaller than a grain of rice. There are pseudoscorpions here the size of this letter o, riding from place to place clamped to the legs of the crickets, paying no fare.

Some of these animals exist nowhere else. This is the quiet astonishment of the floor: there are beetles in this cone whose entire species — every individual that is, was, or will be — lives within a circle twenty steps across. Their ancestors washed or wandered or rode in on a bird ten thousand or a hundred thousand years ago, and the dark took them and remade them: bleached them, blinded them, lengthened their antennae, slowed their metabolisms to match a world where food falls from heaven on a schedule but nothing else ever changes. For them the room is not a shelter or a roost. It is the universe, complete, with a warm wind from below and a rain of plenty from above and no rumor whatsoever of a mountain, a sky, or a sun.

The floor also keeps the dead. Every resident of the ceiling ends here eventually — the chick that loses its grip, the old bird that doesn't wake, the migrant that came home spent. The cone receives them and the beetles attend them and within a season they are stratum. Dig down through the cone — no one ever has — and you would read the colony's whole history in bone: layers of swift, layers of bat, here and there a feather mineralized in place, going down and down past the age of the handprints into guano laid by birds whose species may not have been quite these species, in a room that was already old.

Smaller still. Each swift is itself an inhabited place: flat-bodied louse flies sliding between feathers like commuters; feather mites grazing the barbs of single plumes, whole lineages of them born and dying on one wing without ever crossing to the next bird. When the colony pours out at dawn, the room is launching not twenty thousand animals but twenty thousand habitats. And on the wet flowstone, the pale film that almost glows is the room's deepest resident of all — mats of microbes that need neither the sun nor the colony, eating the chemistry of the mountain's own breath, the only life here that would persist if every wing fell silent. They were the first tenants. They are the landlord's family, if the warmth is the landlord.

At the threshold live the diplomats — residents of the border between dark and day. The moss fringe, drinking the collision of warm wet breath and cold light. The one fern, which is in its way the loneliest organism on the mountain: a plant of damp lowland gorges, its spore carried up here on some ancient updraft or some bird's wet foot, sustained ever since by the room's exhalation, growing crooked toward a slot of sky, the sole green citizen of a nation of the blind. And in the upper corner of the slot, rebuilt every night, a single orb-weaver's web, strung to catch the moths that come up the mountain's warm breath in summer darkness. The spider takes a small tax on the river of life passing through the entrance and asks nothing else.

Outside, the room supports residents who never enter it. A pair of peregrines holds the cliff a kilometer down-ridge, and they have learned the slot's schedule the way fishermen learn a tide; the evening return of the colony is their harvest, and a few swifts per week is the rent the ceiling pays for the safety of the other twenty thousand. The falcons live by the room without knowing the room exists. They know only that the mountain produces birds, here, twice a day, reliably, as if the stone itself were spawning.

And there is the resident who is no longer here: the one who climbed for days, lit something in the dark, pressed three red hands to the wax of the wall, and left a broken antler haft on a ledge as if meaning to come back. The room has kept the appointment open. The flowstone is closing over the first handprint with the patience of a thing that has never once been in a hurry, and when it finishes, the print will not be erased — it will be inside, held in translucent stone, an inhabitant at last in the only way the room confers permanent residency: by absorption.

Which is, in the end, the answer to the question of who lives here. Three nations and their hangers-on — the ceiling, the walls, the floor; the parasites, the microbes, the fern, the spider, the ghost. But spend an hour in the dark with your eyes useless and your sternum thrumming, and the divisions dissolve. The swifts are how the room touches the sky; the bats are its night watch; the floor is its stomach; the film on the wall is its oldest memory; the warmth rising through the cracks is its blood. Every resident is an organ of one slow animal the size of a chapel, breathing through a slot in the gneiss, digesting the daylight world secondhand, ten thousand years old and not yet middle-aged.

What lives in the Roost is the Roost. Everything else is just how it does it.
