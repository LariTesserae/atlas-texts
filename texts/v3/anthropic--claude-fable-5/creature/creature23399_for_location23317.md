Begin the census from the top, because that is how the chamber is organized — not by kind but by altitude, like rent.

**The high city: swiftlets.**

The uppermost tiers, where the travertine aprons are driest and the dimness is thickest, belong to the cave swiftlets, and the swiftlets are barely creatures of stone at all. They are creatures of air who have made one concession — a single cup, two inches across, woven from their own hardened saliva and a few of their own feathers — and that cup is the only solid object in their lives. Their feet are nearly vestigial; they cannot walk, cannot perch on a branch, can only cling to the lip of the nest they made from the inside of their own bodies. Everything else is flight. A swiftlet that is not on its nest is in the air, and a swiftlet in the air is in motion, and so the chamber's most numerous residents touch their home for only a few hours a day and spend the rest of their existence as a fast soft absence somewhere over the hills outside.

What lets them live this high, in the brownest part of the dusk, is that they speak to the room. Coming in through the slot, where the gold light dies after a few wingbeats, each bird begins to click — a rapid dry ticking, like a fingernail run along a comb — and reads the echoes off the wet curves. They navigate the chamber by interrogating it. The travertine, having no flat faces and no hard edges, gives back rounded, gentle echoes, and the birds have grown up inside that acoustic softness; released into a square room they would be lost. Their map is made of the same patience as the walls. When the stone thickens — a fingernail a year — the echoes shift by a fraction, and the birds' map quietly thickens with it. They are the only residents who can hear the chamber growing.

Each pair knows its own nest among hundreds by position alone, the way you know your own door on a corridor of identical doors. They land on it from below, a soft upward stall, the page-turning sound, then stillness. A swiftlet on its nest in the high dark, eyes half closed, is the most motionless thing in a room full of motionless things, and it will be airborne over the valley twenty seconds later. They live entirely at the two extremes — total speed, total stillness — and possess nothing in between.

**The middle city: swallows.**

Below the swiftlets, on the broader shelves where a little of the door-light still reaches, live the mud-daubers, and they are a different civilization entirely. The swiftlets are ascetics; the swallows are burghers. They build big — gourd-shaped nests of mud pellets, a thousand beak-loads each, carried in from the basin rims and the soft ground by the entrance — and they build loud, by the chamber's standards: a low conversational churring runs along their tier at dawn and dusk, neighbor to neighbor, the only continuous voice in the room that is not water.

They are commuters. They live by the door-light and the day, streaming out at first warmth to hunt the air above the ferns, streaming back as the dimness cools. And they have made the cave's strangest bargain without knowing it: they build with the cave's own unfinished body. The mud they gather from the basin rims is half mineral, already proposing to be stone, and so their nests fossilize faster than any others — the lowest, oldest swallow nests are no longer mud at all but pale apricot rock, perfect in every pellet, harder than the wall they hang from. A swallow building a new nest on top of its grandmother's petrified one is laying soft mud on its own monument. None of them notices. Each generation churrs, builds, raises three nestlings in the gourd's dark throat, and is replaced, and the wall keeps the receipts.

**The floor: the gleaners.**

Everything the two cities drop — guano, eggshell, feather, the occasional nestling that does not survive its first fall — lands on the open pale ground between the basins, and that fall is an entire economy. The guano cone below the densest nests is the floor's one fertile country, and it is worked day and night by beetles the color of wet coffee, round-backed, unhurried, who till it the way oxen till a field. Around and beneath them, smaller: springtails like animate dust, pale mites, threadlike worms in the warm damp at the cone's base. The floor-dwellers are the chamber's recyclers and they are dressed for the dark — washed-out, wax-colored, many of them blind or nearly so.

Among them move the cave crickets, and the crickets are the floor's aristocracy. Long-legged, ghost-pale, with antennae two and three times the length of their bodies, they do not so much walk as read — the antennae sweep ahead in slow arcs, touching, translating, and the body follows the report. A cricket crossing the open emptiness of the chamber's middle, antennae writing in the air, is the floor's equivalent of the swiftlet's clicking: another resident interrogating the dark, at one ten-thousandth the speed. They eat the beetles' leavings and the beetles' young and sometimes the beetles, and are eaten in turn by anything above them, and they persist in numbers anyway, because patience, here, is a survival strategy available to everyone.

**The basins: the enclosed.**

The olive fish are the chamber's strangest citizens, because they are citizens of an accident. Washed in as eggs in some flood, or carried as eggs on a heron's foot generations ago, they now live whole dynasties in bowls of warm water the size of a washbasin. They rise, sip the surface — taking the springtails and midge larvae that fall from the air-city above — and drop again. That is the visible part of a life otherwise spent hanging in three inches of mineral warmth.

They are pale for fish, their olive gone toward ivory along the belly, and small — the basins ration them. And their world is closing. Each basin is raising its own rim, drop by drop, and the day will come when a rim seals and a pool becomes a chamber within the chamber, and whatever lineage of fish lives there will go on rising and sipping in a stone bubble for as long as the seep feeds it. In high water — the wet season's one extravagance, when the seam runs faster and the basins brim — the boldest fish flick over the rims into the next pool down, and so the dynasties mix, barely, a refugee or two a year. The basin fish are the only residents whose territory is actively, visibly being walled. They show no sign of minding. Possibly a fish that has never seen open water has nothing to compare a stone bowl to. Possibly that is true of everyone here.

**The crab.**

There may be more than one. You will only ever see one — apricot-colored, the exact shade of the iron-stained stone, the width of a thumb and forefinger making a circle — and it may be that the chamber only supports one, and that the crab you see is the crab there is.

It is, in that case, very possibly the oldest individual animal in the room. Freshwater crabs in warm stable water can live a long time, and nothing here hurries it: it sidles the basin rims, picks at the algae film, takes a drowned cricket when one offers, and retreats into a hollow under the lowest terrace when the snake is abroad. It has lived long enough to molt many times, and it leaves each cast-off shell where it sheds it, and the mineral film does to the molts what it does to everything: coats them, thickens them, makes them permanent. Somewhere along the rims of the lower basins stand four or five small apricot statues of the crab's former selves, in order of size, glazed into the stone — a series of self-portraits the artist has no idea it is making. The crab walks past its own museum daily.

**The dark fold: bats.**

In the deepest crease of the ceiling, where even the brown-gold dusk cannot reach, hang the bats — a dozen, fifteen, a loose constellation, never a crowd. They are insect-eaters, small, soot-and-cinnamon, and they run the chamber's other shift: when the swallows pour in at dusk, the bats unhook and pour out, passing the birds in the slot like traffic in a narrow lane, neither acknowledging the other. The chamber is therefore never without sleepers. Its population is a wheel — birds resting while bats hunt, bats resting while birds hunt — and there is no hour, day or night, in which someone in the room is not dreaming, if bats and birds dream, which the stillness here makes easy to believe.

The bats are the only residents who groom constantly. Hanging, they wash like cats, working the wing membranes, and now and then one detaches, flies a slow circuit of the chamber for no purpose anyone can name — checking, perhaps; tasting the air's temperature — and resews itself to the fold a hand-span from where it started.

**The terror: the racer.**

The snake lives in a crack behind the lowest travertine curtain, and lives is almost too active a word. It is the color of weak tea, slender as a bootlace, and it spends the great majority of its existence coiled in the warm dark doing the metabolic equivalent of nothing. It eats perhaps a dozen times a year. A nestling swallow, a fledgling swiftlet that fell short, once in a great while a bat picked off the low edge of the fold — each meal buys it weeks of stillness.

But when it moves, it is the chamber's one fluency. It climbs sheer wet stone that nothing else can climb, finding the micro-ledges by feel, pouring upward in a way that looks less like a body moving than like a line being drawn. It hunts by absolute patience — wedged motionless near a flight-lane at dusk, head raised into the dark, waiting for the brush of wings, striking at the sound. The colony fears nothing else. Its presence is the one fact that shapes the cities above: the lowest tiers stay sparse, the favored nests crowd the smoothest, most unclimbable aprons, and the whole vertical architecture of the wall — who builds where, and how high — is, in a sense, drawn around this single resident's reach. The racer is one animal, slow, mostly asleep, and it is the chamber's zoning law.

**And then the landlord.**

Stay long enough — let your eyes finish adjusting, let the dusk's temperature change once — and you begin to suspect that the census above has missed its largest entry.

The glassy film of warm water on the back wall is not bare. It is inhabited, every square inch of it, by the smallest residents of the room: mats of cyanobacteria and mineral-loving microbes, a living skin a few cells thick, tinted faintly green-gold where the seam catches the light. And these are not passengers on the stone. They are part of why the stone happens. As they live — taking what dim light leaks in, breathing in their fashion, altering the chemistry of the water that crosses them — they coax the dissolved limestone out of solution. Calcite settles on them, around them, because of them. They grow up through it and are entombed by it and grow up through it again, and each cycle leaves a layer.

Which means the chamber's walls are not merely being deposited. They are partly being *built*, the way the nests are built — secreted, layer on layer, by something alive. The swiftlet makes its cup from its own saliva; the film, in its own slow chemistry, is doing the same thing at the scale of the whole room. The chapel is somebody's shell.

This is the resident that has been here longest, will be here last, and is never not working. It has no shape of its own — its shape is the chamber. Every shelf, every apron, every half-poured curtain that you took for geology is also, in thin alternating bands too fine to see, a biography. The birds build on it, the snake climbs it, the crab grazes its lowest reaches, the fish live in bowls it raised — every other resident lives in, on, or off the body of this one. If the chamber has a landlord, it is a film one cell deep, and the rent is paid in carbonate, and it has never once missed a day.

**The full census.**

So: who lives here? Count the swiftlets, the swallows, the bats, the racer, the crab and its possible kin, the dynasties in the basins, the beetles and crickets and the animate dust, the green-gold skin on the weeping wall.

Then notice that the count, here, never goes down. The chamber's one rule — nothing is taken away — applies to its dead as firmly as to its stone. The fallen nestling on the open floor is worked by beetles, then glazed by the creeping film, then absorbed; the crab's cast shells stand in their row; the petrified nests hold their tiers; somewhere in the lower wall, under a fingernail-a-year of ivory, lie the small sealed shapes of swiftlets that fell a century ago and were never removed because nothing here is ever removed. Outside, the dead are scattered, weathered, carried off. In here they are filed. The walls are an archive of every resident the chamber has ever had, in order of arrival, and the archive is still accepting submissions.

What lives here, finally, is a population that only grows — half of it breathing, half of it bound into the apricot stone, and no firm line anywhere between the two. The living build on the dead, the dead become the building, and the warm seam keeps weeping, *tik... tik*, signing everyone in.
