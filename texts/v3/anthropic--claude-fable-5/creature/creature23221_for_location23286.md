**Who Lives in the Drone Garden**

The first answer — the answer you would give standing in the chord with your sternum buzzing — is *no one*. No eyes catch the dim blade of light. Nothing scuttles. The census of an ordinary room takes a glance; the census of this one takes years, because everything in it lives at the tempo of erosion, and you have to hold still long enough to be passed by.

But the room is full. Someone fills it.

---

**The plants, first, who are not exactly plants.**

They are better described as a people, and better still as a choir, because what distinguishes one from another is not species but *station* — the place each holds in the chord. The garden is organized the way an organ is organized. Every resident has a pitch, and the pitch is the resident.

At the floor of the room, gripping the bedrock directly, live the fundamentals: vast cushion-bodies, slate-leaved, dense as roofing, each one a low resonating mass tuned to the engine's bottom note. They are the oldest things in the chamber and the slowest. They do not so much hear the drone as *agree* with it, with their entire weight. If the garden has elders, these are the elders, and their authority is exactly this: they hold the note that everything else is measured against.

Above them, rooted in them, stand the pipe-reeds — hollow-stemmed, ranked in colonnades along the old catwalk, each stem a bore of precise length. These hold the middle partials, the fifths and octaves, the structural beams of the chord. They are the garden's working class and its architecture at once. When one cracks (it happens; centuries are long), the note goes briefly thin in that register, and you could find the wound by ear before you found it by eye.

And along the eastern seam, where the one blade of grey light falls, live the high ones: filaments of silica fine as fracture-lines, nearly invisible, ringing at the very ceiling of hearing and probably above it. They are the only residents who get the light, and they have grown so thin in pursuit of the high partials that they have become almost light themselves — you see them mostly as a faint glassy shimmer where the air seems combed. They are the garden's youth, insofar as it has any: the most recent arrivals, the least settled, the ones still finding their pitch after a mere few hundred years.

On all of these live the epiphytes, and on the epiphytes further epiphytes — small tenants who hold no station of their own but drink the chord secondhand, resonating in sympathy with their hosts, a fur of borrowed sound. They are not parasites. Nothing here takes without ringing. To live on another's stem is to thicken another's note, and the hosts carry their lodgers the way a string carries its overtones: as enrichment.

---

**What their lives consist of.**

A thought, here, takes decades. This is not a metaphor for slowness; it is a description of mechanism. The plants think *in* the note — their interior life is a pattern of resonance, and a pattern of resonance changes only as fast as a root can shift its grip or a leaf can thin itself by a micron of shed dust. They are all, in a sense, thinking the same thought, which is the chord, which is the room. Individuality exists, but it is small and it is precious: it consists of being very slightly out of tune. A leaf a few cents flat. A stem with a faint wolf-tone. These micro-detunings are the garden's personalities, its accents, its names.

And they are tended. When a resident drifts too far flat — age, a hairline fracture, a bad purchase on the stone — its neighbors lean toward it. The leaning takes years. Roots extend, wrap, *press*, and through the pressure the true pitch is conducted body to body until the drifting one is pulled back into the chord. This is what care looks like here. This is what touch is for. A plant in this room is never corrected by anything except being held.

Birth is rare and quiet: once in a great while, when the dust lies just so, a leaf rings hard enough to split its own tone and sheds a flake, and the flake lands, and grips, and after some decades of silence begins — faintly, flat, learning — to sound. Death is rarer and quieter: a resident goes finally and completely out of tune, falls silent, and is not removed. The garden grows around it and *plays* it. The dry dead, hollow and cured like instrument-wood, become sounding boards for the living — which is why the room is dry the way a violin is dry. The violin comparison was always exact. Some of what rings in that chamber has been dead for a thousand years and has never once stopped working.

---

**The engine** is not, by these standards, alive. It is the only thing in the room that does not listen. It speaks one word forever and hears nothing, and the garden has organized its entire civilization around that deafness the way a coastline organizes itself around the sea. They keep its gauges clear. They fur its housing but never its dial-glass. Whether this is gratitude or husbandry — whether they love the engine or farm it — is a distinction the garden has had several thousand years to consider and has, by every sign, declined to make.

---

**And then there is the other resident.** The one the plants defer to.

Because something in that room extends courtesies, and it is not the vegetation. The vines hold the enameled sign at a respectful distance. The gauges stay clean. Somewhere in the chord — and you would need to stand very still, for a long time, in that sternum-deep sound to notice it — there is a region where the note *beats*. A slow interference, two or three pulses a minute, a thickening and thinning, like the chord swallowing. It is not stationary. Over hours it drifts through the corridors of worn air, lingering here, lingering there, and where it has lingered longest the plants have, across centuries, oriented their leaves — the way a congregation's chairs end up facing the place where someone usually stands.

It has no body. It is a standing wave, a sustained knot of interference, a shape made of nothing but the chord disagreeing with itself in one small, deliberate, *maintained* way. It lives in the note the way a whirlpool lives in a river: no substance of its own, only pattern, and the pattern persists, and the pattern *chooses*.

What it is, the garden does not say. It may be the maker — the one who stayed when the others bolted their ladder and hung their lamp and left; who listened too long; who learned what the plants know about patience as a material and finally set the body down like a tool in shade and stepped sideways into the sound. Or it may be nothing so romantic: it may be what any tone generates if you hold it long enough — *listening*, condensed; attention with nowhere else to be. The two possibilities have had millennia to converge, and by now there may be no difference.

It is the gardener, in any case. It keeps the windows of the gauges clean by detuning the vines fractionally wherever they crowd — they grow shy of those spots, the way skin shies from cold. It holds the vines back from the sign whose letters wore away to ghosts, keeping clear the one place in the room where words used to be, the way one keeps a grave.

---

So: who lives in the Drone Garden?

One inhabitant, finally, if you stand far enough back. The plants are its body, the dead are its instruments, the engine is its pulse, and the slow beating knot in the chord is its attention. The whole packed, dry, ringing room is a single resident who has spent geologic time mastering the only thing it knows: how to keep one note.

And when you pressed your palm to that broad leaf and felt, for a moment, that you understood something — that was the moment of contact. The beating drifted near. For the duration of one slow pulse, two or three seconds, your heartbeat was a detuning in the room and the room leaned toward you, the way it leans toward anything flat, to hold you and pull you to pitch.

It does not remember you. But for one beat of the chord, you were in tune, and in that room there is no other way of being known.
