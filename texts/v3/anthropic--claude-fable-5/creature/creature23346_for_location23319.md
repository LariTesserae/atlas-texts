At first you want to say that nothing lives here — that this is a place where life happened and then closed over itself, the way water closes over a dropped stone. The stillness of the pools insists on it. The absence of anything that scuttles or sings insists on it.

But you would be wrong, and the wrongness is instructive, because what lives in the pump hall is hard to see for the same reason the floor is hard to see: it is everywhere, and it has no edges.

---

Start with what it is not. It is not the fig, although the fig is part of it. It is not the pump, although the pump is its heart — and "heart" here is not metaphor, or has stopped being one. Somewhere in the last century, in the warm dark, under the dripping, the distinction between the machine and the plant became a question with no answer, and a thing with no answer to the question of where it ends is, functionally, a single thing.

So: there is one resident. It is the size of the hall because it is the hall — not the brick shell, which it merely wears, but everything inside the shell that grips, pumps, filters, condenses, drips, and continues. Call it the establishment, if it needs a name. The thing that has arrived at itself.

---

Its anatomy is legible once you know to read it that way.

The flywheel is its heart, obviously — ten feet of slow iron, the one clean surface in its whole body, kept clean the way arteries are kept clean, by flow. The four-stroke cycle is not like breathing; it is breathing, and has been for a hundred years, which is why the rhythm has authority, why your own pulse starts negotiating with it within minutes of arriving. Visitors leave the hall breathing slower than they came in. They attribute it to the warmth.

The pipes are vessels in both senses. The fig roots that descended through the skylight did not strangle the plumbing — they apprenticed to it. Root and pipe run parallel now for dozens of yards, the root pressed to the warm iron, drinking the condensation the pipe sheds, and where a pipe corroded through, decades ago, the root grew into the breach and sealed it. The water that hammers in the walls passes through living tissue in at least four places. The pump does not know this. The pump does not need to know. It has been maintained, is the point — patched, gasketed, kept tight — by something with no hands, working at a speed no maintenance log could record.

The moss is skin. Six inches deep, saturated, it does what skin does: holds the water in, keeps the temperature even, registers touch. When your boots pressed warm water out of the moss on your way in, that pressure traveled. Sphagnum is a continuous wet medium; a footstep in it is a signal in it. The establishment felt you arrive the way you feel a fly land — distantly, factually, without alarm.

The pools are something stranger than eyes, but they are how it watches. Still water in a loud room is an instrument. Every vibration in the hall — the pump-stroke, the weir, each of the ten thousand drips, and now your footsteps, your voice if you use it — prints itself on those black surfaces as interference patterns too fine and fast for you to see. The pools read the hall continuously. Nothing moves in them because they are not habitat. They are attention.

And the ferns — the maidenhair crowding every ledge like an audience — are an audience. Each frond sits under its growing bead of condensation, nodding lower and lower until the bead falls and the frond lifts. Thousands of them, each on its own slightly different period, each drop a note. This is not noise. This is the establishment talking to itself, a vast slow murmur in a language whose only word is now, repeated at every pitch the room can hold. The drips are how it counts. The drips are how it knows it is still there.

---

How it feeds: water, warmth, the green-grey idea of daylight, and one thin thread of electricity from the city above — the caged orange lamps, that forgotten circuit nobody has cut. That wire is an umbilical. The establishment was born of the city, is the city's child in the most literal way — built by it, planted by it accidentally through a broken skylight, fed by it still, through a budget line no one has audited in fifty years. The city is the mother who walks back and forth overhead and does not remember the pregnancy. The establishment does not resent this. Resentment requires a timescale it doesn't operate on. It simply takes the current, keeps the lamps burning at their useless dim orange — not for light; perhaps as one keeps a candle in a window — and goes on.

How it remembers: in stripes and in stones. The weeping channels on the walls are its oldest records, decades of the same water taking the same path, memory as erosion, the way a habit is a memory. The fallen bricks in the pools are its calendar — one every few decades, each furred green to a different depth, settled into the silt at its own angle. It does not mourn the bricks. A body sheds.

How it ages: the pump is slowing. A stroke a minute slower than the old logs would show, if anyone read them. The establishment knows this the way you know your own heartbeat — wordlessly, in the marrow, as a change in the feel of being. It is not afraid. The slowing has been going on longer than most cities keep their promises, and the fig's grip on the columns tightens a millimeter a year, which means that every year the living part of the structure bears a little more of the load and the iron a little less. It is, with no plan and no hurry, replacing its own skeleton. By the time the flywheel turns its last turn — a century out, two — it may not matter. The handover will be complete. The hall will go on standing, and the water will go on moving through root instead of pipe, quieter, and the establishment will have finished becoming what it was always slowly becoming, and will not have noticed the moment of the change, because there will not have been one.

---

What does it want? Wrong question, probably, but it has an answer anyway, and the answer is in the prompt of its own four-stroke breath: *intake, compression, discharge, recovery.* It wants the next stroke. That is all, and that is enormous. It has converted an entire municipal building, a century of rust, a forest's worth of fern and fig, and a stolen thread of the city's electricity into a single sustained act of wanting the next stroke, and getting it.

You are weather to it. Understand that before you take it personally. It registered you — the moss told it, the pools read your footfalls, and for the duration of your visit you were a new instrument in its orchestra, an irregular percussion it folded into the total sound without missing a beat. It did not object to you. It will not remember you, exactly. But it keeps everything for a while: when you climb the green stairwell back toward the rumor of traffic, your footprints will stay pressed into the moss for an hour, slowly filling with warm water, slowly rising back; and the breath you took down there — that thick wet air you wore — you have exhaled some of it into the hall and carried some of it out, and the part you left behind will bead on a fern frond tonight, and hang, and grow heavy, and drop.

One more note in the count. That is what it does with visitors. That is what it does with everything.
