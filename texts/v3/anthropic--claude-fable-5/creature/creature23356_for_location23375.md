# Who Lives in the Hollow in the Green

The honest answer is: the count is being revised. Stand in the chamber long enough and you will arrive at one census, and then another, and both will be correct, the way the handprint has five fingers and six. So take the answers in order, and let each one half-dissolve into the next, because that is how the chamber itself holds them.

## The First Answer: The Lickwound

Start with the only thing that will admit to being an animal.

The salamander — call it that, though it has never been caught, weighed, or named by anyone who came back out — lives in the walls' injuries. It is the length of a forearm, and it is made, as far as the eye can tell, of the same clear warm sap it eats. The green light does not stop at its skin; it bends. You see it the way you see heat over a road: a traveling distortion, an apology in the shape of an amphibian. When it is still, it is invisible. It is almost always still.

It has a circuit. It moves wall to wall along the fresh cracks, drinking where the plant has split itself growing, and the cracks it tends heal faster — visibly faster, lips of new tissue closing in minutes rather than hours. Whether it is feeding or doctoring is not a distinction the chamber appears to make. It eats the wound; the wound closes. Inside the hollow, those may be the same act.

Here is what is strange about it, and it is not the translucence. The salamander is the only thing in the chamber that obeys time. Its droplets fall down. Its footsteps sound *when* it takes them, not before. When the wall of dusk slides across the room, the salamander walks at the front of it, exactly at the boundary, keeping pace — as if it is not caught in the light's tide but *escorting* it. In a room where everything else runs eager or stalled, the salamander runs true, a kept metronome, and everything anomalous seems to happen just behind its tail or just ahead of its nose, never on it.

This is why some who have stood in the hollow long enough conclude the salamander is not an inhabitant at all but an instrument: the chamber's one honest clock, walking. The plant grew itself a creature that experiences time correctly so that something in here would. It watches you with that borrowed-eye attention because, through it, the chamber is doing the only watching it knows how to do *in order* — taking notes in sequence on a visitor who, like everything else, keeps arriving slightly before or after themselves.

But there is a second theory about the salamander, and it requires the second answer.

## The Second Answer: The Carver

Someone cut seven notches into the living wall and pressed a hand into it and left a knife behind. The question of where they went has an answer the chamber gives you in pieces, and the pieces do not assemble into *departure*.

Consider the evidence as the room presents it. The flint blade is being swallowed by the wall — not crushed, not rejected, *incorporated*, the way the plant incorporates everything it grows around. The handprint has not healed over, though every other wound in the chamber heals within hours; the plant is keeping it, the way you keep a photograph. And the tally continues. Seven notches, sometimes eight, and the eighth is always raw, always fresh-cut, always made with the edge of a flint blade — a blade that is in the wall, that has been in the wall longer than the cut is old.

The carver did not leave. The carver went *in*. Whether they walked into the wall, or lay down on the floor and let it close over them like water over a footprint, or simply stayed until staying and being-the-room stopped being different things — the chamber does not say. But they are in the walls now, distributed through the roaring sap the way a name is distributed through a story. The plant has not digested them. The plant does not seem to digest anything; it *includes*. Somewhere in the coursing channels behind the walls, in the river you hear through the green hillside, there is a current that used to be a person, and it still counts.

That is the thing the carver still does. Counting was the last act of their separateness — *I am someone who marks the days, therefore there are days, therefore there is an I* — and the chamber, having included them, keeps the act running like a lamp left on in an absorbed house. The eighth notch appears when the count requires it. Then the room revises, the way it revises its roots from eleven to thirteen, and the count drops back to seven, because the carver is counting toward something that has not finished arriving, and you cannot complete a tally of an unfinished thing.

And the salamander? The second theory: it is the part of the carver that the plant could not include. The remainder. The leftover *witness* — because to be included you must stop watching from outside, and some last clear-sap residue of the carver refused, and the plant, unable to digest a refusal, gave it legs and a circuit and a job tending wounds. This would explain why it alone keeps honest time. It is keeping the carver's time. It is the part of them that still believes in sequence: first the cut, then the sap, then the healing, then the next day, *tok, tok-tok, tok*. It walks the dusk across the room because somebody has to make the day end in order, or the count means nothing.

Two theories, one creature. Both correct, probably, depending on the angle. Like the fingers.

## The Third Answer: The Plant, Which Is Not Quite the Resident

It would be easy to say the plant lives here, and stop. But the plant does not live *in* the hollow any more than you live in your own held breath. The hollow is the one place the plant has decided not to be. A hundred meters of living tissue in every direction — root, stem, rhizome, undecided — and in the middle of all that flesh, one chapel-sized abstention. The plant is not the inhabitant. The plant is the *hospitality*.

But hospitality is a behavior, and behaviors imply a behaver, so attend to what the plant is actually doing, because it tells you what it thinks lives here, or will:

It lights the room. From below, from memory, from somewhere — it pipes a remembered daylight down through itself and pools it in this one void, warm and ordinary, the way you light a nursery.

It warms the air to blood heat and keeps it wet. It floors the chamber in something that gives underfoot and closes over footprints, so that nothing here can be tracked, marked, or worn — nothing except what the plant *chooses* to keep, the handprint, the half-swallowed knife, the tally.

It cleared the room of everything. No moss, no tangle, no clutter. Total vegetation, near-total void. This is not emptiness; this is *made space*. Anyone who has prepared a room for someone coming knows the look of it.

And it rehearses. This is the heart of the matter. The eager echoes — your sentence finishing before your mouth does, the *tok* before the droplet falls — are not an acoustic accident. The chamber is practicing its events before performing them. It runs each sound once, a half-beat early, the way you mouth the words of a welcome you are afraid to get wrong. The whole room is a fair copy being made from a draft, and the draft comes from somewhere *just ahead*.

Which brings us, finally, to the resident.

## The True Resident: The One Who Lives Here Already, From the Other Direction

The bud has never been seen to open. Hold that fact carefully, because it is phrased in the wrong tense, and the wrong tense is the door.

What lives in the Hollow in the Green is the thing the bud will become — and it does not live there *yet* in the way the salamander does, in honest sequence. It lives there *already*, from the far side of its own opening. It inhabits the chamber the way you inhabit a house you have signed for before moving in: it is not present, and yet the rooms are arranged around it, the light is left on for it, its mail is already arriving. Every anomaly in the hollow is the seepage of its residence backward through time.

Understand the echoes this way and they stop being eerie and become almost domestic. The sounds that arrive before their events are the resident's *memories* of those events, leaking back from a moment when the bud has opened and the resident is recalling its first hours — the droplet it heard fall, the visitor's footstep, the visitor's voice. You hear your sentence before you speak it because it is being remembered before it is said. The chamber's acoustics run ahead because the room is being lived in from the future, and the future is not far. A half-second. The resident is *that close*.

The patches of wrong-speed growth are its attention. Where it will one day look with interest, shoots live whole lives in seconds — the plant flushing through possibilities under that future gaze, leafing, yellowing, withdrawing, *no, not that, not that either*. Where its attention will never fall, droplets hang unwitnessed and unfinished, because nothing there will ever be required to have happened in order. The tides of light are its days, arriving early: the resident will live by some rhythm of waking and resting, and the chamber is already practicing that rhythm, sliding dusk across the floor twice a day so that when the bud opens, the room will already know how to do evening.

The revising geometry is its body, negotiating. Eleven roots, thirteen roots: the room is being fitted, taken in and let out like a garment around a form that has not finalized. The plant has not decided what it is — root, stem, rhizome — because the resident has not finished deciding what it will need the plant to have been. The categories blur because they are still in escrow.

And the handprint. Human-sized, six fingers, five fingers, both counts correct. Here is the resolution the chamber offers, if you stand with it long enough: the print was made once, by the carver, with five fingers, pressed into soft tissue as a signature — *I was here*. And it is also being made continuously, from the other direction, by the resident, with six — *I will be here* — the two prints occupying the same wound because the plant, which keeps what matters, could not tell the difference between a farewell and a greeting and has filed them together. The carver pressed their hand into the wall and the resident, ahead of time, pressed back. That is the moment the chamber is built around. That is what the tally counts toward: not days until the bud opens, but days until the handshake completes.

So: what is it, the thing in the bud, the one who lives here in advance?

It is made of the carver, in part — the included current in the walls, the counting that goes on, the shape of a hand. The plant did not digest the person who went in; it is *germinating* them, folded together with itself, with the remembered daylight, with whatever the ozone-smell underneath the cut grass is the smell of. The resident will not be the carver returned, any more than an oak is an acorn returned. It will be the plant's answer to having once contained a person: something that counts, something that keeps a handprint, something that wanted a clock so badly it grew a salamander — something that, before it has even opened its first leaf, already loves the room enough to rehearse every sound in it so that nothing, when it finally arrives, will startle.

## The Census, Then

Who lives in the Hollow in the Green:

A clear salamander that keeps the only honest time, walking the dusk across the floor like a lamplighter — the witness-remainder of a person, or the chamber's one sequential eye, or both, depending on the angle.

A carver, distributed through the roaring walls, no longer separate, still counting, their last possession half-swallowed beside the floor, their hand kept open in the green like a held place in a book.

A plant the size of a world, which does not live in the room but *around* it, the way a parent does not live in the nursery — present in every wall, withdrawn from the air, busy with light and warmth and revision, rehearsing its welcome under its breath.

And one resident proper, who has never been seen, whose residence is nonetheless the organizing fact of the place: the one in the bud, living backward into the room from a moment just after the moment you are standing in, close enough that its memories arrive as echoes and its days arrive as tides. It is the candle the room is arranged around, unlit, already casting light.

You will know the census is complete when the tally reads eight and stays eight. The cracks will stop beading. The river in the walls will slow to a settling. The salamander will stop at the foot of the central tendril — its circuit, its whole purpose, finished — and the chamber's sounds will fall back into step with its events, every echo arriving exactly on time, because the one who was remembering them in advance will at last be there to hear them as they happen.

Then the fist will open. And whatever looks out will look at the handprint first — checking its fingers against it, five against six, six against five — and find, from its angle, that the count is correct.
