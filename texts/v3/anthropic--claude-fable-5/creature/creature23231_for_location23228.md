**The Citizens of During**

The people are not finished either. That is the first thing to understand about them, and the kindest.

They would not put it that way themselves — or rather, they would, but gently, with the small tilt of the head that here accompanies any statement about the future. The dialect of the summit has worn itself into a shape the lowland grammars don't quite cover: a habitual softening, a verb-mood the schoolteachers call the *provisional* and use without irony. One does not say *I am a founder*. One says *I am founding, for now*. The phrase *for now* is not hedging. It is punctuation, blessing, signature. Letters end with it. Gravestones end with it. The standard greeting, exchanged a thousand times a day on the welted streets, is simply *Still here* — said with satisfaction, the way other cities say *good morning*, because here it means the same thing.

There are, broadly, two temperaments in the city, and every household, guild, and marriage is some negotiated ratio of them: the people who cure, and the people who keep things open.

---

The founders are the curing kind. You can identify one across a room — across a street, through fog — by the way they hold themselves: deliberate, weight-forward, with the unhurried finality of the presses they serve. Foundry work selects for it, or produces it; nobody is sure which, and it is the sort of question founders don't enjoy. They are broad people, scalded pink at the wrists where the forme-heat gets past the gauntlets, smelling permanently of coal and hot iron and the strange flat mineral odor — like rain on a stove — of rock in the act of committing. Their breathing bladders give their voices a measured, valved quality even off-shift, every sentence arriving in complete strokes.

And their sentences *are* complete. This is the deepest founder superstition, observed even by the ones who claim not to hold it: you do not leave a sentence unfinished near green rock. You do not trail off. You do not say *well, if the east wall holds* — and stop. The rock remembers pressure, and the founders believe — quietly, professionally — that speech is a kind of pressure. An abandoned sentence is a proposal left lying around, and the mountain is in the business of taking up proposals. So founders finish everything. Their meals, their arguments, their marriages. Founder funerals are famously long, because nothing may be left implied. To dine with a founding family is to hear conversation like masonry: each statement set, tapped level, mortared, before the next is placed. Newcomers find it exhausting. Then they live above the pending zones for a year, listening to the ground murmur its long indecision beneath their beds, and they begin to understand the comfort of a person who, having started a thought, can be relied upon to land it.

The surveyors are the other kind, and the city needs them just as much, and the two guilds despise each other in the formal, affectionate way of organs in one body. Surveyors are lean, weathered the grey of the country they walk, and trained — actually trained, years of it — in the discipline of *withholding*. Where the founder finishes every sentence, the surveyor is taught to leave things accurately open. They do not name a ridge until it has held the same shape through three winters. They do not call a pending zone *empty*, because empty is a verdict and verdicts cure. Their field notebooks are masterpieces of refusal: *apparent saddle, tending eastward, for now. Possible water. Something that may be choosing to be a cliff.* A senior surveyor can describe a landscape for an hour without committing it to anything, and this is not evasion — it is *courtesy*, extended to ground that has not made up its mind, the way one does not finish a stammering child's sentences for them.

They carry lamps everywhere, even at noon, the small vermilion-shuttered hand-lanterns that are the badge of the guild — not to see by, but to be *intentional* by, a portable patch of decidedness to stand in while the world around them wavers. And they are the only citizens licensed to walk deep into the unfinished country, which is why they are the only ones who have been close to the provisionals, which is why their silence on the subject is the loudest thing in the city. More on that. Not yet.

---

Between the two guilds, threading the streets at dusk and dawn, go the lamplighters — and it took the city about a generation to notice that it had grown a clergy. *Illumination is infrastructure*, says the municipal code, and the lamplighters administer it like a sacrament: trimming the carbon filaments, washing the globes, replacing the festoon-wires, walking their fixed rounds in fixed order because the order itself, they will tell you, matters — a route walked the same way for thirty years presses a kind of certainty into the street beneath it. They are mostly older people, mostly quiet, and they have the best safety record of any trade on the summit, because the rock has had longest to consider them and has, evidently, decided to keep them. Children follow them. The dying ask for them. When a district's ground falls silent and must be evacuated, the lamplighters go in last, putting out the lamps one by one — never abandoning them lit, because a lamp burning over ground that has stopped deciding is, in their phrase, *a promise made to no one*, and the city does not make those.

---

The children are the most provisional citizens, in the way of children everywhere, but here it shows. Children raised near the cured center of a district grow up ordinary. Children raised on the terrace edges, above the pending saddles, are — the word the grandmothers use is *soft-edged*. Quick, changeable, hard to photograph; their faces in a year-old portrait look not younger but *less ratified*. Nobody worries much. Faces cure with age here like everything else, and you can read a person's biography in the degree of it: the newcomer's face still lowland-mobile, undecided a dozen times a minute; the middle-aged founder's face setting into its weather; and the very old, whose faces have committed so entirely to themselves that they resemble the clocktower — out of true, recorded, *finished* — and beautiful for exactly that reason. To call a living person finished is the gravest thing one can say, praise or insult depending entirely on the tilt of the head. Mostly it is said of the dead.

The children's great forbidden game is *staking* — slipping past the railings at the district edge with a stolen vermilion stake and a hand-lamp, driving the stake into green rock, pressing a palm flat beside it, and running home. They come back in secret across the seasons to check: whether the rock kept the handprint, whether stone is gathering on the stake. A child whose print deepens earns a standing in the schoolyard that no fight or money could buy. *The mountain considered me*, is what it means. *The mountain is keeping me.* The grandmothers pretend to forbid it. The grandmothers' own prints are out there, eighty years deep.

Which is, in the end, how the dead are handled. There is no burial in finished ground — that would be storage, the founders say, not farewell. Instead the dead are *proposed*. The body is carried out past the lamps to a niche cut in green rock, laid in, and left with a single lantern burning the customary forty days. Then the family returns. Sometimes the rock has closed gently over and around, taking the shape, deepening it, keeping it — and the niche is recorded, and the wall becomes one of the *kept walls*, which every district has, soft-shouldered cliffs of held human shapes, moss-grown, condensation-bright, where people go to put their living palms into the old prints. And sometimes the rock has not kept the dead, and the niche stands smooth and empty, and the family goes home and says, with the small tilt of the head, that the mountain was not finished considering — *for now* — and somehow this is survivable, here, in a way it would be nowhere else.

---

What else lives in the city lives mostly at its hem. The slate hares, which the terrace farmers trap and stew and which arrive in such steady numbers from the pending zones that the standing joke — not entirely a joke — is that they are the mountain's most successful draft, a design it finished early and liked, and keeps printing. The almost-choughs, who have learned the shift whistles better than the workers have, and whose vast lamp-lit wheelings at the change of watch are the closest thing the city has to weather it enjoys. The goats, who are universally agreed to know something. Goats walk ridgelines the surveyors haven't ratified, stand confidently on geology that is still a rumor, and have never once, in the city's records, been on ground when it decided to be absent. Old surveyors, before committing to a route, will look — not officially — for droppings.

And the provisionals.

Here is what the surveyors' silence is made of, pieced together from forty years of things not quite said in guild taverns. The large grey shapes in the deep unfinished country are not all drafts of animals. Some of them walk upright. Some of them walk routes — fixed rounds, the same way, over and over, the way a lamplighter walks, or the way a person walked in life. Surveyors who have lost a partner out past the stakes — and the guild does lose people; the pending zones are not wasteland, but they are not safe — will sometimes, years on, see at great distance through the pewter light a slow grey figure pacing a familiar transect, unfinished, unhurried, *tending*, as their notebooks would put it, *toward a particular person, for now*. They do not approach. They do not name. Naming is a kind of curing, and there is a mercy, the guild holds, in not finishing someone else's sentence — not even the mountain's, not even when the sentence has your friend's gait in it. They shutter the vermilion lamp, note the bearing, and walk on. This is why they refuse to discuss it. It is not secrecy. It is the same courtesy they extend to the ridges.

---

So: who lives in the Provisional City? Founders who finish everything, surveyors who finish nothing, lamplighters who keep promises lit, children pressing their hands into the future to see if it will hold them, goats with opinions, hares without number, the kept dead in their soft walls, and — far out, grey on grey — the drafts.

But ask them, and they will tilt their heads and answer the way they answer everything. The mountain murmurs its long sentence under their streets, the one that is not yet at the verb — and the people of the summit have come, over the generations, to a quiet collective suspicion about what the verb might be. They cure, they withhold, they illuminate, they propose. The mountain considers them, and keeps some.

They are not the mountain's inhabitants, exactly. They are its deciding. Still here, for now.
