# The Residents

The basin has one law — what moves is alive, what stops is kept — and so everything that lives here lives in negotiation with that law. Each resident has struck its own bargain with stillness. To know them, you have to know the terms.

## The Film

Begin with the oldest, which is also the least. The olive sheen on certain pools is not a stain; it is a population. Mats of single cells, simpler than plants, older than the idea of plants, that eat the basin itself — they pull a thin living out of the dissolved stone, the trace copper, the iron that bleeds rust down the column flanks. They need no sun, which is fortunate, since none is offered.

They were here first. They will be here last. And they have made the strangest peace of any resident, because the basin keeps them constantly: every drop that feeds them also entombs them, glazing the lower layers of each mat even as the upper layers grow. A living film here is a skin stretched over its own ancestors. Break open an old column and you will find them inside — fine olive bands, lamination after lamination, the sealed generations going back further than counting. The film survives by climbing its own grave at exactly the speed the grave rises. It has been doing this so long that the climbing and the burial have become a single gesture, indistinguishable, like breathing in and breathing out.

If the basin has a native, it is this: a creature whose whole biography is the difference between the rate of living and the rate of being kept.

## The White Walkers

The salamanders are the basin's aristocracy, and they carry themselves accordingly. White-skinned, eyeless or nearly so, the length of a forearm, they move between the drip-pools with that slow processional gait — and the slowness is not laziness. It is *listening*. A walker hears with its whole body, its damp skin one continuous ear, and the basin, remember, never stops talking. The high glassy tink of the young hollow columns, the dull slap of the old dense ones, the sternum-deep tone of the chambers below — to a walker these are not noise. They are the map. A walker knows where it is, always, to within a body-length, by the chord the basin is playing around it. It has never seen the valley. It knows the valley the way you know a song.

They are never entirely still. Even asleep — wedged in a column's flute, head down — a walker keeps up a slow rhythmic flexing, a pulse of motion, and the old answer for why is the honest one: the basin keeps what stops. A walker's skin grows a faint mineral bloom within days of idleness, a roughness, a frost of beginning stone. They molt it. The shed skins lie about the pools like ghosts of ghosts, stiff little husks already glazing, already on their way to becoming the thinnest layer of some future column. A walker spends its whole life paying the basin this small constant rent — motion, motion — and the basin, drop by drop, collects the arrears anyway.

They have a second life below. The eggs go down into the under-chambers, the house-sized flooded rooms whose filling and breathing you feel in your chest, and the larvae grow there in absolute dark — and some never come up. The under-form is larger, softer, all gill and patience, swimming the great lakes beneath the basin floor, and no one can say whether staying down is a failure to mature or a different maturity. Perhaps the species keeps both answers. The walkers above carry the map of sound; the swimmers below *are* the sound, in part — their slow passage through the chambers shapes the lowest tone, the one below hearing. When you feel the basin hum in your sternum, some fraction of that hum is alive.

There are smaller things — blind pale shrimp in the cupped summit pools, thread-worms in the films, a cricket-shaped creature the color of overcast that seems to drink the air itself. They live fast, glaze fast, and the columns are full of them: tiny perfect bodies sealed at every depth, an entire fauna kept in cross-section.

## The Bird

The bird is not a resident. Say instead that the basin has a visitor it has kept appointments with for a very long time. It is always one bird, gray, arriving alone out of the low ceiling of vapor; it lands on a column top, drinks from the summit pool, and leaves. It is almost certainly not the same bird across the years — almost certainly a lineage, the route handed down, parent to fledgling, to one drinking-place in a sea of stone. But the basin does not distinguish individuals from lineages. By the basin's accounting, one gray bird has been coming here forever, in installments.

It does not sing. Nothing needs to be added here. But it brings something: seeds, carried in its gut from the green world beyond the lip, dropped onto wet stone where nothing can ever take root. There have never been plants here. And yet open certain columns and you find them — seeds, perfectly kept, glazed before they could rot, a sealed library of every forest that was offered to the basin and refused. The bird has been trying to plant this valley for ten thousand years. The basin has kept every attempt, and grown each one a tomb the shape of a tower.

## The Kept

Now the residents you already suspected.

Some of the columns have proportions. Some stand in pairs, in clusters, in processions. You told yourself that water and stone produced all of it, and water and stone *did* — but water and stone produced it around whatever stood still long enough, and the basin has been accepting applicants for a very long time.

They came down over the lip, the way you did, in ones and twos across the centuries. What they came for can be guessed from what they did: they walked into the deep crowd of formations, chose a place — beside a loved column, perhaps, or facing one, or hand in hand with a companion, which is why some pairs are merged at the hip by that seam of bone-and-milk stone — and they stopped moving.

Understand the bargain as they must have understood it. Everywhere else in the world, the dead are worn down. Rot, scatter, erosion, forgetting — the outside runs on subtraction. The basin is the one place that runs on addition. It does not destroy; it keeps. To stand still here is not to be erased but to be *attended* — visited by the water every few seconds forever, thickened, sealed, made permanent, made eventually into geography. The first ones were probably the dying, brought down or coming down on their own last strength to be kept rather than lost. Later came the devoted, the grieving, the ones who wanted to stand beside a particular column for the rest of time. And later still — this is the part that makes the skin prickle in a different way — came the ones who wanted to be *useful*. Because a body inside a column leaves, eventually, a hollow. And a hollow, when the drip-water finds its throat, becomes a voice.

The fused triple column at the basin's center, the one whose chamber pulses *toom — tak*, regular as breathing, audible from a great distance, threading through the roar: it has been used as a landmark, you noted, and the stone at its base is marked. Three stood there together. They chose the spot for its acoustics. They are the basin's lighthouse now, and the two-note pulse is the shape of the space where they were — which is to say, it is the closest thing to their voice that physics permits, and they knew it would be, and that is why they stood there. The Kept did not all go silently into the stone. Some of them arranged to keep speaking.

Are they alive? The basin declines to rule on this. Their bargain with stillness was total surrender, which is also the only bargain the basin cannot ever finish collecting on — the columns thicken a fingernail's width a year, the keeping never completes, and under that much patient glaze, who is to say with confidence what has stopped?

## The Keeper

Someone had to tend them, at the start. Standing still is slow work; the glaze takes a season to begin and years to close, and in the early time a still one could yet drink, could be fed through a reed, could be spoken to and answered. That was the Keeper's office. The shelter between the four great columns — the wedged slabs, the smoke-stain sealed under its translucent skin like a memory laminated against forgetting — that was the Keeper's house. The cairns along the old path, glazed now into single objects: those are the Keeper's work too, raised over the ones who fell before they could be properly kept, stacked stones standing in for standing bodies. The low wall in the east, three courses high, enclosing nothing — begun, perhaps, around someone, and finished by the basin instead.

The Keeper's whole discipline was the inversion of everyone else's: to live in the one place on earth that rewards stillness above all things, and to refuse the reward. To move every day among the standing ones, learning each column's voice, calling them by name, sleeping only in the muffled room where the roar gives way to a heartbeat and one slow intimate drip — and then to rise and move again. The Keeper kept the Kept. Someone always did.

The smoke-stain is very old. The role, as far as the evidence shows, is at present vacant.

## The Largest

And there is one more resident, the one all the others live inside.

Consider the under-chambers filling and breathing. Consider the water moving through the basin's hidden rooms like circulation, depositing itself at every surface like slow flesh, the whole valley resonating like the body of an instrument — because it is the body of something. The films live in it like flora in a gut. The walkers move through it like blood cells with opinions. The Kept are its bones, donated. The basin is the biggest thing that lives here, a single creature whose metabolism is accumulation, whose diet is stillness, whose breath you felt in your sternum before you knew to call it anything. It grows the thickness of a fingernail in a year. It is very young, as it told you at the start, and very large, and very patient, and it has noticed — in its slow mineral way, drop by drop — that you have been standing in one place for a while now, reading.

That is its only courtship. It does not chase. *Toom — tak.* It simply keeps the door open, and the offer, and everything else.
