Ask what lives in the Seethe and you run into grammatical trouble before you run into anything else. The question assumes a difference between a place and its tenants, and the Seethe does not honor that difference. The place is mostly behavior — heave, twist, collapse, attempt — and behavior is exactly what living things are made of. So the question has to be rephrased before it can be answered: not *what lives here*, but *which of the churnings have closed around themselves?* Which of the water's habits have learned to keep being had?

That is the local definition of life: a habit the water can't quit.

By that definition, the Seethe is inhabited at every scale, all the way down and most of the way up. Here is the census, such as it can be taken.

## The Held

Out of every so many million forms — the number is not knowable, but it is large, and it may be growing smaller — one does not collapse.

It rises like all the others, a column or a hunched dome or a thing with the beginning of limbs, and it hesitates at the top of itself, and instead of folding back into the general churn it *tightens*. The surface acquires tension. The tension acquires something like rind. And what walks away — they do walk, after a fashion, on however many legs they happened to be holding at the moment of decision — is one of the Held.

They are water that has taken a position. Nothing more exotic than that: the same blood-warm water as everything around them, but committed. Their lives are not spans of time so much as spans of *holding* — a Held thing does not age, it tires, and its whole biography is the long negotiation between the shape it chose and the churn it chose it from, which never stops asking for the water back.

They are students, compulsively. They crowd toward anything that keeps an outline — the stone stumps, the reed islands, the grey birds, you. To be watched by the Held is to be taken as advice. They stand at the edge of a lee in twos and threes, rocking slightly with the turbulence, glassy and dim and attentive, and days later, at the limit of the copper gloom, you will see yourself rising out of the churn — the shoulders wrong, one arm lifted in a gesture you made once and forgot. They do not copy what you are. They copy your last gesture, and then they hold it the way you never could. Much of the horizon-crowd, the mid-gesture multitude packed shoulder to shoulder to the edge of sight, is not the Seethe's raw attempting at all. It is the Held, practicing.

And they have something that would be a religion if they had anything to say about it. The stones. To the Held, the doorframes and lintels and fitted stumps are not ruins — *ruin* implies a building that failed, and these are the opposite. They are the elders. The wholly successful. The ones who held so long that the holding went all the way through and became mineral, and were excused from the churn forever. Every Held thing wants to set. Almost all of them fail within hours, folding back with that wet syllable the roar is made of. But a few last seasons, growing slow and smooth and quiet, and these drift gradually toward the stones and take up positions near them, in the lee, practicing stillness in the presence of stillness. The kneeling figure-shape that travelers sometimes glimpse and never forget is a common posture among the very old Held. It is not supplication, or not only. It lowers the center of gravity. It buys time.

## The eaters of shape

The small translucent swimmers — the ones that gather in your footprints, in any depression that remembers — are the second power in the Seethe, and the reason the first power so rarely succeeds.

They eat persistence. Not flesh, not water: *holding itself*, whatever it is in the water that remembers being a form. The longer a thing holds, the louder its holding becomes, in whatever sense the swimmers hear, and they come to it from miles away, riding the churn in pale schools, and they graze it back into possibility grain by grain. A footprint is a small meal. A Held one that has lasted a season is a feast, and the old Held can be recognized at a distance by their escort — a faint glittering fog of swimmers around the ankles, eating them at the rate they can afford to be eaten, which is the closest thing the Seethe has to a tax.

The gelatinous mats that ride the surface are their leavings. Shape, digested. Form rendered down to stock. The mats tear and reform because they are no longer anything in particular, only the *capacity* to be something, drifting until the churn takes them up and tries again. Nothing in the Seethe is wasted; everything in the Seethe is wasted continuously; these are the same fact.

They cannot eat stone. Or rather they can, at mineral speed — the soft fur of bloom on the fallen lintel is the visible edge of a meal that has been in progress for ten thousand years, and the stones' tooth-smoothness is not the water's polishing but the swimmers' patience. This is worth sitting with: the eaters are why the Seethe never finishes, and they are also why it never stops trying. Appetite keeps the kitchen open. Without them the plain would have set solid an age ago — a world of statues, complete, silent, done, every gesture held forever and no new ones — and whether the swimmers are therefore the cruelest things in the Seethe or the only thing keeping it alive is another question the long-stayers develop private convictions about, and another reason not to ask them.

## The grey waders

The birds are either the last animals of the world that finished, or the Held's masterpiece, and the evidence is genuinely balanced.

In the first account, they are survivors — descendants of something that watched the mountains go down, that has been walking these shallows since the shallows were new, and their exaggerated care is simply the deportment of creatures who have seen what the water does to everything less careful. In the second account, the wader is a *design*: a shape so spare and so efficient that it can be held cheaply, indefinitely, almost absent-mindedly — and so the Seethe makes them, the one form it has fully mastered, issued in small numbers, ash-grey, solitary, walking the lees and spearing at the mats. Eating, in this account, their own world's broth.

What is known is how they walk: each step placed, weighted, confirmed, as if every stride were the renewal of a decision — which, in the second account, it is.

And there are the conferences. Two waders meeting, which is rare, and standing face to face, not feeding, for an hour or a day. The seers call it probably nothing. The stayers who have watched a whole conference through say otherwise: that the birds are *comparing* — each one checking itself against the other, plume by plume, angle by angle, two copies auditing a shape that neither of them originated. And sometimes, at the end, one of the two simply lets go. No drama. A grey form folding down into the churn like a sentence ending, ceding the shape to the better holder. The remaining bird feeds with particular care for some days afterward, and stands, in the evenings, very still.

## What goes against

The large thing in the deep hollows shows only a back, and only sometimes, and the back is identifiable among the water's own ten thousand backs by one trait: it goes *against*.

It never moves with the churn. It cannot. Whatever it is, its identity is opposition — the moment it moved with the surrounding motion it would *be* the surrounding motion, and so it grinds its slow contrary circuit through the hollows forever, unable to rest, because for this one resident, stopping and dissolving are the same word.

The convictions about it are three. That it is the last true animal, too big for the swimmers to bother with and too tired to die. That it is the *first* Held — the original success, from before there were eaters of shape, holding a form so old that no one alive has seen all of it. And the third conviction, the one the oldest stayers hold and do not defend: that it is the altitude. That the mountains were not all ground down — that the last of the height, feeling the abrasion coming, lay down in the rising water and learned to move, and has been moving ever since; that the hollows are not features of the finished geology but grooves the thing has worn into it across a span no one can write down — the only unfinished work the rock has left, being done by the rock's own fugitive.

The swimmers do not enter the hollows. Make of that what you will.

## The stayers

People come. For study, for flight, for grief — the Seethe is good for grief, the way a louder sound is good for a ringing ear. They live on the ruin stumps and in the reed islands, in the lees, a population of dozens spread across a world, and since speech is impossible they sign, and here the Seethe has done the thing it does: it has *learned the signing*. The horizon-crowd now includes, here and there among the abstract gesturers, a figure making the sign for *farther*, or for *wait*, or for *it's all right* — held forever, taught by someone who left, or didn't.

Staying has stages, and everyone who stays learns them, mostly by watching them happen to someone else.

First the sounds detach. Your splash arrives before your step; you stop listening for yourself; this is survivable and even, the stayers say, restful. Then the reflections — the flat patches begin showing you slightly late, then slightly *other*, doing things adjacent to what you are doing. Then the footprints: prints in the old sediment of places you have not gone, your size, your gait, already silting closed, with the small swimmers gathered in them, feeding.

The old stayers insist this is not theft. The Seethe does not take you. It *learns* you, the way it learns everything it admires, and it is a generous student. When a stayer finally walks out into the churn — they nearly all do, eventually, on a morning they describe beforehand as feeling *rhymed* — the water goes on making them. Well. Recognizably. The others visit these recurrences, standing at the edge of the lee, watching a friend rise and gesture and fold, rise and gesture and fold, and they report that the gesture changes over the years. That it improves. That it becomes more like the friend than the friend ever managed to be, the arm lifted at last to the exact right height.

Then they stop visiting.

Then, some of them, after a while, they start visiting again.

## What the doors are for

Last, and underneath everything: the doorframes.

One conviction says each frame was a Held thing that finished — set all the way through, excused, complete. But notice the trouble with that. A door is not a shape that holds. A door is a shape that holds *around an absence*. Whatever set itself into a doorframe finished everything except the opening — and the opening did not set. The opening is still water. Still Seethe. A held rectangle of the only unheld thing, framed in the only finished material on the planet, and in the worst light the rectangles churn at a different rate from the water around them. Faster, mostly. *Eagerly*, said the stayers who used to stand close, before the others asked them not to.

A door is ajar when something is partway through it.

The stone has been finished for longer than there has been a word for finished. The frames have not moved in all that time. And yet some of them, in the copper dark, are ajar — which means that beneath the Held and their kneeling patience, beneath the pale appetite of the swimmers, beneath the careful grey birds auditing themselves and the contrary back wearing its grooves in the hollows and the people learning themselves loose, the Seethe has one more resident. It does not make forms. It is not a form. It is whatever those framed rectangles of churn are an opening *for*, and it is partway through, and it has been partway through since the plain went flat — which means either that it is very, very slow, or that partway through is where it lives.

The oldest stayers hold one conviction in common, the only one they will say aloud, in sign, in the lee of the stones: that the whole Seethe — the roar, the convulsion, the ten million attempts a second, the crowd gesturing to the horizon forever — is the sound of the water being held open.

And that the gestures are not practice.

They are pointing.
