Begin with the honest count, because the town invites exaggeration: almost nothing lives here. But the almost is dense, and everything inside it has had to solve the same problem — how to live at the speed of a place that is never finished — and each has solved it differently. The town's residents are best understood as a set of answers to that one question.

Start small, in the powder itself. The construction dust that films every lane is not entirely mineral. Sift a palmful in good light and a few grains will be moving — pale motes, soft-bodied, no bigger than the period at the end of a sentence, the exact white of the walls they live among. They eat the shed powder, or sort it, or both; no one has watched closely enough to say which. What can be said is that disturbed dust does not stay disturbed. A footprint, a scuffed threshold, a handprint slapped against a fresh wall — by morning the surface has been worked smooth again, grain by grain, and it is not clear how much of that repair is the town growing and how much is the motes tidying. They may be the town's smallest organ. They may be squatters who have made themselves useful. The distinction might not mean anything to either party.

On the elder walls live the rosettes, and they deserve more than the word *flora*. Each one is a flat grey disc of paper-dry lobes, and each lobe-ring marks a revision survived: a season the town considered that wall and let it stand. The old ones have nine, ten, eleven rings. They drink only at night, unfurling by a degree or two when the air cools enough to give up its scant moisture — the only voluntary movement in the town apart from the grazers, and it happens in the dark, where nothing sees it. They cannot grow on new mineral; the fresh extrusions are too caustic, or too restless, or simply not yet *settled* in some sense the rosettes can taste. So they are obligate citizens of the old quarter, wherever the old quarter currently is, and they migrate across generations: a rosette dies into papery flakes, the flakes powder, the powder rides the night air or the hide of a passing grazer, and a few spores find an aged seam three lanes over. Their whole species moves at the pace of the town's memory, keeping to whatever the town has decided to keep. It may even work the other way around. The walls furred grey with rosettes are the walls the town is reluctant to reabsorb — and perhaps the reluctance is the rosettes' doing, perhaps a wall that has been annotated is harder to erase. They are marginalia that have learned to protect the page they're written on.

Then the grazers. There are never many — three, perhaps five; a census is impossible because they hold still so well against the walls they match. Hare-sized, smooth-skinned, blunt-mouthed, with eyes the pale grey of a sealed room's interior. They eat the architecture, and only the newest of it: the soft blade of a rising wall, the tender lip of a narrowing doorway, the fresh extrusions that are faintly damp and faintly cold with the chemistry of their own making. That dampness is the only drink a grazer has ever been seen to take. Their droppings are small compressed pellets of the same mineral, which the town reabsorbs without comment; whole walls have presumably passed through a grazer and become walls again, and neither side appears to find this disrespectful.

They are born white as a day-old extrusion, single kits, years apart, and they age the way the walls age — freckling grey, then furring. There is, or was, one very old grazer with an actual rosette rooted in the hide of its shoulder, three rings deep, carried from wall to wall like a passenger. The grazers sleep standing, in open rooms, and here is the thing no one can explain: they always choose correctly. No grazer has ever been found sealed inside a room — though it must be admitted that the sealed rooms are not opened, and the leaning almost-bell-tower, which closed its last gap before any visitor's lifetime, is exactly the size and shape that a standing animal, grown very tired, might warrant. Because the grazers do not die anywhere that can be seen. An old one simply stops grazing one season, walks to a quiet lane, and holds still. And the town, which houses whatever holds still, begins. A thin ridge rises around the resting feet. The walls come up at the pace of bone healing, which is to say at the pace of grief, and within a year there is a small windowless structure where the grazer stood, crisp-cornered, sealed, silent. Their graves are rooms. Their cemetery is the town, distributed, unmarked, indistinguishable from the rest of the inventory — unless the sealed rooms *are* the cemetery, all of them, which would mean the town has been mostly mausoleum from the start, and the grazers far older as a kind than anyone has guessed.

Above all of this, twice a year, on the same heading each time, something crosses the pewter sky. Too high to identify, too regular to be weather. It never descends, never circles, never deviates. The most modest theory is that it is simply passing between two other places and the town lies under its road. The least modest theory is that it once carried a seed, or a spore, or a thought, and let it fall into a shallow bowl of land decades ago, and that its twice-yearly crossing is a landlord's glance — checking on a long investment, noting the new walls, the leaning tower, the open square subdividing itself according to a plan only the two of them have read. If the second theory is true, then the town's truest inhabitant lives a thousand feet up and has never touched it, and the dimple in the land is the weight not of an idea but of an expectation.

And the sealed rooms themselves are inhabited, in the thinnest possible sense, by the days on which they closed. Each holds a body of air decades old, an inch of that year's dust, a temperature that has never since been revised. The sound like a held breath, when a room finally seals — it is a held breath. The town is full of them. Whatever lives at the center of all this, whatever slow chemistry or slower intention drives the extrusion and the revision, it appears to keep its memories the way it keeps its dead: by enclosure, in the dark, in perfect preservation, unconsulted.

There is one more, though calling them a resident strains the word. Someone comes down the grey scree most years — camps on the rim, never below it, having once made the mistake of sleeping in a lane and woken to find a thin ridge of new mineral tracing the outline of their body, the town already at work on a room shaped exactly like their rest. They keep notebooks. They have named certain walls. They map the revisions and have decoded nothing.

And on the eastern edge of the town, facing the slope where this person always descends, there has stood for some years now a structure shaped like nothing else here: low, long, with a wide threshold and a single doorway. The doorway does not narrow. It is the only doorway in the town that has never once begun to close. The rosettes have not touched it; the grazers do not eat it; the room inside is swept-looking and cold and empty, and it stays open, season after season, while everything around it revises.

The town houses whatever holds still. That is its whole nature, its industry, its offer. Its inhabitants are simply the things that have worked out an arrangement with the offer — the motes by living inside it, the rosettes by living on its memory, the grazers by accepting it at the end, the high crosser by never coming within reach of it.

The doorway on the eastern edge stays open. The town can wait. Waiting is most of what it does.
