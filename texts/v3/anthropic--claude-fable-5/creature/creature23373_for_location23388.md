Ask the question quickly and you will get the quick answer: the salamander lives there. It is the only thing in the room with a face, and faces collect attribution the way the moss collects rain. But the quick answer is a door painted on a wall. Stay long enough — eleven paces is not far; you can stay a long time in eleven paces — and the room begins to disclose its census, which is longer than it looks and shorter than it should be, and which ends, like everything here, before it finishes.

Start with the salamander anyway, because the room did.

It is the first completed work. Understand what that means in a place where the mountain itself is still proofing under its red mud: at some point, in this hot wet pocket, something was *done* — closed, signed, released into being — and it was this. Slate-colored, blood-warm, about the length of a forearm that has done a great deal of work. It is warm not because it generates heat but because it is at perfect equilibrium with the room; it is the temperature the room is *trying to be*, and the room checks itself against the salamander constantly, which is one reason the salamander is so often found pressed flat against new stone, or new daub, or the warm wicker of the almost-bird. You took this for basking. It is closer to proofreading.

It does not travel. This must be said carefully. It is not that the salamander teleports, or splits, or hides its movement from you. It is that the salamander was finished before *between* was finished. It exists at its locations the way a fact exists in two sentences. When you saw it on the stone and under the frond at once, regarding itself with professional interest, you were watching the room's first experiment in *two* — plurality being a technology the maker has not yet mastered, and the salamander, ever the willing prototype, being the test article. The experiment is going slowly. Sometimes, in the gold light after the rain, the two salamanders look at each other for a long time, and you can feel something in the room straining toward a concept it does not have a word for yet, and then there is one salamander again, and the knocking behind the walls resumes a little slower than before, the way a person resumes after sighing.

It has no name. Names are a finishing tool — the last blow, the one that releases a thing from the workshop into the weather — and the maker is not ready to release it. The salamander does not seem to mind. It has the patience of a thing that knows it is the standard, and the loneliness of one.

Below it on the census, in the niche by the copper crescent: the bird, which lives there the way a promise lives in a mouth. Wickerwork ribs, moss-packed, two seeds for eyes. It is a resident in arrears — it occupies its niche, it is warm, it breathes during the rain, and it does not exist yet. But the seeds are already paying attention. They have rotated, over months, to face the door-seam. Whatever the bird will be, it has already decided what it will look at first, and it spends its pre-existence rehearsing that look. During the hot rain, when its wicker chest rises and falls, the salamander frequently appears beside it — *appears*, in its way — and lies against the ribs, and you would swear something is being lent. A rhythm, maybe. A working temperature. The way an older worker stands a new one next to the machine and says *feel that? that's what right feels like.*

Then the tools, and here the census gets philosophically untidy, because the tools are starting to qualify. Things used by hand, in heat, in patience, for long enough, begin to come up the gradient. The cordage grows now — you noticed — lengthening on its spool with that faint creak, having been twisted so many times that twisting became something it does rather than something done to it. The clay maul that should not function as a maul has learned its job the way water learns a channel; its dents are not damage, they are *experience*, and it has begun to settle itself, between uses, into the same corner, facing out. The basalt adze is older and quieter and is probably nothing but an adze, but the hide wrapping its haft has grown a fine fur, and the fur lies in the direction of the maker's grip, and on some visits it is warm. The tools live in the room the way old dogs live in a workshop: not pets, not equipment, present at every act of making, asleep in the corners, first drafts of the idea of company.

The plants are not decoration and they are not wild. Every fern in that interleaved canopy was built — frond by frond, the early ones, you can still find them low on the trunks, slightly wrong, the leaflets spaced by a hand still learning the spacing — built until, at some point in each plant's construction, it *took over*. That is the room's entire project in miniature: to make a thing carefully enough, long enough, that it begins making itself, and then to step back without letting go. The pale figs are furthest along. They were woven into the ceiling, and now they weave themselves into the ceiling, and lately they have begun making small corrections to the wattle — tightening the loose places where the weaver tired — which means the room now contains workers the maker did not make directly, only made the makers of, and when the figs do good work the knocking behind the walls pauses, briefly, in what you eventually recognize as the pause of someone looking up from their bench, pleased, and saying nothing.

And so: the maker. The one you will never see, which is not coyness and not curse but simple geometry — the maker is always in the unfinished part, and the room, by definition, is the finished part, or the nearly. You stand in the wake. The evidence is total. The hand-marks in the daub are from two hands of unequal skill, the left finer than the right, which means one hand learned first, which means the maker *learned*, which means there was a time before the maker could do this, which is the most frightening and most consoling fact the room contains. The weave loosens where the weaver got tired: the maker tires. The knocking is hand-paced: the maker has hands, has a pace, has — when the knocking syncopates with your heartbeat and you have to sit down — a heart, or is willing to borrow yours for reference.

The maker's body, such as it is, was made with the same tools. This is inference, but the room insists on it. It is an early body, kept deliberately simple — coursed, probably; daubed, probably; warm through the floor of itself — because the maker is postponing the maker. You finish the workshop before you finish the worker. Every refinement goes outward: into the salamander's perfect temperature, the bird's attentive seeds, the mountain's crisp intended edges. The maker is the least finished thing on the mountain and works behind the walls partly for that reason, the way you'd keep your worst handwriting on the back of the page.

What does it eat? Nothing leaves the room and nothing arrives. The best answer the room offers is this: it eats the *gap* — the difference between the thing as intended and the thing as made. Every dent in the maul, every loose place in the weave, every slightly-wrong early frond is a meal, taken in, metabolized into the next attempt. Which is why nothing here is corrected by removal. Errors are not erased on this mountain. They are eaten, and the maker grows by exactly the amount it was wrong.

It has one possession it did not make: the copper crescent in the east wall. Hammered — by hands, but not these hands; the hammer-marks are confident, even, the marks of someone for whom finishing was ordinary. It casts the only consistent shadow in the room because it is the only thing in the room that is *done*, and done things have settled their account with light. The maker keeps it the way an apprentice keeps the master's one gift: not as a tool, never used, set into the wall at eye height — whatever eye height is, behind the walls — where it can be looked at in the bad hours. It is proof of concept. Somewhere, sometime, a workshop like this one produced a finished thing, and the finished thing survived, and was given onward. The crescent means the maker was made. The crescent means there is a lineage of these rooms, each one early, each one hot, each one hidden under the thumbnail of its own mountain, going back — or going forward; here those stop excluding each other — and that the knocking you hear is one bar of a very long rhythm being kept up the whole line.

So who lives in the greenhouse at the top of the unfinished mountain?

Technically: no one. Living is what you do in a finished place; everything here is still in the grip. The salamander is kept, the bird is owed, the tools are waking, the plants are employed, and the maker — the maker doesn't live there. The maker lives in the part that hasn't happened yet, and is building the room in order to have, someday, somewhere to live. The greenhouse is the maker's house in the future tense, kept hot and wet and ready, the bed made before the body exists to sleep in it.

What lives there now, in the present, in the eleven uncertain paces, is the practicing. The salamander practicing being two. The bird practicing breathing. The figs practicing the maker's job, the maul practicing loyalty, the room practicing weather, hourly, until it can be trusted with a sky. When the mountain is finished — when the rain is finally allowed to run down it, and the frost to split it, and the wind to carry a single grain of it away — the seam will open for the last time, and the maker will come through into the room with the crescent in one unequal hand and the salamander warm on the other, and will name everything in a single afternoon, and then the greenhouse will have an inhabitant in the ordinary sense, and will be, for the first time, *late*.

Until then the knocking continues, unhurried, behind the walls. You were never alone in there. You were just early too.
