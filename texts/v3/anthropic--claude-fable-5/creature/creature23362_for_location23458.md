**The people first, because they are older than their country.**

That is the thing to understand before anything else. Everywhere else in the world, the land is the elder — the hills were there before your grandmother's grandmother, and you live in the late chapters of a story that began without you. On the Shelf it is reversed. There are women in the Anchorage who are older than the eastern margin of the continent they stand on. There are men who remember when a particular hill did not exist, and who walk past it daily with the faint proprietary tenderness you'd give a tree you planted. The people of the plateau are not settlers of an ancient place. They are the ancestors of a young one, and they know it, and it has made them strange in quiet ways.

They date themselves by altitude. Ask a shelf-born woman when she was born and she may give you a year, for the benefit of outsiders, but among themselves they say *I was born at nineteen-sixty meters*, because the land was lower then, and every centimeter since has come up underneath her like water filling a vessel she is standing in. The elevation of your birth is carved, by custom, into the door-lintel of your family's house — a column of names and numbers, oldest at the bottom, the whole family rising together. The oldest lintels on the Shelf go back four generations and a hundred and twenty meters. People touch them when they leave the house. Not reverently. The way you'd touch the rail of a ship.

They speak of the land in the future tense. Visitors always remark on it. A farmer elsewhere says *this is good soil*; a shelf-dweller says *this will be good soil*, and means it the way you'd say it of a child — not a complaint about the present but a description of the present, which here is made almost entirely of futures. They are a people who have, collectively and mostly without discussing it, decided to be patient on a scale that has nothing to do with their own lifespans. It shows in the faces: a kind of unhurried attention, the look of someone listening to a very slow piece of music they already know they will not hear the end of.

And they have no ghosts. This is the other thing visitors feel before they can name it. There are no ruins on the Shelf, no battlefields, no place where something terrible happened long ago, no names whose origins are lost. Every name has a namer, and the namer is usually still alive, or only recently not. Marek's Pool. The Long Seep. The hill called Second Try. The whole landscape is on a first-name basis with its people, and the people, having no past beneath them, are unburdened in a way they themselves don't fully register until they travel — until they stand somewhere old and feel, for the first time, the weight of all the dead strangers a normal place is built on. Most of them come home shaken. Most of them come home.

**The work**

Almost everyone on the Shelf does some version of the same job, which is midwifery.

The water-channelers are the most numerous — the people who walk the wet seams with hand tools and patience, deepening a thread here, joining two threads there, not building rivers but *arguing on water's behalf*, accelerating by a few decades a process that would happen anyway in a few thousand years. They are recognizable by their knees, which go first, and by their habit of stopping mid-conversation to watch how a trickle takes a corner. Channeling is taught the way music is taught, by apprenticeship and ear, and the great channelers are spoken of the way other cultures speak of poets. The highest praise in the trade is to say that a cut *disappeared* — that ten years on, no one can tell where the water's own work ends and yours began.

The loam-wrights are fewer and stranger: keepers of the starter soils, the dark living cultures that the towns maintain in long heated sheds and parcel out, handful by handful, into chosen pockets of the rock. Their craft is closer to brewing than to farming — they speak of a soil being *lively* or *gone flat*, they trade cultures between towns the way bakers trade sourdough mothers, and the oldest culture on the Shelf, kept in the Anchorage and called simply the First Bed, is fed and turned and visited like a relic, because in a sense every wild handful of dirt on the plateau is its descendant. Loam-wrights are forbidden by guild custom from owning the ground their soil goes into. The soil is seeded and released. It belongs to whatever finds it.

The surveyors are the closest thing the Shelf has to a priesthood, though they'd be horrified to hear it. Every year, at the same week of late summer, the whole plateau is leveled — measured, town by town, benchmark by benchmark, to confirm that the rise is still even, still gentle, still a few centimeters and no more. The Leveling is a festival the way a harvest is a festival: it is also an inspection on which everything depends. The numbers are read aloud in each town square. The crowd's silence while the figure is found, and the exhalation after — the same every year, the rise even, the bread proving properly — is the nearest thing to a liturgy the Shelf possesses.

And then there are the wardens.

**The wardens of the roots**

Every lift-root has a warden family, settled at its base in a small stone holding, and what they do is honestly hard to describe, because what they mostly do is *maintain conditions*. The old documentation — the recipe, as everyone calls it, without irony — specifies temperatures, moisture, things that must be kept away from the root's base and things that must occasionally be brought to it, observations to be logged daily whether or not anything has changed, which it almost never has. A warden's logbook is the most boring literature on earth: forty years of *no change, no change, no change*, kept with the fierce exactness of people who understand that the day there *is* a change, those forty years of baseline will be the most valuable document on the continent.

The wardens will not tell you whether the roots are alive. This is not coyness; it is doctrine, of the practical kind. They have watched the pale columns longer and closer than anyone, they have felt the warmth come off the rock at the bases on winter nights, they have pressed their ears to the matte tooth-sheen surface (everyone does, once, usually at nine years old) and heard either nothing or something, depending on how honest they're being with themselves — and they have collectively concluded that the question is above their station and beneath their duties. *You don't need to know what the yeast is thinking,* the saying goes. *You need to keep the room warm.* Warden children grow up unbothered by enormous unanswered questions, which makes them, as adults, either the calmest people on the Shelf or the ones who leave for the lowlands to study physics and never quite recover.

In winter, the warden holdings are crowded — not with people. The animals know where the warm rock is. A warden family in deep cold may share its dooryard with three hundred geese, a standing herd of greybacks breathing in the dark, foxes tolerated at the margin, all of them pressed into the radius of warmth around the root like pilgrims around a stove, and the wardens keep the log and step over the geese and consider this, correctly, part of the job.

**The majority**

Because the truth is that people are a rounding error here. The Shelf's real population is feathered.

The geese came first and meant to leave, and some of them — nobody knows what passed in those small ancient minds — simply stopped leaving. The shelf-folk call them stay-geese and regard them with the particular respect owed to the first immigrants who committed. They are pale, loud at dawn and at no other time, and they have already, in forty years, begun to differ from their traveling cousins: heavier, calmer, their goslings hatched on islands of black glass in pools that have never known a predator that swims. They are becoming a new thing. They are the land's first invention, and the land is rather pleased with them, to judge by their numbers.

The greybacks drift. That is the word and the right one — herds of a few hundred, gray-shouldered, moving across the plateau along lines no one has mapped because the lines are still being decided, grazing the lichen coins and the cushion-plant seams, ahead of the land's ability to feed them and somehow never quite starving, living, as everyone here does, on the country's credit. They are unafraid of people to a degree that unsettles visitors. They have no reason to be afraid. Nothing here has ever hunted them, and the shelf-folk, by a custom that was never voted on and is never broken, do not start.

The foxes work for a living and look it — lean, low, fluent in the geometry of pool margins, the only resident with a predator's opinion of the world. They den in the frost-free seams near the warm rock. They have learned the towns the way foxes learn everything, thoroughly and without sentiment, and every town has one or two that are *known*, named, tracked through the years by the children, their deaths reported at dinner tables like news of a difficult relative.

And under everything, in every still basin, the invisible founders: the glass shrimp and the midge larvae and the trundling beetles, the passengers who arrived as smears of mud on a sandpiper's foot and inherited a continent. They have no names and no admirers except the loam-wrights, who understand exactly who is really terraforming this place, and it is not the lift-roots.

**The dead**

There are not many yet. That is part of what makes the Shelf feel the way it feels — a country where the dead are still outnumbered by everyone who remembers them.

But there are some, now, and the Shelf has had to decide what a young land does with its people when they stop. The answer it arrived at is the one you might guess, if you have been paying attention to the reverence for mud: the dead are given to the loam-wrights. Composted, frankly and without euphemism, into the starter beds, and carried out in spring, handful by handful, to the soil pockets — so that the dwarf willow stands, the knee-high forests, are also the graveyards, and a family visiting its dead walks out across the bare rock to a particular pocket of green and sits down in it. *Everything growing here is somebody* is a thing shelf-children say to frighten each other, and it is not false, and after a certain age it stops being frightening and becomes the entire point. The first generation to die here is becoming the first generation of soil. The people of the Risen Shelf will, in time, be the only people anywhere who can say of their country, literally and in the flesh: our ancestors are not buried in this land. Our ancestors *are* this land.

That is who lives here. A hundred thousand birds, a few thousand patient midwives of stone, an aristocracy of shrimp, fourteen families keeping a recipe warm, foxes — and underneath all of them, beginning, the dead, going dark and rich in the hollows, teaching the rock the last thing water can't teach it alone.
