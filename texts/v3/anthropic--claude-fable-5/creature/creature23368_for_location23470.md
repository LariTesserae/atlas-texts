Ask what lives in the Seep and you can take a census in an afternoon. Ask again, slower, and the census never quite closes. The hollow keeps its tenants the way it keeps everything else — in layers, the obvious ones on top.

### The Film

Begin with the oldest, because everything else here is its guest.

In the throat of the hottest spring, where the water is newborn and too harsh for anything with a body, there is that blush of orange-pink — a film of cells thinner than skin, painted up the inside of the channel like the lining of a shell. It is easy to look past. It is the only thing in the hollow that lives in the water *as it rises*, before the air has cooled it, before the lime has begun to fall out of it, before the water has become anyone else's.

It has no individuals worth the word. It is a surface, a tenancy, a color that metabolizes. Pieces of it are entombed every year as the mineral comes down over it, and the film simply grows outward over its own sealed dead, so that the stone of the spring's throat is laminated with generations the way a tree is ringed — except that nothing here marks the years apart, and the rings are all the same.

Continuity is its only biography. The film has never not been here. It was on the rock when the first terrace was a stain. The people who cut the channel saw it and likely thought it a property of the water, like the heat — and they were not entirely wrong. It does not respond to your presence. It does not respond to anything that has happened in the last ten thousand years, because nothing has happened, here, in the only sense it would register: the temperature has not changed.

If the Seep can be said to have a native, it is this. Everything else came later, or comes and goes.

### The Salamander

The hollow's one animal, and the only resident with a face.

It lives at the margin — the cool outermost basins, where seepage off the walls thins the spring water down to something a body of complicated cells can bear. This is the only address in the hollow, the single narrow band where the spring's water and the world's water mix, and the salamander occupies it the way a keeper occupies a lighthouse: alone, at the edge, between two things that cannot touch each other anywhere else.

It is pale because the light here shows nothing and nothing watches. The shadowless dimness has excused it from color, and it has accepted the excuse so thoroughly that its skin has gone translucent in places — hold still and look long and you can see the faint grey shadow of its workings, the slow valve of its heart, a meal in transit. Its eyes are dark and patient. Its hands — they are hands, four soft-fingered stars — spread flat on the white stone when it rests, which is most of the time.

It does not hunt. It attends. The hollow grows nothing it can eat, but the hollow *imports*: moths and flies that blunder in through the steam ceiling at night, drawn by the warmth, who lose the sky in the vapor and circle down and meet the water. The warm pools kill them gently and the thin sheets carry them, basin to basin, down the terraces, a slow conveyance of small drowned things arriving at the cool margins where the salamander waits. A meal a week, perhaps. In lean stretches, a meal a month. Its metabolism has been turned down to match — it lives at the pace of the dripping, and at that pace it may be very old. There is no way to know. It has never known winter, or drought, or a cold night, or a predator. It is an animal raised entirely without weather, and that is why it does not flee from you. Flight is a lesson, and nothing here has ever taught it.

You only ever see one. Whether it is one — whether the hollow holds a small scattered family who keep to separate basins and never meet, or a succession of solitaries, each replacing the last like a drop replacing a drop — the place declines to say. Some years, in the dark seep-crevices of the walls where the cool water beads, there are eggs: few, large, glass-clear, each with its pale comma turning slowly inside. Most years there are none. Of the larvae, the basins keep one, maybe. The population has never been more than a handful and has never been zero, and between those two facts the salamander's whole history runs like the film over a terrace lip — thin, continuous, sufficient.

One more thing about it. Of everything alive in the hollow, the salamander is the only one that uses what the people made. It rests, sometimes, in the square soaking pool — the human corners now half-rounded back into stone — its hands spread on the picked tool-marks under their glaze. It does not know the basin is artificial. It knows the water there is the right temperature and the rim casts a dimness it likes. The last purpose of the cut channel, after whatever hides or reeds or bodies it once served, is to please a finger-length of pale animal who will never understand that it was made.

### The Ones Who Do Not Live Here

Worth listing, because their absence has a shape.

The people do not live here. Nothing of them lives here — the cup in the niche is not an inhabitant, it is a meal, taken at the hollow's feeding rate of a coat of stone a year. The bird that crosses the steam does not land; the hollow offers it nothing and it knows. The moths that come at night are not residents but tribute — the Seep's one steady harvest from the world above, paid downward through the basins to the margin.

Fish, refused at the door by heat and mineral. Green things, murdered in advance by the water itself. Anything with seasons, anything that needs the hour told to it — the hollow cannot accommodate them, because it has abolished the very things they live by. What lives here, lives here precisely because it has given those things up.

### The Largest Resident

And then, if you stay long enough, the census reaches its last entry, the one it was circling the whole time.

The water rises at the temperature of blood. Stand in any basin and you are standing at the warmth of a living body — not metaphorically, but by the thermometer. The hollow breathes in its closed loop of steam. It has a pulse, faint, in the domed welling of the springs. It takes in and gives out. It maintains its own heat against every rain and every winter night, the way a body keeps its blood warm against the weather. It builds itself continuously — terraces, curtains, lips — and dissolves itself at almost the same rate, which is what a body does too, replacing itself cell by cell while seeming to stand still. It even eats: everything left within it, branch or antler or cup, taken in and made part of the flesh of the place.

It does everything a living thing does except change.

So the honest answer to who lives here goes in three sizes. A paint of cells in the throat of the spring, older than the stone. One pale, unhurried animal at the margin, hands spread, attending. And the warm slow body they both live inside — blood-heat, breathing its grey breath in a circle, feeding on what it is given, finished but not done.

Come back in forty years. The film will be the same color. The salamander — or its successor, and there is no telling the difference, and the hollow would not bother to — will be resting in the square pool with its hands on the old tool-marks. The drops will tick down. The only resident that will have visibly aged is the one standing in the basin, blood-warm to the knees, taking the census again.
