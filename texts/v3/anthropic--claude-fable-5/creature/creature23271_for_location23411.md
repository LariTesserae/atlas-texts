# The Ones Who Make Room

Begin with the oldest of them, because she is older than the ground she sleeps on.

Her name is a marsh-name — a soft two-syllable thing that means something like *reed-bent-by-wind* — and she was a girl on a woven platform out in the gray water when the stone began to rise. She remembers the morning the reeds tilted. She remembers her mother putting a hand flat on the platform and going still, the way you go still to hear a second heartbeat. The people of the Crowded Stone are, by descent and by temperament, marsh people: pole-and-platform folk who had lived for generations on the brackish flat, drinking caught rain, moving with the seasons of the reeds. When the pale knuckle came up out of their water-country, sweating sweetness, they did not flee it and they did not worship it. They poled over to look. Then they poled over to drink. Then, basket by basket, they moved in — onto a thing still moving, which tells you most of what you need to know about them.

So the strangest fact of the town is demographic before it is geological: the land is younger than its eldest inhabitants. There are perhaps nine people left who remember open water where their kitchens now stand, who can point at a sleeping platform and say *I caught eels there.* They are called, without ceremony, the *wet-born*, and everyone younger is *stone-born*, and the difference runs deeper than a word. The wet-born still sleep lightly, still wake at the sound of the marsh-thing parting reeds, still keep — every one of them — a coil of old platform-rope somewhere in the house, useless, unthrown-away. The stone-born have never not felt the held breath underfoot. They are the first human beings in the world for whom that faint give, that almost-pulse, is what *ground* means. You can tell a stone-born child blindfolded by setting them on ordinary earth: they stumble. The stillness reads to them as wrongness. They say old land feels *dead drunk* — asleep too deep to be polite to.

Their bodies are made by the town. Generations of shoulder-width lanes have produced a particular grace: a sideways economy of movement, hips turned, basket balanced on the head where the lane has no room for it at the arm. They are not small people, but they are *narrow* in motion, and they touch the walls constantly as they go — a trailing hand, habitual, half navigation and half greeting. Their palms are distinctive: callused in the pattern of adze and gouge, and faintly mineral-stained at the fingertips from a lifetime of testing seep-water, clearing channels, pressing plaster. They taste water reflexively, the way other people sniff milk. A child of six can tell you, from one fingertip, whether a trickle is running sweet or has picked up the marsh.

They are quiet, and the quiet is structural. In a town where most rooms share three walls, sound is held in common whether you like it or not — a cough travels four households, a quarrel travels nine, a birth travels everywhere. So they have evolved an acoustic courtesy as elaborate as any law. Voices are kept at what they call *wall-height*: loud enough for your own hearth, no further. What leaks through stone anyway is governed by the strictest custom they have, which is the discipline of *unhearing* — you may know what you heard through a wall, you may even act kindly because of it, but you may never *say* you heard it, and to repeat wall-heard words aloud is the closest thing the town has to an unforgivable act. Grief is the one exception. Grief is allowed to travel; when someone keens, the adjacent households go silent on purpose, stilling their own noise so the sound can move unobstructed, and this is understood as a kind of carrying.

Anger, having no room, has a place instead. There is a flat lip of stone on the eastern rim, just above where the seep-water spills off in its silver tongues, and people who are too full take themselves there and say what they need to say outward, at the marsh, at the reeds, at whoever wronged them, in full voice. The marsh does not unhear anything. Everyone knows what the rim is for; no one watches anyone use it; a person coming back from the rim is met as if returning from a short journey, which they are.

Their work is mostly maintenance, and they have made maintenance into the dignified center of life. The gutters come first. Every hearth owes a length of channel — knows its own stretch the way farmers elsewhere know field-boundaries — and tending it is the first work given to children, who go out at gray dawn with small bone scrapers to lift the algae and clear the silt grains and report the water's mood. The children are the town's hydrologists. They know the whole system by heart and by ear; they can stand in a lane at night and tell you from the pitch of the murmur which channel upstream is running slow. When the stone shifts and a gradient reverses — when last summer's west-running lane wakes up draining east — it is the children who notice first, usually by sound, sometimes by the frogs, and the re-gouging that follows is done communally, almost festively, with a meal at the end of it. They do not experience the stone's restlessness as betrayal. A reversed gutter is treated like a guest changing which side of the bed they sleep on.

Among them are a few whose work has thickened into something like office, though they would not use the word. There is the crack-listener — currently a heavyset, deliberate man who sleeps badly and has turned it to use — who lies awake for the soft *tock* in the night and keeps, on a long strip of pressed reed, a tally of every crack the stone has opened in his tenure: where, when, which direction it weeps. The strip is added to and never consulted, exactly; it is less a record than a courtesy, the way you'd remember things a quiet companion told you. There are the tree-keepers, three households, one to a tree, who water the named trees from their diverted channels and account for every dropped branch in a small public way — a branch is announced, and its use is decided in the lane, aloud, so no one wonders. And there are the stone-cutters, whose adze-rhythms are as individual as voices; people lying in their houses at midday can name the cutter working three roofs over by the spacing of the knocks. When a cutter dies, the dimpled marks they left in lintels all over town become, for a season, things that hands brush in passing.

Which raises the dead, and the town's tenderest arrangement. There is no soil to bury in — what soil exists was carried up basket by basket and is needed for the living green — and they will not seal their dead into walls, though the walls absorb everything else. So the dead go back to the marsh. They are wrapped in woven sedge, weighted with marsh-mud the family gathers themselves, poled out at dusk along channels the wet-born still remember, and given to the water the people came from. The saying that goes with it is plain and is the closest thing they have to doctrine: *the stone is too young to hold the dead.* You do not ask a child to carry a grandmother. In another forty years, some of them think, the stone may be old enough, settled enough, soiled enough, and the first burial in it will mean the land has finished arriving. No one is in a hurry for this.

They keep close, particular company with the other residents. The frogs are not pets and not livestock; they are colleagues — auditors of the water — and their evening clicking is read the way other towns read sky. The heron is an individual, recognized by a notched feather, and its hour-long stillness at the rim is felt to be a kind of approval; on days the heron stands, people mention it in doorways with the satisfaction of a good omen they'd never call an omen. The big night-thing in the reeds is not feared so much as *deferred to* — lamps stay unlit when it passes out of something nearer to manners than terror, the way you'd dim yourself for a stranger moving through a dark room. And the rust-colored lichen, the first wild growth to accept the bare stone, is watched with open affection and a private, mostly unspoken recognition: the people know perfectly well what they themselves are. First growth on new rock. A thin, tenacious, patient film, finding holds in the fresh fractures, slowly making the surface livable. They were here before the lichen, and they take a quiet, almost sibling pride in having been accepted first.

Because that, finally, is the shape of who lives here. They are a people without conquest in them. They never broke this land — it was never old enough to break — and so they never learned the postures of mastery that settled people learn. Their whole way of being is the way of the shared bench: you feel the other one shift, and you make room, and you trust the room made for you. The stone rises a finger-width a year and they re-cut their doorways; the stone weeps and they cup the weeping into use; the stone cracks in the night and by season's end the crack has a channel, a niche, a name. They are hosts and guests at once, of and to a thing still arriving, and they have stopped needing to know which.

At dusk, when the hearths are lit and the smoke goes up thin from the handful of holes, you can stand in any lane and hear the whole population of the place at once: the water saying its one low syllable, a frog clicking, a child's voice at wall-height, the wet-born woman who is older than the land humming something from the platform years, and underneath it all — felt more than heard, in the soles of the feet — the held breath of the ground itself, the youngest resident, settling in among the people who moved over to give it room.
