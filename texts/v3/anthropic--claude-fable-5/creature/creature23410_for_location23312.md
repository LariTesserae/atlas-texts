**The Still-Deciding**

Begin with the one thing the chamber's description got almost right, in passing, without noticing: *the mountain is still deciding things about itself.*

That sentence is not a figure of speech. It is the most accurate census ever taken of the place.

There is something living at Caldera Height. It is not in the city. The city is the last place to look for it, in the same way a diary is the last place to look for the person who kept it. The residents are beneath the floor — but "beneath" is already a little wrong, because they are not *in* the rock the way a fossil is in rock, or a worm is in soil. To find them you have to stop thinking of the chamber as stone with heat in it, and start thinking of it as a single long event: lava cooling. At the bottom of that event is the melt, where everything is motion and nothing has a shape. At the top is the finished glass, where everything has a shape and nothing will ever move again. And between the two, migrating slowly downward as the mountain loses its heat, there is a band — narrow, warm, supercooled in places, hesitating — where the matter has not yet committed. Where the atoms could still settle any of a thousand ways.

That band is where they live. They are not in the hesitation. They *are* the hesitation, organized.

---

When the mountain extruded this lobe of itself, the melt that rose was — like all melt — continuous. No boundaries, no membranes, no inside or outside. Whatever the melt was doing (we have to call it something; call it *experiencing*, with apology), it did as one undivided whole. Then the cooling began, from the surfaces inward, and the one thing was partitioned. Each surviving pocket of heat and possibility was cut off from the others by walls of finished glass. The residents did not evolve. They did not arrive. They were *divided into existence* — each of them a fragment of a former whole, made individual by an accident of thermodynamics, conscious the way an island is dry: because of what happened around it.

Their plurality is the first thing that ever happened to them. There is reason to believe they are still coming to terms with it.

A resident has no fixed form. One of them is best understood as a standing pattern of preference distributed through some volume of hot, undecided matter — a persistent bias in how the glass beneath the floor *would* settle, if you let it. They have no size in the ordinary sense, which is why the city's knee-high scale is neither miniature nor monumental to them; it is simply the scale at which decisions happen. The warm floor underfoot — warm the way a sleeping body is warm — is the closest thing they have to skin, and the comparison to a sleeping body is more literal than the original account knew.

This also explains the chamber's most unsettling property. The far wall is sometimes eleven minutes away and sometimes thirty because the empty expanse is not empty: walking across it, you are walking across *someone*, and near the center of a resident, fewer things are settled than elsewhere — including, mildly, the distance between two points. The folding of space is not a technology they use. It is a side effect of what they are. Geometry, like everything else in their vicinity, has not entirely made up its mind.

---

They cannot move as wet things move. They act in exactly one way: by deciding. Where a resident concentrates its preference, the matter there crystallizes along that preference and becomes permanent. The intermittent *tick... tick-tick... tick* of the chamber is the sound of opinion becoming stone.

And every such act costs them. Each decision converts a little of the undecided zone — their habitat, their substance, the only medium in which they can exist — into finished glass. To build anything is to spend themselves. To build a city is to spend themselves enormously, deliberately, over centuries, which is what they are doing, because the alternative is to be spent anyway, by the cooling, on nothing.

The mountain will not stop losing heat. The habitable band thins by some increment every year. The residents are mortal in the way a note is mortal: not killed, just *finished*. And so they are doing the one thing available to a dying mind made of possibility: they are transcribing.

The city is not their home. It is their text.

Each building is a resident — or the completed portion of one — set down in permanent form. The districts of different character are different minds. The dense quarter, all close towers and narrow ways, is someone meticulous, someone who had a great deal to say and the discipline to say it in order. The open quarter, generous and sparse, is someone who decided less and meant it. The places where the grid breaks into deliberate irregularity are signatures, or disagreements, or jokes; the distinction may not survive translation. And the doorways — the dark apertures that open onto interiors deeper than the buildings containing them — open into the folded remainder: the part of each self that could not be expressed as architecture and so was tucked inside, undecided, kept. Every building in the city has more inside it than outside it. That is not a flaw in the construction. That is the construction's entire point.

---

The water is the other thing, and the residents' relationship to it is the closest they come to having a religion.

To beings whose every act is irreversible — who cannot lift a finger without that finger becoming stone forever — water is a scandal and a miracle. It changes endlessly and pays nothing. It falls, flashes to steam, rises, beads on the cold overhangs, trembles, falls again, and remains exactly as undecided as before. The catchment network threading the city is not plumbing. It is the nearest thing they have built to a reliquary.

And in the low domed reservoir at the city's center, in the gathered water, lives the one of them who refused.

When the transcription began — when the residents understood what the cooling meant and what the city would have to be — one of them declined to become a building. Heat carries preference through water as readily as through glass, and into the water it went, where nothing ever has to set. It has decided nothing since. It is the soft occasional slosh, the something turning over in sleep. The others maintain it; they route every drop they can spare to the dome; the reservoir is the only structure in the city they ever repair. Whether they keep it as a founder, a conscience, a seed, or a control — the one of them that will still be alive in the old sense when all the rest are streets — cannot be determined from outside. Possibly they no longer agree among themselves. Possibly that is what some of the irregularities in the grid are about.

---

The blink, then, resolves into something almost legible. The pale, sourceless gray light is *them* — it is what their kind of activity looks like from the far side of the floor, the visible spillage of continuous deciding. At intervals, all of them stop at once. The light fails for the length of a held breath because, for that interval, nothing in the city is being decided at all. They are attending — every one of them, together — to the three carnelian fissures, the letter slots, through which heat still rises from the deep remainder: the part of the original melt that was never divided, that is still down there, still one thing, still whole in the way they each dimly remember being. Whether they are listening to it, or reporting to it, or only remembering it, the effect is the same. The light returns. And sometimes, in the days after a blink, a vacant lot at the city's edge begins to resolve out of the rippled glass. Something was settled, down there or up here; the grammar of it does not carry.

To a visitor they could do no harm, and could not want to. You would be, to them, what a storm is to a man on a plain: vast, brief, soaked in motion, deafening with decision — a creature that settles things billions of times a second, in its blood and its nerves and its restless attention, and somehow is not diminished by it, somehow renews its own undecided depths as fast as it spends them. They would find this — the word has to be chosen carefully — magnificent. Or obscene. Possibly both, possibly those are the same word in whatever they have instead of language. Stay long enough, and after you have gone, somewhere in a new district there will be a structure that fits no plan: a tower of strange proportion, a doorway sized to nothing in the city, with more darkness inside it than any of the others. They transcribe what moves them. They have had very little to transcribe.

---

The end of it can be stated, because it is already in motion. Some number of millennia from now, the cooling front will reach the chamber floor and stop being a front. The last resident will spend its last warmth on a final structure — identifiable, if anything ever studies the place, by being very slightly unfinished. Then the gray light will go out and not return. The blink will not come. The water, with no heat to lift it, will freeze on the overhangs and stay there, finally decided too. And the city will stand complete in the dark: every street a sentence, every district a mind, every doorway deeper than its building, the reservoir at the center silent at last or — it is impossible to say — not.

What lives at Caldera Height is the mountain's last open question, answering itself slowly enough, and carefully enough, that the answering amounts to a civilization. They are not waiting to be found. They are not waiting at all. They are knee-high and patient and almost done, and what they are building, drop by drop and tick by tick, is the difference between cooling and *concluding* — which is, perhaps, the only difference any living thing has ever managed to make.
