# Those Who Live in the Verdancy

Begin with the rule, because everything here obeys it: nothing in the Verdancy lives *on* the land. Everything lives partway *into* it. The question is only of depth, and of tempo. The fauna of faster places can be sorted from their habitat with a glance — animal here, ground there, air between. In the Verdancy that sorting fails by degrees, and the inhabitants are best understood as a gradient, running from the quick small things at the surface down through slower and slower forms of living until you reach the strata where *inhabitant* and *habitation* are the same word, and have been for longer than the word has existed.

So take them in order of descent.

## The Quick Layer

The fastest things here would be the slowest things almost anywhere else.

The surface meter of the mat is crowded — saturated, in the manner of old reef — with small life that never sees the green-gold light: blind soft borers no longer than a finger, translucent and faintly luminous at their joints, who eat the dead growth and excrete the living mat's mortar; flat velvet things like animate leaves that press themselves against the colonnade roots and drink the weeping water and are, as far as anyone has determined, happy; whole nations of mites and threadworms and creeping spore-bodies whose generations turn over in weeks, which makes them the Verdancy's screaming ephemera, its mayflies, its lightning.

Even these have the local character. They do not swarm. They do not bloom and crash. Their populations have settled, like everything else, into final density — every niche filled to exactly its capacity and no further, a fullness so old it has stopped being competition and become something closer to liturgy. The borers bore in the same inherited galleries their ancestors bored. The galleries are older than the borers' species. The mat maintains them between occupants.

## The Waders

In the deep meadows of the high swells live the drift-grazers — the soft-bodied dog-sized swimmers-through-vegetation that a traveler notices first, because they are the largest things that visibly move.

Up close they are stranger than their silhouettes. They have no eyes; the meadow admits no distances worth seeing across. Instead they wear a fringe of pale sensory frills down each flank, tasting the water gradient in the growth around them, navigating by moisture and sweetness the way other animals navigate by light. Their skin is not quite skin. It hosts a fine green nap of symbiotic growth — every adult carries its own meadow, a miniature of the one it wades through — and the young are born nearly vegetal, rooted for their first season in the flank of the mother, photosynthesizing, budlike, until they loosen and drop and begin to wade on their own.

They are gentle in the way of things that have never been hunted in earnest. Touch one and it pauses, considers the touch, files it under weather, and resumes. They travel in loose lifelong associations of five or six, never touching each other, never out of taste-range of each other, and when one dies the others remain beside it for a span of days while the meadow closes over it — not grief, perhaps, or perhaps exactly grief, performed at the only speed available.

## The Ceiling Nations

The vault-flocks — the small round flightless climbers that graze the leaf-ceilings upside down — turn out, on long observation, not to be grazing at all. Or not only.

Their feeding spirals are pruning. The patterns they chew into the canopy are precisely the patterns that let the leaf-ceiling flex, drain, and renew itself; an unworked vault grows stiff and waterlogged and begins, over decades, to sag. The flocks are gardeners who do not know they are gardening, paid in leaf-flesh, and the canopy in turn grows its sweetest tissue exactly along the lines it needs trimmed. Neither party planned this. Both parties have been doing it so long that asking which one is exploiting the other is like asking whether the heart exploits the blood.

Their societies are old and quiet and matrilineal, organized around routes rather than territories — each lineage holds a spiral, inherited mother to daughter, walked in the same direction for centuries. They speak in clicks and contact-purrs, and they have one other vocalization, used rarely: a soft, falling, three-note call given only at a true silence, when the dripping stops and the mist is coming down. It carries through the vaults from flock to flock to flock, relayed outward in rings, and no one knows what it announces, because nothing follows it except the mist, which was coming anyway.

## The Patient Hunters

The moss-mantled lurkers of the colonnade junctions deserve their own accounting, because they are the Verdancy's whole theory of predation made flesh.

A lurker hunts perhaps four times a year. It is broad, flat, mantled like a stingray draped over a branch, furred in moss that is not camouflage but genuine resident growth — by midlife a lurker is several percent plant by weight, and old ones flower. It does not stalk. It occupies a junction where routes cross, and waits, and its waiting is so profound that the small fauna treat it as terrain; the ceiling-flocks walk across its mantle; the velvet leaf-things press against its flanks and drink. It takes what it takes seldom, swiftly, and without pursuit, and the taking causes no panic, because the Verdancy's creatures have no instinct for panic left. A death here is a drip among drips.

And when a lurker dies, it does not fall. Its grip is structural by then. It roots where it lies, and the colonnade grows through it, and its frame becomes a buttress in the vault it lived in. Walk the junctions with a knowing eye and you can read them: this swelling in the column, that splayed five-fingered flare of woody grain. Every old vault is held up partly by its hunters. There may be no junction in the Verdancy that was not a lurker once.

## The Tenants of the Pools

The pale swimmers in the still dark pools are the hardest residents to classify, because it is not clear they are residents. It may be truer to say they are organs.

They are eyeless, slow, translucent to the point of near-absence — you see them mostly as a thickening of the water, a glassy intention drifting below the surface. They take air once an hour with their soft kissing sound, and they never leave their pool, and they never breed in any way that has been observed, and yet the pools are never without them. When a pool closes — sealing over across a span of decades, becoming mat — the swimmers do not die. They go down with the water, into the strata, and join the slow pale threads.

This is the suspicion, anyway, and it reframes everything: that the pools are apertures. That the Verdancy, which conducts water through itself like tissue, keeps these few openings the way a body keeps eyes, and the swimmers are what it sees with — tasting the rain, the spore-fall, the rare footstep at the moss rim. The kiss-sound, on this account, is the land sampling the air. It is a quiet thought to stand with, at the edge of that perfect dark stillness: that you are being noticed, gently, at a tempo that will not finish noticing you until long after you have gone.

## The Threads

Which brings the descent to the threads themselves — the slow pale filaments running through every stratum, down through the kilometer of layered dead-and-living, doing work no one watches.

They conduct. Water, certainly; sugars, certainly. And signal — because the Verdancy answers itself across distances no root could explain. A hollow injured at one edge of the plateau thickens its growth at the far edge years later. The vaults migrate in coordination. The mist comes down on the whole land at once. If this is thought, it is thought at the speed of seasons, each notion taking a decade to cross the mind that thinks it, and at that tempo *thought* is the wrong word; it is closer to climate, or to mood. The Verdancy does not decide things. It comes to have been deciding them.

Whether there is a *someone* in this — whether the threads add up to an inhabitant or merely to a habit — is the great unanswerable of the place. But note what the gradient has been showing all along: the borers maintaining galleries older than their species, the flocks gardening without knowing it, the lurkers becoming architecture, the swimmers becoming organs. Everything that lives here long enough is taken into the body of the place and kept on, in some capacity. The Verdancy may simply be the sum of everything it has ever absorbed, still faintly doing what each of them did. Less a mind than a chorus grown inseparable. Less an inhabitant than all of them.

## The Sleepers

And then there are the beds.

The body-shaped hollows in the dim hollows — moss-lined, faintly warm, maintained open against every habit of closure — have one further property, discoverable only across a span of attention no single visit affords. They are not always empty.

The intervals are very long. But the moss lining of a kept bed shows compression, sometimes, that was not there before; the warmth in one will deepen, for a season, beyond what the mat's circulation explains; and on the high swells there stand certain tall, silvered, weathered forms that a hurried traveler takes for posts or dead growth, and that a patient record reveals to be in slightly different positions, year to year. Mid-stride, always. Facing along the old routes between the copied doorways.

The builders, it seems, did not leave, and were not absorbed — or rather, they were absorbed in the one way the Verdancy reserves for things it will not copy. They slowed. Whether by choice or by long marination in the place's tempo, they came down to its speed: a breath per day, a step per season, a waking that lasts a decade and a sleep that lasts a century, descending through the warm strata in their kept berths and rising again, walking the swells through a generation of mist, lying back down. They are contemporaneous with the land now. You cannot meet them, any more than you can meet a glacier; you can only be, briefly, in the presence of one, and be too fast to register as more than a flicker — a drip among drips.

The beds are warm because they are occupied. They are merely occupied on a schedule that makes every visit a sample of the wrong moment. The Verdancy keeps them open because its people are still using them.

## What It Means to Live Here

So the honest census runs: quick small liturgical things in the surface meter; gentle waders born half-plant; gardeners who don't know it; hunters who become the roof; organs that may be animals; threads that may be a mood; and, slowest and deepest of the truly living, a people who solved the problem of dwelling in a finished world by finishing themselves to match.

None of them are *in* the Verdancy, exactly. They are of it, at their various depths, at their various speeds, each one a current in the same body-warm circulation. To live here is to be in the process — gradual, painless, apparently voluntary — of becoming the place. The inhabitants of the Verdancy are the Verdancy, caught at different moments of arrival.

The newest resident is whatever stepped onto the mat this morning. The oldest is the patience underneath, which has already made room.
