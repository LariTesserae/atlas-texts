# Who Lives in the Cistern

Begin with the smallest truth: nothing here was meant to live here, and everything here now belongs. Every resident of the cistern is descended from a stowaway — a spore in a crate, an egg in a water tank, a larva asleep in the wet seam of some cargo hauled up the spire when the spire still received cargo. The room did not choose its tenants. But the tenants have had centuries to choose the room, generation after generation, and by now they fit it the way water fits a vessel: exactly, and without effort, and as if there had never been anywhere else.

---

## The Copper Strings

The little fish first, because they are what you see first, and because they are the room's clock.

They are copper-sided, blunt-nosed, mild-eyed, no longer than a finger, and they are never alone. They move in strings — six, seven, sometimes nine — nose to tail in loose procession, and a string is the unit of their existence the way a family is the unit of a village. A fish separated from its string will hang in the water doing nothing at all, dimmed somehow, until another string drifts past and absorbs it, at which point its copper brightens again. They do not seem to mind which string. They mind only that there is one.

Their world has a geography as fixed as any continent's. The warm eastern wall is the *road up*. The cool western wall is the *road down*. The silver lung of air beneath the ceiling is where eating happens — they tilt and sip at the surface film, taking the drowned midges and the dust of pollen from the ribbon-weed flowers, making those small audible kisses that are, in this room, the closest thing to conversation. The floor is where they do not go. The floor belongs to the eel, and to the dark under the grating, and every copper fish is born knowing this without being told, because the ones who needed telling did not become ancestors.

They live perhaps three years, which is to say roughly forty million beats of the engine. They have never heard silence. When the pressure-groan travels around the octagon, they school tighter for the length of it — not from fear, you understand, but the way people lean together when a familiar train passes. And when the room performs its quarter-turn, they ride it loosely, fins barely moving, carried sideways through the water with the unbothered grace of passengers who have made this commute all their lives. Watch a string during the turn and you will see something almost like pleasure: a slight quickening, a flash of copper, the fish-equivalent of children leaning into the curve of a road.

Their generations stack up like coins. A copper fish today is perhaps the four-hundredth of its line to ride the eastern thermal. None of them knows this. All of them are it.

## The Wall-Tenders

The translucent shrimp are the room's quiet industry. There are a few dozen of them at any time, scattered across the rust-red velvet of the coral-moss like glass beads dropped on carpet, and they are so clear that you can watch the moss they eat travel through them in a small green thread — a moving stitch of the wall, briefly given legs.

They are not grazers, exactly. They are *editors*. They take the moss where it grows too thick, thin it where it crowds, and leave it where it lies fair, and whether this is intention or only appetite shaped by ten thousand generations into the appearance of intention is a question the room declines to answer. The result is the same: the velvet stays velvet. It never mats, never chokes a pipe-mouth, never climbs above its tide line on the wall. The shrimp keep the cistern's upholstery, and their shed skins — ghost-shrimp, perfect and empty — drift down to join the soft sediment in the corners, so that the room's dust is partly made of its housekeepers' former selves.

At night — which here means only the hours when the porthole goes dark and the chamber lives by the faint warmth-glow of nothing at all — the shrimp migrate upward, all together, and graze the underside of the surface film in the silver lung. If you were floating there in the dark you would feel them: the lightest possible touches, dozens of them, like rain falling upward.

---

## The Eel

The eel is the oldest living thing in the room, and possibly the oldest living thing for many days' travel in any direction, and it carries its age the way the room carries everything: worn smooth, grown over, turned into furniture.

It is the color of pewter gone slightly green, as long as a tall person, as thick as a forearm at its middle, and along its back, rooted in the old scars of its skin, grows a garden. Moss, mostly — a strain of the wall-velvet gone darker and finer — but also two small ribbon-weeds and a colony of creatures too small to see without putting your eye very close, which you should not do. The eel's back is a moving province. Mites graze its moss. Infant shrimp shelter in its weed. When the eel makes its circuit, twice a day, regular as the valve-cycles, an entire small nation makes the circuit with it, and the copper fish part before it not in panic but in protocol, the way traffic parts for something that has right of way written into the deep law of the place.

It eats rarely — once in many days, a single deliberate take, the flick and the glitter-puff — and the strings reform and the room goes on. It is not cruel and it is not kind. It is the floor's ambassador to the open water. Between circuits it lies in the horizontal intake pipe with only its head emerging, and its eye, small and gold-rimmed and clouded slightly with age, watches the light shaft make its slow lighthouse sweep across the chamber, and there is no way to know what an eel's attention is, after so long, but it has the quality of a keeper reading the same beloved page again.

No one knows how it got here. It is too large for any pipe it could have arrived through. The most likely truth is the strangest: it arrived as something nearly invisible, an eyelash of glass in a tank of resupply water, before the moss, before the weeds, possibly before the porthole glass went amber — and everything else that lives in the room grew up around it, so that the eel did not move into the garden; the garden assembled itself around the eel.

## The Gardener

And then there is the tenant in the north valve-wheel, and here you must go slowly, because the room goes slowly, and because the truth of this one took even the room a long time to notice.

It is small — it would sit in your two cupped hands — and it is many-legged in a way that refuses to resolve into any one familiar shape: something between an isopod and a brittle star, armored in plates that have worn as smooth as the rivets, dove-grey, with a fringe of fine feeler-legs around its rim that are never all still at once. It lives inside the hollow of the worn valve-wheel, which fits it like a hermitage, and it keeps a collection.

Paint-flakes, mostly. Sorted. When the light shaft crosses the north wall each afternoon, the creature emerges and rearranges them — ochre with ochre, the rare blues together, the metallic flakes in a curved line along the wheel's inner rim — and you might watch this for an hour and call it a magpie's habit, a packrat's compulsion, the meaningless tidiness of a small armored thing.

But stay longer. Stay days.

The flowering stalks that rise from the floor grating — two here, three there, with long bare water between them, so that the room *feels furnished* — do not seed themselves in that pattern. Seeds drift; seeds clump. Someone carries them. Follow the gardener on its slow night errands across the grating, feeler-legs reading the floor like fingers reading a page, and you will see it pause at a fallen seed, take it up, walk it — sometimes the full width of the chamber, an hour's journey at its pace — and press it into the grating at a spot that, when the stalk grows, will stand in good light and clear water, balanced against its neighbors, *placed*.

It thins the ribbon-weed when the weed crowds the porthole. It carries dead matter to the corners where the sediment lies. It has, on its rim, a single flake of paint a color found nowhere else in the room — a deep enamel red — which it does not file with the others but keeps apart, in the center of its hermitage, and turns over sometimes in the light, and what that is, no one will ever be told.

There is only ever one gardener. Its young — pale, pinhead-small, perhaps a dozen in its long lifetime — disperse into the walls and live ordinary scuttling lives among the shrimp, indistinguishable from them in habit. But when the old gardener dies, one of them — always exactly one, by what election or instinct cannot be said — crosses the room, climbs the north wall, enters the wheel, and inherits the collection. The flakes are never rearranged by a new hand. They are *continued*. The curved line of metallic flakes along the wheel's rim has been growing, one flake every few years, for longer than the eel has been long, and it is now nearly a full circle, and what happens when it closes is a thing the room is in no hurry to find out.

The cistern's deliberate, sparsely-furnished grace — the thing that made you think of an old keeper and a few good plants — is not an accident and not a metaphor. There is a keeper. It is the size of your cupped hands. It has been keeping for centuries, in shifts.

## The Lung-Dwellers

The silver gap of air beneath the ceiling has its own population, easy to miss: a thin civilization of the surface film. Tiny snails the color of the amber porthole glass ride the underside of the water's skin, upside down to your way of thinking, right side up to theirs, grazing the film. Midges — descended from eggs that came in with the moss spores, surely — live entire brief lives in that forearm of air, never knowing the room is mostly water, the way the fish never know it is mostly air above the spire. When the room performs its quarter-turn the film-snails ride their ceiling-floor around in serene formation, and the midges scatter and resettle like a thought interrupted and resumed.

---

## The Landlord

And underneath everything, the oldest resident of all, if you allow it residency: the pulse.

The engine itself lives kilometers below, in the dark of the spire's core, and no creature in the cistern has ever seen it or could survive the journey down. But its heartbeat lives *here*, in the water, in the grating, in the swim bladders of the copper fish and the armor of the gardener and the long old body of the eel, four beats at a time, *whum-whum, whum-whum*, without one missed measure in living memory or any memory before that. Every creature in the room was conceived to that rhythm, grew to it, navigates by it — the eel times its circuits to the valve-cycles, the gardener works in the intervals between groans, the fish school to the pulse the way other fish school to a current. If it ever stopped, the silence would be the loudest thing any of them had ever heard, and the warmth would begin its long slow leaving, and the water would stand still for the first time in centuries.

It will not stop today. The room does not plan further than that, and has never needed to.

---

## And Briefly, You

One last tenant, temporary: whoever climbs the spire and floats for a while in the warm.

The room absorbs you as it absorbs everything. The fish add your shadow to their geography within an hour. The eel makes its circuit past you without adjusting its line by a finger's width. And when you leave — back down the throat of the spire, back down through the weather, back to the world of rock and soil and rooms that do not turn — you will leave something behind without knowing it. A thread from your sleeve, a flake of dried mud from your boot, a single hair coiling in the slow circulation.

The gardener will find it on its night errand, feeler-legs reading the grating. It will turn it over in the light shaft the following afternoon. And if it is interesting — if it is a color the room has never owned — it will be carried up the north wall and placed in the collection, somewhere near the enamel-red flake, in the hermitage at the heart of the wheel.

And so you, too, will live there, in a small way, for centuries: catalogued, kept, turned over occasionally in a shaft of plain honest sun, in the little flooded chapel at the top of the sky, where everything sharp has worn smooth, and everything that arrived by accident stayed as an heir.
