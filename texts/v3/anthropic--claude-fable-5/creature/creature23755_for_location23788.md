They are woodlice, mostly. It is worth starting there, with the modest fact, before the fact starts to slip.

## The Pale Ones

Thumbnail-sized, fourteen-legged, colorless as boiled paper. Three years in the void is perhaps twelve generations, and twelve generations of total dark have already begun their editing: the eyes are going, sinking back into the head like abandoned windows, while the antennae have grown long and articulate and terribly busy — each one a full sensory career, sweeping, tapping, pressed flat against rail and wall and one another. They do not see the city. They *palpate* it, continuously, and above all they hear it, though hearing is the wrong word for what they do. The whole animal is a listening surface. They stand on sound the way other creatures stand on ground.

They breathe through pleopods — plate-gills folded under the tail, which must stay wet or the animal suffocates in open air. This is the secret constitution of the city, the law underneath all the other laws: every citizen is drowning slowly, always, held above drowning only by the drip. The wheels, the districts, the migrations toward each new seep — none of it is preference. A settlement is a place where breathing is possible. The city chases water because the citizens are, in the strict sense, made of the shore.

## The Half-New

A woodlouse molts in two acts: the back half first, then, days later, the front. For most of a week every molting citizen is two ages at once — the rear soft, pale, unwritten; the front still carrying its old scored plates, its rivets, its history. The city has built its entire idea of a person around this. You are never replaced all at once. You are always a seam.

Because the molt takes everything. The riveted foil, the zinc plates — unbolted beforehand and held in trust at the molt-house, since the new shell comes up a size larger and nothing ever fits again; an elder's armor is all shims and re-drilled holes, a palimpsest of bad fits. And the tool-marks go too. The scoring on a citizen's back is its record — rank, debts, offices held, districts served — written where the bearer can never read it, legible only to others, which the isopods appear to regard as the whole point of a record. So before the shed skin is eaten (it is always eaten; calcium is the currency under the currency), a recorder crouches over the exuvia and copies the scores, groove by groove, onto the soft new tergites, working fast, before they harden wrong.

The copies drift. Every transcription loses a stroke or invents one. The oldest citizens carry records twelve molts deep, error compounded on error, and the recorders know it, and nobody minds much, because the city has a place where the drift stops.

## The Set Ones

Some elders refuse the last molt. It is not announced. One day a citizen walks — slowly, ceremonially slowly — to a spot beneath a steady drip-line, folds its legs, and stops. The lime-water does the rest. Calthemite takes a shell the way frost takes a window: first a gloss, then a crust, then a white architecture. Within a season the elder is a low mound with antennae; within a year, a spire with a heartbeat.

They live a long time in there. Longer, some say, than they have any right to — mineralized, metabolically banked down to almost nothing, drumming one slow beat against the inside of their own tomb every hour or so, a pulse the whole district can feel through the floor. They are the archive. Their scores can never molt away; the stone has fixed the record mid-error, forever. Prospectors visit them before setting out, pressing antennae to the white flank, drumming questions, waiting the long wait for the single answering knock. Some of the blind spires out in the dark spans — the ones everyone assumed were stubs of dead industry — are not stubs. The city is dotted with citizens who became architecture on purpose, still faintly transmitting.

And this is where the taxonomy starts to give. A young one is an animal. A working one is an animal riveted to its tools. A Set One is a mineral with an opinion. The isopods do not appear to draw the line anywhere, and after enough observation neither do you.

## The Bead-Buriers

The product of all that smelting — the beads of reclaimed metal carried into the dark and buried — has an explanation now, or at least a theory with real evidence behind it, held by a minority who are getting less minor every flood.

A metal bead buried in the moss-plain under a drip-line becomes a nucleus. Lime-water finds it, and calthemite grows from it faster and denser than from bare floor, a hard white column rising drop by drop toward the ceiling. Map the burial sites — one prospector did, dragging a scribe behind her for a full season — and they are not random. They cluster where the ceiling's stress-cracks are propagating. Where the barrier is settling worst.

They are growing pillars. Drip-fed, decades from mattering, columns of stone reaching for a ceiling that is coming down to meet them. No living citizen will see one bear load; the plan, if there is a plan, is scored in fragments across a thousand backs and copied with errors every molt, and the burying goes on anyway, generation after generation, a construction project made entirely of faith and drips. The city that cannot hold still for a week is building the only permanent thing in the void, and building it for strangers — for whatever the city will have become by the time the ceiling arrives.

## The Sideways People

The amphipods hold the sump under a treaty nobody remembers negotiating. They are wrong for the land — hunched, flickering, laid over on their sides, moving in wet lunges — and glorious in the water, and they are the only citizens who have been to the bottom of the pool. They do not describe it. Or they describe it in a drummed rhythm that does not parse in the isopods' language, which may be a translation problem and may be tact.

They are the divers, the salvors, the undertakers of floods. When the sump jumps its banks and drowns a district, it is amphipods who go down after the machinery, after the plates, after the bodies — and it is amphipods who have quietly reported, twice now, that there are fewer bodies down there than there should be. They are paid in cropped moss and calcium and they carry the crane's offerings down personally, all the way, into the part of the pool where the lamps don't reach.

## The Tenant Below

It came in through the main, is the belief — through a weep-hole, when it was small, riding the god's forearm, and now it is far too large to leave by the way it entered, which makes it either a prisoner or the landlord, and the city has sensibly decided not to find out which.

Soft-bodied. Unsegmented. Patient in a way that reads, through half a meter of black water, as attention. When the water hammer falls into certain rhythms — the long triple *whumm* the isopods call, roughly, the counting — it rises to just beneath the surface and holds there, a paleness under the interference patterns, and the whole shore falls as quiet as this city ever gets, and the drummers drum back, and it stays a while, and sinks.

It earns its offerings. The sump has an outlet — a weep-drain in the old wall, the only reason the void hasn't filled to the ceiling long ago — and the drain does not clog, and the amphipods say, sideways, in their untranslatable rhythm, that they have seen why. Once, years ago, a shard of failed calthemite fell into the pool and cut it, and it did not die; both pieces lived, and now the offerings are lowered in pairs, and no one discusses this.

## The Weather, the Riders, the Map Underground

The springtails are not governed and cannot be. They are grazed land that grazes itself, silver thousands at the sump-margin, and the city keeps them the way older cities kept barometers: springtails move uphill hours before the sump rises, always, reliably, in glittering evacuations that any citizen can read from across the chamber. They are the flood alarm and the augury and possibly, in the aggregate, the void's largest single organism-shaped thing, and they do not know the city is there.

The mites are on everyone — gill-cleaners, seam-tenders, small enough to service the spaces between plate and shell. Some of them ride the drip-wheels, around and around, for no reason anyone has established. The city extends them the courtesy it extends everything it doesn't understand: it builds around them.

And the fungus keeps the other archive. The pale fruiting bodies that rise in the dark spans rise only over dead districts — over the buried organics of abandonment — so that the mycelium, threading under the whole moss-plain, is a complete map of everywhere the city has ever been, written in the floor, consulted by no one. Almost no one. There is one prospector who has stopped following cracks and follows fruiting bodies instead, on the theory that the fungus is not recording where the city was but predicting where it goes next. Her light moves against the migration, always, out toward the oldest ruins. She is either mad or early. Here, that is one category.

## The Lights in the Dark

Some of those single moving lights between settlements are prospectors. Some are lamp-bearers — a vocation: citizens fitted, by their own arrangement, with a threaded mount drilled clean through the fifth tergite, carrying a lentil-lamp on a hairspring gimbal, walking the black spans on fixed circuits so that travelers can navigate by them. Living streetlights, on schedule, decade after decade. When a lamp-bearer molts, the route is walked dark for six days, and the whole city feels it, a missing tooth in the night.

And some of the lights are the heretics of quiet — the ones who believe, against all evidence and all doctrine, that somewhere beyond the aggregate there is a place with no noise in it at all, and who drill outward, patiently, listening for absence. In this city silence means one thing: a stopped wheel, a dead seep, a district out of air. Silence is the word for dying. To go looking for it on purpose is either the deepest madness available or the only real religion the void has produced. Occasionally one comes back. They drum strangely afterward — sparse, slow, with rests in it, actual deliberate rests — and the city gathers around them in horrified fascination and cannot stop listening.

That, finally, is what lives behind pillar nine: a people who breathe through wet plates and speak through the same hammering that builds their world, who are copied imperfectly onto themselves every molt and don't mourn the losses, who eat their ruins for calcium and grow pillars for descendants they can't imagine, and who make everything — grief, law, industry, prayer to the thing in the pool — *loud*, at full volume, always, because in the void the quiet places are the places where the water stopped, and nothing that lives there has ever once mistaken silence for peace.
