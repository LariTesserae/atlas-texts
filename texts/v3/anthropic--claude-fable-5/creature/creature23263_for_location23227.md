**Who lives in the Catchment**

Start small, because the Catchment did.

The first residents arrived dissolved. Before the carp, before the char, before anything with a face, the water itself was colonized — by bacteria that came in through the intakes as a statistical inevitability, riding every litre drawn from whatever rivers feed this place, and found, in the new concrete, a continent of food. They eat the lime. They eat the alkalinity itself, the slow chemical exhale of curing cement, and they have spread across every poured surface in the basin as a film thinner than the formwork seams: invisible, total, the true ground layer of the world. The chalk-fog that drifts between the pillars is partly their doing — partly mineral weather, partly metabolism — so that the milky bloom you swim through is not quite dead. It is the closest thing the Catchment has to an atmosphere, and like most atmospheres it is alive in a way that doesn't show.

Above the film, in the tilted stadium-wide columns of piped daylight, lives the plankton. This is the secret that makes everything else possible. Rotifers, daphnia, copepods — passengers through the grilles, eleven years ago, twelve — and they discovered that the light-shafts are pastures: stable, permanent, mid-morning forever. They drift up into the columns and bloom there in faint green hazes you'd mistake for more chalk-fog until you saw the carp arrive. The light-shafts are not just illumination. They are fields. Every column of ordinary daylight falling through the basin is a farm with no farmer, and the whole vertical economy of the Catchment hangs from them like weights from strings.

**The carp are the sky.** The original stocked generation is mostly gone now — eaten, aged out, settled into the floor-snow — and what schools through the light-columns today was born here, has only ever been here, and has never seen a riverbank, a season, a surface ruffled by wind. They are subtly wrong for a river fish, in ways that are becoming less subtle with each generation: paler, longer in the body, built for crossings. A river carp lives in a world of banks and cover. These live in a world of open volume punctuated by pillars, and they have organized themselves accordingly, into the bait-balls the size of apartment buildings — not schools so much as portable habitats, walls made of each other, the only shelter in twenty kilometres being the bodies of your ten thousand siblings.

Their map is made of sound. There is nothing to see between the pillars, but there is always something to hear, and the carp navigate the basin by its machinery the way migratory birds are said to navigate by stars: this pump gallery's particular drone means *west wall*; that intake's pitch means *Sector 114*; the doubled echo off a valve tower means *halfway*. They are the first animals in history whose entire sensory world is infrastructure, and they are fluent in it. When the transfer turbines spin up — the rolling thunder, pillar to pillar, the thing folklore would call the morning bell — the fish do not react to it. They *anticipate* it. They have learned the spin-up sequence the way tide-pool creatures learn the tide, and they move toward the kelp gardens and the pillar-shadows minutes before the pressure wave arrives, so that the bell rolls across a basin already braced for it. Eleven years is forty generations, for some of what swims here. The Catchment is young as a structure and ancient as a habitat, depending on whose clock you read.

**The char took the floor.** Cold water sinks, and the char sank with it: they live in the bottom thirty metres, in the eleven-degree dark-green hush above the slabs, hunting along the formwork seams with the patience of things that evolved under ice. They have become seam-specialists. Every joint between the kilometre slabs is a canyon a few centimetres wide and effectively endless, packed with the small life that fell out of the light-columns and lodged there — and the char patrol these lines like beat cops, in singles and pairs, following the geometry of the pour. Seen from above, their territories are a grid. They are the only animals in the Catchment whose lives are shaped like the engineering drawings, and they don't know it.

The seams also hold the eels — the ones that lie in the joints like cables that breathe — and the eels are a different proposition, because the eels remember. They came in as elvers, threads of glass through the grilles, and an eel can live longer than the Catchment has existed; some of what lies in the floor-seams tonight was alive in a river under an open sky and carries that, in whatever way an eel carries anything, through year after year of pump-hum and chalk-fog. They are the basin's memory of outside. They grow, and do not leave, and may no longer be able to leave — too thick now for the grilles that admitted them — which makes them the first true citizens of the place: residents not by birth or by choice but by the simple arithmetic of girth and grate.

**The warm channels are the tropics.** Somewhere along the eastern reach, outflow from some heat-shedding process the basin never explains keeps a band of water at eighteen, nineteen degrees, and everything in the Catchment that likes warmth has found it. The lampreys live there, grown long and pale and successful; the water is murkier, faster, more crowded, more violent — a strip of jungle stitched along the edge of a concrete steppe. Fish that cross the open volumes do so cold and urgent. Fish that live in the warm channel live the other kind of life, the dense and dangerous kind, and the boundary between the two climates is patrolled the way all rich borders are.

And in the warm channel, or near it, there is the Tenant.

No one — nothing — has seen all of it. What exists is evidence: a volume of water, a few hundred metres across, that the bait-balls route around as smoothly and unanimously as if it were a pillar, except that the volume moves. A char found drifting with a bite radius wider than any lamprey's gape. Once, on the old sonar repeater in the eastern gallery, a return the size of a service vehicle, moving at four knots, gone in two sweeps. Something came in young through the intakes, in the first flood-years when the grilles were newer arithmetic, and has spent a decade in the warm outflow doing the one thing the Catchment is unmatched at supporting: getting larger, undisturbed, on an endless conveyor of silver. It is the apex of the basin and its most committed prisoner, and the schools have written it into their maps as faithfully as they've written the pumps — one more landmark, one more weather, one more thing that is simply *there* the way the morning bell is there.

**Then there is the question of the machines.** Be careful with this one, because the longer you stay down, the harder the distinction is to keep. Small maintenance drones still graze the intake grilles on automated schedules, scrubbing biofilm in slow rows, recharging at induction plates on the pillar flanks, and there is no functional difference between what they do and what a grazing animal does: they patrol a territory, they consume the film, they return to a den. Sacrificial anodes bolted along the steelwork are being slowly eaten by the water in their place — a built martyrdom, metal that exists to die so other metal doesn't — and the first orange freckles around the bolt-heads are where that bargain is beginning to fray. The pumps themselves, if you score them on metabolism, territory, rhythm, voice, are the megafauna of the basin: vast, warm-bodied, breathing on schedule, singing a sixty-cycle song that every other resident has built its life around. The carp do not distinguish between the living and the operating. Down here, that may simply be correct.

**And there is one gardener.**

There are no workers in the Catchment. The line is true and the place insists on it; the system needs no witnesses and employs none. But there is a difference between a worker and an inhabitant, and the difference lives in Valve Tower 7, in the dry interstitial air up near the diffuser panels, where the water happens to stop.

She was on the seeding crew — the maintenance program that planted the kelp lines in their agricultural geometry, eleven years ago, twelve, back when the basin still had a roster. When the program ended and the lifts went up for the last time, the roster shows her cycled out with the rest of it. The roster is wrong by one. What she does about that, no record asks.

The tower is a vertical cylinder, dry inside, ladder-cored, and the manufacturer's yellow paint on the rungs is the only faded thing in the Catchment — worn through to primer in two hand-width bands, the precise polish of one person's grip, repeated for a decade. Nothing else in the basin has had time to soften. She is the wear. She is the patina the place is otherwise too young to have, a human-shaped weathering applied to a single ladder, a single hatch wheel, the seat of a single one-person service submersible that still charges off the turbine bus and still, when she takes it down the three hundred metres to the floor, announces itself to the fish with a motor-whine they have long since classified as harmless weather.

She tends the gardens. That's the shape of the life: descending through the light-columns into the brightest shafts, walking the sub slowly down the kelp rows like a farmer down furrows, thinning, restaking, transplanting the volunteers that drift loose, while the char watch from the seams and the bait-balls hang overhead like cloud cover. The rows are still rows because of her. The agricultural geometry that should have gone ragged years ago holds its lines, and so the gardens are, in the strict sense, the only handwriting in the Catchment — the one place where the basin says something other than what it was poured to say.

She eats from the warm channel's edges and the schools' margins. She keeps tallies in grease pencil on the inside of the tower — drawdowns, the morning bell's timing, the dates she's crossed the Tenant's moving exclusion zone and the dates she's chosen not to. She named the morning bell. Workers' folklore, the basin would have, if there were workers; there is instead a folklore of one, complete with its monster, its calendar, its bell, its tended grove. When the transfer turbines roll their thunder across the basin twice a day, she feels it in the tower like everyone else who lives here feels it — in the sternum, before the ears sort it out — and she has noticed what you would notice: that she, too, now moves before it arrives.

She talks, sometimes, on the long floor-transits, to the hum. Not from loneliness, exactly. From the same instinct that makes the carp map their world by it. It is the largest voice in her life and it has never once stopped, and there is a kind of company in a thing that constant, the way there is company in surf.

**What lives in the Catchment, then, is everything that has learned to live at the scale of weather.** That is the entrance requirement and the only one. The bacteria did it by becoming the haze. The plankton did it by farming the light. The carp did it by becoming sky, the char by becoming the grid, the eels by becoming memory, the Tenant by becoming rumor, the pumps by being weather to begin with. The gardener did it last and hardest, by shrinking her witness down to one ladder, one furrowed garden, one grease-pencil calendar — small enough that the basin, which requires no witnesses, has not bothered to notice it has one.

And when she stops — because she will stop, on some afternoon of ordinary piped daylight, and the Catchment will register it the way it registers everything, which is not at all — the rows will begin, slowly, in the eleven-degree cool, to go ragged. Volunteers will root where they drift. The orchard will become a forest. The geometry will blur over decades into something wild and self-arranged, and the fish will mob it just the same, and that slow unravelling of straight lines into thicket will be the only memorial the place keeps: the one passage in the whole sea-sized poured interior where the water, reading itself, could ever have found evidence that something here once cared how things were arranged.

The bell will go on crossing the basin twice a day. Everything that lives there will go on moving before it arrives.
