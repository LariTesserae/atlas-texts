**The People of the Steps**

Whoever lives at Caldn is visible first as a vertical line. Everything native to the terraces is horizontal — the water lies flat, the steam goes up in diffuse sheets, the kettle-dogs are barely more than ridges, the birds fold themselves into the geometry of the rims. So the eye finds a person at half a mile: an upright dark mark moving slowly along a causeway with the lamps burning beside it at noon, unhurried in a way that reads at first as laziness and then, on the second day, as fluency. Nobody hurries at Caldn. Hurrying makes noise, raises heat in a body already wrapped in heat, and gets you nowhere faster — the causeways are narrow, the planks are slick with condensation, and the next building is a quarter mile off no matter what you do about it. The residents walk the way the water moves from terrace to terrace: in thin continuous films, without splash.

There are a few hundred of them. They do not have a name for themselves that they use with any conviction. Outsiders say *Caldians*; the residents, asked where they're from, say "the steps," and consider the question answered.

Their bodies have made the same accommodation as the buildings. The perpetual humidity has softened them — skin pale and faintly pearled, palms without callus even on the rim-cutters, because nothing here is ever dry enough to chafe. They sweat very little; sweat would be useless in air this saturated, and bodies that stay here long enough seem to stop bothering. Instead they run warm and still, like the pools. Their hair hangs heavy and never quite dries. Their voices are pitched to carry about three feet, which is the distance the air allows, and this single acoustic fact has shaped them more than the heat has: at Caldn you cannot call out, cannot shout across a basin, cannot summon anyone. To speak to a person you must go to them. Every conversation begins with a walk, sometimes a long one, over water that could cook you, and so nothing trivial gets said. The residents are not taciturn — sit with one on a porch and they will talk for hours in that low even register — but they have no small talk, because no talk at Caldn is small. It all cost a journey.

For everything else they have the rest-language: a vocabulary of stillness and small visible motion, readable at causeway distances through steam. A raised tool means *come*. A hand laid flat on a railing means *wait, the cycle is about to turn*. Standing with one's back to a building means *the door is sealing again, bring the saw*. Children learn it before they learn to speak aloud, the way children elsewhere learn to point.

The work sorts them into a handful of callings, all of them forms of slow argument with the stone. The **rim-cutters** spend each season freeing what the travertine has begun to claim — sawing stilts loose from their own pale anklets of new rock, chiseling door-seams, reopening the pipe-junctions where mineral fattens like scar tissue. It is endless, and they like it; the cutting season has a holiday's rhythm, all the scattered households out on the terraces at once, the white dust of the cut stone drifting down onto the water and dissolving back into it, going home to come again. The **valve-keepers** tend the engines, which mostly means listening to them, which mostly means breathing. They time their work to the soft periodic exhalation of the relief valves, doing the delicate tasks in the long quiet between breaths, and after a few years they breathe with the machinery without knowing it. This is how you recognize someone raised at Caldn anywhere in the world: every few minutes, mid-sentence, mid-task, they pause and sigh once, deeply, in unison with a town that is not there.

They cook in the pools. There is almost no open flame in Caldn — fire is regarded as a crude, jumpy, noisy way of doing what the plateau does gracefully — and the kitchens are baskets and lidded vessels lowered on hooks into water of known temperature. Each household keeps its own map of heats: the pool that poaches an egg, the pool that cooks the little gray crustaceans (rimfleas, on the table; the children net them by the thousand and never make a dent in the herds), the cooler shelf where bread-dough, set in a sealed tin, steams itself into something dense and faintly mineral that nobody who grew up on it can find elsewhere. They drink the water once it has fallen four terraces and cooled, and it leaves its signature in them: their teeth are very white and slightly translucent at the edges, their bones knit fast and heavy, and the old ones crackle when they rise from a chair, a sound like a terrace lip settling, which they find funny rather than alarming. In great age their eyes sometimes go opaline — a soft fogged blue-white, like the glass over the flywheels — and the others lead them along the causeways by the hand, and the rest-language gains a tactile dialect.

They keep kettle-dogs the way other towns keep cats: not owned, exactly, but named, fed, allowed onto the porch steps where they lie eating glasswings out of the air with that motion too quick to hear. A kettle-dog that has basked near a household for decades becomes a fixture in the most literal sense — its stone dorsal ridges thicken until it moves only a few feet a year, and the family waters it, and grandchildren are shown it the way grandchildren elsewhere are shown a tree planted at a birth. There is at least one, behind the foundry, that may no longer be alive in any way an outsider would credit, except that its nostrils stay clear of crust, which the foundrymen point out as proof, and which is, on reflection, proof.

Their dead they give to the water. There is a high pool, hotter than the rest, called the Settling Pool, and a body laid in it on a frame of plank is, within a season, sheathed; within a generation, stone; within two, part of the rim, indistinguishable except to the family, who know exactly where. The residents walk over their grandmothers daily and the water pours its soundless film across them, and this is not grim to anyone at Caldn — it would not occur to them that it could be. The broken edges of old terraces show ancestors in cross-section the same way they show the ghost-lines of old causeways, and children climbing there will name the shapes matter-of-factly: that one is the schoolteacher, that pale long line was a fisherman of rimfleas, that one nobody remembers, which is understood not as a failure of memory but as a completion. The plateau, they say, is fair. It takes a layer and it gives a layer. Each year the children leave something small on a rim — a button, a coin, a milk tooth — and check it through the seasons as the stone closes over it, learning by direct instruction the single fact the town is organized around: that everything here is being kept.

They are not religious about the heat, though some of the very old say that something turns over far down in the bore, slowly, and that the warmth is its sleep, and that the synchronized breathing of the engines is the engines listening. The sensible majority says it is just rock and pressure and the ordinary tendency of coupled systems to fall into step. The town breathes once every few minutes either way. The wading birds lift their heads either way. Most residents have stopped finding the question interesting, which an outsider eventually recognizes as the deepest strangeness of the people of the steps: they have made peace with not knowing whether they live *on* something or *in* it.

Newcomers arrive, a few each year, drawn by work or quiet or the rumor of cheap power. Most leave within a season — not because of the heat, which can be borne, but because of the moment, usually at night, when they first consciously notice the whole basin exhale at once, and feel their own chest move with it, uninvited. The ones who stay are the ones who find, in that moment, that they have already breathed out, gladly, and were waiting for the town to catch up.

So: what lives at the Terraces at Caldn. A few hundred soft-skinned, low-voiced, heavy-boned people who cook in the ground and bury their dead in the architecture; their half-stone animals; their breathing machines; the white birds and the gray herds and the glittering insects of the films. That is the honest census. But stand on a causeway long enough, with the lamps burning at noon and the steam going up silent into the enormous light, and a different accounting suggests itself — that the plateau is the resident here, single and very large and very patient, and that the people are simply its newest layer, the one that still walks around, still talks in low voices, still keeps the count of what is taken and what is given, while the stone, a hair's width per year, comes up gently around their ankles to write it all down.
