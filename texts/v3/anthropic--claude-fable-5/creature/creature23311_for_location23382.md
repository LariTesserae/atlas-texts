Start where the description started: overhead, with the roar. But the roar resolves, if you stand under it long enough, into nations — plural — and the nations into parishes, and the parishes, eventually, into lives.

**The bats are not one thing.** The great central vaults belong to the free-tailed kind, the small swift ones, packed so densely that the ceiling is not stone with bats on it but bats with occasional stone showing through — five hundred bodies to the square meter, each one gripping the rock with toes finer than wire, folded into itself, breathing. They roost in heat of their own making; press a bare hand to the wall a meter below a major cluster and the stone is warm, the only warmth in the country, a fever the colony generates and the rock stores and gives back. Their pups are born in early summer in nursery chambers — vast pink acreages of the hairless young, left clinging to designated walls while the mothers hunt, and every returning mother finds her own pup among millions by voice and smell, landing in the seething crowd within a hand's-breadth of where she left it. This is the single most repeated miracle in the Underroost and it happens several million times a night and no one has ever seen it fail in a way that mattered.

Out toward the dimmer margins, where the free-tails thin, the larger bats keep to themselves: horseshoe-faced things that hang singly, wrapped in their own wings like dark fruit, each one spaced from its neighbor by exactly the distance of its dignity. They do not join the evening rivers. They slip out alone, late, after the thunder, and hunt low and slow among the scrub on the plateau, and come back before the swiftlets have fully settled, and they regard — insofar as a bat regards — the great roaring commonwealth of the free-tails the way a hermit regards a city: useful to be near, intolerable to be in.

And there is a third kind, rarer, that does not leave at all. Pale, large-eared, found only in the chambers farthest from any doline — bats that hunt *inside*, hawking the moths and crickets and emergent flies of the guano fields, gleaning crawling things off the very floor. They have made the cave their entire world: roost, hunting ground, nursery, grave. Some individuals among them, the old ones, may never in their lives have flown under open sky. They are the first true citizens you meet — the first creatures for whom the Underroost is not shelter from a world but the world itself.

**The swiftlets are the bats' opposite number,** and the symmetry between the two nations is so exact it feels negotiated. The birds take the day shift in the dark; the bats take the night shift in the light. The swiftlets nest in the twilight zones near the dolines and feed in the open sky, and they navigate the black corridors between by the clicking the rock never stops hearing — each bird a small sonar, each pair of clicks a question the walls answer. Their nests are the place's one architecture: little brackets of hardened saliva and moss and their own feathers, glued to overhangs in colonies of hundreds, tier above tier, like the balconies of an abandoned opera house. A pair returns to the same bracket year upon year, repairing, thickening, so that the oldest nests are layered like tree rings and have outlived the birds that founded them several times over. The swiftlets are the only inhabitants who *make* anything, and what they make, the visiting humans take — but slowly, and not all, which is perhaps why the taking has been permitted for so long.

**At the dolines live the borderers** — the creatures of the seam between worlds, and the seam is the most dangerous address in the country. Twice a day the rivers of bodies pour through the skylights, and twice a day the borderers collect their toll. On the rims above, falcons and a single pair of dusk-hunting owls have learned the schedule to the minute; they do not chase, exactly — they stoop through the column and close their feet and rise with what the column gave them. On the walls of the shafts themselves, wedged into cracks at exactly the height the exiting bats fly, cave racers wait: slender, pale-gold snakes, half their length anchored in the fissure, the free half hanging out into the air, swaying, striking blind into the river of wings. They catch perhaps one bat in a thousand strikes. One is enough. They are the only snakes in the world that fish the air, and they have been doing it here for the entire short life of this place, which means they learned it elsewhere, in some older sister-cave, and brought the knowledge with them when the roof first broke — colonists with a trade.

Below the racers, on the talus slopes under each skylight, work the giant centipedes — the hand-length ones from the floor, but here grown bold, climbing the lower walls at dusk to take roosting bats larger than themselves, envenoming them at the wing-root and riding them down. And on the talus itself, attending every doline like a congregation, the scavenger ring: beetles, crickets, the blind white crabs come up from the pools, waiting for whatever the falcons fumble, the racers drop, the centipedes leave. Nothing that falls through a skylight, living or dead, reaches the bottom unclaimed.

**The floor nations are the oldest democracy here and the strangest.** The guano fields are not a surface with animals on them; they are a habitat with depth, like soil, like sea. The top layer belongs to the crickets and roaches, the glittering visible multitude. A hand's depth down live the larvae — beetle grubs, fly larvae in their seething billions — turning the fresh fall into the soft black peat below. Deeper still, in the old compacted strata, live things that have never seen even the grey pillar-light: blind beetles gone wholly white, mites, pale pseudoscorpions hunting the mites, threadworms, a whole compressed vertical ecology in which each centimeter of depth is a century of rain from above. The fields breathe; they ferment; they are measurably warm. In the deepest chambers the guano generates its own faint heat and its own weather — the ammonia haze that stings the eyes is the exhalation of this buried nation digesting the sky's daily gift.

The pools have their own people. The white crabs you see at the margins are the amphibious gentry; in the water itself live eyeless shrimp, translucent, their hearts visible beating, and small colorless fish in the deeper pools, descendants of something washed down a fissure in some single ancient flood, now so changed they could not survive the river that sent them. They move slowly. Everything in the water moves slowly. The pools are the one place in the Underroost where the economy thins out, where the rain of plenty barely reaches, and the water-dwellers have answered scarcity the way deep creatures always do: by needing almost nothing, by living absurdly long, by treating each year as the rich surface treats an hour. The oldest fish in the farthest pool may predate the handprints on the wall.

**And the handprints — the people.** They do not live here; the description is right that the place does not permit residence. But they live *with* it, in the sense that a coastal people live with the sea. They come down at fixed seasons — you can read it in the knapping debris, all of it from working-fresh edges, the toolkit of brief visits — and they come for the swiftlet harvest and the crab pools and, it may be, for something less edible. The handprints are all at the second doline, all at the same height, all left hands, blown in red ochre: not art exactly, but attendance. A register. *We were here, we took what falls, we touched nothing that hangs.* The charcoal smear low on the block is the remains of a single small torch-light, used once, near the wall, as if someone needed light for the length of one careful task and then put it out — because they understood, or were told, that fire and the roof do not mix, that one panicked colony is a catastrophe, that the contract is: borrowed light only, build nothing, mark the wall, go up. Whoever they are, they are the Underroost's one species of pilgrim, and the place has made them — as it makes everything — punctual.

**Last, the citizens of the true interior,** beyond the farthest pillar of light, in the chambers no doline has yet broken open. Out there the rain of guano thins to nothing, the roar fades to a rumor in the rock, and what lives is what can live on rumor: a white beetle every few meters, a strand of fungus on a drowned cricket, a single translucent millipede a year's walk — at millipede pace — from its nearest neighbor. They are the frontier of the country and also its future, because the roof is young and the collapses are not finished; somewhere out in that black, a chamber that has been sealed and starving since the beginning is one rainfall, one frost, one tremor away from its own skylight. When it opens, the rivers of wings will find it within a season. The racers will bring their trade. The fields will begin to fall and rise. And the starving white pioneers who held that darkness alone for ten thousand years will find themselves, overnight, citizens of the loudest, richest, most crowded country under the plateau — which is the one piece of weather the Underroost still has, the one change it is still capable of: not seasons, but annexation. It does not grow older. It only, very occasionally, grows larger.

That is who lives there. A warm-roofed city of millions that runs on schedule; hermits at its edges; a snake that fishes the air; a buried nation eating the sky secondhand; slow white elders in the water; pilgrims who sign the wall with their absence; and out past the last light, alone in the unopened rooms, the patient ones — waiting, without knowing they are waiting, for the ceiling to break and let the country in.
