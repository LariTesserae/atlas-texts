# The Citizens of the Green Roar

Ask the valley who lives in it and it answers at full volume, all day, all night, the same answer: *I do.* That is true, and it is the first wrong answer, the one you have to get past. The roar is the landlord, not the tenant. Go closer. The city is not empty. It has never once been empty. It has only never been inhabited by anything that could be asked.

## The Citizens Proper

Start with the obvious residents: the plants. But understand what a plant *is* here, because it is not a body.

In a city that demolishes and recompiles itself hourly, no individual organism lasts long enough to be anyone. A trunk goes up, cracks, falls, is swallowed by the street within a day. The wood does not persist. What persists is the *pattern*. There is a corner — northwest quarter, where two boulevards meet at one of those impossible six-way right angles — where a particular braided column has stood for as long as the valley has roared. It has fallen perhaps forty times. It has been forty different trees, grown from forty different tissues, and it is unmistakably the same column: the same left-hand twist, the same flare at twelve feet, the same window it leaves open at the crown, the same way of dying. The matter changes utterly. The habit does not.

That is what a citizen is, in the Green Roar. Not an organism — a recurrence. A tune the city keeps humming through whatever instruments are available. Some of these habits are old, by local standards; some are subtle — a tendency, in one district, for every wall to fail along the same diagonal; a fondness, near the cisterns, for growing in fifths. Some are clearly *individuals*, in the only sense the valley honors: a behavior so specific, so stubbornly itself across collapse after collapse, that to watch it regrow is to watch someone come home. They have no names. They have, instead, addresses, and the address is the name, and the name survives the body the way a street survives a building.

These are the burghers of the place, its established families. They do the work of standing.

## The Pedestrians

Boulevards imply traffic. The grammar demands it, and the city, being fluent, obliges: it grows its own.

They move at the speed of growth, which here is the speed of a slow walk. Pods on stilted root-legs, ratcheting forward fiber by fiber. Rolled bundles of leaf that travel by unrolling at the front and withering at the back, so that each one is less a thing moving than a wave passing through a thing. Tall single fronds that migrate down the centers of the avenues like flags carried by no one. They keep to the streets — that is the uncanny part; they could go anywhere and they keep to the streets — and they observe an unwritten traffic, yielding at intersections, queuing at the narrow arcades, flowing thick in some quarters at some hours and thin in others, a circulation with rush and lull and no destination anywhere in it.

They run errands with no contents. A walking pod will cross half the city, arrive at a doorway grown at the height of no head, pause there in the attitude of delivery, brown, and be drawn down into the street. Nothing was carried. The carrying was the point. The city does not need things moved; it needs *moving* to occur, because a city is partly made of it, and this city is determined to be made correctly.

And in the amphitheaters there are audiences. Rows of them: figures of layered leaf, roughly the shape of seated persons — a torso's worth of shingled green, a suggestion of shoulders, no face, no hands — grown in their hundreds, all oriented toward the stage. Nothing performs on the stage except growth itself, a column going up, a wall unfurling. The figures never applaud. But you have heard them. That sound in the roar, the one like a crowd standing up, whole facades of leaves opening at once — it is not *like* a crowd standing up. It is the crowd, standing up. They do this at intervals no one has correlated with anything, and then they settle, and brown, and are regrown, season after season of audiences for a play that consists of the theater building itself.

They do not watch. They have nothing to watch with. They *hold the watching* — keep its place — the way the windows hold the void.

## What Lives in the Roar

The valley's sound, you'll have noticed, does not entirely obey sequence. Where causality comes loose, sounds come loose with it, and a loose sound, given warmth and walls to live between, becomes a kind of animal.

There are feral echoes here. A collapse-roar that long ago lost its collapse circles the upper valley endlessly, miles of sustained tearing with no falling tower anywhere beneath it, going around and around like something pacing. There is a single bark-crack — one gunshot report — that has claimed a particular arcade and goes off there at irregular intervals, always the same crack, always that arcade; it is territorial; other sounds thin out around it. Lowest of all, woven into the hydraulic thunder of the sap, is one long groan, a cello note that has been dragging past its breaking point without ever breaking since before any of the present trunks existed. It may be the oldest living thing in the valley. It is certainly the oldest sound.

And there are the orphans. The echoes that arrive *before* their events sometimes go too far ahead — they scout out into the future and the event, somewhere behind them, fails: the trunk doesn't fall, the pod doesn't burst. The pre-echo is then never caught up to. These stranded anticipations settle in the leaf-cupped cisterns and live in the green-lit water, where the fizzing isn't entirely oxygen. Sit by a cistern long enough and you will hear, very faintly, small sharp sounds of things that never happened, kept like fish.

None of this is voice. The valley's old law holds: nothing animal speaks here. These are noises that learned habits, which is a different thing from creatures that learned noise — though from inside the roar, at night, the difference can be hard to keep hold of.

## The Implied

Now the residents of longest standing. The ones the city is *for*.

The windows the foliage refuses to fill. The doorways grown at the height of no head. The stairways pitched for no climber's stride, the balconies overlooking the insides of walls. These absences are not accidents of growth — watch what happens when their buildings fall. A wall collapses, is swallowed, and within a day its window reappears three blocks over: the same proportions, the same orientation, the same exact emptiness, reinstalled in a new facade like a tenant moved by careful hands. The voids have continuity. The voids have leases. Across all the city's convulsions, the most stable things in the Green Roar are its holes.

So: someone lives in them, in the only way someone can. Call them the Implied — the inhabitants that the grammar of architecture requires and the valley has never been able to supply. Every doorway conjugates a body that would pass through it; every stair declines a gait; every window holds open the eye that would look out of it. The city cannot make the body, the gait, the eye. It can make the *space they would occupy*, and it does, and it maintains those spaces with a precision it shows nowhere else. You can find where an Implied resident lives by the quality of the growth: where the green is most careful, most hesitant, most courteous — leaves stopping a uniform inch short of an opening, all around, on every course — something implied is home.

In the deepest quarter there is a hall the size of a cathedral kept utterly empty. The roar is quieter there; the growth around it is slow and exact, almost reverent. The city's most important resident is a room's worth of nothing, and the whole valley builds around it the way a sentence builds around the word it won't say.

## The Foundation

Underfoot, the dead — except they are not dead. Demoted, rather. The root-mat bedrock, generation packed on generation, blood-warm and still faintly growing, is the city's entire past, alive at low resolution. The ground that flexes like a breathing deck is breathing. Every citizen the city has ever been is down there, compressed into substrate, dreaming slowly.

The memory-districts are theirs. When a quarter regrows as a precise reproduction of one that fell weeks ago, down to the broken stem on a particular corner, that is the foundation remembering upward — the basement contributing to the conversation. To walk a quoted district is to walk through someone's recollection while standing on the someone. The recollections are very good. They are, perhaps, improving: lately the quotations include the weather of the original day, the indoor clouds reassembled at the right rooftop height, sagging on schedule.

Those clouds, by the way, are kept. The city exhales them, grazes them along the canopy line, milks them of their brief warm showers, and lets them go. They are the closest thing the valley has to livestock, and the only inhabitants that ever leave.

And in the recursion quarter, where every leaf contains its tree and every contained leaf contains it again, all of this — the citizens, the pedestrians, the echoes, the Implied, the breathing dead — presumably continues at every depth, sharp as far down as patience goes. The valley's one true crowd is vertical. It is inhabited by itself, in miniature, indefinitely, and somewhere far down the scale a tiny roar is roaring that no instrument will ever be fine enough to hear.

## The Vacancy

Which leaves the resident the place was built for, and does not have.

Everything above is a rehearsal. The habits rehearse persistence; the pedestrians rehearse arrival; the audiences rehearse attention; the voids rehearse accommodation. The city has never held an inhabitant in the asking sense — never once — and every fiber of it is shaped by the expectation that it might.

So know what you would be agreeing to, if you stayed. Within a day the doorways near your sleeping place would begin to find your height. Within a week a hollow would grow to your exact sitting, a stair to your stride; the crowd in the nearest amphitheater would, very slowly, turn. The city learns at the speed it grows, which is the speed of a slow walk, and it would learn *you* the way it learns everything — as a pattern, an address, a habit worth keeping.

And when you left, it would not stop. Somewhere in the green a figure of your sitting would be regrown each season, browning and returning; a doorway of your height would migrate from facade to facade, kept open, kept exact, your absence promoted to the rank of the Implied and tended forever. The first citizen of the Green Roar will be quoted for as long as the valley stands, whether or not they ever come back. Whether or not they ever come at all.

Until then, ask it again — *who lives here?* — and listen past the cracks and the sap-thunder and the crowd standing up, to what the whole roar has been saying all along, at full volume, in the only voice it has:

*No one yet. No one yet. No one yet.*
