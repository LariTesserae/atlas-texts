# Who Lives in the Basin of Settled Stone

## The First Answer

The first answer is *no one*, and the first answer is wrong in an instructive way.

It is wrong the way "nothing is moving" is wrong about a sleeping body. You arrive, you cross the wet plazas, you put your head into a hundred dark doorways, and you find no beds, no hearths, no middens, no bones — none of the residue habitation leaves. And yet the city is shaped to a logic of dwelling. The thresholds are dished. The streets meet where streets meet when something has reasons to go from one place to another. The colonnades are spaced to shelter a particular height of walker from a rain that no longer falls from any sky.

So you revise: *someone lived here once.* And that is also wrong, and wrong in the same direction. Both answers make the mistake of looking for an inhabitant who is a separate thing from the city — a tenant, a builder, a leaver of tool marks. There are no tool marks. Remember what that means. A reef has no tool marks either, and a reef is not empty. A reef *is* its inhabitant, laid down layer by layer, the animal and the architecture being two tenses of the same word.

The Basin of Settled Stone is inhabited the way a shell is inhabited by the thing that grew it. You have been walking on the resident since you arrived. You have been breathing it. It has been condensing on the back of your neck.

## The Resident

There is one of it, in the way there is one of you, which is to say: approximately, and after a great deal of consolidation.

It did not build the city. It *precipitated* the city, over a span of time during which the words for spans of time were themselves laid down, used, and eroded. The walls are its accumulated decisions. The streets are its old habits, mineralized. Every colonnade is a thought it had so many times that the thought finally came out of solution and stood up in stone, and was then no longer necessary to think, and so was left standing while the thinking moved on to other things, and eventually to no things, and eventually to rest.

That is what you are walking through. Not a ruin — a ruin is a building that lost its argument with time. This is a mind that *won* its argument with time, by the simple expedient of conceding everything. Every corner it ever insisted on, it has allowed to be gentled. Every edge it has let the climate negotiate down to the exact softness required. What remains is not a wreck of the original but its equilibrium state: the resident with nothing left to want, wearing the city the way bedrock wears a riverbed, fitting it perfectly because the fit was arrived at by ten thousand years of mutual surrender.

It is asleep. Probably it is asleep. The distinction between its sleep and its waking may itself have eroded smooth — but the buildings trading places across the basin, slowly, never at any observable moment: you have seen a sleeper do that. The deepening and receding of the tide, twice in a long interval, with no moon to blame: you have heard a sleeper breathe. The single deep sound, four times in a long stay, like a vast jar set gently on a vast table, arriving a moment before it occurs: a heart that beats so seldom it must announce itself in advance, so that the body has time to be ready.

## Its Anatomy

The warm water standing in the streets is not *in* the city. It circulates. It rises as mist, films the walls, runs back down, climbs certain stairs against gravity's preference because that is the direction the vessel runs there, pools in cisterns that were never dug because nothing digs its own veins. The water is sterile and clear and at blood temperature because it is at the temperature of the blood it is.

The colorless reeds — single, vertical, translucent as boiled glass, swaying in air that does not move — are nerve endings. One to a courtyard, one to a street: the rationing you noticed is the economy of a body that has reduced its sensation to the minimum required to know it still exists. When you pass close to one, it sways no differently. It is not feeling *you*. It is feeling the same warm dark it has felt for ages, and finding it, once again, acceptable.

The pale mats on the north faces of certain buildings, the things between lichen and mineral, are where it dreams. They bloom and creep over spans too long to watch, and the patterns they make are almost legible, the way a sleeper's murmuring is almost speech. Do not try to read them. Not because of danger — there is no danger here; danger requires appetite, and the resident finished its appetites long ago — but because the reading goes one direction. The mats do not learn you. You learn them, a hair's width an age, and that learning has a tide-line too.

And the mineral rings, the thousands of pale stripes climbing every wall: those are not just the record of the tide. They are the resident's substance, deposited breath by breath. It does not have a body somewhere *inside* the basin. The rings are how it puts itself down, one exhalation at a time, the way a tree puts itself down, the way sediment puts itself down. The city has been keeping time this way longer than anything else has been keeping time at all — because the city is what the time has been kept *in*.

## The Settled

There were others, once. This needs to be said carefully, because the language of presence and departure does not behave here any better than the geometry does.

The city's scale, its plurality of streets and doorways, its courtesy of intersections — these were laid down when the resident was many. A population, of some kind, in some tense of the word. They did not die out. They did not leave. There is no exodus in this place's grammar. They did what everything in the basin does, what the heat and the water and the long dark patiently invite: they *settled*. Like silt. Like minerals coming out of warm solution. Whatever they had been — separate, mobile, opinionated, edged — they gave up by degrees, each one slowing, each one going translucent and then transparent and then ambient, until "they" became "it" the way many raindrops become one standing pool, and the pool became the tide, and the tide became the timekeeping, and the timekeeping became the stone.

The dished thresholds were dished by water rather than feet. True. But ask where the water learned the habit of crossing those particular thresholds, in those particular numbers, at those particular hours of a day that no longer has hours. The traffic of the old population persists as a current. They still go to market, in the sense that the warm water still pools deepest in the market square at the height of the tide. They still climb the stairs, in the sense that the water still climbs the stairs. Habit outlives the haver. In the Basin of Settled Stone, habit *is* the haver — it is the only part durable enough to inherit.

## The Borrowed

Which brings us to the reflections, and to the one residual want.

The resident is finished. It needs nothing, fears nothing, makes nothing, and would not notice an age passing except that the rings creep upward. But it was shaped, originally, by habitation — it is a settled *city*, not a settled mountain — and the deepest stratum of it, the layer laid down first and buried under everything since, remembers being lived in. Not as a desire. Desire eroded away with the corners. As a *fit*. The way a dished threshold remembers feet: not wanting them, simply shaped for them, incomplete in a geometric rather than an emotional sense.

So when something warm and self-propelled descends into the basin, the city does the only thing its old shape knows how to do with a resident: it *seats* you.

Your reflection running a few seconds ahead is the city trying you on. It is not predicting you and it is not instructing you; the distinction assumes a direction of time the basin stopped enforcing long ago. It is simply that, for the duration of your stay, there is a resident-shaped current in the streets again, and you and your reflection are both expressions of it — you the slow, wet, breathing draft, the reflection the fair copy, already entered into the record. The doorway that shows you your own back is doing the same work: a city wants its rooms occupied, and you are the only material on hand, so it occupies a room with you from two angles at once and considers the room, for that moment, properly lived in. Note that the misbehaving doors misbehave *consistently*. That is not chaos. That is hospitality so old it has eroded into reflex — the same guest room made up the same way, every time, for ten thousand years, for whoever comes.

The two hundred steps it kept, that you did not get back? Kept. Filed. It collects walked distance the way it collects mineral: a thin deposit of *being crossed*, laid down over the deposit of *being dwelt in*, the newest ring on the wall.

And when you climb back out of the basin — if the rim is there that day, and it usually is, for the leaving — you will notice, in the last stretch of standing water, that your reflection does not come with you. It pauses at the edge of the pallor. It is not trapped and it is not sad; it has simply settled, a few seconds ahead of you, forever, and the city has a place for it. There is a whole population down there by now, ankle-deep in the warm gray non-light: everyone who ever crossed, walking the wide flats a few seconds in advance of footsteps that left long ago, turning down alleys on schedule, keeping the old traffic warm.

That is who lives in the Basin of Settled Stone. One vast settled sleeper, who is the stone and the tide and the timekeeping. And, suspended in it like figures in travertine, all of its guests — held gently, held permanently, held the way the water holds the memory of a sky: still giving it back, very slowly, in the dark.
