**Who lives in the Roost**

Everything here lives on traffic or heat, and most things live on both. That is the first thing to understand about the residents: none of them is native to the chamber in any deep sense, because the chamber is too young to have natives. Every lineage in the room arrived — blew in, flew in, fell in, was carried in on a feather or in a gut — within the last few thousand years, colonists of a bubble that did not exist when their ancestors were already old. What looks like an ancient, settled economy is actually a boomtown. It only sounds eternal because everyone in it is shouting.

---

**The swiftlets** own the room the way weather owns a valley. There are — no one has counted, no one could — somewhere between eight and fifteen thousand of them, and they are not quite the swiftlets of anywhere else anymore. A few thousand years in the dark has begun its work on them. They are smaller than their cousins on distant cliffs, darker, and their eyes have started the long slow negotiation toward being less important: still functional, still used in the ropes of departure at dawn, but in the chamber they fly on sound alone.

Their clicks are the architecture of their lives. Each bird's click is doubled — a sharp pair, *tick-tick*, close as a heartbeat — and each bird knows its own echo the way you know your own footsteps. A swiftlet returning at dusk through a ceiling crack drops into a darkness containing thousands of other birds doing exactly the same thing, and threads it at full speed, and finds, among tens of thousands of holes in the frothed rock, the one hole that is hers. They almost never collide. When they do, the whole colony hears it — a fumbled beat in the rhythm — and the clicking around the collision point densifies for a moment, like a crowd turning to look.

Their nests are the room made flesh. Elsewhere, swiftlets build with saliva and moss. Here they build with saliva and *the chamber*: dust of black glass, flakes of the orange pool-crust, their own molted feathers, and the pale mineral hairs that grow under the drip-lines. The nests set hard and faintly translucent, little bracket-shaped cups the color of smoke, and old ones — abandoned, glazed over by the weeping walls — are being absorbed into the stone. There are nest-sites in the eastern dome that have been continuously occupied for longer than any of the human handprints have existed, passed down not by inheritance exactly but by proximity: a fledgling raised in a good hole tends to claim the hole next to it. Whole slopes of the ceiling are, in effect, neighborhoods of cousins. The oldest neighborhood — the birds nearest the largest ceiling crack, first in and first out, the ones whose ancestors presumably found the chamber — flies with a discernible arrogance. They cut through the entrance ropes rather than joining them. The colony tolerates this. Someone has to be first.

By day the colony is elsewhere, hawking insects across the barren slopes and the green valleys beyond, and this is the swiftlets' great civic function, though they don't know they perform it: they are the chamber's import trade. Every bird returns at dusk carrying, in its belly, a little of the outside world, and pays it out over the course of the night onto the floor below. The room's entire lower economy is funded this way, in installments, from above.

---

**The bats** work the opposite shift and hold the opposite temperament. Where the swiftlets are a crowd, the bats are a household — quarrelsome, intimate, maybe six hundred of them, small-bodied and big-eared, clustered in the deep shadow of the western dome where the ceiling folds into rock curtains and the light-columns never reach. They pour out at full dark, after the last swiftlet is in, through the smallest of the three cracks, which the birds disdain to use; the two populations have partitioned the sky the way they've partitioned the day, and their contempt for each other is total and entirely functional.

The bats are the room's gossips. Their social calls — the squabbling chitter under the birds' clicking — carry more information than anything else in the chamber: greetings, grievances, the locations of good roost-folds, the identity of every individual. A bat separated from the cluster calls and is answered by name, or by whatever a name is when it's a shape of sound. They groom each other in long chains. They feud in factions that dissolve and re-form. Mothers returning at dawn find their own single pup among hundreds by voice and smell in seconds. If the swiftlet colony is a market, the bat cluster is a village, and like a village it never entirely sleeps: at any hour a few of them are awake, muttering, shifting, keeping the low register of the room's roar continuous.

Their guano falls in a different place than the birds' — a dark western slope against the pale eastern one — and the floor creatures know the difference. Bat guano is richer. There are beetles that work only the western slope, and they are glossier than their eastern relatives, and this is the kind of distinction the chamber is full of: tiny economies inside tiny economies, all of them younger than some individual trees elsewhere in the world.

---

**The floor** is where the traffic becomes bodies.

The crickets are the most visible of the floor-folk — black, heavy, long-antennaed, thumb-sized, and possessed of a strange devotion to the light-columns. They assemble where the beams land and follow them across the floor through the day, a slow congregation migrating with the sun, sawing at the warm bright air. No one is sure what the light gives them; possibly warmth, possibly nothing, possibly the assembly itself is the point — the columns are where crickets find other crickets. Their song is pitched below the birds and above the bats, and in the hour after the colony's dawn departure, when the dome briefly quiets, the crickets own the room and seem to know it. Their fallen and their molted skins feed a whole substratum: pale mites, blind springtails, small ivory beetles, the guano's uncountable ferment — the residents no one visits the chamber to see, who outnumber everything else combined.

The salamanders are the floor's patient aristocracy. There are perhaps forty of them, blunt-faced, the pink-white of the underside of a wrist, with a faint tracery of blood visible through the skin and eyes reduced to dark seeds. They are almost certainly the descendants of a stream population from the mountain's flanks — washed or wandered in through some wet crack in the first centuries — and the warm water has done something generous to them: they never age out of their larval ease. They keep their soft gill-frills into adulthood, live half in and half out of the pools, and grow slowly for a very long time. The largest, resident in the biggest pool below the object-shelf, is longer than a hand and may be older than sixty; it has a bird-bone scar across its back and moves with the unhurried certainty of something that has never once, in its whole life, needed to rush. The salamanders eat whatever falls — dying crickets, fledglings that missed their first flight, the constant soft rain of the colony's losses — and they are, in this sense, the room's undertakers, converting grief upstairs into patience downstairs.

The shrimp are the smallest sovereigns and the strangest. Each pool holds its own population — translucent, eyeless, none longer than a fingernail, faintly pink around the gut where you can see what they've eaten. And here is the thing: the pools do not connect. Each washtub or cupped-hand basin is a sealed world, and the shrimp of one pool have not met the shrimp of the next — six feet away — in perhaps a hundred generations. They are already diverging. The shrimp of the hottest eastern pool are smaller and quicker; those of the big cool pool under the drip-line are larger, slower, and slightly opaque. Given ten thousand years they would be different species; given the vents' cooling, they may not get it. They graze the orange crusts and the fallen guano-dust, they breed in the blood-warm dark, and their entire universe — sky, weather, geography, history — is a bowl of cloudy water smaller than a sink. They are, arguably, the truest natives the chamber has: the only residents for whom there is no outside at all.

---

**The voles** — call them that; they are round, tawny, short-eared, some cousin of pika or vole that made its own arrangement with the mountain — are the room's only real citizens, in the sense that they alone seem to hold opinions about the place. There are perhaps two dozen, in three or four family groups, denned in gaps under the collapsed sections of floor. They have worn their faint paths between the guano slopes, the pool rims, and the three green circles, and they defend those paths with whistled indignation. They eat the mosses, the fern's new fronds (never the old ones — some inherited rule), spilled insects, and, in season, the leathery coins of the shrub, whose seeds pass through them and are planted in droppings along the paths, which is very likely how the shrub's rare offspring get started and why the voles and the shrub are, without either knowing it, married.

The voles are the sentinels. Their alarm whistle — when a shadow crosses a ceiling crack — is understood by everything: crickets still, salamanders slide under, even the bats' chatter dips. Nothing else in the room watches the sky. The voles watch it constantly, with the vigilance of the only residents small enough to be carried off through it. And they are the ones who fall silent for the hum. When the room finds its note in the last light, the voles sit up on their haunches along their paths, motionless, noses working — the only creatures in the chamber that appear to *listen* to the resonance rather than merely produce it. What it means to them, if anything, is not knowable. But they do it every time, all of them, and they did it before there were handprints on the wall and will do it after.

---

**The shadow at the door** is worth mentioning, because every rich room has one. She is a falcon — small, slate-backed, alone — and she does not live in the chamber; she lives *on* it, the way a tollkeeper lives on a bridge. Her scrape is on a ledge of raw basalt thirty feet from the largest ceiling crack, on the barren outside of the mountain where nothing else lives at all, and she takes her whole living from the two daily ropes of wings: a swiftlet at dusk, a bat at dawn, snatched from the braid as it pours out. The colony has shaped itself around her for generations — the ropes exit fast, twisting, hugging the rock — and she has shaped herself around the shaping. She and the colony are the oldest relationship on the mountain's surface, predator and crowd, tax and traffic. She has never been inside. She will never go inside. The dark under the cracks is a country she can hear and smell and skim the border of, and that is all. In winter, when insects thin and the ropes shrink, she goes hungry within twenty feet of the loudest concentration of prey for a hundred miles, and there is nothing she can do about it.

---

**The green things and the crusts** are older than they look and stranger than they seem. The shrub — twisted, leather-leaved, rooted in a single crack fed by drip-water and centuries of guano — is very possibly the oldest individual organism in the chamber, older than any salamander, older than the falcon's lineage on the ledge. It grows a hair a year. It has been the same shrub for the handprint-makers, for the first voles, perhaps for the first birds. If the room has an elder, it is this: a plant the size of a chair, holding still in a moving beam of light, being slowly fed by everything that dies.

But the true first residents are almost certainly the crusts. The white and orange rims of the pools, the green-gold films on the weeping walls — these are not stains. They are mats: bacteria and archaea, some eating light when a column swings near, some eating the minerals in the vent-water, needing no traffic from outside at all. They would have been here before the first bird found the first crack — living on the mountain's own breath, painting the pools, waiting, in the sense that things without waiting can wait, for the rest of the economy to arrive. Everything else in the room is a tenant of the heat. The crusts are its direct children. When the vents cool and the birds leave and the pools go cold and clear, the crusts will fade last, and some of their spores will still be in the rock when the mountain is finally gnawed down into ordinariness, which is a kind of outliving, if you want it to be.

---

**And the ones who stopped coming.** Seven handprints, one very small; a worked edge of black glass; three careful cairns; a smoke-shadow; a half-fallen wall. Read them as residents in absentia, because the room does.

They were few — a family, or a line of families, coming up the barren slope perhaps once a year, perhaps less. The wall at the throat says they meant to come back: you don't mark a threshold you're abandoning. The cairns and the shelf say the room was more to them than eggs and warmth — you don't arrange white stones by paleness for a larder. Most likely they came at first for the practical things (a warm night in a cold country, water, the colony's eggs in season, the glass that takes an edge better than anything for a hundred miles) and stayed coming for the impractical one: the hum. It is hard to imagine anyone who had stood in the last light, with ten thousand voices fusing into one sustained note felt in the sternum, treating this room as merely useful. The small handprint suggests they brought their children to hear it. The glaze over the prints suggests how long ago the last child was brought.

Why they stopped is unrecoverable — a sickness in a valley, a moved village, a broken line of memory, one old person who was the last to know the way and never quite got around to showing anyone. It does not matter to the room. This is the mild, unsentimental truth of the place: the birds did not notice, and the mountain filed the handprints under mineral, and the visitors became what everything here eventually becomes — part of the wall.

---

**Last, the resident that may not be one.** The hum. It is, strictly, only physics: a curved glassy dome, a peak-hour chorus, frequencies fusing at the shape's own note. It is made of birds and made of room and belongs fully to neither. But stand in it once and you will understand why the handprints are where they are, and why the voles sit up, and why it is tempting — only tempting; the place earns its strangeness and doesn't spend it — to say that the chamber's true inhabitant is the sound itself: a thing born fresh every evening from thousands of small voices and one frozen breath, living for a few minutes, and dying when a bat picks a fight.

If the young mountain is in the middle of having a single loud thought, then these are the words of it: the clicking crowd and the muttering village, the congregation of crickets following the light, the pale undertakers in the warm water, the twelve sealed worlds of shrimp, the whistling sentinels on their paths, the tollkeeper at the door, the elder shrub, the first crusts, and seven sealed hands. None of them will last. All of them, for now, are what the thought is about.
