Everything lives here. That is the answer the basin shouts before the question finishes forming — everything, in densities that break counting, the flush itself, the whole screaming census. But a census is not the same as an answer. Begin with the obvious tenants and work inward, because the basin is built like its own sound: layered, and the truest inhabitant is at the bottom of the stack, in the register below hearing.

**The chorus-dwellers** come first, because they come first in everything. The frogs of the basin are less a group of species than a single distributed organ. They have given up their eyes at different rates — some retain a pale disc of lens, useless, like a fontanelle that never closed — but none of them have given up an inch of their voices. A frog here *is* its frequency. Its body is a resonating chamber first and an animal second: throats that balloon to grotesque fractions of body size, skulls with hollows tuned like the bellies of instruments, and in the oldest lineages the tuning has become heritable architecture, so that you could sort the species blind — everything here is sorted blind — by the pitch their bones want to make. They are territorial about *bandwidth* the way other animals are territorial about ground. A young frog does not search for an empty pool; pools are everywhere. It searches for an empty frequency, a sliver of unclaimed spectrum between its neighbors' calls, and when it finds one it pours itself into that gap and defends it for life, narrowing its voice year by year until it fits exactly. Their wars are wars of jamming. Their courtship is a precision so fine that a female can find one male in a wall of ten thousand by the single hertz that makes him himself.

Above them, **the stridulators** — the insect sheet, the tinnitus layer. These are barely individuals at all. They live fast, breed in fog-like clouds, and their continuous shriek is less communication than infrastructure: a carrier wave the rest of the basin has learned to read *through*, the way a thing is known by the shadow it casts. Most predation in the canopy works this way — not by hearing prey, but by hearing the small local deadening where a body absorbs the insect-noise, a soft spot in the bright sheet. Everything in the basin, it turns out, hunts holes. Remember that.

**The crabs** own the floor. Eyeless, the pale of boiled things, in numbers that make the clinker fields shift like shallow water — and they are the basin's recyclers, its undertakers, its janitors and, lately, its first laborers. The nut-cracking is not instinct. It is patchy, pit by pit; some pools have knocking crabs and some do not, and the knowledge travels — a crab raised by hand away from a knocking pool never learns, and a naive crab dropped among crackers learns in a season. They are passing something other than genes between generations now, in the dark, with their bodies. They have favorite anvil-cracks, worn faintly smooth, the closest thing the basin has to a tool that stays put — to a *place* something made. They have begun, very dimly, to defend these cracks. The first property in the basin is a notch in the rock the width of a nut, and the first arguments about property are conducted, like everything here, at the top of the lungs: a claw-drumming threat-rhythm, crab to crab, over who knocks where.

**The hummers** — the megafauna — are the basin's slow center of gravity. They are descended from something that browsed the old forests before the mountain came; they walked onto the first cooled flows when the moss arrived and never left, and the flush remade them in two directions at once: huge, because food is everywhere, and gentle, because nothing yet exists that can kill them. Their eyes are vestigial pearls. Their hum is produced in a chambered throat the size of a barrel and it travels through rock better than through air — they are in contact through the ground, all of them, all the time, a basin-wide lattice of greeting and position and mood, felt in the soles of every foot in the forest. They are also, it must be said, the closest thing the basin has to memory. They live long — far longer than the boom-crash cycles around them — and they carry the map: where the pits are, which tubes flooded, which flows are still too hot, where the forest was before the last black tongue came down. When a new flow comes, the hummers move first, hours early, in a particular low descending tone that everything else has learned the meaning of, and the forest empties along the lines of their humming like water finding a channel. They are the basin's bell. Whole assemblages of smaller life — the dung crabs, the groomer-birds, the things that live their entire lives in the wet shadow under one belly — have never existed apart from a hummer and could not. To be born in the basin is, for a great many creatures, to be born *into the territory of a hummer*, the way one might be born into a parish.

**The thorn-flocks** roost in the high black, and they are the loud rumor of mind. Each flock is a few thousand strong, blind, bound together by a vocal weave so dense and so individuated that flock members can be a quarter mile apart in absolute dark and still be, functionally, a single animal. They have names — not names like ours, but signature calls, learned in the nest, mimicked by others to address them, which is the same thing. They have dialects; a flock displaced by a flow and forced into a neighbor's range can be picked out by ear for a generation. And they have the thorns. The stripping and shaping of a probe-thorn is taught — a fledgling shadows an adult for a season, fumbling, dropping, being corrected with a particular impatient buzzing note that exists in their repertoire for no other purpose. That note may be the most important sound in the basin: a sound that means *not like that*. A correction. The seed of instruction. The middens beneath their roosts, those drifts of smoothed inch-long spikes, are growing measurably year by year, and in two or three of the oldest roosts the birds have begun pushing the middens into low rims, ridges of discarded tools around the colony — by accident, perhaps, or perhaps not entirely, and that *perhaps not* is the first wall anyone has ever considered building here.

So: that is who lives in the basin, by daylight reckoning, in a place without daylight. But every one of them, frog and crab and hummer and flock, lives the same way — by announcing itself, constantly, painting its existence into the air. The basin's whole population shares a single fundamental act: *making sound*. Which means the basin has one more inhabitant, defined by the only other option.

**The quiet lives here too.**

It is an animal — let that be said plainly, because the basin's residents are not sure, and their unsureness is the medium it swims in. It is the thing inside the moving hole in the chorus. There are not many; the basin could not feed many, and more to the point the basin could not *bear* many — a forest organized entirely around voice can tolerate only a few patches of true silence before the map fails. Perhaps a dozen in the whole lowland. They do not meet. They know each other only as distant absences, holes hearing holes, and what passes between them at those moments — wariness, recognition, something else — is conducted in a medium no one else can read: the shape of nothing.

Its body is a refusal of acoustics. Where the rest of the basin grew throats, it grew baffles: a deep, dense, structurally chambered pelt that does not reflect sound but drinks it, the way the rock drinks rain — fringed at every edge, layered like the canopy itself, so that the insect-shriek which silhouettes every other body in the forest enters this one and does not come back out. It is large, soft, slow until it is not. Its footfalls are spread across pads split into independent cushions that take the clinker edge by edge, soundlessly, on ground where even rain is audible. It does not echolocate — echolocation is a voice, and it has forsworn voice so completely that its kind may no longer be able to call at all; no one has ever heard one. It hunts and navigates by pure listening, and here is the thing that makes it the basin's true and central resident: *it is the only creature that hears the basin whole.* Every frog knows its band. Every flock knows its weave. Every hummer feels the lattice. The quiet one, voiceless, claimless, holding no frequency because it holds none, takes in the entire chorus at once — every layer, every territory, every courtship and threat and birth announced into the dark — and from it assembles, continuously, the one complete map of the basin that exists. The chorus rearranges itself week to week and would be lost in its own turbulence, except that something walks through it nightly, listening to all of it, an archive on four feet.

It kills seldom. It does not need to do more, because its real effect is not predation but *discipline*. The chorus has architecture — those clean stratified bands, that fanatical precision of pitch — and the architecture exists because of the quiet ones. Sloppy callers, drifters, frogs that smear across two bands, birds that fall out of the weave: these are the ones the hole closes over, because a clean chorus is legible and the illegible parts are where it feeds. Over generations it has edited the basin's sound the way rivers edit valleys, where there are rivers. Here there is this instead: a shape of fear moving through the dark, and a million voices singing more exactly because of it. The basin's only law is enforced by its only silence.

And so the two rumors of what is coming face each other across the flush without knowing it: in the high roosts, the thorn-flocks, all noise and naming and correction, mind as conversation; and below, threading the black, the quiet ones, mind as attention, the listeners who own the whole map and say nothing. When the mountain finally sleeps and the rain learns to gather and the first true stream cuts its first soft line across the softening rock, whatever rises out of this basin to meet that older, brighter world will be descended from one of these two — the thing that learned to speak, or the thing that learned to hear — and the basin, which cannot stop screaming long enough to wonder, does not yet know which of its children is listening hardest.

Down in a collapse pit, over warm black water, the crabs are knocking. Far above, a flock corrects a fledgling. Somewhere between, a patch of perfect quiet pauses, takes it all in, and moves on.
