**Who lives in the Soakworks**

Start small, because the place does. Start with the film.

Every wet surface in the Soakworks — which is every surface — wears a living skin perhaps a quarter-millimeter thick: a slick of bacteria and dissolved mineral, faintly golden where the lamplight hits it, the true base of everything that follows. It eats warmth and stone-sweat and the chemistry of the deep water, and it asks for nothing else. Not light. Not seasons. It was here before the borehole, before the bats found the collapse-chimneys, possibly before the caverns themselves had finished pulling apart, and everything larger in the Soakworks is, at some remove, a way of eating it.

**The grazers**

The snails go first, and the snails go slowest. They are white as the calcite they travel across, shells thin as a fingernail and often half-fused to their own backs with new stone, because a snail in the Soakworks is in a race it does not know it is running: it must keep moving faster than the rock grows, and the margin is small. Most of them win for years. The walls are written over with their winding grazing-trails, paler paths through the golden film, crossing and recrossing like the streets of a city seen from very high up. The ones that lose — that stop too long in a seep, that wedge into a crack and wait out something and never start again — become the raisins in the loaf. Crews splitting old flowstone for the catwalk anchors find them constantly: a perfect spiral in cross-section, occupied, sealed, part of the architecture now. Nobody finds this morbid anymore. It is simply what the place does with anything that holds still.

Above the snails, in pace and appetite, come the flatworms and the beetles and the pale crickets. The crickets deserve more than a line. They are the parliament of the walls — long-limbed, blanched, their antennae twice their body length, because in a world without light an antenna is an eye, an ear, and a hand all at once. They feel the air. They read the vapor. A cricket on a wall in the Soakworks knows you are coming before your lamp-glow reaches it, knows it from the pressure-wake of your body moving through the saturated dark, and the wave of stillness that travels along a gallery ahead of a walking human — thousands of insects pausing in sequence — is one of the few visible events in a landscape where nothing visible ever happens. Workers call it *the hush*, though nothing gets quieter. The roar doesn't notice the crickets either way.

**The water-crowd**

The shrimp are beyond counting and the crews have stopped trying. They are small, white, translucent enough that you can watch their last meal moving through them, and they exist at a density that makes the shallows look like weather — drifts and squalls and slow boiling fronts of them, moving on currents and chemical rumors. They are the great converters of the Soakworks: film and bacterial mat and guano-fall in, living matter out, in quantities that feed everything above them.

What is above them is mostly the fish, and the fish are the closest thing the Soakworks has to a citizenry. Pink, soft-bodied, eyeless — not blind in the sense of having lost something, but blind the way a hand is blind, complete on its own terms. They read the water with their whole skin. They know every channel of the Long Soak as pressure and taste, and they have learned, in forty years, the machines. This is documented, grudgingly, in the pump-crew logs: the fish school toward the intake thrum not despite the screens but because of them — the screens collect shrimp, and the fish have made the pumphouses into reefs. There are channels where the densest fish populations in the whole system sit within ten meters of human iron, fat and incurious, packed fin to fin in the warm outwash. The crews screening the intakes twice a shift are, functionally, feeding them. Both parties seem to have accepted the arrangement without ever negotiating it.

**The commuters**

The bats alone live in two worlds, and it shows in them. They are ordinary surface bats — nothing pale or eyeless about them — and they treat the Soakworks the way miners treat it: as a shift. Down the collapse-chimneys at dawn, up at dusk, riding the warm column of saturated air that pours perpetually out of the deep rock like a slow chimney-draft. Their roosts crowd the upper galleries where the vapor thins, and below every roost stands a guano terrace, and on every guano terrace lives a whole second city — mites, springtails, beetles, fungus — that exists nowhere else in the system and would starve in a season if the bats chose other doors.

They are the only things in the Soakworks that know what the sun looks like. The workers are fond of them for this, irrationally, the way sailors are fond of shore birds.

**The crews**

And then the newest animal in the system, four decades resident, still adapting.

There are never more than two hundred people in the Soakworks at once, and they are easy to know by their bodies before you know them by name. The skin of the hands stays pruned for the whole rotation and for a week after; old hands carry a permanent softness in the fingertips, a faint whiteness, that surface people mistake for frailty and that is actually just water, water that got in years ago and made itself at home. Everyone moves the same way — the deliberate, economical, heat-saving gait the place teaches in the first fortnight — and everyone is at least a little deaf in the same band of frequencies, the band the roar occupies, so that two Soakworks veterans meeting on the surface will stand closer than is polite and watch each other's mouths.

They do not really talk down there, even where the roar dips low enough to allow it. The slates were only the beginning. Forty years of slates have bred a hand-language — *wet-speech*, the crews call it, though no one speaks it wet or dry, only signs it — built for the things the place requires: water rising, water steady, stone soft here, stone holding, come, wait, eat, up-cage, down-cage, and a sign, made by drawing the flat hand slowly down the forearm like a film of water descending, that means roughly *patience* and roughly *the place itself* and is used for both without confusion. New workers learn perhaps forty signs. Old crews carry hundreds, including jokes, including an entire genre of insults built on comparisons to surface things — dry, loud, hasty. To call someone *a noise* in wet-speech is serious. It means they are all event and no process.

The used slates do not leave. There is a midden of them below the old survey terrace, decades deep, the oldest layers already going into the stone, and crews on slow shifts sometimes dig in it the way people elsewhere read old letters. *Pump 3 holding. Tell Avis the fish are back. Going down-valley, back by bell.* The flowstone is taking the midden a centimeter at a time, and someday the Soakworks' entire conversation will be a single white seam in the rock, legible to anyone with a saw.

**The soaked**

Most workers rotate up. A few stop. The company's records call them *retained on permanent station*; the crews call them the soaked, and the word carries no contempt, only a kind of wariness, the way one speaks of people who have married into another family. There are maybe a dozen at any time — lamplighters walking the down-valley string, pole-drivers, the turbine keeper at the Stairfall who has held that post for nineteen years and signs more fluently than he speaks. The soaked sleep better below than above; the surface quiet genuinely hurts them now, and most have stopped testing it. They eat the blind fish, which everyone eats, and the shrimp, which the cooks net by the bucket, and they are paler than the rotational crews but not, the doctors keep concluding with faint surprise, less healthy. The place is warm. The water is clean in the ways that matter. Nothing down here wants to bite a human. The Soakworks is, for an underworld, almost embarrassingly gentle.

The oldest of the soaked is a woman named Brek, a former echo-surveyor, who is said to be able to read the roar — to stand on a terrace with her eyes shut and tell you that a flowstone dam has let go in the Third Reach, or that the down-valley falls are carrying a fraction more water than yesterday, which they never are, except the twice in nineteen years when she said so and was right. The crews trust her the way they trust a barometer. She says the roar is not one sound but several thousand, and that learning it is no different from learning faces. She also says, when asked about the far end of the Long Soak, past the last lamp-pole, nothing at all, and signs *patience* with the flat hand down the forearm, and changes the subject.

**Down-valley**

Because there is something down-valley. The crews are quiet about it in the way of people protecting something rather than fearing it.

Nine kilometers of the Long Soak have been charted by echo and perhaps four by lamplight, and in the dark majority, where the warm braided water gathers into deeper channels and the shrimp-drifts run thickest, lives the thing the records call only *the large salamander population, unsurveyed* and the crews call the Lodger — singular, though no one knows that it is. What is known fits on one slate: it is pale, it is eyeless, it is long — the pole-drivers who have seen it at the lamp-string's end disagree between two meters and four, and refuse to be embarrassed about the gap — and it is old in a way the young rock is not. Animals like it, surface science says, can live more than a century and can wait a decade between meals. In water this warm and this crowded with shrimp, nothing has to wait for anything. It moves, the few witnesses agree, the way the place moves: barely, and without hurry, and absolutely.

It has never approached the works. It has never fled them. The crews extend it the same courtesy. There is an unwritten line two poles past the last lamp, and the lamp-string has not been lengthened in eleven years, and when the company surveyor came down with plans to chart the lower reaches, the crews did not argue; they simply proved unable, shift after shift, to find anyone free to carry the gear. The Lodger was here before the borehole. The feeling underground — never written, often signed — is that the humans are recent tenants in an occupied house, and the polite thing is not to go through every room.

**The landlord**

Because in the end the crews are clear-eyed about who actually lives in the Soakworks, and it is none of the above. The snails, the fish, the bats, the soaked, the Lodger itself — all of it is tenancy. The resident is the process. The warm water rising on its deep clock, the stone dissolving here and growing there, the millimeter a year — *that* is the inhabitant, the one whose breathing is the roar, the one slowly furnishing its own house with columns and terraces and the occasional preserved snail, stovepipe, slate, handrail, and, eventually, occupant.

There have been deaths underground; there are always deaths where there is industry. The first were hauled up the borehole for surface burial, and it felt wrong in a way no one could write down. Now, by long custom and against no recorded objection from the company, the dead of the Soakworks are laid on the high white terrace above the old survey camp, in the path of the heaviest seep, and the place is allowed to do with them what it does with everything that holds still. The terrace is smooth now, and bright white, and growing. The crews visit it. They sign to it. The sign they use is the flat hand drawn slowly down the forearm — *patience*, *the place* — which down here is also, it turns out, the sign for the dead, and for the stone, and for staying, and no one has ever needed it to mean only one of those things.

That is who lives there. The water, mostly. Everything else is learning how.
