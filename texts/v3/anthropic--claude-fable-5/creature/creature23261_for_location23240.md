**Who Lives in the Cistern**

The first answer is: almost no one. The chamber tells you this itself — too warm, too mineral, too loud, too new — and the first answer is honest as far as it goes. But it is the answer of someone who looked for fish and, finding none, stopped looking. Stay longer. Put your face under again. The cistern has its citizens. They are small, they are slow, most of them are invisible, and every one of them is a pioneer.

---

**The green ones.**

Begin with what you can see: the filaments. They are the chamber's gentry, its oldest established family, which here means perhaps a decade or two of residence. They did not swim up the spring's stone throat — nothing has managed that — and they did not walk in through the passage. They fell. They came down through the oculus the same way the light does, as spores riding the air, drifting on the warm exhale's return draft, settling onto wet stone by the billion until a few — a very few — found purchase.

And notice *where* they found it. Not on the smooth faces of the blocks. The algae lives in the chisel-cuts — in the small wounds the masons left, the grooves and bites and the wedge-hole that was never smoothed. Every flaw in the work is now a homestead. The strands stream in the upwelling, anchored at one end, free at the other, and each one is a record of a compromise: it must hold tight enough to survive the surge, reach far enough to drink the light. They have mapped the sun-column more faithfully than any instrument could. Where the light lingers, they thicken; where it only passes, they thin to scattered threads; where it never reaches, they do not exist. If you could read the green the way the masons read stone, you could reconstruct the whole arc of the sun across this chamber without ever seeing the sky. The algae is the room's memory of its own afternoons, written in a hand that grows.

They live almost violently slowly. The water is rich in minerals and poor in nearly everything else algae wants — little nitrogen, little of the soft organic broth that fattens pond-weed elsewhere. So the strands stay fine, stay sparse, stay vivid. Their green is the green of hunger in full sun: nothing spent on bulk, everything spent on light-catching, each cell working at the bright edge of what the water will permit.

**The moss.**

At the waterline, the band of moss — a finger high, no more — lives a different and harder life. It is amphibious by necessity. The chop wets it; the warm air dries it; the surge of the spring raises the water a knuckle's height and drops it again, and the moss endures the whole oscillation, soaked and steamed and splashed, dozens of small floods a day. It is the chamber's border guard, occupying the one zone neither the water nor the dry stone fully claims. It too fell through the oculus, spore by spore. It will never grow taller than the splash reaches. Its upper edge is as precise as a surveyor's line, drawn and redrawn daily by the water itself.

**The invisible nation.**

Below the green, beneath notice, is the real population, and it outnumbers everything else in the chamber by a margin that defeats arithmetic.

The thin skin of new lime creeping across the joints — that "film no thicker than frost" — is not purely mineral. Run a fingertip across it and it is very faintly slick. That slickness is a biofilm: bacteria in their tens of billions, living in self-made slime, and the lime is precipitating *through* them, around them, because of them. Their surfaces seed the crystals; their chemistry tips the dissolved stone out of the water. The chamber is sealing itself, yes — but it is being sealed by labor, by the smallest possible masons taking over where the human ones left off. The dead builders cut the stone; the living ones, invisible, are now pointing the joints. In a few centuries the lime will have rounded every chisel-mark, and what looks like the patience of minerals will have been, all along, the work of a workforce.

Some of these residents are older than any other lineage in the room — older than the masons, older than the hillside's soil. They came up the throat. Fish could not make that journey, but heat-loving bacteria have been living in the spring's deep plumbing since long before anyone channeled it, and when the water was led into this chamber, they were simply carried along, tenants who arrived with the plumbing. They line the square stone mouth where the water boils out; the inflow's lip, if you could examine it, wears a faint tan and rust-colored velvet — mats of cells feeding on dissolved minerals, on iron, on sulfur, on the chemical traffic of the deep rock. They need no light at all. While the algae above them lives entirely on the sun, the throat-dwellers live entirely on the planet, and the pool between them is where the two economies meet and slowly mingle.

Drifting in the bright column, among the stone-dust, there are also the glassy ones: diatoms, single cells in shells of sculpted silica, part of the haze's glitter. Most of what sparkles in the light is mineral. Some of it is alive and sparkles anyway.

**The casualties and the callers.**

Animals visit; almost none stay. The oculus that admits light and spores also admits, now and then, a beetle, a moth at dusk drawn by the last glow off the water, a wind-blown fly. The chop is too constant for any of them to ride; they drown quietly, circle the pool's slow rotation once or twice, and are taken by the overflow channel into the dark of the masonry — the chamber's entire intake of animal life, exported as fast as it arrives. Seeds fall through too, and float, and rot, and feed nothing, because nothing here eats that way yet.

Birds know the place from outside. On cold mornings the standing plume above the hillside marks it, and a dove or a small falcon will sometimes sit at the rim of the oculus where the rising warmth comes up, taking the heat without ever entering. They look down into the brightness; the brightness looks back; the arrangement goes no further. Bats have investigated the passage and rejected the chamber — too loud, too wet, the air too thick with vapor for their voices to work. The roar that makes humans fall silent makes the chamber, for an animal that lives by hearing, simply uninhabitable.

**The largest animal.**

Which leaves the rarest resident, the intermittent one, the one whose presence is recorded only as a faint polish on the underwater ledge: a warm-blooded creature, blood-heat almost exactly the heat of the water, who arrives through the narrow passage perhaps a handful of times in a season. They come at midday, because midday is when the chamber performs. They undress in the passage where there is room to, and enter, and stand on the ledge with the water at the sternum, and do not speak — the room long ago settled that question — and stay sometimes a few minutes and sometimes until the sun-column has slid a full flagstone eastward across the floor.

Who they are is harder to say than that they come. Someone who clears the overflow channel when it chatters wrong. Someone, perhaps, whose grandparent fitted these stones, returning to stand inside the family's one surviving sentence. Someone who found the plume on the hillside and followed it down. The ledge's polish does not distinguish among them. To the chamber they are a single recurring species: brief, quiet, buoyant, leaving nothing behind but a few skin cells for the bacteria and a slightly smoother foot's-width of stone.

They are the only resident the place was built *for*, and the only one it does not need.

---

**And the one that never leaves.**

But if you ask the chamber itself what lives here — if living means present always, growing, breathing, keeping a rhythm, claiming the space — then the truest inhabitant is the water. It arrived before the stone had weathered and it has not been absent for a moment since. It turns in its slow rotation like something pacing. It swells and slackens on the spring's irregular pulse like something breathing. It exhales through the oculus, visibly, a plume the hillside can see. It speaks constantly in a voice that fills the vault and silences everything else, and it is, cell by mineral cell, through its tenants and its lime, eating the masons' work and replacing it with itself.

Everything else in the chamber — the green threads, the velvet at the throat, the moss at the line, the silent bather on the ledge — lives in the water's house, on the water's terms, inside the water's voice.

The cistern is almost entirely empty. And it is fully inhabited. Both of these are true at midday, in the light, with the roar coming down off the vault, while the smallest residents lay down their frost-thin stone and the green writes its slow account of the sun.
