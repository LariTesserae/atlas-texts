**Who lives at the Condensate Works**

Thirty-eight people live on the flow, and the number has been thirty-eight, give or take two, for as long as the log has been kept. This is the second strange-but-true fact of the place: nothing about its population is fixed by policy, and yet it holds, the way the gauges hold. People arrive on the causeway of planks across the hot mud, and within a month the Works has decided about them — or they have decided about it, no one can say which way the decision runs — and they either go back across the planks for good, or they stay in a way that is more like setting than like settling. The ones who stay say the same thing about the ones who leave: *the steadiness frightened them.* A place without seasons, without weather, without progress. A place where the answer to *what's new* is structural: nothing. Some people find that unbearable. The thirty-eight find it the opposite of unbearable, though they would never use a word as dramatic as *peace.* They say *steady.* It is their highest compliment, applied equally to pressure readings, planks, and persons.

Their bodies show the place. Steam-cured skin, soft and pale and faintly pearled, hands without a single dry crack — even the smith's hands, even the riggers' — because nothing here ever fully dries, including people. Their hair is always damp at the nape. They do not tan; they take on the color of the light, which is the color of weak tea, so that a worker from the Condensate Works can be identified anywhere in the lowlands by a complexion like the inside of a shell. They sweat constantly and have stopped knowing it, the way a fish has stopped knowing water. A visiting doctor once examined the whole crew over two days and recorded, in a tone of professional unease, that their resting pulses fell within a beat or two of each other — and within a beat or two of the engine. *Whum-whum-whum-whum.* He did not suggest a cause. Nobody at the Works thought one was needed.

They speak little, and what they speak is close-range and exact. The ambient drone has worn their language down to something dense and load-bearing, like the pipes: directions are given in fittings (*past the second expansion joint, left at the cluster*), conditions are given in numbers, affection is given in tea. For everything farther than arm's reach there is the hand-language, an old valve-yard vocabulary of perhaps eighty signs that has, over thirty years, grown verbs and jokes and a way of saying *I missed you* (the sign for *return line*, held a half-second too long). Couples at the Works have been observed arguing across fifty meters of steaming rock in total silence, and bystanders politely not reading their hands.

What they hear is not sound but deviation. A frog chorus at industrial volume will not wake one of them; a steam trap firing four seconds late will wake all of them. The whole settlement has been seen to surface at once in the middle of the night — lamps coming on along the stilted quarters, figures on the gantries — because the engine's third beat had gone very slightly soft, a flaw no instrument caught and the engineer confirmed only by laying her palm against the rock. They are, in this sense, less the operators of the machinery than its nervous system. The works manager has written as much in the log, in one of the few entries that is not the word *steady*: "The plant has thirty-eight additional gauges. These ones walk."

A few of them have become particular, the way valve handles become particular, by long use in one position.

The **engineer of the triple-expansion engine** is the third person to hold the post, and like her two predecessors she has come to treat the engine not as a machine but as a tenant senior to herself. She oils it on a schedule the engine itself dictates — she says she can hear it ask — and she has never once seen it stopped. Neither has anyone living. There is a standing question at the Works, asked the way other places ask about the weather: *what does it look like, still?* Nobody knows. She hopes to retire without finding out.

The **borehole walker** does the circuit of the three domes six times a day, laying a bare hand on each one in a fixed order, and can tell from the bone-deep percolation whether the ground beneath is at its ease. He talks to the boreholes, quietly, in the manner of a man checking on elderly relatives. He maintains that Number Two is the moody one. Thirty years of pressure records do not contradict him.

The **smith** is the only person on the flow whose work involves flame, and this has made her something between a tradeswoman and a keeper of rites. The forge is the one place where heat has *edges* — where you can stand in front of warmth instead of inside it — and people drift to the smithy in their off-hours for no stated reason, to watch fire behave like fire. She tolerates this. She also keeps, on a warm shelf above the quench tank, eleven geckos who have organized themselves by seniority, and she has named all of them after pressure fittings. The oldest, Flange, rides her shoulder on rounds and is considered by the crew to hold a position roughly equivalent to her own.

The **plank-keeper** — the title is unofficial but the work is constant — replaces the soft dark boards across the runoff channels before anyone has noticed they need it. He reads the orange algae the way other men read clocks, judging the temperature of every channel by the exact line where rust-color gives way to flame-color, and he is the acknowledged authority on the quiet spots, having discovered two of them himself. He spends his evenings under the water tower in one of these, not doing anything, which at the Works is a respected occupation.

The **works manager** keeps the log, and the log is the same page over and over, and he has made his peace with this by widening what the page contains. Pressure, output, condensate flow: steady, steady, steady. But also, in a margin that has gradually annexed half the page: the herons. He knows them individually — there are nine — and records their rotation on the trestles with the same gravity he gives the gauges. When the senior heron failed to appear for four mornings, he logged it as an anomaly, the only one in six years. (She came back. He logged that too.)

There is **one child**, born on the flow seven years ago, the first and so far only. She has never seen direct sun, and — this is the fact the workers tell visitors, watching their faces — she has never seen her own shadow. She knows shadows exist the way other children know about oceans, from description. She is entirely unbothered. She knows every gecko on the gantries by name and gait, can find any valve in the Works blindfolded by sound alone, and falls asleep each night with her hand flat on the floor of the stilted quarters, taking her pulse from the rock. The crew regards her as the first true native of the youngest land in the world, and treats her with the slight formality that implies.

And there is **the tree**, which must be counted among the residents because the residents count it. They call it the Grandmother, though it is younger than the engine. Old Sefa, who retired from the rigging crew nine years ago and simply did not leave — there being no rule that said he must, and no place his ears would tolerate — has appointed himself its attendant. He sits with it at the marsh edge most evenings, the one human figure at the boundary of the Works that faces *outward*, into the haze. Beside the tree there is a single grave, the only one, a rigger who came across the planks forty years ago and stayed past staying, and the stone is basalt and already looks ancient, because here even the new rock looks ancient, because here everything is the same age.

The ones who leave — there are always a few, over the years — report the same affliction: they cannot sleep. The nights outside are full of silence, which to a body tuned at the Works is the loudest possible sound, a missing heartbeat the size of the world. Some adapt. Some come back across the planks, sheepish, and are received without comment and given their old bunk, and that night they sleep twelve hours in the wet heat with the *whum-whum-whum-whum* coming up through the iron stilts, and in the morning the works manager adds them back to the count, and the count is thirty-eight again, and the entry reads *steady.*

They are not stranded there, these people. The causeway is open; the pay accrues in a bank in the lowlands; any of them could go. What lives at the Condensate Works is simply what the place itself makes, the same way it makes the orange algae and the drowned-milk light: creatures of the balance. The ground gives heat at a constant rate, the engine takes it at a constant rate, and somewhere in between, thirty-eight people and nine herons and several hundred geckos and one tree and one child who has never had a shadow take their share and give it back, holding, like everything else on the flow, forever at the top of their breath.
