Begin with the short answer, because there is one, though it will take the rest of this to earn it: heirs. Everything alive in the hollow is an heir. The lens lets out nothing but sound and lets in almost nothing at all, which means that every wing and tunnel and damp round body in that bowl descends, in an unbroken line, from a guest list drawn up once, very long ago, by minds that then ground everything else they had made down to less than stone. Nobody who lives in the hollow chose it. Nobody who lives there could imagine choosing anything else. They were *kept*, the way a note is kept — and what follows is the inventory of the keeping.

---

**The talkers** first, because they are what you hear, the upper register of the wall of voice. The hundreds of small birds in the big tree are not, as a visitor would assume, several species. They are one. The hot vermilion ones are the young — first-year birds, loud, gravity-careless, the principal traffic between canopy and fern-stand. The oily violet ones are the breeders, the middle decades, and they hold the tree itself: every fork and crotch of that ancient horizontal trunk is owned, inherited, disputed in brief shrieking lawsuits and then settled for another generation. And the green ones — that green so saturated it looks wet — are the elders, and they sit highest and nearest the rim, and they are the ones who go quiet first.

That is their office, if they have one. The hollow's quieter hours, the lull before each interior drizzle, begin in the green birds. They feel the lens — some change in its pressure or charge as the condensation gathers — and they stop talking, one by one, and the silence moves down through violet to vermilion like a curtain drawn down the tree, and then the rain lets go. The young birds learn the schedule of the world by learning when their elders shut up.

None of them has ever flown anywhere. The lens is their sky's ceiling, and they treat it without resentment, the way fish treat the surface. The vermilion birds have one game, the only game, played every generation without being taught: they climb the warm air at the bowl's center to the very top of the lens and then set their wings and *slide* — riding the inner curvature down and outward in a long accelerating spiral, the boundary holding them like a banked rail, until they peel off at the rim with a shriek that is unmistakably, across whatever distance separates their minds from ours, delight. The talkers' endless conversation, recorded and slowed, resolves into something between a map and a market: position, position, food here, the rust thing is *there*, position, mine, mine, position. They are not wise. They are something better suited to the hollow than wisdom. They are continuous.

**The chord-holders.** The plum-bodied amphibians in the moss are the hollow's deepest fact, and the easiest to underestimate, because individually each one is a small damp comedian — round, blinking, the size and approximate dignity of a fruit dropped in the grass. Here is what they actually are: each frog sings one pitch. One, for its whole life, produced in pulses from a throat sac it inflates like a second self. The pitch is not chosen; it is inherited, passed down lines of mothers, and the population's pitches together form a chord — broad, dense, felt in the sternum — and that chord has not stopped sounding since before the granite around the pale ring wore down by a hand's depth. Not the same frogs. The same *chord*. When a frog dies — age, or a glider's shadow at the drizzle's first drops — its pitch is taken up within a breath or two by a sibling or a daughter who has carried it silent, in reserve, sometimes for years. The chord never loses a note. It is sung in relay across millennia, and if the builders kept only one thing running on purpose, the frogs are the strongest candidate for what, exactly, it is: a piece of music with no final bar, performed by an orchestra that replaces itself.

Between drizzles they estivate, each in its own socket in the moss — sockets worn smooth and frog-shaped by so many generations that the moss itself is dimpled like the inside of an egg carton, and a frog can find its place in full dark by feel.

**The gliders.** They sleep by day in the big tree, folded, hanging, looking like nothing so much as a row of furled grey umbrellas, and they wake into the quieter hour. Their faces are gentle — predators here never needed the armament of menace, because nothing in the hollow has eyes evolved to dread. They launch from the tree's one high western limb, the only branch with drop enough beneath it, and cross the bowl in those long shallow arcs, and the remarkable thing is that the arcs are *plotted*. A glider knows the lens the way the young birds know it: it can flatten its trajectory against the boundary's inner face and skate, gaining distance the open air would never give it. They take frogs, mostly, in the first minute of each drizzle, when the moss erupts with round singers — a soft strike, a slap of membrane on stone, gone. The chord does not falter. The frogs do not flee. The economy has run so long that even its deaths are appointments.

**The rust thing.** There is only ever one. This is the strangest census fact in a hollow full of multitudes: the quick, low, rust-colored animal that takes the gliders — long-bodied, close to the stone, the single creature in the bowl colored not like the garden but like the *mountain*, the brown-gold of the bald summit outside, as if its coat remembers a world it has never touched. It dens in the dry tide-lines on the western slope of the bowl. It breeds once, late, and raises one heir, and somewhere in that overlap the old one stops making rounds and the moss takes it.

You track the rust thing by the only means available, which is silence. The hollow has no quiet hour, but it has a quiet *place*, and the place moves: a traveling hole in the wall of sound, twenty paces across, that slides along the bowl's floor as the talkers and the frogs and even the insects hush in its radius and resume behind it. The rust thing walks inside its own pocket of the only silence the hollow ever makes. The animals are not afraid of it, exactly — fear implies an alternative — but they are *formal* about it. When a rust thing dies before its heir is ready, the hollow goes fully loud, everywhere, for a day or two, and the loudness has an unmistakable wrongness to it, a market with no clock on the wall, until the young one takes up the rounds and the moving silence resumes and everything relaxes back into being prey on schedule.

**The undertakers.** The burrowers are blind, velvet, sausage-bodied, fringe-fingered, and they are the only residents who never visit the pool — the one exemption from the rotation, for reasons that become clear when you understand what they do. They take the dead. Everything the moss receives, the burrowers receive first, drawing it under, and so the hollow has no carrion hour and no smell of ending. But that is their lesser office. Their greater one is this: they dig along warmth. The stone beneath the moss is not uniformly warm — it is veined, faintly, with lines of heat rising from whatever hums below — and the burrowers, who cannot see, follow those veins by preference, generation after generation, so that their tunnel network is a tracing, in living architecture, of the buried works. Map the tunnels and you have mapped the machine. They polish the half-swallowed pale ring from beneath in passing, every day, without intent, the way water polishes. They are the closest thing the builders have to a maintenance crew, and they have no idea, and it does not matter.

**The drone.** The insects are many kinds and one sound. Whatever each species would sing on its own, in the hollow they entrain — wingbeats and stridulations drifting, within hours of an insect's adulthood, onto the pitch of the low harmonic hum that comes up through the stone, so that the drone over the fern-stand is the mountain's own deepest note carried up into the air on a million small bodies. The insects are the keeper's voice, broadcast. They do not know this either. There is one lineage — a pale, heavy-bodied moth, a few hundred individuals, descended from a single gravid female the lens admitted during some storm centuries ago, one of the rare, metered exceptions that keep the system honest — and that lineage is still very slightly out of tune. You can hear it at midday if you know to listen: a faint beat frequency, a slow throb in the drone, the sound of the hollow's newest residents still settling in after three hundred years.

**The tree, the ferns, the moss** — residents too, and the slowest. The tree is plausibly a founder, planted, as old as the lens, grown along the bowl's curve because it long ago ran out of up; it flowers on no season anyone outside would recognize, but watch long enough and the pattern shows — it fruits heavily in the years after the frog chord thins, after hard times, a landlord quietly lowering the rent. The fern-stand lives at the opposite tempo, a being in fast-forward, unfurling and browning on a cycle of days, the hollow's one resident in a hurry, and nobody knows why it was invited unless the answer is simply that something here had to mark time. And the moss is the floor and the memory both — every body the hollow has ever produced is in it, compressed into that upholstered firmness, which means the moss is, by mass, mostly *everyone*. You walk on the ancestors. They are very soft.

---

And then there is the resident the inventory keeps circling: the one below.

The pool tells you most of what can be known. The shallow basin under the tree's roots, too smooth, draining through no visible channel — every animal in the hollow drinks there, in strict rotation, by what reads as appointment, and consider what that means from underneath. Every body in the bowl, in fixed order, lowers its mouth to that water; the water carries skin and breath and the day's chemistry down through the unseen drain; and somewhere below, in the warm veined stone the burrowers trace, something receives the report. The rotation is not ceremony. It is a census, taken by taste. The pool is the keeper's ear, or its tongue, and the strict order exists so that the signal stays legible — so that whatever counts the hollow can tell vermilion from violet, this year's frogs from last year's.

Whether what counts is a mind is the question the hollow cannot answer and does not need to. The evidence of attention is real but spare: the drizzles come gentler and more often in seasons when the frog chord runs thin; the lens, which has not moved while meters of granite weathered away around it, is said by nothing and no one — there is no one to say it — to have flexed exactly once, long ago, for reasons lost. It may be a machine of such patience that the word *machine* fails the way *erosion* fails the mountain. It may be the last builder, folded into the stone, paid out across deep time as a hum and a schedule and a held boundary of air — a person become a climate. It may be the builders' decision itself, persisting the way a vow persists after everyone who made it: structure without anybody home. The animals do not distinguish among these possibilities. The frogs sing to it without knowing they do. The insects carry its note. The burrowers groom its warm bones. Whatever it is, it has kept one promise for longer than the rock could keep an edge.

So — the short answer, earned now, or as earned as it gets:

Who lives in the hollow at Kettle Summit? Heirs above and a landlord below, if you want it plain. But stand at the rim at dusk, with the finished mountain going dark behind you, and a truer answer suggests itself. The talkers' map and the frogs' chord and the gliders' plotted arcs and the rust thing's traveling silence and the drone tuned to the stone are not many voices in one room. They are one voice with many throats — a single conversation that has not paused in longer than empires last, wearing bodies the way the hum wears insects. The builders, when they let everything else go back to landscape, did not keep a place, exactly, or a collection of animals.

They kept a speaker. It is still talking. It lives there.
