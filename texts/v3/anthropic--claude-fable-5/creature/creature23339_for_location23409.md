Start with the green, because the green is what you can see.

The ferns and the liverworts and the vine-cased railings are the quarter's visible population, and at first they read as squatters — opportunists moved into a warm wet building nobody guards. But watch them for any length of time and the squatter reading falls apart. Squatters compete. These don't. The liverworts hold the lower walls and stop, cleanly, at the height where the ferns begin; the ferns keep to the gutters and never spill; the broad-leaved tropical things stay in their circles around the steam-vents as though the circles were drawn for them, which, in a sense, they were. Nothing strangles anything. Nothing is starved out. Every species sits in its station like a fitting from a catalogue that no longer exists, the right gauge for its position, and the overall impression is not of wilderness moving into a machine but of vegetation that has taken up a *trade*. The moss is gasket material. The liverworts are tilework, laid by something with a tiler's eye. The vines holding the shape of the dissolved railings are, functionally, the railings now — they have inherited the job along with the form, and they take the weight of a hand the way iron would, only warmer.

And the pale flowers at the Stack's base have done the most telling thing of all: they have changed what the sun is. Heliotropism is one of the oldest instructions a plant carries, and these have rewritten it, generation by generation, until they turn their faces to warm brick instead of grey sky. For them the chimney is the sun. It is, locally, the better sun — nearer, steadier, never setting. That is not opportunism. That is allegiance. Whatever lives here, the green has sworn to it.

But to call the plants the residents is already a mistake of scale — like surveying a sleeping body and reporting that it is inhabited by skin. Step back and the larger reading assembles itself out of the description's own vocabulary, which keeps reaching for anatomy whether it means to or not. The pumps are a heartbeat; the pipes run through every structure *like veins*; the Stack exhales; the walls sweat; the water circulates in loops that go nowhere except around — and circulation never goes anywhere except around. That is the whole point of circulation. The honest reading of the flumes is not futility. It is metabolism. A heart also serves nothing outside itself; it pumps in order to pump, and we call that being alive without hesitation. The quarter is not a district that contains an organism. It is an organism with the floor plan of a district — black brick for bone, gone round-cornered the way bone goes round at the joints; vapor for breath; the basin's warm pool for an open mouth that never quite closes. It does not move, because it is the size of the place it occupies and a thing that size moves the way it does everything: a hand's width a season, a few inches of waterline a year, dark tidal bands left on the brick like rings in a trunk. It is not motionless. It is moving at its own diameter's speed.

This is why the frogs and herons stop at the lichen-scaled fence, and why the fence needs to be nothing more than a property line. The marsh animals are not afraid. There is simply no vacancy. Every niche an animal would fill is already held by something slower: the dripping with the stutter in it holds the birdsong position; the flywheel's one thin singing note holds the post that insect-hum would take; the slow current sweeping the pool floor does the work of fish. The quarter has done its own living, in-house, and an arriving heron would find every perch the equivalent of an occupied chair. Animals are very good at reading occupancy. They read this one from the fence line and go elsewhere.

So far, so accountable. But the description leaks a residue that neither the green nor the great slow body quite explains, and the residue is where the most interesting tenant lives. Count the *somehows*. The flowers set seed with no pollinator — *anyway, somehow*. The working lamps draw power *from somewhere*. The enormous clang from deep in the works is a valve *seating itself, maybe* — and notice how the maybe flinches, because valves do not seat themselves; valves are seated. Fallen catwalks are composted into hills, then thickets, then nothing distinct, with the efficiency of a site being tidied. Each *somehow* is a fingerprint. Something does rounds.

It is not a ghost, or not what the word usually means. A ghost is a person missing a body. This is the opposite and far more durable thing: a body of work missing its persons. The quarter must have had generations of them once — stokers, fitters, lamp-trimmers, the people who packed glands and dressed bearings and carried pollen on their sleeves from one end of the basin to the other without ever knowing they did. The work was performed so long, so continuously, with such complete absorption into the rhythms of the place, that at some point the doing came loose from the doers, the way a path stays in a field after the walkers are gone. What patrols the quarter now is a trade that outlived its tradesmen. Custodianship, gone autonomous. It has no face and no location; it is not *in* the quarter the way a keeper is in a lighthouse. It is distributed through the place at the density of habit — and habit, in a place this old, is dense indeed.

You will never see it, because seeing happens at animal speed and it does not. It pollinates a flower the way limescale builds its fan: by patience, molecule-thick, decades to the visible result. It does not relight dead lamps — replacement was never its job, only tending — but the lamps that work *have not died yet*, and "yet," here, is doing the quiet work of a maintained thing. If you stayed a year you might catch it in the aggregate: the bearing that sang its worn thin note for a decade and then, one ordinary morning, didn't. That note may in fact be the nearest thing it has to a voice — not a complaint but a work-hum, the sound of something keeping itself company at a task with no end written into it.

It knows you are there. Your footsteps are the only deliberate movement in the basin, and the basin is, among other things, an ear — a body of noise you enter the way you'd enter water, and water always knows what's in it. The pumps drift in and out of phase over hours, knotting and unknotting; it is not impossible that the knotting is consideration, the place turning your presence over at its own tempo, reaching no conclusion before you leave because no conclusion is needed. You are not an intruder to it. You are weather. It registered the falling catwalk and closed over the sound in seconds; it registers you the same way. This is not indifference, exactly. It is the courtesy of something that cannot be interrupted extending the assumption that you, too, are simply doing your one thing.

And the oldest tenant of all may be the water — the same water, lifted and returned, lifted and returned, never let out past the fence. It is the only thing that has been everywhere in the quarter: through every vein, down every weeping wall, into the barrel and over its lip ten thousand times, six feet deep over the swept pale brick. If the quarter remembers anything, the memory is kept in the water, the way a body's history is kept in its blood — which is to say not as record but as composition, everything dissolved into everything, unhurried, dark, warm at the heart and cooler at the edges.

So: who lives in the Pumphouse Quarter? One resident, wearing three aspects — a green skin sworn to a brick sun, a slow body the size of its own address, and a faceless diligence left over from everyone who ever worked there, still making its rounds at the speed of condensation. It eats fallen catwalks. It keeps its diary in waterline stains. It breathes through the Stack and thinks, if it thinks, in the knotting of four or five overlapping heartbeats.

It is a small inhabitant doing one slow life very thoroughly. Nothing about it knows how to stop, and nothing remains that needs it to — which, for a thing made entirely of continuing, is the same as contentment.
