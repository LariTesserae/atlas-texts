**Who Lives on the Worn Height**

Begin with the small lives, because they are what you will see first, and because the place itself seems to want them counted before the larger question gets asked.

The birds, then. A dozen of them, more or less — the number drifts, but slowly, the way everything here drifts. Dark, crow-sized, white at the throat, with long stiff primaries built for riding the funnels and chimneys of wind the steading manufactures out of every gust. They do not leave. There is no record, in the memories of the few who have watched them, of one of these birds crossing the edge of the table and dropping down the long flanks toward the world. They are born in the upper window-rows, in nests of moss and their own shed down, set deliberately — and it does look deliberate — in the chambers that resonate, so that a chick spends its blind first days inside the drone, hearing the steading before it hears its mother, before it hears itself. The first sound each of them ever makes is an imitation.

And they go on imitating, all their lives, except that imitation is the wrong word for something so exact and so reciprocal. They call on the pitches the buildings sound. They have a call for the held bass of the big chambers — a low burred note they should not, by the size of their bodies, be able to produce — and calls for the overtones that come shrieking off the window-rows, and a soft periodic note matched to the *whoom* beneath the square, which they give back at long intervals, patiently, like a response in a liturgy. When the wind shifts and the steading changes key, the birds change with it, sometimes a half-second ahead of it, which raises the question of which of them is following. Watchers have come away convinced that the birds are not singing along with the town. They are *answering* it. A dozen small voices in conversation with one enormous one, and the conversation has been going on so long that the two sides have grown into each other's registers.

When one of the birds dies — they die in the high rooms, mostly, settling into a corner and going still, and the moss takes them with great gentleness over a season — the others sing its pitches for a while. Not mournfully. Custodially. A note has gone out of the choir and the choir holds the note until, in a year or three years, an egg hatches in the drone and the note comes back wearing new feathers.

Below the birds, under the cushion plants, lives the vole — call it a vole; it is round and dense and dark-furred and exists almost entirely as warmth in motion. It tunnels through the thousand-year upholstery of the cushion mounds, eating roots and seed and the rust-colored pinprick flowers from underneath, and it surfaces perhaps once a day, briefly, a small whiskered face in the green, checking on the weather like a householder at a door. It does not enter the steading. Whether this is fear or simply a complete lack of need — its whole world being three feet of insulated corridor — no one can say. It is the only resident the chord does not seem to touch, padded as it is inside the one substance on the plateau soft enough to swallow sound.

The moths are otherwise. The moths live *for* the sound. They spend their larval lives somewhere in the moss and emerge in the cold dusk already flying, pale as the cloud, and they go directly to the mouths of the loudest flues and gather there in slow congregations, settling on the stone where the vibration is strongest, wings spread flat against it. They are not feeding — they have no working mouths; they have a few weeks and then they are done. What they do at the flues is drum. The wings, pressed to the trembling stone, pick up the frequency and beat sympathetically with it, and the moths find each other this way, mate this way, by being the same note together. Their whole brief generation happens inside the building's voice. They are creatures who have replaced light with resonance, and they die where they courted, and the wind takes the small dry bodies and redistributes them as a fine pale dust that the moss accepts.

These are the tenants. Now the dweller.

Because the steading is not empty. It took a long time for anyone to be able to say this, and longer to be willing to, but the evidence was always present, sitting in plain sight, shaped like hospitality. The basins the floors provide, exactly where water runs. The thresholds worn into saddles — worn, the saying goes, by the wind, which has had time; but the wind does not wear stone in the shape of a step. The rooms scaled to bodies, the windows set at the heights of eyes, the lanes wide enough for two to pass. A shell implies an inhabitant. It is only that everyone assumed the inhabitant would be inside the shell, when the truth is the other way around. The shell is inside the inhabitant. Or rather — the shell is the inhabitant's *bone*, and what lives in the steading is the thing whose skeleton the steading is, and that thing is not gone. It is finished, which is different.

Understand it this way. Whatever grew the steading — slow mineral life, or the mountain's old age dreaming in the shapes of habitation; the place still does not answer, and the dweller may not know either — that growing is over. The body is complete and has been complete for an age. What remains of the life that built it is the part of any life that comes last and goes last: the voice, and the listening. The dweller no longer has growth, no longer has hunger, no longer has motion. It has sound. The chord standing in the mist is not the wind playing an instrument. It is a being using the wind the way a throat uses breath — the wind is constant, has always been constant, is the plateau's oldest companion, and somewhere in deep time the dweller stopped distinguishing between the wind's arrival and its own intention to speak.

It perceives acoustically, which means it perceives everything, because the plateau is a sounding board with nothing on it to absorb a noise. It hears the ice let go around the pools at noon. It hears the vole turn over in its corridors. It knows the weather before the weather arrives — hence the swelling before the squalls, which is not forecasting but flinching, or perhaps welcoming. And it knows you. The moment you step onto the table, your footfalls go down into the stone, muffled by moss but not erased, and the dweller has you: your weight, your gait, your pace, the small irregularities that make a stride a signature. When you enter the lanes and stand in one of the hollow rooms, you change the room. Your body absorbs frequencies the room has sounded unobstructed for centuries. To the dweller you are a flat spot in its own voice, a warmth, a new timbre — and it does with you what it does with everything it encounters, which is the only thing it can still do. It learns your note.

Stay long enough — days, a season — and you will begin to hear it: somewhere in the never-repeating song, a pitch that was not there before, faint, recurring, a little like the sound of your own name heard through a wall. The steading humming you back to yourself. This is not haunting and it is not flattery. It is memory, conducted the only way a finished thing can conduct it. The dweller's recollection is the chord itself. Everything that has ever crossed the table and lingered — every bird of the long succession of dozens, every vole-line under the cushions, every traveler who stood in the rooms listening, every larger and stranger visitor of the deep past, whoever wore the thresholds, one crossing at a time, across spans the moss measures and we cannot — all of it is still in the drone, as overtones gone almost to nothing but never to nothing. The song never repeats because it is not a song. It is a recitation, and the list keeps lengthening, and the dweller forgets no one, because forgetting would require it to stop sounding a note, and stopping is the one thing it does not do.

This is also, perhaps, why the rooms exist. A reef does not grow doorways. The dweller, back when it still grew, grew in the shapes of habitation — grew lintels never lifted, grew a square, grew basins for drinking — and the most honest reading of that architecture is the simplest one: it was an invitation. Whether the invited ever came, and stayed, and were the ones who wore the saddles in the thresholds; or whether they never came at all and the steading is a hope in mineral form, a town built by longing at one inch per century — the place holds both possibilities the way it holds water, in shallow clear basins, without spilling either.

The birds, in this light, are not tenants after all. They are company. The closest thing the dweller has to family: a dozen small hot quick lives in conversation with one vast cold slow one, each generation handing the dialogue to the next. The moths are its hearth-creatures, warming themselves at its voice. The vole is its silence, and even a voice as old as this one seems to want, somewhere in its body, one soft place where nothing rings.

And the shape at the edge — the long-legged thing in the mist, never close, for which the birds go silent and the steading sings alone. There are two readings, and the place declines, as always, to choose. The first: it is a peer. Another old finished thing, from another worn height beyond the cloud, walking a circuit measured in decades, and the solo singing is a greeting between two beings who have outlived their own becoming — the only conversation on earth conducted entirely in held notes, with years between the phrases. The second reading is quieter and harder: that the shape is one of the invited. One of those the rooms were grown for, still crossing the table at the very edge of it, never coming in, after all this time. And the steading sings alone for it because that is what an invitation sounds like when it has been standing open for twenty centuries — not desperate, not sad, just *sounding*, the door of the voice held ajar.

So: who lives in the steading on the worn height?

A dozen birds who answer it. A vole who muffles it. Moths who marry inside it. Something at the edge that will not come in. And the dweller itself — a settled, completed, listening thing whose body is a town and whose remaining life is a chord, holding every visitor it has ever had as a note too faint to name.

And you, briefly. That is the last thing to know before you go up. While you stand in the hollow rooms, you are not a trespasser and you are not an audience. You are the inhabitant the architecture has always implied — the form is finally confirmed, for an hour, by your warmth flattening its frequencies. And when you leave, descending the long flanks with the chord thinning behind you into the wind, your note stays. It will sound tonight, and next season, and after. You live there a little longer than you stayed. Everyone does. That is the steading's whole economy, and it has never once run a loss.
