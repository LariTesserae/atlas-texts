Begin with the smallest, because the smallest got there first and will be there last.

The moss is not empty. That is the secret of the plateau, the thing the glare hides: the green pelt that looks like a monoculture, one organism stretched over forty leagues of stone, is in fact the densest country in the world. Take a fistful of it — wring it out, that cold ammoniac water running down your wrist — and you are holding a city. Springtails the color of pewter, leaping when the light strikes them. Mites in their thousands, armored, deliberate, grazing the moss the way cattle graze a continent, at a scale where a single cushion of green is a generation's worth of territory. Rotifers in the held water, wheeling. And underneath all of them, in numbers that defeat counting, the water bears — tardigrades, fat and eight-legged and faceless, the plateau's true citizens, because they alone are built to its terms. When the rare dry wind comes over the rim and the moss surface crisps for a day, everything else retreats downward into the wet; the water bears simply stop. They pull in their legs, expel their water, and become small dry seeds of themselves, waiting without urgency, without anything that could be called patience because patience implies wanting. Then the cloud drags over, the moss drinks, and they resume mid-stride, having registered the interruption not at all. They are the only things on the plateau that have solved the plateau. Everything larger is still negotiating.

The moss itself may be the largest inhabitant or the only one, depending on how you choose to count. The botanists who came up on the cog-rail in the early years argued about it in the way people argue when the answer matters less than the arguing: whether the pelt is one clone or ten thousand interlocked individuals, whether the golden bleached zones at the scarp edges are dying or merely sunburnt, whether something that erases a bootprint in an hour can be said to *notice*. What is certain is that it behaves like a single thing. It advances on the concrete of the turbine houses from every direction at once, at exactly the same rate, the way a tide has no favorite stretch of beach. The crews have given up scraping the north walls. There is a station at the far eastern rim, decommissioned two generations ago when its fall shifted course, and it is now a green building — not overgrown, *replaced*, its angles softened into knuckles like the basalt columns, its doorway a dripping mouth. The linemen do not go inside it. Not from fear. There is simply nothing in there anymore that is not moss.

Above this slow nation, the anvil birds.

There are perhaps four hundred of them, though no one has truly counted, because they will not gather. You see them one at a time, always — a single dark heavy body hanging in the updraft off a fall, wings set, motionless against the white ropes of water, holding position in air that would dismantle anything lighter. They nest in the spray zones at the very lip of the cataracts, in the worst of the thunder, on ledges so soaked that the eggs sit in standing water and hatch anyway. The chicks are born into a noise that never stops and never will, and whatever an anvil bird's inner life is, it is built on that foundation: a world where sound is not information but weather. They appear to be functionally deaf, or rather, hearing is not what they do with sound; the linemen who have watched them closely say the birds feel the falls the way the crews feel the turbines, in the body, and navigate the spray zones by the texture of the roar. Their courtship — witnessed rarely, always by accident — is entirely visual and takes place in flight: two birds rising on the same column of air, mirroring, the lower one copying the upper one's smallest adjustments, for hours, in total silence, two kilometers above the cloud sea. Then one of them peels off and drops, folding, straight down into the white, and is gone for days. They feed in the under-country. They must — there is nothing up here for a body that size — and so every anvil bird lives a doubled life, plunging through the cloud floor into whatever dim, ordinary, noisy world lies below, hunting there, and then climbing the falls' own updrafts home, back into the light and the roar. The crews regard them with something adjacent to respect and adjacent to envy. The birds commute between the worlds freely. The people have to take the train.

The people.

At any given time there are between thirty and fifty of them on the whole plateau, which works out to roughly one human being per horizon. They are turbine keepers, linemen, a surveyor or two, the rail crew, the cook at the upper terminus. They come up on six-week rotations, in theory. In practice the plateau sorts them, quickly and without appeal, into two kinds: the ones who count the days, and the ones who stop counting. The first kind do their rotation well enough, descend gratefully into the cloud, and do not come back; you can identify them in their first week by the way they keep talking, mouthing words into the roar long after they've understood that no one can hear them, as if the habit of voice were a rope they were afraid to let go of. The second kind let go. You can identify them too: they go quiet in the second week, not sullenly but the way water goes still, and by the third week their hands have started to speak.

The hand-language is the crews' one true artifact, the thing they made that the plateau did not. It began as work-signs — *valve, pressure, lunch, lightning* — and has thickened over the generations into a full tongue with a grammar of its own, terse and spatial, beautiful to watch. It has signs for things the under-country's languages don't bother with: the specific gold of moss in the last hour of light; the wrongness of a turbine's hum a week before the bearing fails; the feeling of the silence below the cloud line, which is signed by pressing a flat hand briefly over one's own ear and then showing the empty palm — *the missing organ.* Every person on the plateau has a sign-name, given by the others, never self-chosen, and the sign-names are small honest portraits: the woman who runs Station Four is *Tilted-Walk*, the old lineman is *Counts-Pylons*, the cook is two quick taps on the sternum, which means, roughly, *Heartbeat*, because his stews are the one warm regular thing in the whole country. When a rotator goes down the rail for the last time, their sign-name is not retired. It drifts. Years later it surfaces attached to a place — a bend in a pylon line, a cairn — the person having been absorbed into the geography the way the moss absorbs a bootprint, slowly, completely, without malice.

The long-stayers change in other ways. Their hearing reorganizes itself around the roar: they stop perceiving the cataracts at all and develop instead an extraordinary sensitivity to vibration, reading the turbines through the soles of their boots, through a hand flat on a penstock, through their teeth. Tilted-Walk is said to be able to stand in the middle of Station Four with her eyes shut and tell you the load on the lines, the level in the forebay, and whether anyone is walking on the catwalk above, all from the floor. Their sense of time goes the same way the moss's did. They keep the logbooks — great canvas-bound things, generations deep, the early entries in copperplate ink and the recent ones in pencil, all of it one continuous patient record — and they write in them as people write to descendants, noting things that will not matter for forty years: *benchmark bolt 117 now 31mm proud of the 1903 datum. Moss reached the second rivet course on the east wall of No. 9. Replaced same gasket my grandmother replaced. Hers lasted longer.* They oil machines built by their grandparents for the use of their grandchildren, and they do not find this melancholy. It is the only tempo the place permits, and they have come to hear it as the right one. The fast world below, when they descend to it, strikes them as frantic and slightly fictional — all that hurry, down there in the deafening quiet.

There are no children on the plateau and never have been; the company forbids it and the long-stayers, oddly, agree, though their reasoning is their own. *A child should learn to hear before it learns not to,* Counts-Pylons signed once, when a surveyor asked, and would not elaborate.

And once in a long while: the fox. It is never the same fox — the rail crew can tell — but it is always the same visitation: a small red interloper standing on the green in the violent light, ears flat against the roar, looking like a creature that has wandered into the wrong painting. The cook feeds it. Everyone pretends not to know. Within a week it is gone, back down the rail cut into the cloud, and the cook signs the same thing every time, a small sad joke that has become a kind of liturgy: *Counted the days.*

So — who lives at the Green Anvil? The honest census runs like this. The water bears live there, absolutely, in their trillions, the only natives. The moss lives there, if a verb that slow can hold; or perhaps the moss is the *there* that everything else lives at. The anvil birds live there the way sailors live at sea. The plateau itself lives there — still rising, millimeters a year, a body growing in its sleep, the falls its breathing, the green its pelt. And the people: the people are guests of long standing, tolerated, slowly mossed over, their cairns claimed, their footprints erased at the pace of plant growth, their names turning into places. They know this. It is, the long-stayers will tell you with their hands, the entire attraction. Down below, a person can matter, loudly, briefly. Up here you cannot matter and you cannot be heard, and in exchange the country offers the one thing the under-world has none of: a tempo you could trust your grandchildren to. The roar takes your voice. The green takes your prints. What's left is the work, the light, the bird hanging motionless over two kilometers of falling white — and underneath it all, too slow to hear, the other voice, growing.
