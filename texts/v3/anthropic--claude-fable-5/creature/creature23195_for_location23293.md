## The Grove People

The two valleys have two different names for them, and they answer cheerfully to both, but among themselves they are only *the grove* — the word does for the trees, the town, and the people without distinction, which tells you most of what you need to know about how they understand themselves. Asked where he is from, a man of the saddle will name his tree before he names anything you would recognize as a family.

They are not many. Perhaps two hundred souls across the forty households, swelling and shrinking with the market fifths, with marriages down into the valleys and marriages up out of them. They are short, deep-chested people — the altitude builds a barrel of ribs into every child by the age of ten — with the heavy forearms and articulate, knot-wise fingers of a people whose whole material culture is lashing, splicing, weaving, and bending. Their hands are permanently water-wrinkled at the fingertips. They have stopped noticing this. Their word for *dry* does not mean the absence of water; it means *near the fire*, a location rather than a condition, because the condition does not otherwise occur.

Their feet are the town's true tools. Bare nearly always, broad, the toes long and slightly splayed and capable of an honest grip, the soles worn to the same smooth dark finish as the handrails they travel. A grove child can hang by its feet before it can speak. A grove elder, knees long gone, still descends a wet root-stair faster than a porter half her age, because she is not walking on it — she is reading it, foot by foot, a text she has known for seventy years.

## The Long Work

The central profession of the grove, the one all others orbit, is shaping — the training of the young aerial roots — and it is a strange profession because no one who practices it ever sees their best work.

A shaper works in a horizon of fifty to eighty years. She walks the trees looking at roots no thicker than a wrist, soft, pale, dangling, and she sees the house they will be when she is dead. She bends them with cordage and hanging stone weights, lashes them across gaps to roots from neighboring trees so they will fuse, wraps the unions in wet moss and binds them and returns every few weeks for years to adjust the tension a finger's width. The plans are not drawn — there is nothing here to draw on that would survive the wet — but knotted: long cords hung in each shaper's house, where a sequence of knots records which roots were started, when, toward what. A shaper's apprentices learn the knot-language before they learn the bending, because the knots are the inheritance. When a shaper dies, her cords pass on, and her successor spends a season just walking the trees with the cords in hand, finding each young training underway, learning what the dead woman intended.

This means every house in the grove is a collaboration between at least three people who mostly never met: the shaper who began it, the shaper who finished it, and the family who lives in it. It produces a particular temperament. Grove people are nearly incapable of impatience in the way lowlanders mean it — they will haggle ferociously over a basket of salt, but the idea of being frustrated that something takes forty years strikes them as a category error, like being frustrated at rain. It also produces the grove's one real form of fame. The shapers whose names survive are the ones whose roots, two generations on, turned out true — a doorway that needed no trimming, a stair whose rise is kind to old knees. People live inside the reputations of the dead and add to them daily by touch.

## The Watchers

If the shapers hold the grove's future, the watchers hold its present, and they are mostly old.

Every great limb in the canopy is known, named, and assessed, and the assessing is done by a loose council of elders whose qualification is simply that they have heard the trees in every weather for sixty years. Their vocabulary for wood is the richest part of the language. A limb may be *asking* — creaking in a particular descending way under wind. It may be *holding its breath* — silent when its neighbors speak, which is worse. The most feared word is the one for a limb that *lies*: sound to the eye, sound to the tap of a stick, but wrong in some way an old watcher feels in the rail under her hand when the gusts come over the saddle.

When the watchers condemn a limb, the whole town turns out for the lowering — ropes, relays, children cleared back, the cut made in stages over a full day, the great weight walked down through the canopy on a dozen lines. It has the structure of a funeral and the mood of a festival and ends, invariably, in an enormous meal cooked over the condemned wood itself, which is the only time the grove has dry firewood and everyone knows it.

## Fire, and the Owing of Fire

Fire here is never made; it is kept. In the saturation of the saddle, starting flame fresh is a half-day's misery of coaxing, so it simply isn't done — embers are banked, carried, fed, and above all *lent*. Every household's hearth descends, coal by borrowed coal, from every other household's hearth, going back past anyone's memory, and the lending is the town's quiet ledger. To carry fire to a neighbor whose hearth has drowned in a storm is to be owed something unnamed and unforgettable. The grove's worst insult, delivered rarely and never forgiven quickly, translates roughly as *one who would let you light your own*.

## The Children

The children belong to the canopy more than to any household. They are posted against the monkeys from the age of six or so — a duty they treat as glorious warfare — and the long-running engagement between the children and the grey troop has the quality of a rivalry between neighborhoods rather than between species. The monkeys are known individually and named, all insultingly. The children are known to the monkeys individually too, and certain children are clearly held in higher regard than certain adults, a fact the adults pretend not to mind.

Grove children learn the town as routes, not places — a three-dimensional braid of ways, including a whole set of paths through the upper branches that adults officially do not know about and unofficially used themselves forty years ago. They learn the dry lines and wet lines of every street before they learn to count. *Down*, in their speech, does not mean toward the ground; it means toward the valleys, the cloud, the elsewhere. The ground is just another floor.

## The Speech

You cannot live inside a roar for generations without it shaping the mouth. Grove speech is pitched to carry — a forward, ringing delivery that sounds like shouting to strangers and like ordinary conversation to everyone born here. For real distance there is the whistle-register: a vocabulary of perhaps sixty calls that cut through weaverbird argument and falling water alike, enough to send *come*, *wait*, *fruit ready*, *limb asking on the east side*, *market people sighted in the cloud* from one end of the canopy to the other. And underneath both runs the hand-talk, born in the gesture-haggling of the market and grown into a full silent companion language, used across the din, used by the deaf old, used by lovers, used by children behind the backs of adults who taught it to them and somehow forgot it could be turned to that purpose.

They sleep through everything and wake to nothing — that is, to absences. A mother sleeps through the bats, the frogs, a gale; she is upright in the dark the instant the frog-chorus near her door stops, because the frogs only stop for a reason. The hawk-silence word does duty here too. The grove's alarm system is its noise, and what the people listen for, all their lives, without knowing they are doing it, is the hole in the sound.

## The Other Residents

The weavers, the pigeons, the barbets, the frogs in their moss-pelt, the monkeys with their criminal records — all of these the grove people would name, if pressed, as neighbors of various standing. The bats are different. The bats live in the one tree no household will ever live in: the old hollow at the center of the saddle, the eldest of the thirty, its heart long rotted into a chamber that breathes guano-smell and a deep animal warmth. The grove's relationship to this tree is not worship — nothing here glows or looms, and they would laugh at the suggestion — but it is something like the relationship to a very old relative. It is exempt. No roots are trained from it, no fruit is contested with the birds in its crown, and its evening eruption of wings is the town's clock, dinner bell, and proof, nightly renewed, that the oldest thing in the grove is still full of life on the inside.

## The Dead

The dead are given to the roots. A grave in the grove is dug among the root-feet of the family's tree, shallow in the thin saddle soil, and within a few decades the roots have closed over and through it, and the person is, in the town's flat, unmystical phrasing, *in the tree now*. This is meant literally and turns out to be true. The trees of the saddle have been fed on the grove people for as long as either has existed, which is why the visitor's first observation — that nobody distinguishes anymore between the trees and the town — is not a figure of speech here but a plain description of the material situation. The handrail worn smooth under your palm was trained by a woman who is now, in part, the rail.

Ask a grove person who built this place and they will give you names, and only slowly will you understand that some of the names are people and some are trees and that the distinction has not seemed important to anyone here for a very long time. Then they will turn back to whatever they were mending — something is always being mended — and the weavers will go on shrieking, and the water will go on falling off the south face into the white, and the town will go on at full volume, as it was before you came up out of the cloud, as it will after you go back down into it.
