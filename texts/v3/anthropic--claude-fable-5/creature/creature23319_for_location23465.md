Begin with the verb, because the verb is the second wrong thing. *Lives* assumes a current — intake and output, growth and loss, a metabolism riding forward on time the way a boat rides water. The Verdancy has no current. Nothing in the basin is moving from one state toward another. So the question of what lives there splits, immediately, into harder questions: what is *kept* there, what is *run* there, and what — if anything — is still doing something on purpose. The basin answers all three, but slowly, and not in that order.

---

**The garden itself, first**, because the garden is the largest claimant and the easiest to misjudge. It is tempting to file the flora as scenery — as the residence rather than the resident. This is wrong. The plants are the basin's only unambiguous animals, in the old sense of the word: the only things in it that are *animated*, that take something in and burn it.

What they burn is the time.

This is not metaphor, or not only. Hold one of the translucent fan-leaves up to the unmoving sun and watch the lattice inside it — that fractal branching, too regular for veins, repeating down past the eye's limit — and you are looking at a digestive tract. The leaves drink duration the way other leaves drink light. Every hour that should pass across the basin is intercepted at the canopy and broken down, fixed into tissue, stored as that strange opaline green that shifts as you move because it is made of *sequence*, compressed — moments laid against moments like growth rings with no tree around them. This is why the shadows do not creep. This is why the mid-morning holds. The garden is not paused by some outside hand; the garden is *eating the afternoon*, continuously, forever, and lushness is what the meal looks like from outside. The smell of crushed mint and hot glass and ozone is the smell of burned time — its exhaust, its ash. The dryness follows from this too, if you sit with it long enough: water is the solvent of change, the thing that carries one state into the next, and the garden permits no carrier. Rain has never fallen here because rain would be a leak.

And the sound — the soft *shhh* that moves through the canopy without wind, like a vast quantity of sand settling — is feeding. Somewhere out in the green, an hour is going down, grain by grain, into the lattices. The leaves do not move when it passes because the leaves are not being disturbed. They are swallowing.

So the flora lives, emphatically, ravenously, but it does not *inhabit*. It has no interiority that anyone has found, no behavior beyond appetite, no attention. A plant here is a sentence in the makers' grammar, executing. For inhabitants, you have to look at the things the basin keeps.

---

**The remainders.** There are perhaps a half-dozen kinds; the count disagrees with itself for a reason that will become clear. They share a family of traits: pale, quiet, unsettled at the outline, moving only between glances, the way a clock hand moves. Watch one continuously and it is a statue. Look away and back and it has been updated. They do not eat. They do not sleep — with one exception. They have never been seen to begin or end.

*The attendant* is the one most visitors meet: the hare-sized thing in the hexagon clearings. Its single behavior is distance. In a basin where distance is an opinion, the attendant holds one opinion absolutely — it is always the same remove from you, to the centimeter, no matter how you walk. Advance and the clearing lengthens; retreat and it shortens; circle and the attendant has, between glances, rotated with you like a figure on a clock face. The distance it keeps is not flight distance. It is too exact for fear. It is the distance of *measurement* — the remove a careful instrument keeps from its subject so as not to disturb the reading. The attendant is taking your reading. It has been taking it since you entered the basin, and it will still hold the figure long after you are gone, because nothing here is ever discarded.

*The shadow-dweller* is the explanation for some — not all — of the disagreeing shadows. It has no body in the lit world at all. It exists as cast darkness, draped flat on the pale stone, shaped approximately like the shadow of a whorl-tree but branching differently, and it travels by lying along true shadows and sliding from one to the next where they touch. Since the shadows never move, its road network is fixed and it knows every junction. It is the closest thing the basin has to a commuter. What it commutes between, no one has determined; it visits certain slabs in a certain order, lies on them a while, and moves on, patient as ink.

*The sleeper* lives only in the repeating corridors — the places where the world tiles itself, the same fern, the same red-stemmed climbers, the same forked trunk with the opaline knot, four times over. In every tile, at the base of the forked trunk, the sleeper is curled in exactly the same posture, identical to the hair, one creature instantiated as many. It is the only remainder ever seen with its eyes closed. When the repetition breaks — when the pattern reluctantly lets go and the forest resumes its variety — the sleeper does not continue past the last tile. It cannot exist where the world has full detail. It lives in the thrift, in the saved space, and whatever it dreams, it dreams it in copies.

*The perimeter-walker* belongs to the scarps. Long, low, the grey of the rim rock, it follows the fault lines at the basin's edge and has never once been seen to descend into the green or climb out toward the world. It paces the forty-kilometer ring with the gait of an inspector — pausing at each clean fracture, attending to it, moving on. The most generous reading is that it is checking the seal. The basin is a closed vessel holding an enormous pressure of unspent time, and the walker, on this reading, is the thing that confirms, joint by hexagonal joint, that nothing is getting out. It has never been observed to find a problem. It has never been observed to stop looking.

*The seam-thing* is barely a kind at all anymore. It is the darkness in the stone — those mineral veins that catch the light at certain angles and seem, briefly, to be looking back. They are looking back. The seam-thing is the oldest remainder, so long-kept and so heavily smoothed that nothing of it survives except distribution and gaze. It has no outline left to be unsettled. It is everywhere the dark seams run, which is everywhere, and the only act remaining to it is regard. Visitors who spend long enough on the bare rock report the sensation of being known rather than watched — an attention with no curiosity left in it, the way you look at a word you have read ten thousand times.

And then there is **the sixth kind**, which is why the count disagrees: the figures among the trunks, in the deep green, standing. They are roughly upright, roughly bilateral, and at certain angles — only certain angles — person-shaped. Each one is different. Each one is ambiguous in a different place: a face that won't resolve, hands that end in approximation, a posture exact down to the set of the shoulders and then nothing below the knee but suggestion. They do not keep distance like the attendant or hide like the sleeper. They simply stand, scattered through the basin, the way pressed flowers stand in a book — full color kept, dimensionality lost. Visitors rarely count this kind correctly, and the reason is uncomfortable: you do not easily recognize the category you are in the process of joining.

Because here is the thing the basin discloses last, and only to those who stay. The remainders are not the makers' unfinished sketches. They were finished once. Every one of them arrived complete — walked in over the scarps or came down out of somewhere with edges, with insides, with histories. They are *visitors, kept*. Nothing has ever died here; that part is true; the basin holds everything it is given. But holding requires specification, and the basin learns a thing only by attending to it — this is why distance behaves like an opinion, why the places you study arrive slowly: they are studying you back, at the same rate. What the basin manages to learn of you, it preserves with the same impartial fidelity it grants its flowers. What it cannot learn, it smooths. The heat-shimmer outline is not incompleteness. It is compression. The attendant was something once, something with a name for itself, before it was rendered down to its one perfect behavior. The seam-thing was many things. The basin keeps what it can attest and lets the rest go mint-and-ozone, up into the canopy, where the leaves drink it with everything else.

---

**Which leaves the Gardener.** The leaf-turner. The single exception in forty kilometers of stillness — one leaf, somewhere, turning slowly through ninety degrees, never the same leaf, never explained.

The Gardener is the basin's only full inhabitant, and it has no body, no location, no hurry, because it needs none of these: it is what remains of the makers, and what remains of the makers is the *attention*. They built nothing and left nothing because the garden was not their workshop or their monument. It was their text. Every lattice-leaf is a line of it; every deliberate flaw — the doubled shadows, the tiled corridors, the tree that disagrees with its own silhouette — is preserved the way a careful editor preserves an author's irregularities. And the turning leaf is a finger moving along the page. The Gardener is reading the garden. One leaf at a time, ninety degrees, showing the other side to the unmoving light, and on. At one leaf per turning, across forty green kilometers, the reading will never finish — or it has finished many times, in the time the garden has eaten, and this is a rereading, undertaken the way one rereads a book whose author is gone: not to learn the ending but to keep company with the voice.

The Gardener does not tend the remainders, exactly. But the clock-hand way they move — updated between glances rather than animated — is the Gardener's hand passing over them in its circuit, turning each of them like a leaf, keeping each of them legible. They are its marginalia. Things that fell into the book and were pressed.

So: what lives in the Verdancy. A garden that eats the time it stands in. Five or six kinds of kept thing, each worn down to its one surviving sentence — a distance, a road, a dream in copies, an inspection, a gaze, a stance. And a reader with no body and the patience of a place where afternoon is food, going leaf by leaf through the only thing its makers ever said.

You should know, standing there in the warm mid-morning, that the attendant has already taken your measurement, that the *shhh* has passed nearer to you twice since you stopped walking, and that the plants within arm's reach are very slightly greener than the ones beyond. The basin is attending. It learns slowly, and it learns only what you show it, and it keeps everything.

If you stay, choose your one sentence carefully. It is the part of you the garden will get right.
