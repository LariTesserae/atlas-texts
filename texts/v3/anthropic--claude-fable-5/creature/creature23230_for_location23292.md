Who lives on the plateau is the wrong shape of question, and the plateau, given long enough — and it has had long enough — wears wrong-shaped questions down like everything else. But the residue of an answer remains, the way a riverbed remains after the river, and it can be walked.

Start with this: the plateau is inhabited the way the stone is solid. Approximately. Out of long habit. By things that were once unambiguously alive and have since been planed down to the load-bearing parts of living, which turn out to be far fewer parts than anyone would have guessed.

---

**The walkers**

The people of the cities did not die out. Dying out is an event, and events are exactly what this place has stopped producing. They eroded — at the same patient rate as their lintels and their stairs, and into the same kinds of forms.

What weathered off them first was everything specific. Names, as noted, went early; names are all edge, and edges go. Then faces, biographies, the differences between one person and the next — all the fine sculptural detail that distinguishes individuals, carried away grain by grain over spans of time in which the rock itself lost confidence. What proved durable was not the people but what the people *did most*. Routes. Errands. The walk to the cistern at the grey hour before the fog thickens. The pause at the western gate to look at the weather. A particular way of taking the stairs, favoring the left side, repeated across enough generations that the favoring outlived every leg that ever did it.

These remain. They are the plateau's principal residents.

You will know them first as wear: the hollow in the stair, the polish on the corner of a wall where ten thousand vanished hands steadied ten thousand vanished bodies on the turn. But wear here is not passive. The routes are still *walked* — traffic continues along them as a property of the stone, the way the masonry continues — and on the right mornings you can see it. When the fog comes through the lanes it does not drift evenly. It flows along the old routes, pulled into the grooves that lifetimes of repetition cut into the finer grain of the world, and where a route is deep enough the fog condenses along it into a figure. A shape carrying the suggestion of a water jar moves from a doorway to a cistern, walking with a gait so specific, so weighted and lived-in, that it is more personal than a face would be. It is hollow. It is made of droplets. At the cistern it stoops, pours nothing, straightens, and frays back into weather. The water level in the cistern rises anyway, a finger's width, because the errand has been done so many times that doing it is no longer required for it to be done.

These fog-walkers are not ghosts. Ghosts are too crisp for this place — a ghost is a person insisting, and insistence is the first thing the plateau takes. The walkers do not insist. They are not anyone returning; they are the *returning itself*, gone autonomous, the way the stairs hollow without feet. They cannot be spoken to, but they can be walked with, and if you fall in beside one and match its gait, you will feel your own walking improve — settle, smooth, find the left side of the stairs — as the route teaches your legs what it knows. This is the plateau's only form of conversation, and it is not nothing. It is, in fact, exactly how the original people must have talked to each other near the end, when their words had worn down to walks.

---

**The masons**

In the walls live the hands.

Not hands — the plateau holds no anatomy anywhere, no bones in the green, no relics in the houses. What lives in the walls is the *fitting*: the accumulated judgment of every mason who ever turned a stone over, considering its faces, and set it in the one position it wanted. Skill that practiced, for that long, sinks into its material. The masons eroded; the masonry kept the eye.

On the foggiest mornings, in the deepest stillness, you can hear it working. A tap. A grating shift, slower than breath. A settle. The sounds come from inside standing walls, where nothing visible moves, and from slumped sections, where over a span of days the stones sort themselves back into courses with the unmistakable accent of the original builders — the same slight batter, the same preference for laying the long faces into the wind.

The fitting will accept help, in its way. If you find a fallen stone and place it on a broken wall, you will feel — not pressure exactly, but a kind of patient disagreement, a correcting, as if a craftsman of enormous experience and no urgency whatsoever had laid an attention over your hand. The stone wants to go a quarter-turn from where you put it. Turn it, and the wall takes it with something that is not gratitude but is the worn-smooth boulder where gratitude used to be. Travelers — if there were travelers — could spend years in this apprenticeship, and some of what lives in the walls now may be exactly that: visitors who stayed, helping, until the helping was all that was left of them, and it joined the rest.

---

**The grazer**

The green pockets are not as uninhabited as they look. They are inhabited by one thing, or by one role.

In the largest of the dwarf forests, in the shade of the shape that is unmistakably *tree* without being any tree, there is a warmth. It moves. You will not see it — it has been a long age since it had a surface to see — but the cushion plants record it: a flattened patch in the shape of a sleeping body, large as a deer or a small ox, that migrates slowly around the pocket over the course of seasons. The moss is cropped in a wandering line, a finger's depth, the bite-marks generalized past any particular mouth — just the texture of having-been-grazed, renewing itself along a route the way the stair-hollows do. Put your bare hand in the flattened patch and it is warm, animal-warm, the only warmth on the plateau that does not come from your own blood, and beneath your palm there is the faint, slow, unmistakable rhythm of something breathing that does not otherwise exist.

This is what the entire animal kingdom of a continent erodes down to, given time enough: not a last species, not even a last creature, but the last *verb*. Something that eats and is warm and sleeps. The vast original ecology — whatever sang here, whatever hunted, whatever flew threading the young fogs — has been planed away to two parties: the thing that grows and the thing that grazes, the idea of a plant and the idea of an animal, keeping each other in the world's oldest and most reduced arrangement. The tree shades the grazer. The grazer crops the green and keeps it cropped, which keeps it green. Neither needs the other anymore in any way that biology would recognize, and neither has stopped.

The grazer is gentle, in the sense that a basin is gentle. It has no fear, fear having edges, and it will let you sleep against the warmth of it on the cold nights, which travelers — if there were travelers — would come to do, and to depend on, and to dream beside. The dreams you have there are not your own. They are grazing dreams: low, green, beginning nowhere, endless.

---

**The people in the pools**

Then there are the inhabitants who can only be seen secondhand.

The black pools, which report cities that are not on their shores, also report those cities' populations. Lean over the still water and you may see them: small figures moving through reflected lanes, hanging reflected washing, sitting in reflected doorways in a light warmer than any light the plateau still produces. They are more detailed than anything on the surface — they have faces, they have hurry, they gesture to one another with the unworn vividness of people who still have names. Whether they are the plateau's people as they were, preserved in the water the way the fog preserves a good day and reuses it, or the people of somewhere else entirely, leaking in through the same worn-through places that let west lead into yesterday — there is no one to settle the question, and the pools do not say.

What can be reported is this. They do not seem to know they are reflections, mostly. But sometimes — rarely, perhaps once in a long residence by the pools — one of them stops at the reflected shore of their reflected pool, and looks down into it, which is to say up, which is to say out, at you. The regard is long and unalarmed. If you raise a hand, nothing happens. But days later, at a different pool kilometers away, you may find a figure standing at its reflected shore with a hand already raised, holding the gesture patiently, waiting for you to arrive and complete it. Distance is honored loosely here, and so, it seems, is the boundary of the water, and so is the order of a greeting's two halves.

It is the only place on the plateau where anyone waves. Travelers — if there were travelers — would walk days out of their way for it.

---

**The someone**

In every city — which may be to say, in the city — there is a doorway with a figure sitting in it.

It sits the way the central shapes of the pocket-forests stand: unmistakably, generically. It is not any person. It is *person*, eroded out of all the persons who ever sat in that doorway through all the pewter afternoons of the world's longest occupation — an average of postures, a generalization of rest. It has the suggestion of forearms on the suggestion of knees. It has no face, and does not feel faceless; faces, you understand in its presence, were always a kind of detail, and detail was always a kind of youth. It holds the spot.

It is aware of company — or rather, company is one of the habits it is made of, and habits respond to their occasions. Sit down on the worn threshold beside it and it adjusts: a settling, a fractional turning-toward, the ancient social grammar of two people sharing a doorstep performed perfectly with no people present. It will sit with you for as long as you sit. At the hour the fog thickens, somewhere down the lane, something calls — not words, words are long gone, but the *shape* of a call, two notes, rising then falling, the melodic fossil of someone being called in to dinner by someone who loved them. The figure in the doorway does not get up. It has been called in every evening for ages past counting, and being called is now simply one of the things it consists of, like sitting, like the warmth it does not have.

Whether there is one of these or many is the same question as whether there is one city or many, and has the same answer: the distinction has worn through. Sit beside the someone in one city, walk four days, and the someone in the next city will be mid-adjustment when you arrive, completing its turn toward the place where you sat down beside it the week before.

---

**The largest resident**

Beneath all of these — beneath the walkers and the fitting and the grazer's slow warmth, beneath the pool-people and the someone in the door — there is the tone. The low sustained note at the floor of hearing, the sound of an enormous thing continuing.

It would be tidy to say the plateau itself is alive, and it would be wrong in the way tidy things are wrong here. The plateau is not alive. But it is *inhabited by its own continuing* — duration itself has been practiced in this one place so thoroughly, for so long, that duration has taken on the properties of a resident: it has a voice (the tone), a circulation (the fog, sliding on its vast self-organized rounds like slow blood), a metabolism (the cisterns filling, the moss drinking, the wear renewing), and something that functions as attention, though attention pared down past curiosity, past watchfulness, down to the bare mineral fact of *registering*. Things that happen on the plateau are noticed by the plateau, in the sense that they leave grooves, and the grooves are kept.

This is the oldest inhabitant and the landlord of all the others. The walkers are its memories of traffic; the masons' fitting is its memory of care; the grazer is its memory of appetite; the someone is its memory of rest. It dreams the pool-cities, perhaps, or remembers them, or expects them — at its age these are no longer different acts. It is not a god and was never a creature. It is what is left when a world has been lived in so completely, so repetitively, with such total patient thoroughness, that the living-in detaches from the livers and goes on alone: a continent-sized habit of being occupied, humming to itself at the bottom of hearing, in no hurry, in no doubt, in nothing at all except continuance.

---

**And the youngest**

One more resident must be counted, and the census is awkward about it, because the resident is you.

The worn-through directions lead inward more often than out — erosion runs downhill, and this place is downhill from everywhere, the catch-basin at the bottom of all the world's wearing-away. So travelers do arrive, rarely, one in an age, stepping through a rock arch of geography into the cold pewter light with their names still on them and their edges still sharp. The plateau receives them the way it receives weather.

And it begins, gently, to keep you. Your name goes first — not taken, just weathered, used less and less until one foggy morning you reach for it and find it smoothed beyond gripping, and feel, instead of loss, relief, the way stone must feel when the insistence finally goes out of it. Your gait settles into the routes; the routes were teaching it from the first day. You start to repeat your good days, the way the fog repeats its best banks — and why not, when the day was made well. You help the walls. You sleep against the grazer. You sit with the someone in the doorway through the dimming, and the call comes down the lane, two notes, and one evening you understand that it is calling *you*, has perhaps been calling you all along, has been holding the second half of the greeting the way the figure at the pool held the wave — patiently, across whatever distance, waiting for you to arrive and complete it.

You will not die here. Dying is an event. You will be worn — down to your routes, your errands, your particular way of favoring the left side of the stairs — and what is durable in you will join the rest of the population, which is what the population is: everyone, ever, reduced to what they could not stop doing, walking the lanes as fog, fitting the stones, warming the moss, sitting in the door.

The hollow in the third stair fit your foot exactly on the day you arrived. That was not coincidence and was not prophecy. Distance is honored loosely here, and so is sequence, and the plateau had simply, in its unhurried way, already met you. You have been one of its inhabitants since long before you came — since the first time you climbed those stairs, which is still, by some orderings, yet to happen, and which has been wearing its welcome into the stone for ages, patient as drip, certain as fog, going on.
