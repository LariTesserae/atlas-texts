**Those Who Live in the Saddle Town**

Begin with the lichen, because the lichen would insist on it, if lichen insisted on anything. By every honest census it is the majority population, the old families, the establishment. It arrived before the first wall and it colonized each new wall as a continuation of the mountain, which, as far as the lichen is concerned, each new wall was. It does not distinguish between the quarried and the native, between the made and the found, and this refusal to distinguish is not ignorance but a kind of judgment — the deepest compliment the mountain's oldest resident can pay to the builders. *You have made more mountain. Very well. We will live on it.*

The lichen lives at the town's true pace, which is to say the pace the town has been trying to teach everything else. A rosette of the rust-orange kind on the south wall of the third house is, by the slow arithmetic of its kind, somewhere between six and nine hundred years old. It has watched — in whatever way a thing without eyes watches, which is by simply being present for — the silvering of the doors, the smoothing of the stair-treads, the comings and goings of perhaps forty human generations. It considers none of this remarkable. It is the only resident for whom the town's stillness is not an achievement but simply the weather.

Below the lichen in age, and above almost everything in dignity, are the cushion plants. There are two of them, possibly three — one in the corner of the long terrace, one wedged at the base of the eastern retaining wall, and a third, disputed, in a crack so deep in shadow that it may be moss putting on airs. The terrace cushion is the elder. It was a green coin when the newest band of masonry was new; it is now the size of a curled sleeping cat, domed, dense enough to knock a knuckle against, and it has spent the centuries since doing exactly one thing, which is continuing. The people who live here do not step near it. This is not superstition, exactly. It is the courtesy one permanence extends to another.

The junipers are the town's middle generation — old by any tree's reckoning, young by the standards of this address. They lean southeast, all of them, in their uniform deference to the wind, and because of this they have the air of a small delegation perpetually bowing toward something just over the ridge. They are the only residents with anything like restlessness in them: they move every day, all day, in the wind, and yet in a hundred years they do not move at all. They have resolved the town's central paradox in their own bodies — constant motion, no change — and perhaps that is why the people seem to like them, planting nothing else, clearing nothing away, letting each juniper keep its thirty paces of emptiness like an inheritance.

And then the people. Count them carefully, because the count matters: there are four.

There is an old woman who lives in the house with the smoking chimney, and she is the one most often glimpsed — the figure crossing the upper terrace with water, unhurried, because hurry at this altitude is a tax the body pays for nothing. She rises before light, not from discipline but because she has risen before light for sixty years and the habit has become a feature of her, like the lean of the junipers. She keeps the goats — there are five goats, which makes the goats, technically, the largest warm-blooded population on the saddle, a fact she would acknowledge with a shrug. She moves the cistern lids. She knows which lane hums in a west wind and uses it as her barometer, and she is right more often than the clouds are.

There is a man, somewhat younger, who is the town's mason in the way that a heart is a body's pump — not by appointment but by function. When frost lifts a flagstone or a wall-joint opens the width of a straw, he repairs it with the same stone in the same way, and within a season the repair cannot be found. He learned this from a woman now gone, who learned it from a man now gone, in a chain of teaching that has no first link anyone remembers and that no one has ever bothered to write down, because the wall itself is the document. He works perhaps twenty days a year. The rest of the time he watches the walls the way a shepherd watches sheep that are behaving themselves: present, patient, unneeded, necessary.

There is a couple in the house at the western edge — the house that takes the cloud first each afternoon, so that their door is always the dampest and their hearth, in compensation, the most often lit. They came up from the lowlands long ago. This is known and never mentioned; the town does not hold origins against anyone, having been built, after all, by people who came from somewhere. They keep the small terrace of hard grain below the saddle's lip, they dry the goat-hides, and once a season one of them walks down the eastern trail and comes back two days later with salt, needles, and news, the last of which they distribute slowly, over weeks, the way the cisterns distribute the cloud.

Four people, then — and here is the thing the visitor's count of "three, perhaps five" was groping toward without knowing it: the number is not an accident and not a decline. The town supports four the way a body supports a temperature. When there were six, two left. When there were two, within a few years there were four again — a niece came up, a man arrived on the trail and stayed. No one summons the newcomers and no one expels the surplus. The saddle simply has a population the way it has a wind, and the people who live here are not the last of a dwindling line, whatever the lowlands assume. They are the current occupants of four permanent positions in an institution older than any kingdom below, positions whose duties are: carry water, keep the goats, mend the stone, light the fire, and be — in their small warm-blooded way — the part of the mountain that notices itself.

They speak little, even to each other, but it is the spareness of people who have already said most things, not the silence of estrangement. Their conversations are largely logistical and conducted across thirty paces of empty terrace at a calm half-shout: *the upper cistern is full. The gray goat favors her left foreleg. Cloud early today.* In the evenings, sometimes, two or three of them sit on the long terrace as the cloud pulls off westward and the stone dries pale, and they say nothing at all, and this is understood by all parties to be the good part of the day.

Around and among these four, the part-time residents keep their appointments. The chough is best understood as a commuter: it lives somewhere on the cliffs below the western face, but it holds, by long custom, an inspection route through the town, and it walks the route on its red feet with the unhurried thoroughness of a small official, checking the gutters, auditing the terrace, finding the same nothing it found yesterday and recording it with one harsh satisfied note before falling away off the saddle's edge into the air. The lizard, by contrast, is a homeowner. It possesses one south-facing wall — the deed is written in sunlight, valid only when the sun strikes the stone directly — and within those hours it is the most settled creature in town, flattened against the warmth, unmoving, doing in miniature and at speed what the cushion plant does at the pace of centuries.

The fox is the night-auditor. It does not live here and would not; the saddle is too open, too watched by wind. But it crosses the long terrace perhaps once a month on its way between one slope and another, and it always pauses at the same place — midway, where the paving is widest and the emptiness most complete — and listens. What it listens for, no one knows. The old woman, who has seen it twice in sixty years and heard its silence many times more, believes it pauses for the same reason a person pauses in the doorway of an old church: not from fear, but because some rooms ask it of you.

And there is one more resident, the strangest one, which is the water. It deserves the word *lives*, because it behaves here as nothing inert behaves. It arrives as cloud, walking up the western slope each afternoon like a habit. It beads on the north faces, finds the seams, threads the finger-width gutters, ticks and whispers down through the town's small stone vasculature, and gathers in the square-mouthed cisterns, where it lies dark and still and waits to be carried. It is the only resident that passes through every other resident's life — the lichen drinks it, the junipers ration it, the goats and the people and the cushion plant are mostly made of it — and it is the town's one perpetual motion, the moving part inside the stopped clock. The builders cut its channels and it has kept their appointment every cloudy afternoon for a thousand years, and in this it is the truest citizen of all: it changes constantly and is always the same.

That, finally, is what lives in the saddle town: not a remnant and not a community, exactly, but a *standing arrangement* — lichen, cushion, juniper, lizard, chough, fox, water, four people and five goats — each occupying its position the way the stones occupy theirs, fitted, load-bearing, replaceable in the individual and permanent in the role. The old woman will die, and the town grieves nothing, because someone will rise before light. The mason's hands will stop, and a younger pair of hands, taught by the walls themselves if no one else is left to teach them, will split stone with swollen wedges and set it so the repair cannot be found. The lichen will not notice the succession. The lichen has never noticed a succession yet.

A thousand years from now, on the eastern trail, a traveler will look up and see the saddle, the small gray town breathing cloud, a thread of smoke in the cold hours, a figure crossing an upper terrace with water, unhurried — and will wonder, as you wondered, who lives in such a place. The answer will be the same then as now: exactly who has always lived there. Which is to say, exactly enough.
