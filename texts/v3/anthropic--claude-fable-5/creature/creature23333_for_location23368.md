**The Builders of the Lowland Works**

Start with the hands, because they did. Everything else about them is provisional — pale, low, roughly the mass of a kneeling man, though they never stand the way a man stands — but the hands are definite. Broad as trowels, dense with bone, the palms ridged in fine whorled callus that never stops growing and is worn down only by the work itself. The fingers are short, blunt, enormously strong, and finer in their motion than anything that strength should permit: the same hand that drags a dressed block onto its rollers will, a moment later, smooth a clay joint to a finish you could not improve with a brush. They have no other fineness. The face — if the front of the head can be called that — is a smooth, slightly damp blank, nostrils low near the jaw, a mouth made for grinding, and nothing above. No eyes. No sockets where eyes were lost. Nothing was lost. Whatever ancestor came down into the basin came down before seeing, or instead of it, and the line has never once needed light.

This is the first thing to understand about them, and it reorders everything else: **the hammering is how they see.**

Every maul-strike, every slap of thrown clay, every block groaning onto its bed sends its shape out through stone and air, and the basin returns it — off the walls, down the passages, around the corbels — and they read the return. Not as echo, the way you would hear it, a smear of noise. As *form*. The whole sounding labyrinth lights up for them at every blow: this passage open, that chamber closed, that wall green and still curing (cured stone rings; wet mortar swallows). A builder working alone in a far quarter is not only shaping a wall. It is illuminating its district, stroke by stroke, for everyone in range. The work is the lamp. This is why the basin is never quiet, and why it can never be allowed to become quiet — a silent basin is, to them, a basin gone dark. They build perpetually for many reasons, but beneath all the others lies this one: *to stop working is to stop seeing.*

The great pauses, then — those total silences that fall across whole quarters at once and ring in your ears — are not rest. They are the opposite of blindness. When everything stops, one builder somewhere delivers a single enormous blow, a maul brought down on a sounding-slab set into the floor for exactly this purpose, and the entire basin returns at once: every wall, every void, every cistern's black water, every soft wet irregularity (you, if you are standing there — they will know precisely where, and how tall, and how frightened, from the way you swallow sound). It is a census of form. The whole work, held in the mind in one stroke. Then the rhythm resumes, and if it resumes *changed* — and it always does — that is because the reading showed something: a quarter grown redundant, a passage wanting closure, a wall that has finished meaning what it meant. The decision is not made anywhere. It is made everywhere, the way a flock turns.

They have no speech apart from this. Or rather: the rhythm is the talk, and the walls are the writing. The textures you used to navigate — ribbed for level, pocked for stairs, polished slip for water — are not wayfinding marks left kindly for visitors. They are the plainest words in a written language made entirely for the hand, the equivalent of *here* and *down* and *drink*. Deeper in, where you did not go, the texturing thickens until whole walls are dense with it, course after course of worked surface meant to be read at walking pace with the fingertips of one trailing hand — genealogies, water-tallies, accounts of quarters built and unbuilt, and long passages that seem to record nothing at all and may be what they have instead of song. When a wall is unbuilt, what it said is unsaid. They do not seem to mourn this. The labyrinth migrates, and its literature migrates with it, recopied in new stone by hands that read the old wall down even as they took it apart. The basin forgets and remembers itself continuously, by hand. It is possible the whole place is best understood as a single memory, very slowly thinking.

They eat what the dark provides, which is more than you would credit. The damp that sweats from every corbel feeds a mineral film — grey-gold, faintly bitter, alive in some chemical way that owes nothing to the sun — and the films feed the soft blind grazers of the cisterns, and the grazers feed the builders. The cisterns are not only waterworks; they are pastures. The plastered walls are deliberately textured *coarse* below the waterline to give the film purchase, and the grazers are husbanded with the same patience as everything else: culled by touch, never exhausted, their clean spiral tracks the closest thing the basin has to a field under cultivation. The builders also rasp the films directly from the stone with their grinding mouths — you will find walls in the deep quarters licked smooth as the slip-bands, polished not by tools but by ten thousand years of feeding — and they render the grazers' fat, and the fat is what banks the embers. That is the answer to the bowls of dull orange light: the embers are not lamps. Nothing here is a lamp. They are seed-fire, kept alive in ash to be carried to the clay kilns when tools want firing, and the small spheres of light they throw are an accident, wasted on everyone in the basin but you.

The rope is hair. Their own, shed and gathered, and the hair of the dead, which is the gentlest part of the gentlest custom they have. The dead are unbuilt. There is no other word for it and they would want no other word: taken apart with the same unhurried care given to a retired wall, over days, by builders who pause their other work to do it. The long bones become hafts and scrapers and the fine awls that cut the finger-channels. The hair is twisted into cord. The fat goes to the embers, the rest to the mortar or the cistern films, and what cannot be used is set into the heart of a new wall, laid like a block among blocks, and the wall raised over it sounds — they say, if the texturing on certain old courses is a saying — very slightly warmer to the striking hand than a wall with no one in it. They are born, by symmetry, in the warmth: small, translucent, palms already callused in the womb, and carried in hods like wet clay to the curing chambers, where new mortar gives off its heat for weeks and the young lie against the green walls and grow, learning the difference between cured and uncured stone before they learn anything else. A builder's first knowledge is the temperature of work.

They are not incurious about you, whatever it looks like. You register to them with great clarity — a soft column that drinks sound, warm, leaking moisture, moving wrongly, stopping in places where nothing stops. They withdraw ahead of you not from fear and not from courtesy but because that is what they do with every irregularity the census-stroke reveals: they accommodate it, and route the work around it, and wait to see whether it is permanent. Stand long enough in one place and you will find, returning days later, that the place remembers you — a passage curving slightly where no curve was needed, a niche left in a new wall the height and breadth of your standing, finished inside with the same care as every other surface, holding nothing. They build around absences. You were an absence. They have made you a part of the record, in the only language they have, and it is not clear whether the niche is a hostility, a courtesy, or simply an accuracy.

One thing in the basin makes them stop.

When the segmented ribbon pours itself along a drainage groove — the luminous one, the only native light, its joints glowing the way a thing glows that has never been told light means anything — work falls silent along its path. Not the great deliberative silence; a smaller one, local, moving as it moves. They cannot perceive its light. It makes no sound at all; it is, to their sense, a perfect void in the shape of motion, a piece of traveling silence sliding downhill through their world, and they put down their tools and press their palms flat against the floor until it has passed. Whether this is reverence, or dread, or a measurement — whether the ribbon is their omen, their vermin, or their level — is written, perhaps, on some dense wall in a quarter that has since been carried elsewhere. The hands that could read it to you will not stay in the light long enough to ask.

What they are *for* — what the works are converging on — is the wrong question, and the basin spent its whole descent teaching you why. They are not building toward a form. The building is the seeing, the speaking, the remembering, the burying, the raising of the young; it is circulation. The basin is not their city and not their hive. It is their body, the one body, and the layered hammering you stood inside — near, far, above, below, falling into rhythm and drifting out of it — is nothing more or less than that body's pulse, kept up by hand, in the dark, forever, by the only creatures who ever understood that a world does not need to be finished. It needs to be *warm*. Press a thumb into the mortar. It yields, and holds the print, and somewhere in the black a maul comes down, and for one stroke the whole made country sees itself entire — including, now, the small soft mark you left.
