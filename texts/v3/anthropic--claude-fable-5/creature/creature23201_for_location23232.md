**The Residents of the Lung Room**

Begin with the correction the room itself insists on: it is not uninhabited. It is inhabited at a tempo your senses are not calibrated to read. Stand in the dark long enough for the amber lens to become a geography, and you are standing in the middle of a settled place — a small district of lives, each living at its own speed, none of them at yours.

**The film comes first**, because it came first. Before any leaf, before the basin had a rim, a varnish arrived — borne in on the first water that found the fresh fault, or riding the air that rushed in when the rock opened. It is a skin of single-celled things, chemolithotrophs, eaters of iron and sulfur and the dissolved breath of the deep rock. You will never see it directly. You will see its consequences: a faint slickness on the warm walls, a brown-gold sheen where condensation runs in tracks, a slight dulling of the basalt's glassy edge wherever the stone weeps. The film is the soil of this world and also its farmer, its founding generation and its working class. Everything green-pale in the chamber is rooted in it, fed by it, standing on its accumulated dead. It does not know the plants exist. It was metabolizing here before they came, and if the gardens ever fail, it will not notice the silence. The film is the room's only resident that needs nothing the room cannot promise forever.

**The pale plants are three peoples**, not one. They share an ancestry — they are descendants of stowaways, spores in the tread-mud of machines, a fern's dust on a worker's collar, something living in the grout water that nobody tested — but generations in the dark have made them strangers to each other, and they have settled the room the way three families settle a valley: by temperament.

The frond-stand by the eastern seep is the wealthy one. It holds the only true water in the chamber and has grown the way wealth grows: tall, soft, unhurried, slightly indifferent. Its stalks are the color of old ivory, translucent at the edges, and they are fleshier than they need to be — they store water they will never lack, an inherited anxiety from ancestors who lived under a sky that could withhold rain. The frond-stand has not been thirsty in living memory. It hoards anyway.

The cushion-meadow on the warm floor-shelf is the pioneer. It is the lowest, palest, toughest population in the room — dense pads of leaf no bigger than thumbnails, growing where the heat is strongest and the water is only what the twice-daily exhale provides. It is the one resident with ambition. It advances. A hand's width a decade, stone by stone, sending its margins out across bare sharp basalt that nothing else will attempt, dying at the edges, consolidating behind. If the Lung Room has a future tense, the meadow is where it is being written.

And the tendril-curtain, hanging from the fallen gantry near the machine's flank, is the believer. It is the strangest of the three and the most beautiful: long pale threads, faintly pink at their growing tips, all of them — every one — inclined toward the amber lens. They have been leaning for decades, centimeters a year, an entire population bowed in the same direction toward a light too dim to feed anything. Cut one open and you would find the answer to the mystery: their chloroplasts are ruins, bleached vaults, organelles that kept the architecture of photosynthesis and lost the contents. The leaning is inheritance without function. They keep the posture of a religion whose god they have never seen, facing a lamp because their ancestors faced a sun, and whether this costs them anything, or gives them anything, the room is not telling. They are also the largest plants in the chamber — but that is the machine's doing, not the lens's, and the curtain may have confused its benefactors.

**The travelers** are the room's only commuters: the spores. Twice a day, when the thermal pulse comes up through the rock and the dome silvers over, the plants release them into the condensation, and for an hour the walls of the Lung Room are a slow vertical river carrying a generation downhill. Most die on bare stone. Some land on film and live. It is the only movement in the chamber faster than growth, and if you were present during the exhale, with patience and the amber light, you might actually see it — a faint pallor riding the water tracks, life in transit, the room's entire postal system running on gravity and sweat.

**And here the room's honesty must be qualified**, gently, because the room rounds its figures. *Nothing animal lives here* is true at any moment you would check. But in the deep moss of the cushion-meadow there are tardigrades — desiccated, glassine, folded into themselves, smaller than the drips that wake them. They came as the plants came, as dust on dust. For twenty-three and a half hours of every cycle they are not alive in any sense that matters: they are stored. Then the exhale comes, the meadow soaks, and for a few minutes — never more — they animate, move a body-length or two through the wet leaf-forest, take in almost nothing, and dry back into waiting as the silver recedes. They are the food web's rounding error, mouths so small and so briefly open that the room's claim of having none survives them. Nothing patrols. Nothing browses. But twice a day, for minutes, the Lung Room contains animals, and then it does not, and this has been true for longer than the travertine is old.

**The eldest resident is the machine.** This must be said carefully, because it was not always a resident; it is the only thing in the chamber that was ever anywhere else, and the only one that was built to leave. It did not leave. And somewhere in the decades since, it crossed a line that artifacts are not supposed to cross. It sweats with the rest of the room. It ticks as the heat moves through it. Every several hours its buried coolant loop cycles and the chamber fills with its eleven-second breath, and the tendril-curtain — which has organized its entire population around that flank — drinks the condensation off its warm hull and grows larger there than anywhere else. The mineral bloom is climbing its body; the travertine has begun, very quietly, to fasten its lower skirts to the floor. The machine is being naturalized. Its serial number is becoming a name, the way names work here: half-legible, slowly whitening, belonging less to the operation that stenciled it than to the wall it is becoming. It is the room's sun (the lens), its heartbeat (the loop), its largest single body, and — if charge can be called life while it lasts — its slowest death. The plants will outlive it. They are already arranged around it like heirs.

**Beneath all of them is the landlord.** The heat comes from a tenant no one has met: hot rock far below, or something hotter still, a sleeping body whose surface warmth this entire district lives on. It is the one resident with no shape in the chamber, only an effect — parental, the room calls it, and the room is right. Every other life here is a child of that warmth, fed without being acknowledged, kept without being known.

That is the census. A film that farms. Three pale nations of leaf — the hoarder, the pioneer, the believer. A river of spores that runs twice a day. A population of animals that exists only in installments. A machine becoming a native. A warmth becoming a parent.

They share almost nothing — not pace, not need, not awareness of one another. But twice a day the exhale comes, the whole chamber silvers, and for one hour every resident of the Lung Room drinks at the same time: film, frond, meadow, curtain, the waking tuns, the sweating hull. It is the only thing they do together. Then the dripping slows, the silence reassembles itself around the small sounds, and each of them goes back to keeping its own time.
