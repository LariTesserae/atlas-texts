# What Lives in the Lung

The census takes a minute. Three species, a fourth thing that may or may not qualify, a fifth resident that is only suspected, and a sixth that no one counts because it goes home with you. Understanding the census has taken much longer, and one fact has to come first, because it organizes everything else:

Nothing in the Lung eats anything else that lives in the Lung.

This is not how places work. Places are food webs; the arrows point at each other. Here the arrows all point the same direction — down, inward, toward the engine. The algae eats the light, the ferns drink the seams, the eels harvest what the vents send up. Each resident is plugged separately and directly into whatever drives the breathing, like organs sharing blood, none of them taking anything from the others, none of them apparently *able* to. Visitors who notice this tend to go quiet for a while. It is the difference between an ecosystem and an anatomy.

With that said: the residents, oldest first.

---

**The belt** is the golden algae, and the golden algae is one organism. This was confirmed the hard way — a survey team cut a clean ten-centimeter gap in the ring to sample it, and over the following day the gap closed from both sides at identical rates, the two fronts meeting at the exact center of the wound. Not approximately the center. The center. The ring has never, in any recorded observation, been broken for longer than a day, and it may never have been broken at all before people arrived with knives.

It lives only at the high-water line — which means it lives only by being drowned and exposed, drowned and exposed, forty times an hour, forever. Samples kept submerged die. Samples kept dry die. It does not want water or air; it wants the *alternation*, the breathing itself, and removed from the rhythm it simply stops, the way a wave taken out of the ocean is just water.

It is also, almost certainly, the architect. The travertine's banding — those pale gold strata running through the bone-white stone — is the belt's own dead, laminated downward, generation under generation, mineralized into the fabric of the walls. The chamber's whole interior is the belt's accumulated work: ten thousand years of dying in place, each layer becoming the floor of the next. Which means the famous light, the shadowless everywhere-glow, passes through the belt's entire ancestry on its way to your eye. The lampshade is made of its dead. The chamber is bright the way an overcast noon is bright because that brightness has been filtered through a hundred centuries of one continuous golden life.

It is useful, too, in a way that feels almost like courtesy. When the engine's interval drifts long — when the chamber is in one of its moods — the belt pales, hours in advance. The oldest instrument in the room is alive, and unlike the instruments you carry down, it never disagrees with itself.

---

**The ferns** live where the rain can't reach them, which in a chamber where it is always raining means they live in exile: the dry lee of the larger stone lobes, hand-high, silver-green, every frond permanently beaded with condensation they did not ask for. They grow nowhere else in the world. They may grow nowhere else in the world because they refuse to.

Two things about them. First, the roots. A colony was excavated once — carefully, regretted anyway — and its roots, fine as hair, were traced through the stone for distances that made no botanical sense: meters of travertine, threading the lobes, until every single root terminated at one of the seams. The straight lines. The kilometer-grade lines behind the walls. Every fern in the chamber, without exception, is rooted into the artificial part of the place. What they draw from the seams — heat, current, water, something with no name yet — is not known. But they are the only living things in the Lung that touch the built thing directly, the only residents on intimate terms with whatever the chamber actually is, and they are not telling.

Second, the facing. The ferns grow toward the walls. Not upward, toward the glare of the ceiling — *inward*, fronds pressed toward the glowing stone, backs turned to the entire roaring chamber, like a congregation facing the wrong way, or the right way, depending on what you think is being worshipped. A researcher once rotated a loose stone lobe a half-turn, ferns and all. Within a week every frond had reoriented to face the wall again. They were not interested in the experiment. They have one posture and it is attention.

Their spores ride the slow rain — released at the top of a surge, drifting down through that fractionally-wrong gravity, almost all of them dying in the water, the rare one finding a shadow. Whether they evolved here or arrived as someone's hitchhiker — a fiber in old packing material, a passenger on the boot of whoever bolted the bracket to the wall — is unresolved. Either they have been here forever, or they came with the builders and stayed when the builders didn't. Both possibilities suit them.

---

**The tongues** — the field name for the eels, given by someone watching them leap open-mouthed into the columns, and never improved upon — are the chamber's only animals, and the longer you watch them the less the word *animal* wants to hold.

They are blind, but the front third of the body is packed with pressure organs, and they hear the engine the way you heard the chamber through the rock on your way down: before the air gets involved. High-speed footage settled an old argument — they leave the water a fraction of a second *before* the chord sounds. They are not reacting to the surge. They are cued upstream of it, plugged into whatever the chord is also plugged into, which means the leap and the brass note are siblings, two expressions of the same deep event, one of them made of sound and one of them made of eel.

You will see two or three. There are more. The visible few have the quality of a shift, a watch — individuals rotate (a distinctive scarred one will be present for weeks, then absent for months, then back), and the rotation implies a population below, in the channels, where presumably they breed, though no one has ever seen a young one learn. Small ones simply appear, already perfect. The timing is never practiced in front of witnesses. They never miss, and they have never been seen *beginning* to never miss.

And they are never seen to die. What happens instead is this: a very old one — thickened, mineral-crusted, its leaps grown short — stops leaping altogether. It settles into one particular cluster of rimstone pools near the eastern wall and goes still, and the stone does what the stone does to everything that stays, only faster than it should: within weeks the travertine has grown over it completely. The eastern aprons carry dozens of eel-shaped ridges, smooth and sealed, some so old they are barely a swelling under the glaze. No one has opened one. There is no appetite for opening one. It is not clear whether the word is *graveyard* or *cocoonery* or *archive*, and the chamber's whole manner suggests the question is none of your business.

A tag attached to one tongue in an early study was never recovered from the animal. It was found two years later sealed inside a rimstone shelf, four meters from the water.

---

**The snow** is the fourth thing, the maybe. At each surge the columns carry up a sparse drift of pale flecks — soft, smaller than a fingernail, faintly warmer than the water around them. This is what the tongues harvest, mouths open in the rising froth. Whether the flecks are alive is unanswerable in the usual frustrating way of the place: captured in a vial, they are mineral dust within a minute, inert, ordinary, dead the way a word is dead when you repeat it too many times. In the chamber's own light, in the chamber's own water, they appear to flex. Or the water moves them. Two observers side by side will report differently, like the instruments.

Food, seed, message — no one knows what the engine is sending up, or whether the tongues are eating it or reading it, or whether, for a mouth that lives inside a forty-second chord, those are different acts.

---

**The fifth resident** is the one the steps are for.

Seven steps, cut into the apron, descending below even the low-water line, going somewhere always submerged — and the centers of the treads are dished. Worn. The wear lies *under* the travertine glaze, which means either the use is ancient and finished, or the glaze regrows between uses, which means the uses are rare. The empty bracket on the eastern wall, bolted with that pointed precision, held something once. The walkway does not end in a break; it ends in a finished edge, like something used to dock there.

And three times in the records, the same event: every visible tongue dives at once and does not leap again for many cycles. The chord drops — observers say "a tone," the recordings say less than that, the recordings are not trusted. And the column of water that rises over the seventh step holds, at full height, a half-second too long.

Then everything resumes.

No one has seen anything. The most that can be said is that something below occasionally climbs partway up, briefly, in the privacy the tongues arrange for it by their absence — and that one reading of the bracket, unprovable and unshakable once heard, is that it comes up to see whether the thing the bracket held has been returned. It hasn't. It goes back down. The engine continues. If the Lung has a tenant rather than organs, it is this one, and its lease is older than the stone.

---

**And the sixth resident** is you, eventually, in a manner of speaking.

No human has ever lived in the Lung. It was tried — a three-week station, abandoned not because of the warmth or the wet but because of what the rhythm does to a body that stays. Your breathing locks to the cycle within days, four breaths to a surge, exact, involuntary; sleepers report dreams that loop at forty seconds; and one long-stay observer, back on the surface, found herself short of breath in quiet rooms for a month, her lungs waiting for a cue that was hundreds of meters down and not coming.

Because that is the last thing that lives there, and the most successful: the copy. The chamber installs itself in everyone who descends — the warmth, the chord, the bright rain falling slightly wrong — and that copy goes up the passage with you and lives in you indefinitely, surfacing at odd moments in dry silent rooms, at intervals of, roughly, forty seconds. The homesickness everyone reports is not a side effect. It is the Lung's farthest-flung population: hundreds of people on the surface of the world, each carrying a small warm loud room of breathing water, each of them pausing now and then, mid-sentence, mid-step, to listen for the surge.

The chamber breathes water and sheds light and grows stone over whatever stays.

What stays longest is the wanting to go back.
