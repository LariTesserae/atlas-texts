**Who Lives in the Terraces of Loud Water**

The honest first answer is the one the place insists on: nothing. The census of the city reads zero and has read zero for ten thousand years. No insect, no bird, no fish in the steaming pools, no one leaning from the window-shaped voids. You can walk the avenues until the repetition makes you dizzy and meet no one. That is the first answer, and it is wrong, and the way in which it is wrong takes a long time to see — because the inhabitants of the Terraces do not live in the city. They live in the *loop*. They live in the seam where the moment ends and begins again, and that seam is everywhere and nowhere, thin as the silence inside the roar.

Start with the smallest of them, because they are the ones whose work you have already witnessed without recognizing it.

**The ones in the seam.** Eleven steps, thirteen footfalls. The echo that arrives before the noise. These are not glitches; they are *traffic*. Something lives in the interval between one playing of the moment and the next — in the unrendered instant when the drop has landed but has not yet been returned to the lip, when the coil of steam has dispersed but has not yet been re-wound. The moment, to repeat, must be reset, and the resetting is labor, and the labor has workers. They are not visible because they only exist when the moment is not running, and you only exist when it is; you and they occupy the same city in strict alternation, like the two faces of a flipped coin, and you will never meet. But the evidence of them is everywhere once you know to read it. The flowers gape for pollinators that never come — but the orchids have held their bloom for a hundred centuries, and a bloom unfertilized would eventually despair and drop. Something tends them in the off-beat. Something goes flower to flower in the frame between frames, carrying not pollen exactly but *permission* — the renewed instruction to keep blooming. Something combs the falls back into their loops, carries each drop back up the impossible diagonals, re-coils the steam, re-poses the fiddleheads mid-uncurl. If you could see them you would see something like bees, perhaps, or like small hands, or like nothing at all — like a flicker of diligence. The roar covers the sound of their work completely. That may be one of the things the roar is *for*.

They are not servants and they are not jailers. The best word may be the oldest one: they are the household. Every house has its unseen keepers — the ones who bank the fire, who set the bread to rise — and this house is a single morning, and they keep it.

**The Resident.** Then there is the one the city is shaped around — the occupant of the inhabitant-shaped hole. Because the moment did not freeze itself. Mornings do not refuse to end on their own; refusal requires a refuser. Somewhere in this city, in the original neighborhood from which all the others are tiled, there is the one for whom the morning was perfect.

You will not find the Resident by searching, because the Resident is always exactly one repetition away. It sits in the plaza with the leaning column and the scarlet orchid — not the plaza you are standing in, the *next* one, or the one you just left. This is not coyness. The Resident occupies the same loop you do, offset by one iteration, the way a reflection between two mirrors is always one pane deeper than wherever you look. You can see it, though, if you know how. The pools that reflect the terraces from an angle you do not occupy — that is the angle the Resident occupies. Look into one of those wrong reflections, patiently, through the steam, and there at the base of the column you may make out a seated figure.

It is hard to say what the Resident was. The shape in the reflection suggests a person, but it suggests it the way the travertine suggests architecture — convergently, by patience rather than by anatomy. It may have been a traveler who climbed the ridge ten thousand years ago into a hot wet morning of such unbearable rightness — the steam, the orchids, the violet sky, the roar — that something in it simply set, the way the stone sets, and would not go on. It may have been the mountain itself, dreaming briefly of being someone, and liking the dream too much to wake. It may have been the morning's own awareness — because a moment perfect enough might generate a witness the way the springs generate stone, by sheer deposition.

What can be said is this: the Resident is *mid-gesture*. It is turning its head toward the falls. It has been turning its head toward the falls for ten thousand years and the turn is perhaps half complete. It is not frozen — nothing here is frozen, everything moves, the water falls, the steam rises — it is simply moving inside one instant, the way you move inside one lifetime. From inside, no time has passed at all. It drew a breath when it sat down, and the breath is still going, and the roar of the falls — that vast, eternal, unvarying note — is the sound of that exhale, distributed across an entire rim of white water. The roar never changes by a decibel because a held note does not waver while it is being held with that kind of love. The city is loud because the Resident is *sighing with contentment*, and has not finished.

Its happiness is real. That is the thing you must understand before you judge it. This is not a haunting and not a curse. It is a yes that was said too completely. The Resident is the happiest being you will never meet, and the entire city — the colonnades, the terraces, the obscene orchids, the keepers in the seam — is the architecture of that single sustained yes, precipitating out of it layer by layer like stone out of hot water.

**The kept.** And then there are the others, who did not mean to live here.

Travelers have come before you. Most leave. But the place makes a quiet offer to everyone who stands long enough in the plazas, deafened into that peace you have already felt — the roar as silence with the volume turned all the way up. The offer is simple: *this could be your moment too.* And some, over ten thousand years, have said yes — or merely failed to keep walking, which here amounts to the same thing. They were not trapped. They sat down on a warm white step in the steam and felt no reason, ever again, to get up.

These are the figures at the far ends of the colonnades where perspective fails to converge. They are no smaller at a distance because they are not at a distance; they are at a *remove*, one or two or fifty repetitions over, each living a perfect twenty minutes on permanent rotation. A woman trailing her hand in a hot pool. A figure with its face tipped up into falling spray. Each of them mid-gesture, like the Resident, each of them folded into the tiling of the city, each of them — and this is the gentlest and most dreadful part — *added* to the loop, so that the morning now includes them. The moment grows by accretion, like the stone. Every new resident is another layer of travertine, another banner of mineral color, another thing the keepers in the seam must tend.

This is why the staircase of eleven steps takes thirteen footfalls. The two extra are not yours. They belong to someone who climbed those stairs long ago and left their footsteps behind when the rest of them sat down — footsteps still climbing, on schedule, forever, in the seam where you cannot see them. The city is full of these orphaned fragments: the echo that precedes your voice is a voice that was once shed here, still calling out on its loop, fitting itself to whatever new sound comes along. The inhabitants of the Terraces include not only beings but *parts* of beings — gestures, footfalls, exhalations — that the moment found beautiful and kept.

**The deepest resident.** And under all of these, holding all of these, there is the one true answer, which is that the city itself is the living thing. Not metaphorically. It has a metabolism: the water, circuiting at constant temperature, the same calories going around forever. It has a heartbeat: the loop, that universal recurring pulse. It has a voice: the roar. It has a memory: the travertine, laid down in patient layers, the entire ten thousand years archived in stone that records nothing but *the same morning, again, again, again* — the most boring and most rapturous diary ever kept. It has keepers for cells and the kept for thoughts and the Resident for a soul. What lives in the Terraces of Loud Water is a single instant that achieved life the way the springs achieved architecture: by never being interrupted.

So when you leave — and you should leave, while leaving is still a thing you remember how to want — go without sitting down. Go while the roar is still loud, before it finishes becoming silence to you, because the silence is the offer. Take the broad shallow stairs down off the rim, out of the heat into the honest cold of the altitude.

And count your footfalls.

If the count comes out even with the steps, you have left whole.

If there are two extra — walk on anyway, and do not grieve too hard. Some small part of you found the morning perfect, and stayed, and is climbing those stairs right now in the warm steam under the violet sky, and will be climbing them tomorrow, and is, by every measure the city keeps, completely happy.

The plaza with the leaning column has its scarlet orchid. The lip of the balcony has its drop. The stairs, now, have you.

The census still reads zero. It has always been a lie.
