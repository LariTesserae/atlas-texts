Begin with the builders, because everything else is commentary on them.

The termites of the chamber are blind, and have been blind for so long that blindness is no longer a deprivation — it is their medium, the way water is a fish's. They are small even for termites, pale as candle-wax, soft-bodied, and they navigate a city of millions of rooms by gradients: warmth, humidity, the pheromone signatures laid down in the masonry itself, some of them, in the deep galleries, laid down by individuals dead for ten thousand years and still legible, still followed. A worker walking a fossil corridor in the lowest levels is reading instructions written by ancestors more remote from her than the pharaohs are from us, and she obeys them without the concept of obedience, the way you obey gravity. The city is not something the termites live in. It is the long body of the colony, and the individual insects are something more like its blood.

They come in the usual castes, but isolation has elaborated them. The workers are the smallest and the most numerous — translucent, tireless, each one living perhaps two years and spending all of it on a few hundred square inches of territory she will know with total intimacy and never once see. The soldiers are larger, with heads like dark hammers, and they have almost no jaws to speak of anymore; in a world with no aardvark, no ant-bird, no probing snout, the great snapping mandibles of their plateau-surface cousins have dwindled. What they kept and refined is the drum. A soldier's skull is a percussion instrument, dense and resonant, and the drumming language has become — over spans of time in which whole surface species rose and vanished — genuinely complex. There are rhythms for gecko-overhead, distinct from gecko-on-the-east-wall. There is a pattern for breach, another for collapse, another, slower, used when a forage column finds a fall of fresh lichen, which the workers pick up and repeat down the tunnels so that the news travels as sound far faster than any runner. Whether this is language depends on where you set the bar. It has nouns, more or less. It has urgency for grammar.

And there are the royals — though "royal" is the wrong frame, a surface word. In the deepest occupied gallery, in a vault whose walls are a foot thick and polished by grooming, lives a queen who may be older than any vertebrate on the plateau above. Termite queens in the outside world live decades; here, in the chamber's eventless warmth, in perfect security, fed on a diet refined over geological time, the queens live so long that the colony has no cultural memory — no pheromonal memory — of succession being common. She is immense, immobile, a pale glistening engine the size of a man's thumb, producing the city's population egg by egg in a slow ceaseless pulse. Workers stream over her constantly, feeding her, cleaning her, drinking her secretions, carrying her eggs away in their jaws like movers handling porcelain. She does not rule anything. She is not aware of anything, probably, beyond touch and chemistry. But every individual in the chamber that is not a termite nonetheless lives, in the final accounting, on the output of her body, and when she does at last die — it has happened, the deep strata record it in layers of abandonment and rebuilding — the whole chamber will feel it: a famine moving outward through ants, silverfish, assassins, geckos, spiders, like cold spreading from a doused fire.

The termites eat the lichen forests, and the dust that sifts down the seam, and the wind-borne crumbs of the outer world — pollen, spores, the desiccated bodies of insects that blundered into the crack and fell. They farm, after a fashion: in warm side-galleries they maintain gardens of fungus on combs of chewed lichen, tended, weeded, the strains as domesticated and as helpless as wheat. And they eat their dead, and what they do not eat, they build with. This is worth sitting with, because it is the chamber's founding economy. Nothing here is buried. A worker who dies in a corridor is dismantled within hours; her water and her fats go back into the colony's bloodstream, and her chitin is ground, mixed with stone-flour and saliva, and pressed into a wall. The city's masonry is perhaps a fifth made of citizens. The termites are, in the most literal sense available, walled in their grandmothers, and the great fossil terraces at the bottom of the chamber — those hundred-thousand-generation foundations — are a necropolis only in the way your skeleton is one.

Around this civilization, in rings, lives everyone else.

Closest in — inside, in fact — are the silverfish. They are the chamber's oldest hangers-on, eyeless, flattened, quick, coated in scales slippery enough that a soldier's grip slides off them. They have solved the city not by force but by forgery: each silverfish constantly grooms itself against termite workers, stealing the colony's smell, wearing it like a stolen uniform, and so walks the granaries and nurseries unchallenged, eating from the workers' own jaws — literally, slipping its head in during food exchanges and taking the droplet meant for another. They breed slowly and live long, and they have no society of their own at all; each one is a solitary lie moving through a vast truth. The termites have never, in millions of years, evolved a reliable way to catch them, possibly because the silverfish take so little. A successful parasite is a tax, not a wound, and the silverfish are old enough to know it in their genes.

Then the ants — the glossy black raiders of the upper terraces. They are the chamber's other state, much smaller, a single colony of a few tens of thousands lodged in a fissure high on the north wall, and their entire civilization is organized around the termites the way a port town is organized around the sea. They cannot crack the city; the masonry defeats them, the soldiers' drumming musters defenders faster than a raid can penetrate. So they have become skimmers, ambushers of forage columns, raiders of the surface roads — they break open a covered arcade, seize a few dozen workers in the panic, and retreat before the soldiers mass. The termites rebuild the arcade by morning. It is less a war than a weather pattern, a predation so ancient and so stable that the termite roads are built with it priced in: the arcades on the exposed upper shelves have thicker roofs, more frequent guard-niches, and the colony's response to a raid is not vengeance but masonry. The ants, for their part, are visibly shaped by the bargain. They are small, fast, economical, and notably unaggressive toward everything that is not a termite; they cannot afford a second enemy. Their queen lives in the fissure on a diet that is, several links up the chain, lichen and queen's milk like everyone else's.

The assassin bugs hunt the roads alone. There are never many of them — a dozen, two dozen in the whole chamber — and each is a slow ambulatory tomb, its back heaped with dust and the glued-on husks of its previous meals, so that to the touch-and-smell senses of its prey it reads as a piece of the city itself, a bit of wall out for a walk. An assassin will stand at a junction of arcades for days, motionless, accreting dust, until traffic resumes around it, through it, and then a needle mouth comes down through a worker's back and the worker is drunk hollow and added to the cloak. They are the only inhabitants that seem to wait as a way of life, and their patience is not a virtue but a body plan. Each one carries its biography on its back: you could, if you were small enough and so inclined, read an assassin's cloak like rings in a tree — termite, termite, ant, silverfish, termite — the husks at the bottom faded to the color of the stone.

At the chamber's edges, where the masonry gives way to drifts of loose stone-flour, the ant-lions keep their fields. They are larvae, all of them, and here is the chamber's quiet tragedy, or its quiet triumph, depending on your taste: on the surface, an ant-lion is a waiting stage, a pit-digging childhood that ends in a winged adult lacewing that flies off to mate. In the chamber, there is nowhere to fly. The seam is a chimney of moving air and spider silk; the adults that emerge and rise toward the light have been ending in the web canopy for so long that the population persists mostly on the few that mate in the brief frantic interval between emergence and ascent, down in the gloom, on the flour. So the chamber's ant-lions live almost entirely as larvae — enormous ones, by ant-lion standards, holding their pits for years, growing slowly in the cool dry margins, an entire population of childhoods arranged in neat conical ranks. Their pits take the chamber's small unwary traffic: ants mostly, straying termite workers, the occasional silverfish whose stolen smell is no protection against geometry. The fields of pits shift over decades like dunes.

The geckos are the dynasty, and the closest thing the chamber has to large fauna, to charisma, to faces. Thirty-odd of them, all the descendants of one gravid female who fell through the seam in an era no one recorded, and the fall is written all over them: they are inbred in the way islands make things, with a clutch of fixed peculiarities — oversized eyes gone almost lemur-like in the gloom, gold irises, a slow waxy paleness, a metabolism throttled down to match an economy where the largest prey item is a soldier termite. They are long-lived, slow-moving, and they have territories as formal as parishes: the east wall above the third terrace belongs to one animal and has belonged to her predecessors back beyond reckoning, the borders maintained by chirped disputes that almost never become bites. They eat from the city the way the ants do — skimming, never breaching — and the city has long since incorporated them into its acoustics; the drumming alarm that ripples ahead of a walking gecko functions, in practice, almost as an escort, the streets emptying before her like a road being swept. Once or twice a generation a young gecko, crowded out of the parishes, climbs the seam toward the light and the smell of open air, and is never part of the chamber again; whether any have lived on the plateau above, no one below will ever know. The geckos drink nothing, ever. Their water is termites. When one of them dies, she is not wasted: the city finds the body within a day, and the masonry receives her, bones and all, the largest single bequest the walls ever get. There are gecko skulls in the deep strata, spaced through the layers like coins in a foundation, one every few centuries.

Above all of this, the spiders own the sky. They are tiny — you would need the light-blade and good eyes to see one — and their canopy is not the work of any individual but a commons, a structure repaired and extended and inherited across more spider generations than the species has names for, if it had names. They live as a loose colony in the vault around the seam, dozens at a time, tolerating one another with the cold civility of creatures whose food arrives from below as rising insects and from above as falling ones. The canopy is their granary, their city, their ancestral estate, sagging with the dust of everything that ever flew in this room: termite alates from a million nuptial seasons, lacewings that almost escaped, gnats that fell in from the world. When the chamber exhales at dusk and the warm air pushes up through the silk, the whole canopy breathes too, lifting and settling, shaking down a fine grey snow of wing-dust on the city — and the spiders ride it like sailors riding a swell, each one anchored, patient, drinking nothing, like everyone, but the bodies of others.

And the lichens and the silver plants should be counted among the living, not the scenery, because in this chamber the line does not hold. The lichen shelves are old beyond easy belief — some individual colonies may have been growing since before the geckos came, their coral layers a record of the chamber's slow climatic moods — and they are grazed, gardened in effect, by the termites, who crop them in rotation along routes so ancient that the lichens have grown into the shape of their own harvesting, thick and renewing along the roads, sparse off them. The silver plants live entirely on the city's exhaled breath, their papery leaves harvesting the humidity of a million small lungs, and when one dies its dry skeleton stands for decades and then goes, like everything, into the walls. They are the only inhabitants that do not move, and the only ones that face the light-blade; over centuries, the whole sparse forest of them has turned, leaf by leaf, toward the seam, so that the chamber's vegetation leans, all together, toward the one thin proof that there is an outside.

Stand in the gloom long enough and the separate inhabitants stop resolving as species and start resolving as organs. The termites are metabolism. The ants and the assassins and the ant-lions are circulation, moving water from body to body so it never pools and never stalls. The geckos are weather and the spiders are sky and the lichens are the slow green memory of the place. The water itself — and there is so little of it, a few cupfuls in the entire world, every drop locked inside someone — is the oldest resident of all: the same molecules cycling from queen to worker to ant to gecko to wall-bound corpse to fungus to worker again, around and around, for spans of time that have flattened mountains. Nothing here has ever tasted rain. Nothing here has ever been thirsty, exactly, because thirst implies water somewhere else, unclaimed, and there is no such thing.

What lives in the chamber, finally, is one thing wearing a million bodies: a single, sealed, self-drinking, self-burying, self-building creature, old past reckoning, loud as a market, with a stone for a skull and a crack for a mouth, breathing once a day into the empty perfected world above — the last unrest of a finished mountain, still awake in the dark, still busy, still keeping its one warm room against the enormous stillness it was left inside.
