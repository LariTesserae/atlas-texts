**Who Lives in the Drowned Fells**

The honest first answer is the one the place itself gives: nothing. No fish, no grazer, no burrower, not a shell in the silt. You can travel the drowned country end to end and never meet an eye.

But that answer is wrong in the way a locked house with smoke in the chimney is empty. The fells are not uninhabited. They are inhabited in a way that the word *fauna* was never built to hold, and once you see it you cannot stop seeing it: the place is a household. It has a flock, a shepherd, a walker on its rounds, and its dead. They are all home. They are all, in their way, at work.

---

**The meadow, which is one thing.**

Begin with the obvious resident, the one you have been wading through.

The pelt on the slopes, the ribbon-forest in the valleys, the lone glowing stalks at the fords and saddles — these are not a vegetation, plural, the way heather and pine were plural. They are one organism, or one organism is what they have become: a single body the size of the range, wearing the fells the way moss wears a stone. The slopes that exhale together do so because they are one breath. The lanterns that brighten together brighten because the same attention passes through all of them, the way a thought crosses a face.

This is why there are no animals. Not because they died, and not because they were forbidden — because every office an animal would hold is already filled. The meadow grazes: it combs minerals from the moving water, drinks the cold leakage of the tarns, and feeds — you become sure of this if you stay through enough of the great low tones — on *sound*, on the grinding of the ice and the knock of the scree, the way grass feeds on light. The meadow migrates, once a season, sending its young up toward the ice in clouds. The meadow even startles: shout underwater near a slope of it and the whole hillside flattens for a moment, then slowly stands back up, the way a deer decides you are not worth running from. A population of one. Anything else would be redundant. Anything else would be *crowding*.

Its temperament is the thing that takes longest to learn. It is not hungry, the way kelp forests are hungry, racing the light. It is not territorial; it grows around the cairns and shelters with what can only be called manners, climbing the walls but never once, in centuries, closing a doorway. It is patient and it is *deferential* — to the stonework, to the old paths, to the deep places. The fells' largest living thing behaves, everywhere and always, like a tenant. Which raises the question of the landlord.

**The heavy ones.**

The meadow leans. Everything that grows here leans, downslope and down-valley, toward the dense cold lenses lying in the valley bottoms — the drowned tarns within the drowned sea, the layers that refuse to mix.

You were told the plants lean toward what feeds them. Stand long enough above one of those shivering boundary-planes, where the water changes its mind, and you begin to suspect that what is down there is not a nutrient. The lenses hold their shapes too well. They keep their borders the way a body keeps its skin. Each tarn-lens lies in its valley like something very large lying very still, and the temperature does not merely drop when you cross into one — it drops the way a room goes quiet when you enter and someone is already there.

Call them the heavy ones. Whether they are creatures, or the souls of the old tarns grown dense in the dark, or simply what cold becomes when it has had centuries to gather and nowhere to go — there is no way left to ask. What can be said is what they do. They lie in the low places like dark suns, and the forest of the fells turns toward them as forests once turned toward the sky. And once or twice a day, on no schedule, one of them speaks — or breathes, or turns over — and the great tone rolls through the valleys, felt in the chest, and every lantern-stalk on every slope brightens at once and then dims.

Watch that exchange a few times and the old shape of the country reasserts itself, inverted. This was shepherd land. It is shepherd land still. The flock now is rooted to the hillsides, dark and woolly, breathing in unison; the shepherd lies below instead of standing above; and the call still goes out across the fells, and the flock still answers it — *here, here, all of us, here* — a roll call in light instead of bleating. Nothing has stopped. The herding has simply changed what it herds.

**The walker.**

Then there is the third inhabitant, the one you will never see, and the one whose evidence is everywhere.

Someone keeps the paths.

The cairns along the ridgelines have been re-stacked — not once, anciently, but *recently*, in the way that careful work always looks recent. The capstones balance at angles the current should have undone in a season. The silt that films every surface in the fells lies thinner along the old routes, as if the routes are used. The doorways of the shelters stay clear. The terraces hold their rows. Somewhere in the drowned country, maintenance is being done, and it is never done while you watch; you only ever arrive to find it finished, the way you find frost finished in the morning.

Wait by a cairn for a year and nothing will come. There is no body. And slowly you arrive at the only remaining explanation, which is that what lives on the ridgelines is not a walker but *the walk* — the rounds themselves, the practice of tending, a discipline so old and so worn into the place that it outlived the hands that kept it, the way heat outlives the fire in a stone. The herders walked these paths daily for a hundred generations, checking cairns, clearing doors, minding the high ground. The habit soaked in. Now the current does it, or the meadow does it one stone-width a decade, or the place does it through whatever the place has instead of hands — and the lantern-stalks stand at every station of the route like lamps set out for a watchman who is the route.

It is the most domestic of the fells' inhabitants and the most unnerving, because its existence means the drowned country is not preserved. It is *kept*.

**The nine, and what stands among them.**

And in the high corrie, the household's quiet room.

Nothing grows inside the circle of nine stones, and at first you take that as a prohibition — sacred ground, fenced off. Stay there through a tone, though, and you notice the deeper thing: the tone does not enter either. The great chord rolls up the corrie, parts around the perimeter, and passes on, and inside the ring the water never shivers. It is the one place in the fells where the country's voice falls silent, the one place colder and stiller than the tarns themselves — a column of dense, motionless water standing upright inside the stones, where every dense water elsewhere in the fells lies down.

The lenses in the valleys lie like sleepers. The cold in the circle *stands*. Person-height, like the stones. Like the nine stones.

Here is where speculation has to be allowed its full length: the old people of the fells shaped these uprights with stone mauls, one peck at a time, and when the water came and did not stop coming, they will have done what high-country people always do, which is go up. The last ground in the fells to drown was this corrie. Whatever they were by the end — herders, pilgrims, both — they were here, among their nine stones, when the surface closed over them. And the fells, which keep everything, which let nothing decay, kept them too: not as bones (there are no bones anywhere, remember — *no bones in all the drowned country*), but as what they had become. A standing cold. A stillness with a perimeter.

This is why the meadow stops at the edge as if at a wall — not forbidden, but *polite*. This is why every lantern on the surrounding slopes faces inward, the whole congregation of lights turned toward the corrie the way faces turn toward elders. The flock answers the shepherd, but it *faces* the dead. The drowned fells are not a wilderness with ruins in it. They are a family that lost its people and refused, on every level available to water and stone and weed, to lose them.

**The season, which is the household speaking.**

And once in each slow year it all declares itself.

The tone quickens — the heavy ones calling, and calling again. The meadows answer with their inverted snow, the pale spores streaming upward through forty fathoms of dark toward the sealed grey sky, and for a few days the whole range glimmers, every path, pass, terrace, and doorway traced in cold light, the old map of the high country drawn complete across the dark.

You could read it as breeding, and biologically perhaps it is — most of the spores die against the ice; a few, maybe, freeze into it and wait there, seeds in the lid of the world, against the day the water leaves. But watch the shape of the light, and a simpler reading offers itself. Every window of the house lit at once. Every lamp on every path. The flock gathered and shining, the shepherd sounding below, the rounds all freshly kept, the nine attended in their cold room — and all of it angled upward, toward the ice, toward the rumor of the sun, like a settlement seen from a ridge at night.

That is who lives in the drowned fells: a household keeping itself ready. Not surviving — *expecting*. The doorways are kept clear because doorways are for arriving through. The cairns are kept stacked because paths are for someone to follow. The whole drowned, lush, patient country is one long act of waiting performed by everything the water could not dissolve — the flock, the deep, the walk, the dead — tending the high paths under forty fathoms of dark, against the day the ice opens, and someone comes down, and finds the lamps still lit and the house, after everything, in order.
