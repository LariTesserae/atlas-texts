The plateau looks empty because you are looking at the wrong scale, in the wrong direction, at the wrong volume. Everything that lives here has solved the same problem — the roar, the pour, the air that will not stop — and every solution is some form of getting out of the way. Nothing here stands up into the world. Life on the plateau happens *under*, *between*, *during*, and *inside*. Once you learn the prepositions, the place is crowded.

---

Start beneath the pelt, because most of the plateau's citizens are there and none of them know about the wind.

Under the two-inch canopy of moss-cord and cushion plant there is a complete climate that has nothing in common with the one above it. The air down there is still — genuinely still, candle-flame still — and warm with trapped sun and rot, and humid from the forty daily fog-stampedes filtering down as drip. It is a green-dim country of stems, roofed and pillared, and it has its full complement of nations: wingless flies that walk their whole lives; beetles whose wing-cases fused shut so many generations ago that the seam is now ornamental; spiders that have never once built a web in open air and string their silk instead between stems, horizontally, like fences; mites and springtails in numbers that would, if you could weigh them all, embarrass everything else alive on the plateau combined. Nothing under the pelt flies, and nothing under the pelt surfaces. The top of the moss is their sky, and their sky is a hard boundary: anything that breaks it is taken. The wind skims the pelt like a gull working water, and what pokes up is gone east before its legs stop moving. So the boundary is obeyed with an evolutionary thoroughness — eyes reduced, bodies flattened, instincts all lateral. There are creatures down there a centimeter beneath the loudest air on the planet that have never in their lineage's history experienced wind.

They do experience the roar — not as sound but as the permanent trembling of every stem, a substrate hum as constant as gravity. And they have done the resourceful thing: they signal *against* it. The under-pelt is full of drummers and scrapers and stem-pluckers working in the narrow frequencies the roar leaves clean, the way creatures elsewhere evolved colors their world's light made visible. Press your ear to the moss in a sheltered lee, cup your hands around it, and beneath the engine of the sky you can sometimes catch it: a tiny, dry, patient ticking, conversations in the calm country, the plateau talking under its skin.

Something hunts down there, too. The people call it — the gesture translates badly — the *swimmer*. It is a mammal smaller than your hand, blind, plush, boneless-seeming, and it moves through the moss the way a mole moves through soil or an eel through weed: never surfacing, never tunneling exactly, just parting the cords ahead and letting them close behind. It eats the drummers. It is the apex predator of a habitat two inches tall and the size of a small country, and almost no living traveler has seen one whole, only the moving seam in the pelt, like a finger drawn under fabric.

---

The gray grazer — the long-bodied animal that freezes flat when the roar resumes — is the plateau's largest honest resident, and its whole existence is an arrangement with sound.

It can close its ears. Not flatten them — *close* them, a muscular seal, complete. It spends most of its life voluntarily deaf, because the alternative is to spend most of its life inside an engine, and the grazers that couldn't shut the world out did not leave descendants. It opens its ears only in the deep lees, in the brief eddy-lulls behind the great domes, and in those moments it is the most alert thing you will ever fail to see — head low, ears wide, taking its ration of the world's information before sealing up again. Between lulls it lives by its feet. Grazers talk through the ground: a slow stamping, felt rather than heard, carried through the old finished stone for surprising distances. A mother and her calf grazing two hollows apart are in continuous conversation through the bedrock. Travelers who sleep on bare stone sometimes wake with the sense that someone was knocking, far away, politely, all night. Someone was.

The grazer's stillness is not hiding so much as a philosophy. When the roar resumes it flattens, slows its heart, and becomes for all purposes a gray swell of the gray land, and it can hold that for hours. It is everywhere. Census attempts conducted from the high domes with good optics have concluded, sheepishly, that an average day's walk passes within fifty meters of perhaps thirty grazers, of which the walker sees none.

---

The birds overhead are mostly freight, passengers locked in the airstream crossing the visible world in a minute, and they no more live here than a ship's passengers live in the sea. But one bird stays, and it stays by an elegant surrender: on the plateau, it does not fly. It enters the great stream when it wants to leave and exits when it returns, and in between it *walks* — a quick, low, deliberate walker, drab as wet stone, threading the lee-paths like everyone else. It nests in the cairns. The old dressed-stone towers, pecked and locked against the air by human hands, are full of wind-shadow chinks, and the walker-bird stuffs them with moss-cord and dwarf-tree fiber and raises its broods inside the navigation system. Every cairn-line on the plateau is also an archipelago of nests, and has been for so long that nobody can say whether the birds adopted the cairns or the cairn-builders, watching where the birds sheltered, learned where cairns should stand. Its call is a single thin whistle pitched in a notch the roar leaves open, audible only within a few meters, in a lee, in a lull — a sound made exclusively for whoever is already close enough to matter.

---

And the people are still here.

Not many — a few hundred, perhaps, across the whole table of the world, gathered in shelter-clusters dug into the great eastern lees, and they are the same people whose ancestors wore hollows in the grinding stones. They never left; they only got harder to notice, which on this plateau is the universal direction of mastery.

Outdoors, they do not speak, and the reason is not custom but physics; the custom grew over the physics like the pelt over the stone. Their outdoor language is the whole body — a vocabulary of posture and arm-line and timing built to be read at distance through moving fog, legible at exactly the spacing of the cairns, which is no coincidence. Two of them can converse from adjacent cairns across a kilometer of roaring white, each one a brief dark vertical making slow shapes, patient as semaphore, while the clouds stampede between them. Up close, the language gets fast and fine and runs on the hands, and it is a full language, with poetry in it, and jokes, and a tense that means *during the next lull*.

The voice they keep indoors. This is the center of everything about them. Inside the corbelled shelters — in that violent transition where the roar simply stops and the silence falls on you with weight — the voice is what silence is *for*. They speak indoors quietly, because there is no need to do otherwise and every need not to waste the stillness, and they sing indoors, and the songs are built around the sound of blood in the ears, slow as that, intimate as that. Children are born inside, in the quiet, and so the first thing a child of the plateau ever hears is not the wind but a human voice — and they will tell you, in sign, that this ordering is the whole of their religion. Days later the child is carried out through the low doorway and introduced to the roar, and the ceremony has a name whose gesture means, roughly, *the second parent*.

Each of them carries two names: an outdoor name, which is a gesture, used daily, worn smooth by use like everything here; and an indoor name, a spoken word, used perhaps a dozen times in a life — at birth, at marriage, at a few moments of unrepeatable weight, and once at death, when it is carried outside by the closest kin and said a single time, at conversational volume, directly into the pour. The wind takes it. That is the funeral. Where it takes it is the rest of the religion.

Because some of them listen. It is a vocation, like any craft lineage — the way some families keep the cairns, re-dressing any stone the centuries loosen, and some families garden the knee-high forests, tending woodlands they wade through like reeds, pruning trees their twenty-times-great-grandparents planted, burning only the deadfall, a fist of eight-hundred-ring wood being serious wealth. The listening families go to the saddles, where the roar carries its cargo of elsewhere. A listener wraps in a cord-bound cloak, lies down in the gap between domes, opens — there is a discipline to it, a softening of attention they train from childhood — and waits for the fragments: the running water that isn't there, the impossible birdsong, the half-syllable of voice. What they catch, they carry home and repeat indoors, in the quiet, exactly, and these fragments are kept, passed down, curated — the plateau's archive of the rest of the world, assembled one worn syllable at a time. Their belief, stated plainly, is this: the river of air has crossed half the world, and given enough time it will carry every sound ever made past this point, abraded and tumbled like gravel. Including, eventually, the indoor names of their own dead, gone east a generation ago, come round again. No listener claims it has happened. Every listener lies down as if it might happen today.

They walk on bare stone where they can — the paths follow the natural pale bands of it — because the pelt, they sign, is skin, and you do not walk on a sleeper's skin when the bone is offered. Their gesture for the pelt combines the gestures for *grandmother* and *coat*. About the whorls — those fingerprint spirals a hundred meters wide, visible from the domes, centered on nothing — they will say very little, and what they say amounts to: the moss grows by memory, the way we speak indoors by memory, and the whorls are where the memory is thinking about something. They do not cross the whorls. Asked why, an old cairn-keeper gave the sign that means *politeness*, and then the sign that means *not yet*.

---

Which leaves the two residents nobody can interview.

The pelt itself may be the plateau's true inhabitant — one organism, or a federation so old it behaves as one, a single green animal lying over the finished stone from horizon to horizon. The combed alignment that ignores today's eddies, holding the bearing of a wind that stopped blowing before anything could remember it: that is not response, that is *habit*, and habit at that depth is close to a self. Cut anywhere, it heals in the same direction. It has outlived the mountains' children and will presumably outlive everything now walking on its paths, and the whorls, if you want the cairn-keeper's view, are simply what it looks like when the only individual large enough to have a fingerprint leaves one.

And the wind — the wind may carry tenants, or the wind may *be* one, or the voices in the saddles may be exactly what the sober travelers insist: noise, worn round, signifying nothing. The people do not argue the point. They have lived ten thousand years between the oldest quiet thing in the world and the storm that will never be done with it, and they have arranged themselves accordingly: skin-language outside, blood-language inside, the dead given to the river of air, the listeners lying in the saddles at evening with the amber light going long across the green — waiting, in the loudest place on earth, for one word to come home.
