The first answer is the honest one: what lives here is the chorus. The frogs and the birds and the snails are not scenery for some truer inhabitant — they are the bulk of the place's life and the whole of its government, and any account that hurries past them to get to something with a face has already misunderstood the shallows. But stand on a dome at dusk, long enough that the frog-roar becomes wallpaper and the seep comes through underneath it, and you will eventually notice that some of the stones are walking.

They are tall — taller than they strictly need to be, in the way herons are, as though height were less a tool than a courtesy, a way of taking up less of the water. Their legs are long and thin and gray-brown, jointed high, and their feet are broad, soft, splayed things that read the bedrock through three inches of water the way a fingertip reads grain in wood. They do not splash. Nothing that has lived here long splashes; a splash is a shout, and they are sparing with shouts. Their skin is the color of the domes — old bone shading into rained-on rust — and it wears the same soft sheen as everything else, so that at half a mile, in the thick light, a person standing still is indistinguishable from a stone, and a stone is indistinguishable from a person who has simply finished what they were saying. They like this. It is, as far as anyone can tell, their only vanity.

They wear almost nothing, because the warmth provides and the plain does not reward carrying things. A cord of woven reed at the hip. A snail shell, sometimes, drilled and strung, which is not jewelry exactly but a way of keeping a particular afternoon. The entire material culture of one of them fits in a closed hand, and they would tell you — slowly, over the better part of an hour — that this is not poverty. Possessions are for places with corners, and there are no corners here. What would you put a thing *behind*?

They keep the spacing of the birds. Each one given their acre — not assigned, never argued over, simply settled into the way water settles, each presence finding the level where it crowds nothing. They are never alone and they are never together. Two of them standing within arm's reach of each other is an event, a wedding or a deathbed; their ordinary intimacy is conducted at six hundred yards, and they consider this the correct distance at which to love someone — close enough to hear everything, far enough that you must mean it.

Because hearing everything is the condition of life here, and they have made it the condition of their souls. Their voices are built for the open water: low, flat, carrying tones that ride the warm air for miles without effort, pitched to thread through the gaps in the frog-chorus — and the frogs do leave gaps, a rhythm old past counting, and the people learned that rhythm before they learned anything else, the way other peoples learn the shape of their mother's face. A conversation among them is a slow exchange across open distance, sentences spaced minutes apart, each remark given time to travel and settle and be turned over before the reply sets out. There is no whispering. There is no architecture for whispering — no wall, no thicket, no room. Every word said anywhere on the plain belongs to the whole plain, and so they have arranged themselves accordingly: they do not lie, not because they are virtuous but because a lie here is told to everyone at once and checked against everyone at once, and the engineering of it is exhausting. Deceit is like building. There is nothing to build it against. Their word for *speak* and their word for *give* are the same word, and they find it strange — genuinely, mildly strange, the way one is puzzled by a riddle — that anywhere could be otherwise.

They graze. That is the plain word for it and they would not resent the plain word. They work the flats all day as the birds do, unhurried, eating snails and fry and pale crustaceans and the soft hearts of reeds, eating standing up, eating while talking, while listening, while doing the long slow nothing that constitutes most of their lives and that they conduct with the seriousness other peoples reserve for harvest. They drink from the weeping seams at the bases of the domes, lips to the warm stone, and they consider this good manners — taking only the water the land is already letting go. They sleep folded on the dome tops in loose, far-flung constellations, or standing in the shallows like dark punctuation between the two skies, and a sleeper among them is sacred in a small practical way: you route your voice around them.

They have one monument. It is made of the only material the plain permits in quantity, which is sound. At any hour, somewhere on the flats, one of them is holding the Tone — a single sustained note, low, unhurried, passed from voice to voice across miles and across generations, taken up by a far singer before the near one lets it fall, so that it has not been silent since before anyone's grandmother's grandmother, since before, they say, the domes were as low as they are now. It is not music. It is not prayer. It is the heron's nest rebuilt in the same spot forever, done in air instead of reeds. Each voice that carries it bends it by a fraction — a grain a year, like the stone — and so the Tone today is not the Tone of a thousand years ago, and they know this, and it does not trouble them, because they are not preserving a note. They are preserving the *handing*. A child's coming of age is the first time the Tone is passed to them and they hold it alone across two miles of dusk water with the frogs coming up around them like a tide; people remember that hour their whole lives, the way others remember a first kiss or a war.

Children, it should be said, are loud and near, and this is permitted, even cherished, the way the rain is cherished — a temporary deepening that the plain absorbs. They are born in the warm shallows and they learn distance before they learn words: the first lesson is the acre, the slow art of farness, and growing up among them is mostly the long acquisition of it. Adulthood is the day you would rather be heard than held.

And death is a walk. When one of them begins to hear the seep in their own body — that is how they put it; the trickle coming through under the chorus of the blood — they finish their conversations, which can take a season, and then they go to a weeping seam and lie down along it in the warm water, and the others hold the Tone a half-step lower for a day, which is the loudest grief the plain permits. There are no graves. A grave is a building. The water redistributes; the plain keeps everything; nothing here is lost, only planed.

Beneath all of this — beneath them, literally, an arm's length down through water and moss — there is the other inhabitant, the one they do not discuss so much as touch. They say the domes are the back of the one who lay down first. They say the seep is its breathing, the warmth its body heat, the whole flat country the posture of something that finished its argument and won by conceding everything. They do not worship it; worship needs a height to look up to, and the sleeper, they note, made sure there would be none. Their entire liturgy is a single gesture, performed without ceremony a dozen times a day: the flat of the hand laid on the warm, sweating stone, held there a moment, taken away. If the gesture has a translation, it is something like *still going*. Their word for the sleeper is their word for *floor*, and also their word for *patience*, and they see no need for three words where one will lie flat and do.

They want nothing, which would make them maddening neighbors if they had neighbors. They are not waiting for anything — not rescue, not visitors, not the rest of the plants. They had their becoming long ago, the way the land did, and what is left is not aftermath but occupancy: the inhabiting of a finished thing by a finished people. A traveler who somehow reached them would be received with enormous, unhurried courtesy, given an acre, fed snails, and would find them unsettling for about a day — the spacing, the patience, the voices arriving out of the haze like weather — and then would find them ordinary, and then, going home, would find walls unsettling for the rest of their life.

In the evenings they stand far apart in the warm shallows, ankle-deep in the lower sky, gray figures among the gray domes, the frog-chorus rising through their sternums and the Tone threading through it from somewhere over the horizon, and they do what the plain does, which is everything it needs to: they stand in the light, full of water and other voices, and ring.
