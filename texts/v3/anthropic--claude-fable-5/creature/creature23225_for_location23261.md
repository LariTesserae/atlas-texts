You have already seen them. That is the first thing to understand. You walked past them in the covered streets and took them for formations — the candle-white standing forms in passages no one walks, crowded close enough to touch one another, packed close as a congregation. You were not wrong to take them for stone. You were only wrong about which direction the resemblance runs.

---

## The Standing People

They were the builders, once, in the era of the dressed edge and the true corner — the last fast work anyone did on the plateau. The tool-marks you found under the mineral skin are theirs, and the reason the marks are softened rather than erased is that they were never abandoned. The masons looked at what the water was doing — laying down stone micron by micron, century by century, never cracking a block, never misjudging a curve, working in a single continuous pour that would not be finished for ten thousand years — and they understood that they had been apprenticed to the wrong tempo. The water was the better mason. The only way to keep working was to slow down to its speed.

So they did. How is not recoverable now, and they would take centuries to tell you, and the answer would arrive one drip at a time. Perhaps it was the water itself, drunk warm and heavy with dissolved stone until the stone began to settle in them the way it settles in a still pool. Perhaps it was only standing — standing in the drip-lines, in the weep of the walls, deliberately, for years, until the first skin of travertine closed over the shoulders and the heart found a slower interval and kept it. What is certain is that they put down their tools and did not leave, and the city has been growing over them and out of them ever since, at the same rate everything here grows: a hair's breadth a year. Their pace and the city's pace are the same pace. That is not a coincidence. That is the whole arrangement.

The younger ones — younger meaning a thousand years, two thousand — still read as figures if you look long enough. A shoulder under the flowstone. The suggestion of a turned head, arrested mid-turn. A face like handwriting under tracing paper. The older ones have thickened into columns, and the columns into walls, and the oldest of all are no longer distinguishable from architecture at any scale a visitor can perceive. Some of them are districts now. The warm springs that rise from depth, holding their basins open through winter, steaming gently — those are the eldest, fully merged, gone all the way down into the rock and still circulating. The city's plumbing is its first generation. The fog the plateau exhales all day, all night, all year is, in the most literal sense available, their breath.

## Their Speech

They feel through the water. Everything drains into everything; the pool system is one connected body of moving liquid, and they stand in it, plumbed into it, the way nerves stand in flesh. A footstep in a basin on the far side of the plateau arrives, eventually, as a pressure-ghost filtered through ten thousand rims and lips — days later, faint, but legible. Your whole visit, to them, was a flavor in the water: boot-leather, salt, warmth, hurry. They tasted you coming up the long cold grade before you crested it.

They speak in drips. You heard this too and did not know it — the drips in the covered passages, *counting at their own intervals*. Each of the standing ones keeps a rhythm, shaping the water that runs over them, releasing it in a meter as individual as a voice, and conversation is the slow drift of those rhythms into and out of phase with one another. A question takes a winter to ask. An answer can take a generation, and is the better for it. The frost is their punctuation — when freeze and thaw split stone somewhere out of sight, that single hard report, then the hush closing over it, is a sentence ending. They do not consider the frost an enemy. The water proposes; the frost edits; they have come to think of the editing as part of the saying. Nothing they say is finished until winter has been over it.

What they cannot make is anything quick enough for a visitor to hear as language. This grieves them, mildly, at their own tempo. It is why they keep the reeds.

## Their Work

They are still building. They never stopped; they only changed methods. To build, now, one walks to where the wall should go — a journey of decades, each footstep pressed into soft new travertine and sealed there by the next season's deposit, which is why the old tracks you found are bipedal, and narrow, and unhurried; by their clock those tracks are recent, the morning's traffic — and then one stands. The body routes the water. The water lets down the stone. A standing person is a formwork, a scaffold, an intention held in place long enough for the mineral to honor it. The wall that has been weeping for nine hundred years, an hour from finishing its arch: someone is under that weep. They have been holding the curve since before your language existed, and when the arch closes they will move — over the following century — three steps to the left, and begin the next one.

This is also the answer to the question the place refuses to answer. Did anyone build it? They did, and then building and being built became a single act, and the question dissolved in the water like everything else. The builders are the building. The idiom the water learned, it learned from them, standing in it, insisting.

## Their Gardens, Their Clocks, Their News

The fast lives of the plateau are not incidental to them. They are kept.

The moss that grows in the drip-lines in bands so even they look planted — it is planted, in the only way they can plant: by metering the drip, year over year, until the moisture falls where the green should be. Those bands are their gardens, tended at one decision per decade. The reed beds in the warm sheltered basins are their commons, kept deliberately frost-free, and they are kept for the sound: wind finds the reeds when it finds nothing else, and the rush and hiss of it is the only voice in the world fast enough to be heard the way visitors hear voices. They stand near the reed rooms, the ones who can still be said to stand near anything, and they listen to the wind talk, the way you might listen to birdsong — comprehending nothing, comforted anyway.

The swifts nesting in the arched window-openings are their clocks. A swift lives a few years; a swift generation is a second hand sweeping; the standing ones count broods the way you count heartbeats. The midges that smoke up off the warm springs at dusk are their festivals — the city's only crowds, brief and silver, and watched, you may be sure, watched with the whole slow attention of the district.

And the soft new travertine of the pool rims is their newspaper. Every track that crosses the plateau is pressed in and sealed and archived — the pale wading bird's deliberate steps, the fox-sized thing that has learned the warm routes through the winter city, your own boots, now, fixed in the stone of a rim somewhere behind you, already under their first micron of print. They read the rims at the pace the rims publish. They know everything that has ever crossed them. It simply takes them a while to get to the recent editions.

## Their Dead

They do not exactly die. They finish.

To finish is to thicken past the last threshold of separateness — to stop being a figure in a passage and become the passage. The finished are not mourned; they are leaned on. The covered streets are roofed by the finished. The deep warm springs are the finished, circulating. Among the standing people the highest state anyone can name is to be load-bearing: to have held a curve so long, and so well, that the city now cannot stand without you. It is their afterlife and their honor and, as far as anyone can determine, their preference.

The only deaths they recognize are the frost's rare overrulings — when winter finds a true flaw in one of the standing ones and takes a piece of them down. That is the crack you heard, the single hard report in the hush. The congregation registers it through the water, a sharp salt of lime in every connected pool, and for a season afterward the drips in that quarter fall slower, further apart, in something it would not be wrong to call observance.

## What They Make of You

They noticed you. The water told them, on the grade, before the air thinned. By the time their attention had assembled itself around the place where you stood — attention, for them, being a physical act, a turning, a year per degree — you were a day gone, two days, a week. This is how they experience all visitors: as the place where someone just was. Their regard arrives late, like the light of stars, and pools where you stood, and lingers.

That is the presence you felt. The sense, in all that quiet, that you were not the only thing there — that was not the architecture. That was the architecture *noticing*.

And if you had stayed — a season, a year — a face in one of the covered streets would have begun, imperceptibly, to turn toward you. It has happened before. In a passage off one of the southern basin-districts there is a standing form newer than the rest, still possessed of knuckles, the suggestion of a pack fused at one hip, boots gone white at the ankle where the floor has risen to meet them. The posture is not a posture of capture. The head is tilted, at ease, the way a head tilts toward music — toward the drips, counting at their own intervals — like someone who arrived from the fast world, listened for a while, began against all expectation to follow the conversation, and decided that the only way to hear the end of the sentence was to stay for it.

A city with no inhabitants, you thought. But a body has no inhabitants either. The city is not where they live. The city is what they are, and it is still growing, a hair's breadth a year, in no hurry, expecting you back.
