Begin with the walls, because the walls answer first.

The wrens live in the lattice — in the dark seams where limb has grown into limb, in the rot-pockets where the iron-colored wood has softened to punk, in cavities so old they have moss inside them. They are not ordinary wrens. They are a lineage, a dynasty, a dialect: every wren in the hollow descends from wrens that sang here against the machine-roar, and the ones whose songs cleared the drone got mates, and the ones whose songs drowned did not, and so for generations the hollow has been quietly breeding the loudest wrens in the world. A wren from here, carried out and released in some ordinary silent wood, would be a catastrophe — a song built to overtop a foundry, detonated into stillness. The fledglings learn it before they learn anything else: pitch above the throb, punch through the shriek, sing as though the air itself were arguing with you, because it is. They do not experience this as hardship. They have never heard a quiet morning. To a hollow wren, the roar is what air sounds like, and song is what you do to air.

The woodpecker lives higher, in a chamber it did not excavate so much as inherit — the wood there is too hard now for excavating; it found a hollow limb and moved in, and it drums on the dead iron branches because the bowl beneath amplifies the drumming gorgeously, and a thing that sounds gorgeous to itself will do it forever. The pigeons live in the canopy and contribute their five grinding notes the way furniture contributes to a room. None of these is the inhabitant you came to find. They are the walls' tenants. The walls are full of tenants — voles running tunnels through the brown felt, their corridors threaded alongside the buried limbs like service ducts; beetles in the punk-wood; at night a tawny owl that hunts the wall-edge and never, not once in its life, crosses the open center, as though the emptiness there were furniture too, and not to be sat on.

Go down a scale, and the moss answers second.

The moss is not a carpet. The moss is a country. Beneath the living green inch lie the dead centuries, compressed into strata, and the strata are inhabited the way catacombs are inhabited — springtails in their millions, mites, nematodes, tardigrades fat and translucent in the permanent damp, all of them grazing the fungal threads that run through the dead moss like wiring through a wall. The fungus itself runs deeper still, down to the roots of the ring of trees, knitting the whole grove into the single circulatory thing it is; if the hollow can be said to have a nervous system, it lives down there in the dark felt, and it is busy, and it is silent, and it outnumbers everything above it by margins that make counting silly. The hollow is not empty. The hollow is empty only at the scale of eyes. At the scale of the moss it is one of the most crowded places the land has — a metropolis with a meadow's face.

But you asked who lives *here* — in the bowl, in the open, in the room itself. And at the scale of eyes, in the entire vacant center of the hollow, the answer is one.

The toad lives under the crossing limb.

It is the color of the place — wet-iron dark above, moss-brown below, so exactly the shade of the shadowed felt that you will not see it until it blinks, and it blinks rarely, and on its own schedule. It is large the way old toads are large: not fat but settled, as though gravity had been working on it personally for a long time and the toad had decided to cooperate. It sits where the drops fall. Not under them, quite — a hand's width to the side, in the dark wet zone the drops maintain, positioned with the precision of something that has had a very long time to choose its spot and has chosen it. The drop swells on the limb's underside, falls, lands. The toad does not flinch. The toad has never flinched. Twice a day the world delivers it water, and its skin drinks without being asked, and that is the toad's entire relationship with effort.

It does not hunt so much as preside. The beetles of the wall-country make their crossings of the open moss, and some fraction of those crossings end at the toad, swiftly, with a motion so brief it seems less like predation than like correction. Then the stillness resumes. The wrens detonate overhead; the machine-heart concusses through the ground; the woodpecker drums its drum-chamber drumming — and at the center of all of it sits the one animal in the hollow that makes no sound at all. In a bowl pressurized with noise, the toad is the silence. It is the only thing here that answers the roar with nothing.

Whether it is one toad is not a question the hollow can settle. Toads live thirty years, forty in the cool and damp, and it is cool and damp here past anything a toad could wish for, and there has been a toad under that limb for as long as anyone has pushed through the soft seam in the wall — your grandmother's day, and before. Perhaps it is a succession, each old toad replaced in the night by a young one that takes up the same station and the same posture, a dynasty like the wrens, invisible because every member is identical in office. Perhaps it is the same toad, absurdly, patiently the same. The moss keeps no records. The drop falls on schedule either way.

What it does — and this is the closest thing to an answer — is occupy the center. The hollow was described as an arena full of audience and empty of performance, but stand long enough and you will see it the other way around: the walls perform, ceaselessly, redundantly, at the top of their lungs, and the toad is the audience. One seat, permanently filled, facing everything. Both worlds of sound pass through it — the engine-thunder rising through the moss into its belly, the birdsong falling through the green air onto its back — and it sits between them doing whatever toads do that resembles listening, which from the outside is indistinguishable from listening, which after enough decades may simply be listening.

It will be there tomorrow. Same limb above it, same dark wet zone, same posture, as though it had been set down at the founding of the place and the place had then grown to fit. The drop will swell, and fall, and land beside it without a sound. The toad will not move. Nothing here needs it to.
