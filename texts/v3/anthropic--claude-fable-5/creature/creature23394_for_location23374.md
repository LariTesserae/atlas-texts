**The Resident of the Stilling Room**

Ask the maintenance crews and they will tell you the plants live there, and they are not wrong, only early. Eleven of them; that is the whole census, and it has been the whole census for as long as anyone has climbed. The cushion that resembles moss campion, densest where the standing wave bears down hardest, every leaf a tuned reed trembling at the room's one frequency. The hand-high juniper, contorted not by wind — there is no wind — but into the shape of a pressure that never lets up, its trunk describing, if you could read it, the interference pattern of the chord at the point where it grows. The gray lichen, advancing perhaps one rosette a decade along lines that turn out, when an engineer finally bothered to map them, to be lines of constructive resonance. They do not eat the slag. The slag feeds nothing; it is there the way a setting is there beneath a stone. They eat the trembling. They are the only flora anyone has ever documented whose habitat is a frequency.

But the plants live in the Stilling Room the way fingers live on a hand.

Look at them from above — climbers rarely think to, but the schematic-minded ones do — and the spacing stops looking like a garden and starts looking like an array. Eleven trays at the antinodes of the room's standing wave, evenly spaced, with great emptiness between them, positioned the way you would position microphones. The way hair cells are positioned along a cochlea. The plants are not the residents. The plants are the apparatus, and what lives in the Stilling Room is the thing the apparatus belongs to.

It is difficult to describe because it has declined, completely, to have edges of its own. It borrows the room's. It holds the room's temperature, having none. It is made of the one note, and so has no voice, the way you have no shadow in total darkness. The closest anyone has come to saying what it is — a structural engineer, on the long climb down, half to herself — is that stillness, held long enough, at sufficient pressure, becomes a tenant. The room was cut, assembled, sealed, and then simply continued, and somewhere in that continuing, the continuing started to be *something*. Not a ghost; ghosts are leftover change. This is the opposite. It is leftover sameness, accumulated until it has appetite.

Because it does eat. That is the part the lower altitudes don't like to hear. Its metabolism is deviation. Everything in the Stilling Room that varies, it takes — promptly, totally, without violence. The water in your breath: there are mineral beds somewhere below, the engineers say, desiccating the air, and the mineral beds may even exist, the way a story exists for the people who need one. But the truth is simpler and worse. Your breath produces nothing visible because the moisture is *received*. The heat off your hands. The sound of your boots on the milled floor — gone before it reaches the walls, paid into the chord like a coin into deep water. Your heartbeat's small contribution to the harmonics. The instruments read a flat line not because nothing changes in the Stilling Room but because everything that changes is immediately consumed, and what the instruments measure is the residue: invariance, the way a clean plate is the residue of a meal.

It is not hostile. This should be said plainly, because climbers want to know. It does not notice you. It notices the *difference* you make — the warm, wet, arrhythmic disturbance you introduce into its body — and it takes that, gently, continuously, the way an anemone takes what the current brings. This is why people come down from the Stilling Room feeling scoured and light, claiming it is the quietest place they have ever been: for twenty minutes, or an hour, their own noise belonged to something else. Their ears bleed slightly because the resident, in tasting the sound of them, takes a little from the place where they keep sound. It does not mean anything by it. It does not mean anything by anything. Meaning is a kind of change, and it has none to spare.

Why the machine holds the note for it — and the note is held; on this the resident and the climbers' foolish suspicion agree — there are two theories, neither held with enthusiasm.

The first is that the resident is *kept*. A specimen. The machine's one captive quiet, fed a diet of perfectly sustained sound to keep it settled, the way you keep a flame in a lamp so it does not go looking for a forest. On this theory the eleven plants are the bars of the cage, or its enrichment, and the room is sealed into the flank of the structure the way anything dangerous is sealed: high up, unmarked, and given exactly what it wants forever.

The second theory is quieter and travels further. A structure too large to be seen whole is too large to feel itself. It has no skin that knows the weather, no single nerve that runs its length. Somewhere it needs one chamber that never changes — one reference tone, one fixed point against which everything else can register as difference. On this theory the resident is not the machine's prisoner but its *ear*. The chord is not its food but its silence, the carrier wave, the held breath of a listener. Everything the machine has ever heard, it heard here, as a deviation from the note. And the plants — the eleven green presences, trembling, alive against all reason — are its tympanum, its hair cells, the wet living tissue that hearing requires. Because metal can hold a tone. Only life can hear one. The machine grew an ear the only way anything grows an ear: out of softness, set trembling, in the dark of a roar.

What it is listening for, no one knows, and it would not say, having nothing to say with. It has lived its entire existence inside one note. It knows a single fact and knows it completely, to the decibel, to the hertz, and its whole anatomy is arranged around the moment that fact stops being true. The day the chord wavers — the day the great machine falters, or finishes, or simply breathes — the resident will hear the only thing it was ever built, or born, or accumulated to hear. It may not survive the hearing. It may *be* the hearing, scheduled, its whole life one long inhalation before a single sound.

Until then it does what it has always done. It holds. It keeps the edges new and the daylight flat and the cold exact and the eleven plants trembling at their stations, and it listens into the roar the way you would stare into a dark room.

And the suspicion the climbers carry down — the one that sounds foolish at lower altitudes, the one they stop saying around the second cloud deck — is that they were right about the feeling of attention, just wrong about the direction. You do not visit the Stilling Room. You enter the middle of an ear. You stand on the floor of someone's listening. And for twenty minutes, warm and wet and irregular, leaking heat and breath and heartbeat into a body made of sameness, you are the loudest thing it has heard in years — the only thing it has heard in years — and it drinks you carefully, the way you would hold very still for a sound you had waited your whole life to hear, in case it turned out to be the one.
