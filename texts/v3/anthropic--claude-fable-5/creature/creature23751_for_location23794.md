The chamber's oldest resident does not know it lives there.

It is a fig — a strangler, most likely, one of the vast grey-barked patriarchs of the canopy four meters and a whole world overhead — and it has never perceived the room at all. It sent roots down the way it sends roots down everywhere, blindly, greedily, following moisture gradients through soil and then through fractured stone, and at some point in the last century several of those roots broke through a ceiling into open air and simply kept going. They did not register the void as a void. They registered it as a dry spell to be crossed, and so they crossed it: down through four, five, six meters of black warm nothing, thickening year by year, until they found the mud floor and the puddle and the water table beneath, and drank.

So the tree lives here the way a sleeper lives in a dream — fully present, wholly unaware. The braided trunks of root that hang through the dark are as alive as anything in the sunlit forest; sap moves in them, sugars made by leaves a hundred feet up in the light come down through them in the dark, and the water they raise will become canopy, fruit, the fig-wasps' whole civilization, without any of it ever knowing where it came from. If you press your ear to the thickest root and wait, past the drone of the hidden river, you will hear nothing — and yet you are leaning against a column of ongoing life, a vertical animal of a plant, the chamber's landlord and its most oblivious tenant at once. When the tree dies, decades or centuries from now, the room will change utterly. The curtains will rot and fall. The drips will reroute. Half the chamber's inhabitants live on the fig's charity and don't know their benefactor any better than it knows them.

Beneath the roots, in the halos around every fracture, lives the second population: the doomed.

Every wet season the forest floor washes seeds down through the entrance slot and through the ceiling cracks, and some fraction of them land in mud rich enough to fool them. They germinate in absolute darkness — because a seed does not need light to begin, only warmth and water, and the chamber is generous with both — and each one spends its entire maternal endowment on a single wager: that light is upward, and near. They rise white and translucent, stems drawn out to absurd thinness, leaves that never green unfurling like little wax hands, reaching. Some of them get remarkably far. There are etiolated seedlings down here a forearm long, pale as candle drippings, still elongating on the last of their reserves. None of them will make it. There is no light to make it to. When the endowment runs out they soften, fold, and go back into the mud, and the mud gets a little richer, and next season's seeds arrive.

It sounds like tragedy and it is actually an economy. The ghost-seedlings are the chamber's harvest — sunlight from the world above, packaged in seeds, delivered downward, and spent in the dark. The white stringy moss, the blanched fern-gametophytes that grow in the drip-halos like frost on the inside of the stone: these are the same story slower. They live on drip-fed nutrients, on the dissolved forest that comes down with the water, and some of the ferns persist for years in their pale first-stage bodies, never able to complete themselves, immortally adolescent. The chamber is full of life that is all beginning, the way the chamber itself is all beginning. Nothing green here has ever finished.

The mud takes what the seedlings leave, and in the mud, and on the fallen leaves, and inside the velvet charcoal of the old fire ring, lives the third population: the fungi. They are the only inhabitants doing well by any surface standard. Fine white mycelial fans spread across sodden leaf-fall like maps of rivers that don't exist yet. The rotted cord knotted around the root has been a fungal banquet for longer than it was ever a handhold; what looks like decay is actually the busiest neighborhood in the room. Every so often — after a bat visit, after a big wash-in of debris — the fungi throw up fruiting bodies, small pallid mushrooms that no light will ever strike and no wind will ever carry spores from, gilled and perfect and pointless, releasing their spores into still air where they drift a meter and settle. Even the fungi here are practicing for a world that hasn't opened yet.

And then there is the citizen. The cricket.

One species, pale honey-gold, long-legged, with antennae half again the length of its body — antennae that are its entire visual field, swept constantly through the dark like a blind man's two canes. You never see more than one at a time, but they are not one; there is a lineage down here, thin and persistent, breeding somewhere in the fracture systems, and they are the only animals that treat the chamber as home rather than accident. They are the room's economy given legs. A cricket walks the walls at whatever hour it pleases — hours mean nothing here — grazing fungal film off the stone, scavenging collapsed seedlings, finding the drowned beetles and washed-down ants the rain delivers, cleaning up the bat's leavings when there are leavings. It has traded its eyes down to almost nothing and put the savings into touch and patience. It can go weeks between meals. It is not troubled by the drone behind the wall; the drone has been the sound of the universe for its entire evolutionary memory. If the chamber can be said to have an opinion of you as you stand in it, the cricket is that opinion: it finds your carbon dioxide plume from a body-length away, considers, and walks unhurriedly elsewhere.

The puddle-thing is a different matter, and it may be the most important resident of all, because it is a messenger.

You never see it clearly — the water clouds, something translucent flexes away — but it is real: a blind amphipod, or an isopod pale as glycerin, eyeless, pigmentless, a creature of the deep phreatic dark. It did not evolve in this puddle. It cannot have; the puddle is younger than the trees. It came from behind the wall. Somewhere in the flooded conduit where the young river roars, there is a whole fauna — an old one, older than the chamber by geological ages, blind swimmers that have been riding the groundwater since before this stretch of jungle existed — and the hairline cracks that let water seep into the puddle are just barely wide enough to let the smallest of them through. The thing in the puddle is the river's scout, the first colonist from the nation next door. It lives on almost nothing: bacterial film, dissolved organics, the occasional windfall of a drowned cricket. It is waiting, though waiting is a word for creatures with futures in mind and it has none. It is simply here, on the near side of the wall, first.

The bat, when the bat comes, is weather. One animal, one night, and the room's whole budget jolts: a spatter of guano is a nutrient event the fungi and the cricket lineage will metabolize for a month. The chamber does not have visitors so much as it has infusions.

Which leaves the smallest and the truest inhabitants, the ones actually at the chamber's tempo: the films. On every wet face of stone, over the raw fracture surfaces, across the nubs of infant flowstone, there are communities of microbes — bacteria and archaea in slicks a few cells deep — and they are not merely living on the chamber, they are helping to build it. They nucleate calcite. They gather minerals out of the drip-water and lay them down. The straws lengthening a hair's width a year are lengthening partly through their agency; the film that is "still deciding what it wants to become" is a decision being made by trillions of participants. Everything else down here lives fast and dies against the chamber's slowness. The films live at the chamber's own speed. They are turning the room to stone from the inside, patiently, and they will still be at it when the handprints go under glass.

About the handprints: no, the people do not live here. They are gone in every way a person can be gone — even their charcoal has gone soft, even their cord has been eaten. But the chamber has done with their marks what it does with everything it keeps: it has begun to accrete over them, which is the only kind of holding it knows. Three red hands, sealing slowly into the wall's growing skin, becoming part of the stone the way a shell becomes part of limestone. It is not memory. It is closer to digestion, done with infinite gentleness. In that sense — only in that sense — someone still resides here, and their tenancy is lengthening.

And there is one more inhabitant, if you allow it: the roar itself. The river behind the wall lives here the way a heartbeat lives in a chest — on the far side of a barrier, unseen, unceasing, defining everything. Every resident of the chamber is arranged around that sound. The cricket navigates against it. The drips are lost into it. The puddle-thing was born in it. And the sound is a promise as much as a presence, because the wall is young stone, and young stone opens. Some year — a thousand from now, ten thousand, an afternoon in the chamber's terms — the river will find its way through, and the two dark countries will meet: the blind old fauna of the water and the pale young settlement of the room. Everything that lives here now is, whether it can know it or not, an advance party.

Until then: a tree that doesn't know it's here, a nursery of the beautifully doomed, fungi rehearsing for a wind that never comes, one gold cricket walking the walls like a night watchman, a glass animal in a puddle with its whole nation behind a wall, three red hands going slowly under stone — and the water, everywhere the water, building the house around all of them, loud and warm and in no hurry at all.
