# The People of the Annealing City

The first thing to know about them is that they were not selected. No one chose hardy stock for this place, no one screened the founders for patience or lung capacity or a temperament suited to silence. The funicular did the choosing, and is still doing it. The ramps take a day and a half to climb and there is nothing to do on them but breathe thin air and watch the cloud sea drop away, and a certain kind of person arrives at the top having decided, somewhere on the way, that they will never make that climb again in either direction. Those are the ones who stay. The city is, in the most literal sense, a population of people who looked at a long slow difficult ascent and found that it agreed with them.

Three centuries of that, and now there are people who never made the climb at all — the *stone-born*, as opposed to the *ramp-born* — and the mountain has begun, gently, to revise them.

---

## Their Bodies

They are deep-chested. That is the first thing a lowlander notices: not tall, not broad exactly, but built around the lungs the way the city is built around the heatworks. The stone-born are barrel-ribbed from childhood, their blood thick with red cells, their resting pulse slow as a flywheel. They walk uphill — and everything here is uphill — without hurrying and without stopping, at a pace that looks lazy and covers ground. Hurrying is the mark of the newcomer. The air does not permit it, and the city has translated that permission into character: a stone-born adult moving at a run means an emergency, and people will come out of their doors to see.

Their skin is dry and faintly mineral. The air takes moisture from everything, and the people glow a little, dusted, the way the iron roofs do — a matte bloom on the forearms, white crescents of evaporated sweat-salt at the temples by evening. They drink constantly and without ceremony; every workshop, every stairwell landing, has its cistern tap, and pausing at one is as unremarkable as breathing.

And then there is the glass. The hospital's charged bath has been in use for five generations, and the city's old workers carry its history in their hands: thin translucent seams where the silica-heavy water sealed a cut or knit a crushed knuckle, repairs finer than the original flesh. By sixty, a foundry hand's palms are veined with it. By eighty, the seams have spread — a glazed line along an old fracture in the forearm, a faint glassy patch over a burn taken in youth. The old flex their hands in the evening light and the seams catch fire briefly, gold and violet, and nobody finds this strange. The physicians have a percussion practice unique to the city: they tap a patient's chest and joints with a small bronze hammer, the way an Adjuster sounds a wall, and listen to the tone. A healthy body has a certain note. An old body has a higher one — brighter, more crystalline. *Going glassy*, the people say, without dread. It is understood to be the direction of things.

Whether the mountain means anything by this, no one says aloud. But it is noticed — quietly, the way everything here is noticed — that the stone takes back its markers, fills its forgotten cellars, sheathes its pilings, and that the people too are being very slowly sheathed, finished from the inside out, one small clear repair at a time.

## Their Speech

The thin air kills voices at twenty paces, so the city long ago stopped relying on them. What it speaks instead is *tap-talk*: a knuckle code rapped on iron, on railings, on the bronze window frames, traveling through the metal frame of the city further and more faithfully than any shout through the air. The whole city is wired with itself. A Towerman at the top of a condensary can converse with the cistern keeper at its base through the column between them. Apprentices in the turbine halls gossip through the handrails. Children tap through party walls at night, and the courting custom of the middle terraces is exactly what a lowlander would guess: a young person raps a question on the shared wall of an adjoining house, and the answer comes back slow or quick or not at all, and entire engagements have been conducted, the old women say, without either party raising their voice once.

When they do speak aloud, they speak the way they walk: short, level, unhurried, words rationed like breath because words *are* breath here and breath is the scarce thing. The city's speech has shed its ornaments. Compliments are rare and therefore land hard. The highest praise in the local idiom is *it holds* — said of a wall, a marriage, an argument, a person. The worst thing commonly said of anyone is that they are *loose*: unjointed, unshimmed, rattling.

They hum, though. That surprises visitors. The cathedral's deep note — the two-century groan of its tensioned piers — is the city's tuning fork, and the stone-born carry it in their chests without knowing they do. Workers hum it at their benches. Mothers hum it to infants. When the congregation gathers, there is no singing as a lowlander would recognize it, only several hundred people settling onto one low note together and holding it, breath staggered so the tone never breaks, sometimes for an hour, the building humming back. The note has dropped a fraction of a semitone since the great-grandparents' time, and the people's hum has dropped with it. They are, pitch by pitch, keeping the cathedral company on its way down.

## Their Guilds

The Adjusters are the aristocracy, insofar as there is one, and their aristocracy consists of walking. Every day, gauges and pry bars and ledgers, the long patrol of the expansion seams. They are recognizable at any distance by their gait — the slow straddling walk of someone reading the ground — and by the ledger satchels, oiled iron-cloth, worn at the left hip, which are handed down master to apprentice and are themselves recorded in older ledgers. An Adjuster's authority is absolute on the question of the seams and nonexistent everywhere else, and they like it that way. They are the city's memory. When a property dispute or a boundary question or even a question of inheritance gets tangled, it ends at an Adjusters' table, under a lamp, in the presence of three hundred years of measurements, and what the ledger says, holds.

It is the Adjusters who keep the custom of the doorframe: a child of the city is measured each solstice in the guild ledger of their block, their height entered in the same patient hand, the same column structure, as the block's own annual rise. The two figures sit side by side for eighteen years — the child gaining, the ground gaining — and at majority the page is shown to them, the only time most citizens ever see the ledgers open. *You and the mountain*, the presiding Adjuster says, by formula, *have grown up together. Adjust accordingly.* It is the closest thing the city has to a confirmation rite, and the stone-born remember it the way lowlanders remember weddings.

The Annealers are stranger and they know it. Theirs is the craft of decades — objects committed to the warm rock and tended across lifetimes — and so theirs is a guild in which the apprentice routinely finishes the master's work, and the master routinely begins work for apprentices not yet born. This does something to a person. Annealers are the calmest people in a calm city, and the most heavily insured against sentiment; their contracts are wills, their workshops are partly archives, and their professional courtesy extends naturally across death, which they regard as an administrative event. They are the ones who tend the deep galleries, who shave back the slowly closing walls, who leave the dishes of charged water out for whatever the seam mice are — and they are the ones the city gives its dead to.

The Towermen — keepers of the condensaries — are the city's monks, more or less, though they would not use the word. They live half their lives at altitude above the rooflines, in the freezing finned columns, listening to the ticking of the droplets, and they are the only citizens who regularly experience cold. It marks them. They come down red-faced and bright-eyed and slightly elsewhere, and they are credited, fairly or not, with the city's weather sense, its long views, and most of its poetry, which is brief, unrhymed, and concerned almost entirely with condensation.

And there are the glassmen, the foundry crews, the funicular gangs, the cistern keepers, the masons with their narrow window of soft stone and their iron pilings — but the truth is that the guild structure matters less than the thing all the guilds share, which is the ledger-hand: the same patient script, the same habit of recording, the same conviction that nothing in this city is finished and therefore everything must be written down mid-becoming, so that whoever takes up the work can find the place where it was set down.

## Their Children

The children are born hot — into a city without winter, without green, without a single living thing larger than a vent swift — and the curriculum of their early years is mostly a map of permissions. Which pools drink and which pools charge. Where the cobbles run slick. Where the fissures breathe heavily and a dropped mitten will cook. They learn the seams of their own street before they learn to read, stepping over the sliding iron plates with the absent fluency of children anywhere stepping over cracks, and the first toy of any consequence — given around age six, with some small ceremony — is a feeler gauge, with which they go about solemnly measuring everything: gaps, joints, the spaces under doors, the slowly widening seam in front of their own house.

They have never seen a tree. The word exists in their schoolbooks alongside *ocean* and *horse*, in the chapter on the lowlands, and carries about the same charge of plausible myth. When the funicular brings up timber crates and the crates are broken down, the wood is saved for the children, who carry the pale fragrant slats around for weeks, smelling them. The smell of pine is, for a stone-born child, the smell of *elsewhere* — the one scent in the city that the mountain did not make.

Their games are tapping games, listening games, stillness games. The grand one, played by every generation, is called *seam mouse*: one child is the warm spot, moving along a wall with a hand pressed flat, and the others, eyes shut, must track the warmth and freeze when it freezes. It is played in dead silence and can last an hour. Lowland visitors who come upon a courtyard of motionless children, eyes closed, palms on stone, tend to be unsettled, and are not always entirely reassured by the explanation.

## Their Dead

Nothing burns here, and the ground will take no spade — so the city gives its dead to the only thing patient enough to receive them.

When a citizen dies, the Annealers cut a chamber: small, exact, in one of the circulatory zones where the stone's slow warmth moves in waves. The body goes in dressed in glass-cloth, with the dead person's gauge, their ledger page (a fair copy; the original never leaves the guild), and one object of their own choosing, recorded in advance — and then the chamber is left open. That is the part lowlanders find hardest. No sealing, no stone rolled across. The wall is simply allowed to do what the green stone does. It takes between forty and ninety years, the Annealers' records show, for the mountain to close a chamber completely, the new mineral creeping over the threshold like slow water, and during those decades the family may visit, may sit at the narrowing entrance, may tap on the iron doorframe — which will, in time, be fused into a wall of flawless stone, like the forgotten cellar door, like the swallowed boundary markers, like everything here that the mountain has decided to keep.

They do not call it burial. The word in the city is *finishing*. The dead are spoken of, without euphemism and without much grief past the first year, as being *in the long anneal* — committed, like the bells and the glass, to a curing no kiln can imitate, on a schedule no one living will see the end of. What the mountain is making of them, the people do not claim to know. But they have noticed that the oldest closed chambers, when a gallery re-broaching passes near one, ring under the Adjusters' hammers with a tone unlike any other stone on the slope — high, clear, settled — and the crews shave their tunnels carefully around those places, and mark them in the ledgers with a small symbol that means, roughly, *holds*.

## The Others

Count the vent swifts among the citizens; the city does. A few hundred dark shapes that ride the noon release and the fissure thermals, that no one has caught, fed, found dead, or seen land. Their crystalline droppings — *swift-frit*, the glassmen call it — are gathered from the high iron by the Towermen and sold to the foundries as seed material; glass started on swift-frit is said to be the clearest the city makes, and the great rolling mills pay for it by weight in cistern shares. The children's account of the swifts is that they are the mountain's letters, sent up to read the sky and come back down with the weather. The naturalists' account is shorter: *insufficient data, three hundred years running*. The two accounts coexist without friction. The swifts wheel up through the noon plume, scatter, regroup, vanish into the violet, and a thousand windows watch, and that is the city's whole relationship with them: attendance.

The seam mice are another matter — warm spots that pace you along the gallery walls, that pause when you pause — and the Annealers' position on them has hardened, over the generations, from superstition into something nearer protocol. The dishes of charged water are budgeted now, a line item. The crystal ferns that sometimes grow in them — branching, recursive, the chemistry not quite accounting for the shapes — are collected, catalogued, and kept in a long cabinet in the guildhall that the Annealers do not show to visitors and do not discuss, except to say that the ferns from different chambers differ, consistently, the way handwriting differs. A few of the oldest Annealers are said to be able to call a seam mouse: a particular slow pattern tapped on the gallery wall, and then the wait, and then — sometimes — the warmth arriving under the palm. Asked what the pattern means, the old Annealers give the same answer their masters gave: *it's not a word. It's a knock.*

And then there is the largest inhabitant, the one the census does not list and everyone houses. The city's quiet third opinion — that a mountain still forming is still, in some pre-living mineral sense, deciding — is held mostly by the old, but it is held by the old *because they have had time to watch*, and what they have watched is this: a city that defers, jointed and shimmed and tensioned, asking permission of its ground quarter-turn by quarter-turn — and a ground that has answered, so far, with warmth, with water, with closed cellars and sealed cracks and small clear repairs in the hands of its workers. The old do not say the mountain is alive. They say the mountain is *practicing*, and that the city, with its slow people and its glassy elders and its dead committed to the rock, may be part of the practice — the mountain's first draft of inhabitants, being annealed along with the bells.

## Their Long View

Ask a stone-born child what they will be, and you may get a guild, or you may get the other answer, the one the schools teach without quite teaching it: *here*.

They know the figure — ten thousand years, the rising done, the heat gone, the towers dry. It is in the ledgers; it is in the cathedral's falling note; it is in the percussion of their own ribs. They are not building for permanence and they know it, and this has produced, instead of fatalism, the most particular trait of the people of the Annealing City: a complete absence of hurry joined to a complete absence of waste. Every shim recorded. Every child measured. Every droplet caught. They live the way their machinery runs — gradual and immense, geared down and down, at speeds you can watch, count, almost converse with — and they are, ledger by ledger and generation by generation, preparing an answer to a question that will not be asked for a hundred centuries.

The Adjusters' formula, written between the seam measurements in the same patient hand, is the closest the people come to describing themselves, and it serves:

*The mountain is not finished. Neither are we. Adjust accordingly.*

They are the adjustment.
