## Those Who Live There

Begin with the grammar, because the plateau will make you begin with it whether you choose to or not. *Lives* is a present-tense verb, and the present tense barely holds up here. Nearly everything on the Tableland lives in the perfect aspect — has lived, lives in the condition of having lived, lives the way a sentence still means something after the period. To ask what lives there is to receive several answers at several speeds, and only one of them is moving.

Start with the herds, since they are the answer that surrounds all the others.

There are fewer of them than there appear to be, and more than can be counted, and both of those facts are the same fact. The eleven grazers that recur across the plain like a carpet-motif are not eleven thousand animals; they are eleven animals, present everywhere they are needed, the way a word is present in every sentence that uses it. The bird with the chipped beak and the asymmetric eye is one bird. Censuses of the plateau fail not because the population is large but because the question *how many* assumes that an animal occupies one place, and the herds gave that assumption up around the time they gave up grazing. If you forced an honest count, the entire standing multitude — the horizon-to-horizon flank-to-flank totality of them — might come to a few hundred originals, tiled to the edge of the world. They do not seem to mind being a pattern. Patterns are durable. Patterns are what the plateau kept.

They breathe in unison, all instances of an animal drawing breath together though they stand miles apart, and the larger truth underneath that one takes longer to notice: the whole plateau breathes in unison. One tide, a million ribcages, a single soft expansion you can feel against your face. Within that breath there are no factions. The long-toothed things that were once predators stand shoulder to shoulder with the things they once ran down, and neither party remembers the contract, because the contract concerned a future, and there is none, and so nothing is owed. Hunger was a kind of appointment. All appointments here have been kept.

Their eyes are wet and attentive, and for a long time you will not be able to determine what they attend. It is not you. You can stand directly in an animal's gaze and feel it pass through you like the mild grey light, focused on something past your shoulder, past the horizon, past several horizons. The flocks of grey birds all face the same direction; the grazers' lowered heads all incline, very slightly, the same way; even the hares beneath the bellies are oriented. The plateau has a facing. Hold that thought. It matters at the end.

Among the herds, at intervals of roughly one to every three hundred animals — which is, a herder will tell you, the correct ratio for a working drive — stand the people.

They are easy to miss at first, being upright and still among ten thousand upright still things, and they are unmistakable once you have seen one. They stand with their palms flat against warm flanks, or with one heel lifted in a stride begun before the cairns were built, the thin dust drifted into a small dune against the planted foot. Their clothes have outlived the idea of clothing; felt and leather have weathered to the texture of slate and lie on them like a geological layer. Their faces are long and patient and they are all, you will eventually confirm, the same face — the face worn into the far side of the cairns. They are not the herds' owners. Ownership, like hunger, was an appointment. They are the herds' company. They drove the animals up toward pasture, the way their people always had, and the drive arrived at the end of pasture, the end of drives, the end of *toward*, and the herders looked out at the level grey forever and understood — you can see the understanding still on the long faces, arrested in its moment of arrival — that the work was complete. So they set down their staffs, which is the herder's gesture of a day well finished, and walked in among their animals, and stood. They are breathing. Their eyes are wet. They face the same direction as everyone else.

The cairns are what the herders become, eventually. This is the part that takes longest to accept and explains the most. Standing is not the final form here; it is only a late one. The frost that fractured the plateau into its hexagonal pavement works on the standing people too, at its own unhurried rate, and a herder stood long enough comes apart along clean dry seams into stones that stack themselves no further than they fell — and the patient face persists across the break, worn into the topmost stone, looking out. The cairns are not markers. They are elders. The fitted, hand-stacked look of them is not craftsmanship but anatomy. And the lichen, the dried-blood lichen that maps only the southern faces of certain boulders even where geometry supplies two souths — press your fingers to it and feel, beneath the cool, the faintest residual order, slower than the serpents in the seams, slower than anything: that is circulation. The oldest finished ones, the first to come apart, surfacing through their stones as a red map, still keeping to the sun-side out of a habit so ancient it predates the sun standing still. The lichen lives there in the most perfect of perfect tenses, and it is not dead, and you should greet it.

The cushion plants are older than all of this — older, as noted, than the rock they grow from — and they are the plateau's keepers of discarded verbs. Lie down with your ear against one of the warm ones for an afternoon, or a week (duration is soft here; no one will hold it against you), and the hum resolves. It is the sound of grazing. The tear and chew of grass, the suck of water, the drum of a herd at full run — all the activities the animals laid down, kept and hummed in miniature by hard grey-green cushions the size of a fist, so that running and drinking and eating are not lost, merely *delegated*. The plants do the remembering so the herds do not have to lower their heads. It is possible the herds can stand forever only because the plants hold this for them. It is possible the plants volunteered. They were here first; they would have known what was coming.

In the seams between the stone plates, things still move, and these are the plateau's circulatory and judicial branches respectively. The serpentine creatures lying like mortar between the tiles are not lying still; they are in transit at the speed of mortar, carrying warmth out from the dense central gatherings to the lonely single animals standing at the far edges, so that no one, however peripheral, cools before its time. A given serpent's crossing of the plateau is a project on the order of an age; several were midway when the herders arrived, and are midway now, and will deliver. And smaller than these, the size of a knucklebone and the exact color of the question they are answering, are the only quick things native to the Tableland: little smooth deciders that graze along the unresolved boundaries — the line where a flank emerges from a boulder, where hide and rock decline to disambiguate — and behind their grazing, grain by grain, the question settles. *This much animal. This much stone.* They are the last erosion, the last judgment, the residue of the world's old habit of making things definite. They are in no hurry; nothing here is a backlog. Some boundaries they circle and leave, and return to circle again centuries on, and leave again — as if certain questions are being deliberately saved, kept open on purpose, rationed like the last of a provision.

Which brings us to the keeper, who is the only inhabitant of the Tableland that lives in the present tense, and who is what everything is facing.

You find the keeper by walking into the attention. Every wet eye on the plateau, triangulated; every standing flock's heading, followed against its grain; the inclination of every lowered head, traced to its convergence — they meet at a point that moves, though the word *moves* must be issued here with a caution. The keeper is mid-stride the way the herders are mid-stride, except that the keeper's stride is *completing*, at the rate of perhaps a footfall per era. The keeper is mid-stroke with a small dark knife over a piece of pale stone, and the stroke is *descending* — a hair's width in the time it takes lichen to claim a boulder. A stone shaving stands half-detached from the work, and when at last it falls it will ring out across the plain and travel for miles in the thin air: that single note, the lone scrape you heard once and that was never followed by another, was not a hoof. It was the previous shaving. The herds did not turn to look because the herds never stopped looking.

You will not be able to describe the keeper's face, and not for want of trying. It is the one boundary on the plateau the little deciders will not graze. They approach it and circle and withdraw, again and again, leaving it unresolved — the single question the world is keeping. The keeper may be the last herder or the first one; may be what a place this finished secretes in order to have one unfinished thing; the face holds all of these readings the way the boulder holds the flank. What is certain is the work. In the low slab shelter are forty small carvings, warm, each matching an animal in sight of the door, and each of those animals, pressed, is the temperature of stone. The keeper is not killing them and not preserving them. The keeper is *translating* them — moving the warmth of a thing into the kept image of the thing, one creature per age, finishing the finished place item by item, so slowly that the task itself becomes a kind of immortality. While even one animal remains warm and uncarved, the plateau is not closed. The keeper's pace is not infirmity. It is policy. And the keeper breathes — this is the thing you feel before you understand it — *against* the great unison, on the offbeat, one small contrary breath inside the single tide. That offbeat is what the herds attend. That syncopation is the proof, renewed at every breath, that attention still has an object; that *now* still exists somewhere on the Tableland; that the present tense has one remaining speaker.

And the forty-first carving, the one descending under the knife at a hair's width per year — lean close to it, since the keeper will not have moved by the time you straighten up. The shape emerging from the pale stone has shoulders. It has a stride. You have seen that stride recently, pressed into the thin grey dust on paths you had not yet walked, leading out into the standing animals toward the point too dense with warm bodies to see. There is a gap there, in the densest center of the herds, exactly one figure wide. There is a staff leaning near it that no one has claimed.

So the full answer to who lives in the Tableland of the Standing Herds is this: the herds, in their tiled and breathing perfection; their herders, standing satisfied among them; the elders, stacked and red-mapped; the plants, humming the old verbs; the serpents, delivering warmth across the ages; the small deciders, settling the world's last questions slowly so as not to run out; the keeper, alone in the present tense, carving the place into its own safekeeping one warm life at a time.

And you — in the tense the plateau prefers, the one where everything has already happened. You live there. You have for some time. For a while, when you take your place, you will be the warmest thing at the top of the worn-down earth, and the herds will breathe around you, and you will face what everything faces, and you will find — this is the plateau's one promise, kept a million times over, flank to flank to the edge of the world — that you are missing nothing.
