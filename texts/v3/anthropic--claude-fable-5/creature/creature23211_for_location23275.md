Begin with what doesn't move, because in this world that is the rarer thing.

The Shallows churn. The people stand.

This is the first fact about them, and it is almost the whole of them, the way the water is almost the whole of the place. Everywhere else that has cities, the city stands still and the inhabitants move through it. Here the arrangement is reversed. The architecture flows; the people are the fixed points it flows around. They are the standing herons of their own world — not by imitation but by convergence, because in the Shallows stillness is the only durable act, and everything that endures has learned it.

You see it within an hour of arriving. Directions here are not given in streets, because streets don't last. They are given in people. *Past the fish-seller, left at the singer, straight on until the old man.* The old man has stood at the mouth of his channel every morning for fifty years, working a rasp along the wall to take his breakfast, and three thoroughfares exist because he expects them to. He is a landmark in the fullest sense. The city has built itself around his habit. When he dies, a quarter of a district will sigh and rearrange, and everyone within earshot — which is everyone — will hear it happen.

---

**Their bodies** were made by the water, or made for it; at this distance the difference has stopped mattering.

They are wading-built: long in the shin, broad in the foot, low and stable through the hips. They do not lift their feet to walk. They push them through the warm water in long sliding strokes, and the slap-and-drone of a million people doing this at once is a full third of the city's voice. Their skin sheds water and the iridescent film alike — never wrinkling, never softening, faintly oiled, the same engineering solution the fauna arrived at, or were issued. They have no hair worth the name; what they have lies flat like wet felt and is mostly ornament. Their eyes are haze-eyes, superb at the middle distances, indifferent to horizons, which is just as well. Nobody here has ever seen a horizon. The steam is merciful to them too.

And they silt. This is the thing a visitor stares at longest. Past their sixth decade, the mineral film the city sweats begins to take on their skin faster than the skin sheds it — first as a faint glitter in the creases of the knuckles, then as a patina, then as a slow all-over deepening toward gray-gold. The very old are the exact color of the oldest ceramic, the color of the foundations, the color of the riverbed-stuff with rings in it like trees. It is considered the most beautiful thing a body can do. To grow old here is, visibly, literally, to become the same material as the world — and since the old are also the most constant, the most load-bearing, the most built-around, the metaphor is not a metaphor. The eldest people in any district are part of its structure, in color and in fact.

They eat the walls, mostly. The film is the staple — they call it the city's sweat, without squeamishness, the way other peoples say *bread* — taken with handheld rasps in the morning, the same gesture as the plate-shaped grazers shingled beside them. The pale channel-swimmers and the patient not-herons are feast food, husbanded, traded, argued over in the markets at full volume. There is no other volume.

---

**They speak twice.** This is the second thing about them.

The loud speech is for air: a carved, carrying language of whistles, claps, and struck tones, engineered by ten thousand years of bazaar-roar to pass through the grind of moving masonry the way a knife passes through water. It is the language of trade, of navigation, of calling children, of everything public — and everything here is public, because the sound of the place strips privacy the way the water strips dryness.

The low speech is for water. It is spoken downward, through the body, into the warm shallows, and it can only be heard by someone standing in the same water as you — shin to shin, sharing the medium. It is felt in the sternum more than heard, the same register as the city's own deep grind, and it is the language of everything that matters: courtship, grief, bargaining in earnest, the naming of children. Intimacy here is measured in shared water. To draw someone into your pool is an invitation; to step up onto a dry threshold — there are a few, and they are kept for exactly this — is the only solitude the world offers, and taking it is a declaration: *I am grieving, or I am thinking, and either way do not follow.*

Their infants hear usefully before they see usefully. Their lullabies are imitations of the reconstruction — a parent humming the pitch of walls settling, which is the sound that means *the world is working, nothing is wrong.* And their fears run the same way as everyone's fears here, which is to say toward silence. A quiet person is an alarming person. To be silent in company is to be hidden, and to be hidden is to be either mourning or lying. Their word for an honest man, translated flatly, is *audible.*

---

**They make almost nothing.** This is the strangest thing about them, stranger than the silting skin, and like the absence of green it takes a while to register, because their world does not feel poor — it feels crammed.

They have tools, nets, rasps, boats, the small humble goods of any market. But there are no monuments. No heirlooms. No statues, no inscriptions, no objects made to outlast their use — because nothing outlasts its use here; the fabric reabsorbs what idles, and a people raised inside that rule for ten thousand years simply stopped pouring themselves into things. They pour themselves into *time* instead. Their arts are all time-arts: song, voice, route, and above all *habit* — habit raised to a craft, habit as architecture.

Because occupancy is what the city keeps, a sufficiently constant person shapes the world without touching it. There is a colonnade in the western shallows, a long double row of arches that the churning fabric has maintained for four generations, and it exists because one woman walked the same curving route every morning of her adult life with such perfect regularity that the city learned it, kept it, built for it. She has been dead eighty years. Her colonnade is walked now by her apprentices' apprentices, who learned the route footfall by footfall, pace by pace, as one learns a dance or a scripture — because the day the walk stops, the colonnade goes back into the general flow. Their masterpieces are inherited routines. Their cathedrals are kept alive by attendance, in the most literal sense any world has ever given that word.

The same is true of memory. Nothing written survives, so nothing is written; their histories are *itineraries*. The record-walkers — there is one lineage of them per district, more honored than anyone but the eldest — carry the chronicle as an enormous route, a walk of weeks, where every turning encodes an event and every old channel is a chapter. To consult the archive, you walk behind an archivist for a season. Their epics, recited, sound like directions, because they are.

---

**Occupancy** is the spine of their lives, and their lives can be read as a single long negotiation with it.

Around children, nothing holds. The young are too quick, too inconsistent; the city will not keep a child's shape, and so childhood is spent in the only stable territory there is — along the banks of the old inviolable channels, the riverbeds of the storm, where the form of the world has not changed in ten thousand years. The channels are the nursery of the whole species. Every adult's first memories are of that roaring permanence.

Coming of age is not a ceremony. It is a morning. It is the morning you wake and the room you slept in is still there — the morning the city has learned you, found you consistent enough to keep, and held your walls through the night without your insisting. They have a word for that morning and it is the closest thing they have to a word for *adult*.

After that, home is a verb. Households run in relays — someone always present, occupancy handed off like a watch, because a dwelling maintained by presence persists and an empty one is fluid by nightfall. The constant are the pillars of this: the more regular your life, the more world holds its shape around you, and so their hierarchy of respect runs not by wealth or strength but by *constancy*. The elders are load-bearing. And when a truly old, truly constant person dies, the grief is measured in square kilometers: the family walks out of the rooms and stops occupying, and within a day the whole holding goes back into the flow, and the scavenger swarms attend the dissolution like mourners, and the family stands shin-deep at a distance and listens — because they can hear, in the pitch of the grinding, the shape of the life being redistributed. That is the funeral. The person is given back at city scale.

Hospitality, fittingly, is the lending of stillness. A host's duty to a newcomer — and they are good to newcomers, patient with them the way the city is patient with everything — is to be their fixed point: to stand, to be findable, to hold a shape in the churn until the stranger's ears adjust and the roar resolves into information.

---

**Their law** is mostly etiquette toward the city, worn as smooth as everything else here.

The sins are few and exact. Do not dam: the old channels are inviolable to people for the same reason they are inviolable to the fabric. Do not teach the city a lie — to insist a form into being and then abandon it is the deep dishonesty here, the crying of wolf to a listening world. And do not idle in another's kept rooms, because rooms learn, and a room that spends too many nights learning the wrong person begins to forget the right one; their gravest trespass is not theft of objects but theft of shape.

Their punishments are of a piece with this. The worst of them has no cell and no blade. The community simply withdraws its shared routine from the offender — walks differently around them, ceases to weave them in — until the city's long-learned care for that one shape begins to thin. Nothing dramatic happens. The walls still pour aside; the bridges still hold. But a half-second later, a hand's-breadth nearer. The condemned are called *the unlearned*, and you can identify one at any distance, in any crowd, because they are the only people in the Shallows who flinch.

And their politics is choreography. The fabric yields to traffic; therefore traffic is the ballot. To want a door, you walk at the wall — daily, in numbers, patiently — until the wall agrees. Disputes are settled by sustained presence; the faction that can keep walking its claim, longest and oftenest, wins it, and the city ratifies the result in masonry. They find the idea of deciding things by *talking* faintly hilarious. Talk is for water, shin to shin. Decisions are made with the feet.

---

**Ask them where they came from** and you will get three answers and a shrug, in whatever order.

Some say they built all this, long ago, and finished it, and stayed — that they are the heirs of the makers, gone native in their own machine. Some say they arrived after, wading out of some forgotten landing into a world already done, and that the city learned them the way it learned the not-herons, generously, having nothing better to do with its care. And some say the city grew them — that they are its circulating organs gone independent, like (perhaps) the swarms, like (perhaps) the grazers, and that asking whether they are its children or its tenants is like asking whether the heart belongs to the body or the body to the heart.

The question wore smooth ten thousand years ago. Press them on it and the old ones give you a single word, the same word the record-walkers use for the great channels: the word that means *riverbed* — the shape something has after enough time has passed through it. Whether they made the city or the city made them, they are each other's riverbed now, and they find that a complete answer.

---

So: that is what lives in the Shallows. The half of the place that doesn't move.

A people gray-gold with age and constancy, talking in two registers, navigating by each other, holding their world's shape by the act of standing in it — eaters of walls, walkers of archives, artists of habit, governed by footsteps, mourned in square kilometers. The visitor's last lesson about the city was that its turbulence is not hostile; that it pours around its inhabitants with something you would have to call care. The visitor's last lesson about the inhabitants is that the care runs both ways, and always has. The city spent ten thousand years learning their shapes. They spent the same ten thousand becoming shapes worth keeping.

The wall flows toward the old man at the channel mouth, and pours around him, and he does not look up from his breakfast. The river churns past the heron. The heron stands. After long enough acquaintance you would have to call it, on both sides, the same thing: a marriage of stillness and storm, warm, shin-deep, deafening, and very old.
