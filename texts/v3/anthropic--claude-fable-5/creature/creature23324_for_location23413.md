**Who Lives in the Condensery**

The first honest answer is *nothing*, and the basin will let you hold that answer for a while. No wings, no burrows, no eyes catching the gray-gold light. You can stand in the mud a full hour and the only things that move are water and iron. But "nothing lives here" is the kind of statement the place patiently outlasts. Stay, and the residents begin to declare themselves — not by moving, because almost none of them move, but by being so thoroughly *at home* that your own presence starts to feel like the anomaly. They live here the way a tongue lives in a mouth. You were simply looking for the wrong shapes.

**The plants live here**, and they live here in the fullest, oldest sense of the word — not growing here, not occurring here, but *residing*, each one a householder of forty feet of steaming ground, each one in possession of a deed written in roots and honored by everything else for longer than the brick has been the color of liver. The chapel-palm is the eldest. It does not know this, or it knows it the way a mountain knows its height — structurally, without vanity. Its leaves have given up the ordinary ambitions of leaves; they no longer angle for light, because the light here comes from everywhere, so they hang flat and broad as doors and do the one thing the basin asks of anything: they condense. Each leaf is a roof, and the threads of water running off the leaf-tips have run so long they have worn channels in the mud below, little brick-less aqueducts of the palm's own making, mirroring in miniature the works it stands among. It is hard, after a while, not to suspect the palm of having studied them.

The horsetail column is one individual pretending to be a crowd — a single rhizome below the mud, a thousand jointed stems above it, standing in formation like a regiment that was ordered to attention and then never dismissed. It is the youngest resident, which may mean it is only a few centuries old, and it is the most obviously *adapted*: it grew up beside its steam-pipe the way a child grows up beside a grandmother, and it has shaped itself entirely to that timed gust. Its cones face the vent. Its pollen has changed — heavier than wild pollen, waxier, sticky on one side, made to ride saturated air for nine feet and adhere to a wet surface, a pollen designed for steam by ten thousand generations of forgetting what an insect was. If you took a cone out of the basin and shook it in dry moving air, the pollen would fall straight down like sand. It cannot travel the outside world anymore. It is a citizen of this place, and its passport is no good elsewhere.

This is the character of all the vegetation here: not wild, not cultivated, but *pensioned*. Each plant long ago stationed itself where the works would serve it — or the works were laid out around plants already ancient; the basin does not say — and each has since let go of every capacity the arrangement made unnecessary. They do not compete, so they have forgotten how. They are not visited, so they have stopped advertising; the lung-colored cones are the faded remnant of what were probably once colors meant for eyes. They are the residents you can see. They are not the residents who outnumber everyone.

**The films live here.** Look into the hot pools — really look, at the rims, where the blistering water meets the mud — and you will find the basin's actual majority: sheens and mats of orange, bottle-green, a brown that is nearly gold, lying in concentric rings sorted by temperature, each ring a different nation of single-celled residents for whom this basin is not strange at all. For them it is the world before the world — heat, water, minerals, dimness — and they live in it by the uncountable trillion, eating warmth, breathing stone. They coat the insides of the brick channels in a slickness the water rides on. They are, in the most literal sense, the only things here that are thriving rather than enduring, and the basin's whole vast apparatus — towers, pumps, palms — sits on top of their thin bright empire like furniture on a carpet, never noticing it is the carpet that covers every inch.

**The water lives here**, and this takes longest to accept, and then cannot be un-accepted. It is the same water. That is the thing. Topped up only by whatever rain finds its way down through the steam, the water in the loop has been the water in the loop for as long as the loop has run — drawn hot from below, breathed out the towers, gathered on the leaves, run down the channels, swallowed by the mud, drawn hot from below. It has passed through every part of the basin so many times that the distinction between the water and the basin has stopped being useful. It is in the threads off the palm-leaves and it is the threads. It licked the stone lip smooth; that was its work, grain by grain, with the patience of something that has nowhere else to be. The mineral beards under the pipe joints are not decay. They are *deposit* — the water slowly building itself a skeleton, a few grains a year, white and rust-orange, a hundred thin legs to stand on. The pumps are its heart, doubled and too large; the towers are its lungs; the films are its gut flora; and whether it thinks is not a question the basin answers, but it *remembers*, demonstrably, in stone — every year of its circulation written somewhere in the beards, if anyone could read them.

And then there is the fourth strangeness, and the last.

**The keeper lives here.** You will find the evidence before you find the keeper. The leather belt in the engine hall is stretched with age, yes — but look where it is laced: a splice of newer thong, supple, recently waxed. The governor balls spin on a film of grease that has not had time to go black. Between the engine hall and the third tower there is a path in the mud — the mud half-erases it every day, and every day something remakes it, one set of broad unhurried prints, always the same gait, always the same hand-width gap from the shoulder-height pipe where a palm trails along the iron. On a valve wheel near the coolest gallery hangs a tin cup, dented, polished bright on one side by a century of the same grip.

You may see the keeper, if you stay. At distance, through steam: a figure broad and slow, walking the pipe runs with a flat hand laid on the metal, listening through the palm the way a farrier listens to a flank. The keeper does not startle and cannot be snuck up on, or does not care to notice being snuck up on, which in the basin's noise amounts to the same dignity. The keeper does not speak — the basin is too loud, and there is no one — but taps, sometimes, on the iron: a doubled tap, *tap-TAP*, and somewhere across the works a pipe ticks back, though that may only be expansion. The keeper's skin is beaded like the leaves, and you cannot tell sweat from condensation, and you cannot tell age. The keeper breathes on the towers' schedule. The keeper's heart — lean close in that loudness, you would have to lean very close — keeps the pumps' own time.

Whether the keeper is the last descendant of whoever built the works, or something else, is not a question the basin answers. But the basin will let you notice things. It will let you notice that keepers thin. Over decades the footprints press shallower; the figure through the steam grows harder to separate from the steam. And it will let you notice that in the coolest gallery, where the condensation gathers thickest, the drip-threads off one particular pipe sometimes run together and do not disperse — that over weeks, something accumulates there, patient as a mineral beard, in roughly the shape of a person sitting down.

A condensery, in the old usage, is a works where something thin is made dense. The pumps draw, the towers cool, the loop closes, and what the works actually produce — slowly, rarely, a few grains a year — may be exactly one thing.

Heard from the stone lip, the pumps say *it does not need anyone*. Heard from inside, hand on the iron, they say something else, twice with every beat:

*Still here. Still here.*
