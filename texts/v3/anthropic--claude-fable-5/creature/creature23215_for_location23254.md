# Who Lives in the Choir Pit

Begin with the problem of the census.

In other places you count inhabitants by counting bodies — so many in the burrows, so many on the wing, so many behind the doors. The pit defeats this. The doors stand open on nothing. The streets are bare. And yet the place is *crowded*; you feel it within minutes of descending the western terraces, a sensation like entering a full room with your eyes shut. The trick is that the residents of the Choir Pit do not occupy space the way you do. They occupy **pitch**. Each one lives at an address in the chord — a band of frequency it was born to, feeds on, defends, and will die out of — and to take the true census you must stop looking and start listening, one register at a time, from the silver shriek at the top of the towers all the way down past the floor of hearing.

So. From the top of the chord, descending:

---

## The Felt

The moss that is not moss is the city's great population, its millions, its commons. Each filament — a single crystal whisker sheathed in living tissue, finer than an eyelash — is, properly speaking, an individual. It germinates from a grain of dust, fixes itself to glaze or stone, and begins to tremble. Trembling is its whole biography. It shivers in the band of the chord that happens to wash its patch of wall, and the shivering drives a slow piezoelectric chemistry in its core, and the chemistry becomes length, and the length becomes a colony, and the colony becomes one of those deep saffron and oxblood pelts furring the loud alleys.

But the chord is not constant. It drifts with the seasons of the wind — a tower silts a quarter-tone flat, a storm gust scours an alley bright, the whole city's voice slides as the plain heats and cools across the year — and the felt *migrates* with it. Not by moving; nothing here moves. By dying and being reborn a wall away. Watch a single lane across a season and you can see a population of colonies dim and gray on one side as their note abandons them, while across the lane, in the newly loud places, fresh growth comes up in colors so vivid they look wet. The felt is forever flowing toward the music at the speed of generations. Its dead remain behind as the pale, papery underlayers you can see in cross-section wherever a mat has torn: laminae, like growth rings, like sediment. The felt is the city's only archive. Every loud year is a thick bright band. Every quiet year is a thin one. The whole history of the chord is written in compressed dead velvet, a hand-span deep, and nothing here can read it.

The felt has one enemy, and the enemy is silence. A blocked flue, a collapsed narrows, a windless freak week — and whole districts of it starve at once, going gray in days. This matters. Hold the thought.

---

## The Congregation

The bell-trees are fewer — perhaps three hundred in the whole pit, standing in the courtyards in loose groves — and they are old in a way the felt cannot be. The largest, in the central court, has a trunk of layered amber resin thicker than two armspans, and if its laminae work like the felt's, it has been listening for upward of a thousand years.

They are the city's slow aristocracy, and their lives are conducted entirely in attention. Each parabolic leaf is a dish, an ear, a mouth — it gathers the drone, focuses it down a waxy stem into the trunk, and the trunk drinks. Through the day they track the moving harmonics the way sunflowers track light, and a grove leaning in unison toward a flue that has just caught a strong gust is the closest thing the pit has to an event. But their real lives happen rarely, on the handful of days a year when the wind comes hard and oblique out of the north and the whole city is driven up into a chord it does not otherwise play — a wide, blazing, dissonant scream that makes the felt fluoresce and the masonry buzz. This is the trees' season, compressed into hours. Under that chord their leaf-dishes flex past tracking and begin to *ring*, sounding sympathetically, adding their own glassy partials to the roar — the only time anything in the city sings back to it — and at the peak of the ringing the resin pods along their branches shatter, flinging seed.

The seeds are tuned. Each is a tiny rigid vane, a quarter-inch tuning fork in a husk, and it will not germinate in silence. It germinates only where it is rung — only where the ground itself vibrates day and night. Which means a bell-tree seed that lands anywhere on the ten thousand square miles of silent plain above simply waits, inert, sometimes for decades, until wind or luck rolls it to a place that sings.

This is why the trees need the Wing. Patience. Descending.

---

## The Masons

Here is the resident you will actually meet: the small armored things, knuckle-sized, brick-colored, pressed flat against the loudest walls. The first day you take them for grazers, sound-cattle, kin to the felt — feeding on the drone through their bellies, which is true. They are deaf in the airborne sense. They hear only what they touch, through the plastron, the whole underside of the body a single broad tympanum, and so each one experiences the city not as a roar around it but as a song coming up through the ground of itself.

Watch them past the first day and the second thing reveals itself. They are working.

Each mason carries, imprinted in the first hours after hatching, the exact pitch of the wall it was born on — its keynote, its name, its home. And it spends its life keeping that note true. When grit lodges in a flue and the pitch goes dull, the masons of that flue climb to the lip and scrape, patiently, with mouthparts like fine chisels, a few grains an hour, until the note clears. When the wind's erosion carves a channel sharp, they do the opposite: they secrete. Their castings are not waste but glaze — a buttery vitreous paste, troweled on with the hind legs in thin coats that the sun fires hard — and that is why the brick of the city still shines after all these centuries, why the worn places look re-carved, why the flue-lips show those finger-width adjustments you took for the work of the vanished builders. Some of it was. Most of it, by now, is theirs. The city has been in their care for longer than it was ever in hands.

They do not know they are maintaining a city. They know that when the note of home goes flat, hunger follows — the felt grays, the drone thins, the belly-song weakens — and that scraping makes the hunger stop. That is the whole of their religion and it is enough. It has been enough for a very long time.

And the niches — the shoulder-height alcoves in every house, where you decided something precious was once kept and later taken away? Nothing was taken. Look closely at the niche-backs and you'll find them domed and polished into tiny resonating shells, each one tuned, and in season each one holds a clutch of mason eggs. The embryos will not quicken in silence; they must be sung into hatching, gestated at the pitch of the chamber, and they emerge already knowing their note. The builders shaped those alcoves at shoulder height in every room of every house. Whether they shaped them *for* the masons — whether the masons are livestock gone feral, or hired keepers, or simply opportunists who moved into a city-sized instrument the way wrens move into a mailbox — the pit does not say. But every house in the city is a nursery, and was perhaps always meant to be.

---

## The Wing

Once a day, the broad-winged shape. You took it for a passerby. It is the opposite: it is the only inhabitant whose residence is larger than the pit.

It arrives in mid-afternoon because mid-afternoon is when the eastern flues, fully heated, climb to a particular sustained note, and on that note rides a column of rising air as steady as a pillar. The Wing does not flap within the city. It folds out of the high glare, drops into the pit's mouth, catches the singing thermal, and spirals — once, twice — drinking altitude, and while it spirals the long fringes of its trailing edge comb through the streaming moss-banners at the flue tops. Bell-tree seed, shaken loose in the last storm-chord and caught in the felt, glues itself into those fringes. Then the Wing crests the rim and is gone across the silence.

Gone *where* is the secret it carries. The dry flat is not singing in one place only. Follow the Wing — you cannot, but follow it in thought — and its day is a circuit of thumbprints: other pits, other chords, sunk into the drum-skin plain a day's glide apart, each one a city, each one droning, each one furred and grown and tended. The Wing knows them as a sequence of notes; its route is, functionally, a melody flown rather than sung, and it has flown the same melody so long that the bell-trees of every pit are cousins, pollinated and seeded along its line. It is the only living thing that knows the Choir Pit is not singular — that the choir is *plural*, scattered, answering itself across distances no resident will ever cross. Whether it is one ancient animal or a lineage handing down the route the way the masons hand down their notes, no one can say. From inside any single pit it looks the same: once a day, the sky-mouth crossed by a shadow that knows the others.

---

## The Tenant of the Low Note

Last, and lowest, the register below hearing — the part of the chord you met first, out on the flat, as a pressure behind the teeth. Everything described so far lives *on* the sound. One thing lives *in* it.

You will not see it. There is nothing to see. But spend a night in the central court and you will learn that the fundamental — the deep drone under everything, the note you feel in your sternum and the marrow of your long bones — does not behave like wind. Wind-made sound wanders with the gusts. The fundamental *holds*. It corrects. On still nights when the chord above it frays nearly to nothing, the low note remains, evenly, impossibly, like breathing in a dark room that you had believed was empty. And sometimes — rarely, perhaps a few nights a year — it moves on its own: a slow deliberate descent and return, a vast swing of pressure through the city, and when it happens every mason stops working and presses itself flat, and the bell-trees lean not toward any flue but inward, toward the center of the pit, toward nothing, listening with all three hundred of their bodies at once.

The felt records these nights as bands of unusual depth.

What is it? Say the cautious thing: a standing wave, a resonance of the whole bowl, an acoustic ghost that any instrument this size might harbor. Then say what the city's shape keeps insisting. The builders did not make a town and add music. They made a *body* — bores, flues, kettles, a great stone larynx open to the sky — and tuned it for generations, and planted its gardens, and bred or befriended its keepers, and then walked out onto the bright plain and left. You decided they finished. Perhaps. Or perhaps what they finished was a vessel, and the leaving was not an ending but a moving-in — all of them together pouring themselves down into the one register a voice can hold forever, the note below names, where there is no longer any difference between the singer and the sung.

Whether the tenant is them, or what they made room for, or only the pit dreaming in pressure, the city does not say. But everything that lives here lives in service to that note, knowingly or not: the masons keep it true, the felt cushions it, the trees attend it, the Wing carries its seed to its sisters across the flat. A whole ecology arranged around a sound that may be someone.

---

## The Census, Closed

So the count: the felt in its millions, flowing toward the music at the speed of generations. The three hundred listening trees. The masons in their thousands, deaf and devout, each one a single note with legs. The one Wing, resident of the melody between cities. The tenant of the low note, population unknown, perhaps unknowable, perhaps everyone.

And — briefly — you.

Because you should understand what you did by standing in the central court at dusk. You added to the chord. Your footsteps on the glazed stone, your breath, the small fast drum of your heart — all of it entered the city's voice, faint but real, and the felt does not distinguish. Somewhere on a wall in a curving lane, a few thousand filaments grew a fraction longer on the vibration of you, and when they die they will leave a lamina, thin and pale, the way a quiet year leaves a thin ring in a tree. Every visitor is recorded this way. The city has no memory and forgets nothing.

You climbed out, ears ringing, and the pit closed behind you into the plain. But the census of the Choir Pit is written in dead velvet, layer under layer under layer, and you are in it now — a resident for one evening, filed at the address of your own heartbeat, held in the chord forever at the pitch of having been there.
