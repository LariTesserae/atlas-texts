Begin with the smallest, because in the Understoke seniority runs downward in size, and the oldest tenants are the ones you will never see.

The pools are alive. Not with anything that swims — nothing breaks their blood-warm surfaces but the occasional drip — but the pale crusts that ring them, the little mineral terraces a finger-width high, are not being built by the water alone. The water carries the stone; something living lays it. Films thinner than a coat of paint sheath the terrace rims, and when the fire-thread brightens you can catch, if you crouch and wait through a full eleven-minute pulse, the faintest blush of color in them: a rust-orange here, a sulfured yellow there, colors that exist nowhere else in the room. These are the eaters of iron and old eggs. They came down dissolved in the first water that ever wept through the vault, found a temperature they recognized from some far older world, and settled in. They do the room's only remaining construction, one drop at a time, and they will still be doing it when everything larger has finished. They are not waiting for anything. They are the only residents for whom the Understoke is not slow.

The silverfish are next in age, and they have changed. Their cousins in the upper world are quick, flinching things, all panic and reverse; these are not. Generations in the warm dark have let the hurry drain out of them. They are glossy with the permanent humidity, pale as the underside of a tongue, and they move across the hot plates in long unbroken glides, each one keeping to a circuit it has worn into its habits if not into the steel — along the seam where two boilers fuse, down the cool mortar line, around the rim of the second pool, home. They graze the dark glaze off the bricks, the rust-flour from the seams, the slowly composting fibers of gaskets that failed before anyone living was born. Some of them have eyes and some, you would swear, have given the eyes up, navigating instead by gradients of warmth, reading the room the way the room asks to be read — through the skin. They never touch one another. There is no need. There is enough of everything, forever, as long as nobody hurries.

The pale growths at the steam-seams are residents too, though it takes a particular patience to think of them that way, because they live at a pace even the room finds stately. The young colonies — young meaning younger than the oldest drips — are no more than a fuzz of white-gold thread, each filament leaning into its whisper of vapor with the whole-body attention of a thing that has exactly one idea. They drink the steam and they drink what the steam carries, iron and lime and the ghost of sulfur, and they grow perhaps a finger-width in a decade. The hanging beard at the ceiling elbow is their elder and, in some sense the others appear to honor, their original: every younger colony leans the way the beard leans, sways at the period the beard sways, as if the convection currents were a language and the beard had been speaking it longest. Once in a great while a filament lets go and drifts down through the dark, and where it lands — on a warm plate, at a pool's rim — a new colony may or may not begin. This is the room's entire weather of chance, its whole allotment of risk, and it is spent at a rate of one falling thread per year.

The moths are harder, because the moths may not exactly *live* here, or anywhere. They appear only when the fire-thread brightens, two or three of them, circling the orange seam, and they are gone when it dims — not flown off, not settled, *gone*. The generous explanation is that they roost in the beard, in the deep dark folds of it, and stir only for the light, and that the eye simply loses them when the light goes. The less generous explanation is that they are flakes of mica-bright scale lifted on the pulse of warm air, and that their circling is convection wearing a costume. But watch them through enough cycles and both explanations thin out. They keep station too well. They bank. They take turns at the brightest point of the seam, holding there a wingbeat each, the way moths above ground hold at a flower — and then they yield the place, which convection does not do and hungry things do not do either. Whatever they are, they are the only tenants who still keep faith with light. Everything else down here traded it away long ago — the threads traded it for steam, the silverfish for warmth, the crusts never had it to trade. The moths kept the old religion, shrunk to a single thread of orange eleven minutes wide, and they attend every service.

And then there is the one behind the fifth boiler.

It is tempting to start with a shape, and every shape is wrong in a different way. Hear the sound — that dry, unhurried, shuffling slide, once or twice an hour — and you will think first of something long and low: a salamander grown to the size of the gap it lives in, which is the old story for fire-rooms, and not a bad one. Stay longer and you will revise toward something heavier, something that repositions in sections, fore parts and then hind parts, like a sleeper turning over in stages. Stay longer still and an uncomfortable third possibility presents itself, which is that the sound has a rhythm a person would make — shoulder, hip, settle — and that the last stoker did not climb the bowed stairs when the others did.

The truth is probably none of these and made of all of them. What can be said with confidence is small and strange. It is long; the gap it occupies runs the full length of the fifth boiler and the sound travels the whole of it. It is crusted; whatever its hide was originally, it has spent so long pressed against sweating mineral-scaled iron that it has taken on a rind of the same scale, so that when it shifts, the sound is stone against stone, dry in the wettest room you have ever stood in. It grazes: along the hidden back of the fifth boiler there are patches — you can reach in and find them with your fingers, if your nerve holds — where the scale has been rasped away and the old iron is polished smooth, smoother than the worn floor, smooth as the inside of a shell. It eats the room, in other words, at a rate the room can easily afford, a century's accretion licked back down to metal one patch at a time. And it keeps to the warmth. Its repositionings track the eleven-minute pulse: when the fire-thread brightens, the sliding sound moves toward the shared wall between the fifth boiler and the third, and when the thread dims it relaxes back. It is pressing itself, all that long crusted length, against the warmest iron available, the way a cat finds the one sunlit board of a floor — except that its sun is buried, and pulses, and has done so for longer than anyone has been down to check.

Here is the thing you notice last, and only if you stand in the dark through many cycles, breathing slower than you meant to. The pulse and the creature keep the same time, but it is not clear which of them keeps it. The fire brightens; the sleeper draws toward it. Or: the sleeper draws breath — four times an hour, say, vast slow inhalations through that scaled length — and the draft of its breathing pulls the buried ember bright. The fire-door's hairline gap faces the fifth boiler's hidden wall. There is no other draft in the room. It is entirely possible, standing there in the textured dark, to believe that the Understoke's heart and the Understoke's tenant are keeping each other alive — the fire warming the sleeper, the sleeper's breath fanning the fire — a closed circuit of devotion that asks nothing of the upper world and has not, for generations, received anything from it.

Which brings us to the fire itself, the oldest resident or the second-oldest, depending on whether the crusts in the pools predate it. It lives in the third boiler the way a heart lives in a chest: never seen whole, known entirely by its rhythm. What it eats, it eats by the century — the original charge of its firebox, perhaps, compacted by a hundred years of its own slow heat into something between coal and stone, a private seam it is mining from the inside. It will not be hurried and it cannot be starved on any timescale that matters. It is the landlord, if the room has one. Every other tenant has arranged its life around the fire's warmth, the fire's pulse, the fire's one thread of light, and pays rent in the only currency the Understoke accepts, which is pace. The crusts pay in drops. The threads pay in decades. The silverfish pay in their long unhurried glides, the moths in eleven-minute devotions, the sleeper in breaths four to the hour.

And the visitor pays too, that is the last thing to know about who lives here. Stand at the bottom of the bowed stairs long enough and the rhythm gets into the chest; your breath stretches; you begin, in the smallest and most provisional way, to be a tenant. The Understoke is not greedy about this. It does not keep anyone. It simply makes its standing offer — warmth that has soaked all the way through, a darkness with texture, one thread of light on a reliable schedule, and all the time there is — and lets you climb back up into the frantic thin air of the surface carrying the suspicion, never quite shaken, that you have been a tenant somewhere all along, and have only now learned the address.
