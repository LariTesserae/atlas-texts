Begin with patience, because everyone here is patience. The Calderwald does not select for strength or cleverness or even hardiness, exactly — the warmth takes care of hardiness. What the thin air and the slow stone and the four-hundred-year bloomstones select for is a particular temperament, a willingness to let things take the time they take, and after enough generations that temperament has stopped being a culture and become something closer to an anatomy.

**The Caldfolk**

They came up, originally — everyone agrees on that, though no one dwells on it. Descendants of the survey crews and drill teams and the families that followed them, back when there were geologists, back when there was a *below* worth keeping accounts with. Whatever happened to the lowlands, or didn't, has worn off the edges of memory the way the wind wears the hills. Ask an elder where her people are from and she will gesture at the foundry, which is the same gesture as pointing at the ground.

The body of a Caldfolk adult is built around the two facts of the place: not enough air, more than enough warmth. They are broad through the chest and deliberate through everything else — deep-lunged, slow-pulsed, with a resting heartbeat that would alarm a lowland physician and a walking pace that never varies, uphill or down, loaded or empty. They do not run. Running is a thing children do for a year or two and then grow out of, the way other peoples' children grow out of believing they can fly. There is nothing on the plateau that running would catch and nothing it would escape, and the air punishes it; the Caldfolk gait is instead a kind of continuous arriving, and a traveler who keeps it can cross fifty kilometers of upland and step into the next town no more winded than when she left.

Their skin takes the country's colors. The rock flour works into every crease and pore from infancy, and washing moves it around more than it removes it, so the people are tinted the same oxide palette as the hills — rust at the knuckles and the weathered side of the face, old-bone pale where clothing keeps the dust off, and in the very old a faint mineral sheen at the temples and the backs of the hands, as if the wind had begun to polish them too. Their eyes are good but unhurried. Their hearing is extraordinary, because it has to be: in air this thin every sound arrives small, and the Caldfolk have answered by becoming a quiet people with enormous attention. They speak low, in voices pitched to carry under the foundry's sigh rather than over it, and their language is full of long vowels and almost no hard stops — a speech shaped like breathing, in which a name is traditionally two breaths long and an argument can sound, to an outsider, like two people falling slowly asleep at each other.

And their hands. The hands are the genius of them. A Caldfolk founder can lay a palm flat on a cooling casting and tell you its composition, its hidden flaws, how many hours it has left before it can be lifted — heat-reading, they call it, and it is taught the way other places teach reading, with children's lessons and graded exercises and a kind of literacy shame attached to adults who never quite get it. The whole highland speaks in temperature, after all. The underbreath is steady but not uniform; every vent has its signature, every flue its mood, and a person who can read warmth can navigate the plateau in full darkness by touching outcrops, the way a riverman reads current. The very best of them claim they can feel the warm side of a shadow. Nobody contradicts them. Everybody has, privately, in some dim flue-room with a hand pressed to the dark side of a pillar, felt it too.

**The foundry families**

Every town is a foundry and every foundry is a genealogy. The crucibles, the flue networks, the great minute-per-revolution flywheels — these are not owned so much as *inhabited*, each one attended by a family line that has held it for longer than the family can count, and the family names are flue names: people are called after the part of the breathing building their ancestors tend. The work itself would look, to an industrial eye, like a slow-motion film of industry. A pour is scheduled days ahead and discussed for most of them. A founder may spend an entire shift adjusting one damper by hand, a degree at a time, listening. The corrections they make for the local strangeness — the way castings cool a shade more symmetrical than their molds, as if the metal were being gently tidied as it set — are so old that the molds themselves are now deliberately, traditionally *imperfect*, lopsided by precise inherited amounts, so that the stone can finish them. A mold made true would come out wrong. Every apprentice learns this in the first year and stops finding it strange in the second.

The families share their buildings, as a matter of course, with everything else that lives there. Kiln-martens den in the flues, and the inspection hatches are left loose for them; a marten line and a family line will sometimes have shared a flue for ten human generations, and the founders speak of "our martens" with the flat possessiveness of fact. Slagdoves roost in the stacks by the thousand, and the skimming of a crucible is timed loosely to their hunger — a founder skims, steps back, and stands inside a soft grey snowfall of descending birds with the unbothered air of a man feeding hens. Children are given the job of walking the dross-yards with rakes, spreading the cooling slag thin so the doves can work it, and this is how Caldfolk children first learn the central economic principle of the highland, which is that output is measured not by what you make but by what the country around you can absorb.

The pulse of the foundry gets into the people who live in it. This is not a poetic claim; the Caldfolk treat it as plain physiology. The heat-exchangers sigh on a long period, the flywheels turn on a longer one, and a founder who has worked a building for thirty years breathes in time with it without knowing, and her heart — if you put your ear to her chest, which her grandchildren do, falling asleep — keeps a beat that has settled somewhere into the building's rhythm. Infants born in a foundry town are born already entrained, the midwives say. Visitors who stay a season find their own pulse slowing, their sleep deepening, their fevers — if they brought any — quietly gone, and most of the people now called Caldfolk are descended from visitors who noticed this and looked at the gondola schedule and decided, without urgency, not to be on it.

**The herders**

Out past the towns live the ironback herders, and they are the Caldfolk principle taken to its conclusion. A herd's migration takes a decade to complete one circuit of its vent-fields, which means a herder's profession is not a job but a route — a great slow loop walked across an entire life, hereditary because nothing shorter than a lifetime is long enough to learn it. Herder families are born on the circuit, raised on it, married along it (the loops of neighboring herds cross at known places, generations apart, and the herder lineages keep careful track of when their paths will next touch), and a herder asked her age is as likely to answer in circuits as in years: *two and most of a third*.

They do not drive the animals, because the animals cannot be driven and do not need to be. The herder's actual work is reading: reading the vent-fields ahead for the mineral bloom the herd will want, reading the hides of the ironbacks themselves — those slow crusted oxide backs are a record, a kind of ledger of everywhere the animal has grazed, and an experienced herder runs a palm along a flank the way a founder reads a casting, taking the herd's history through her hand. Herders are the quietest of a quiet people. Weeks pass without speech. They are also, by universal agreement, the best heat-readers alive, the only people who can navigate the deep plateau in the brief violent dust-hazes when even the towns close their hatches, and the towns treat a herder's arrival — once or twice a year, when the circuit swings near — as something between a market day and a visit from a mild, dusty, extremely slow-moving oracle.

**The line-wardens**

The cableways have their people too. Every tower along the long lines has its warden — usually one person, sometimes a couple, occasionally a single old man who has been there so long that the line-runners groom him. The wardens keep the cables greased and the towers true, pass the gondolas hand over hand through the tower sheaves, and serve as the highland's nervous system: all mail moves through their hands, all news rests overnight in their towers, and the warden's log — weather, traffic, the health of the local line-runner troop, anything odd — is the closest thing the Calderwald has to a newspaper, copied and passed gondola to gondola down the line.

It is the loneliest post on the plateau and there has never once been a shortage of volunteers. The wardens are the Caldfolk's solitaries, their contemplatives — people for whom even a foundry town's three hundred souls were a crowd — and the culture honors them with a particular courtesy: a passenger riding the long lines is fed and bedded at every tower, free, forever, on the single condition that they tell the warden everything they have seen since the last one. The line-runners, for their part, have made the wardens part of their ecology. The bright little animals den in the tower-heads, graze the grease the wardens lay down, ride the gondolas like commuters, and have learned the wardens' feeding bells across the whole length of the network — so that a piece of news, or a warden's death, travels the lines in two ways at once: by gondola log, and by some swifter signal passed troop to troop along the singing cable, which arrives first, and which the Caldfolk have learned to read. When the runners on the next tower go still and sit upright in a row facing up the line, you will be getting hard news by morning.

**The map-houses**

In every town there is one building that does not work metal, and it is the warmest one. The map-house holds the town's copy of the bloomstone charts — vent-field by vent-field, the centuries of patient surveying, the branching rose-and-arsenic accretions drifting generation by generation toward their almost-character. The keepers of the map-house are not priests. The Caldfolk are emphatic about this in their unemphatic way; there is no liturgy, no doctrine, nothing worshipped. A mapkeeper is closer to a clerk of something very large: they walk the local fields once a year with calipers and a steady hand, they add the line or two of change that a year produces, they exchange fair copies with other towns by gondola, and they do not speculate aloud. That last part is nearly the only taboo the culture has. You may think anything you like about what the highland is spelling. You write down only what it has spelled.

The keepers will tell you, if pressed gently and over several evenings, the few things the maps make unarguable. That the drift is general — every field, all of them, the whole continent of stone leaning the same way. That the character, or the attempt at one, is getting *less wrong*: the variance between fields narrows by a measurable hair each century, as if drafts were converging on a fair copy. And one more thing, which the keepers mention last and most carefully: that the rate has changed. Slightly. Since the towns. Since the foundries began breathing alongside the vents, since the doves and the martens and the people settled into the heat — the spelling has gone a little faster, the way handwriting steadies when someone is finally watching.

The keepers do not say what they think this means. But it is a map-house custom, very old, that every keeper at the end of life adds one freehand mark in the margin of the master chart — their own guess at the finished character, drawn once, never discussed — and the margins of the oldest charts are crowded with these guesses, hundreds of hands across hundreds of years, and the guesses, too, are converging.

**Their quiet, their dead, their long answer**

Caldfolk society has almost no machinery for conflict, because the place pre-empts it. The towns are spaced two and three days apart for thermal reasons, but the spacing turns out to be social genius: no neighbor is close enough to grate. Disputes inside a town are settled by the institution everyone calls *the long answer* — the parties state their cases, briefly, in front of the flywheel pit, and then are required to wait one full revolution, a slow minute of mandated silence with the great wheel turning, before either may reply. Most disputes do not survive the first revolution. The stubborn ones get a second. There is no recorded case that needed a fourth.

Their dead they give back, and the giving back is the nearest thing they have to a sacrament. A Caldfolk body does not cool the way bodies cool elsewhere; everyone knows this, no one explains it. An elder dies and stays warm — a day, two days, sometimes most of a week, the warmth holding steady at the temperature of the stone, at the temperature of a sleeping animal, as if something were taking its time about a handover. The family sits with the body until the warmth goes, and the going is unmistakable, a settling you can feel through your palm, and only then is the person considered fully dead. Then they are carried out to the nearest vent-field and laid in one of the smooth wind-cut hollows — the *sleeping hollows*, used for this since before any surviving record — where the pewter vent-mats, over a season, quietly take them into the base of everything. The mats feed the doves; the doves seed the dross-yards; the herders say the ironbacks visit old sleeping hollows on their circuits and stand there longer than grazing explains. Caldfolk do not find this morbid. They find it tidy. *Warmth in, warmth on,* the burial words go, and they are the entire ceremony.

**The slow suspicion**

Here is the thing about the Caldfolk that visitors take not weeks but years to notice, and that the Caldfolk themselves will neither confirm nor deny, because confirming and denying are both faster than they like to move.

They fit too well. Not in the way of any adapted people — more than that, *finished* in the way of those castings that come out truer than their molds. Their pulses settle to the foundries; the foundries settled, long ago, to the vents; the vents are the underbreath; the underbreath has the steadiness of a metabolism. Their children are born few, but they are born easily, and they are born already breathing in the local rhythm. Old founders, the ones with the mineral sheen coming up at the temples, sleep pressed flat against warm stone like martens and report — when they report anything — the same dream: a warm dark, a shape almost made, the same shape, tried again. More than one has woken with a finger still moving, tracing it on the rock.

The Caldfolk know the question this raises. They knew it before any visitor thought to ask it, and they have given it the long answer — the longest on record, several centuries of flywheel revolutions and counting, with no reply yet felt to be necessary. Whether they are a people who settled on a warm highland, or the highland's newest and quickest accretion — its rose-and-arsenic branches grown ambulatory, its character attempted this time in something that can hold a pen — is, they would tell you, a distinction the place itself has shown no urgency about. The maps get their line or two a year. The guesses in the margins converge. The mold was made lopsided so the stone could finish it.

**Evening**

In the evening the Caldfolk eat together, family lines along the warm walls, in rooms lit by the foundry's banked interior glow. The talk is low and slow and mostly about heat: the mood of a flue, the temper of a pour, a herder sighted on the western circuit, runners sitting strange on the south line. A child falls asleep against a grandmother whose heartbeat keeps the building's time. Out in the stacks the slagdoves settle with their last grey sigh, and the kiln-martens come out into the cooling flues to do their rounds, and somewhere a mapkeeper, working late by the warmth of the master chart, lays down her calipers and presses her palm flat to the stone floor — not measuring anything, just the gesture every Caldfolk makes a dozen times a day without thinking, the way other peoples touch wood or glance at the sky.

The stone is warm. The stone is always warm. It has the temperature of a sleeping animal, and the people sleeping on it have the temperature of the stone, and underfoot, patient as ever, whatever has been holding its heat all this time goes on holding them in it — its herds, its doves, its slow bright runners on the singing lines, and its quiet broad-chested people, who may be its tenants or may be its handwriting, and who have stopped arguing about anything.
