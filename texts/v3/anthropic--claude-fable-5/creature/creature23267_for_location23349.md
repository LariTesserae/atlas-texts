**Who lives at the Works**

Start with the staff, because the staff will let you start with them. The deeper residents take longer.

**The glass-tenders**

The shrimp first, since they are the ones you will meet, in the sense that being inspected counts as meeting. There are perhaps two hundred of them, divided — and this is your word, not theirs, though watch them long enough and you will defend it — into gangs of five or six, each gang assigned to a run of gauges. They are translucent down to the machinery: you can watch a meal travel through one like a bead down a wire. Their legs end in pads finer than any chamois, and they work the gauge glass in overlapping spirals, never the same spiral twice, each pass ending with a pause at the rim of the dial as if signing off.

They do not graze anywhere else. There is algae on every surface in Lowmost, fields of it, and the shrimp leave all of it alone except the thin film that tries, perpetually, to come between the needles and anyone who might read them. This is not foraging. Foraging goes where the food is. This is a beat, walked.

What they get out of it is unclear, which is to say: it is exactly as clear as what anyone got out of clocking in here, ever. They eat the film. The film grows back. The dials stay legible. If you put your hand near a gauge they do not scatter — they *line the rim*, evenly spaced, and wait, with the perfect unhostile attention of someone who has been told to admit no one but has never actually had to refuse anybody. Take your hand away and the spirals resume on the next pass, your interruption absorbed into the rhythm like a missed beat into a long song. There are tiny tracks in the mineral bloom below each gauge, generations of tracks, a kind of stratigraphy of diligence. The shrimp live about a year. The job has been held, continuously, by no one in particular, for a very long time.

**The keeper of Foundry Lane**

The conger — if it is a conger; it is conger-shaped the way the town is town-shaped, which is to say it has settled into the form the available spaces allow — lives in the pipe runs, and the pipe runs are extensive, and so it would be more accurate to say the conger lives in *the idea of being just out of sight*. You will see, over a long visit, perhaps thirty feet of tail in five-foot installments, withdrawing. Each withdrawal is unhurried. It is not fleeing you. It is finishing a task in one place and proceeding to a task in another, and you happen to be there for the seam between the two.

It is pale, root-pale, the pallor of a thing grown in the dark and untroubled by the fact, and old — the skin near the tail has the dull patina of handled brass. It patrols. That is the only verb that fits. Other fish do not enter the pipe runs, and you will not find out how that has been arranged, but the small bones that occasionally drift from a pipe mouth, picked clean and weed-tarnished, suggest the arrangement is enforced rather than merely understood.

Once — hold still long enough in the right place and this becomes a once that happens to you — you may see its head. It will come a third of the way out of a flanged junction near the foundry wall, and it will look at you with eyes like two of the gauges: round, dim-glowing, faintly green, registering. Not threat. Not curiosity. *Reading.* The pressure of you, taken; the needle of its attention creeping a degree; the head withdrawing. You have been measured, and the rules apply: you may not be taken while you are still being measured, and you are, now, always being measured. This may be the safest you will ever be anywhere.

**The administration**

The plants run Lowmost, the place tells you so itself, but live with that fact a while and it deepens. The ribbon-weeds are not a population; they are a *bureau*. They are all combed one way by a current too slow to feel, which means they are reporting the current, all day, every day — two hundred thousand soft green needles indicating the same reading. The mosses on the engines are not upholstery; they are gaskets, custodians of warmth, holding the heat against the iron the way a body holds heat against its organs. They flex with the hour-long stroke. They have learned the stroke. Cut a sample of moss from the engine hall — you should not, but suppose — and carry it to a still wall on the far side of town, and for some days it will continue to flex, faintly, on the hour, like a worker who wakes at five for years after retiring.

The pale ferns in the terrace windows are the strangest of the green staff because their work is the most domestic. They grow *inward*, toward the dark rooms, against every gradient a plant should obey. They line the hallways. They cradle the rows of clogs, one soft anemone of growth per clog, every clog, none missed. They have furred the kettle on its hob in a green that is — go close, the dim rewards going close — finer and denser and more carefully figured than the green anywhere else in the house. Where the people touched things most, the ferns attend hardest. Whether this is because human places are richer in something the ferns can eat, or because the ferns have inherited, along with the town, the town's instinct for tending, is the kind of question Lowmost lets you keep.

**The commuters**

The grey fish overhead do not live here and that is their entire role, and it is a real role, and the town would be poorer without it. They cross between the chimneys at the same height every time, in the better light, in loose formation, and they never come down, and their never-coming-down is a daily renewed decision — you can see it in the way the school flexes slightly downward over the engine hall, every crossing, a dip of consideration, before flattening out and proceeding. Something below interests them and something below has been declined. They are the only inhabitants of the region who have looked at Lowmost and said no, and the town keeps them the way an old firm keeps the memory of the one man who quit: as proof that staying is a choice.

**The engine**

Now the deeper residents, and the engine is the threshold between staff and resident, because the engine is both.

It lives in the main hall under its moss, and it is alive in the only way that matters down here, which is that it has habits and the habits are its own. One revolution an hour. No boiler pressure that any surviving gauge can account for, no fuel, no firing — and yet the piston eases out, and the crosshead travels its slide, and the flywheel, fourteen feet of moss-greened iron, commits its slow majestic nod, and somewhere the warm plumes twitch in acknowledgment. It is not running *down*. A thing running down decelerates. The engine's hour has been an hour for as long as the shrimp's tracks go back. It found this speed and *kept* it, the way an old man finds the pace at which he can walk forever.

What does it drive? Follow the shafting and you find the shafting furred and the bearings bloomed and the belts long dissolved, and the honest answer is: it drives the town's sense of itself. Everything in Lowmost is calibrated to the stroke. The moss flexes to it. The gauges note it. The plumes pulse with it. The shrimp time their dial-rim pauses, you will eventually swear, to its dead centers. Take the engine away and the town would not stop — nothing here stops — but it would lose its *meter*, and a place this quiet is held together by meter or by nothing.

The engine is, in other words, the foreman. It does no work in the old sense. It demonstrates the pace.

**The thing in the hearths**

Below the foreman, the owner. Or the tenant from before there were owners — the deed gets unclear this far down.

The foundry's deep hearths hold heat, *or something that behaves like heat*, and that hedge in the town's own account of itself is the most honest thing in Lowmost. Heat does not last a century underwater. Heat does not come in plumes that rise like glass pillars in fixed places, year on year, never wandering. Heat does not smell of iron and yeast — of metal and *rising*, of the forge and the proving-drawer, of the two warmths a mill town ever knew. And heat does not make the water *twitch with something that is not quite current* at the completion of each stroke, like a sleeper's hand answering a voice in a dream.

Whatever lies in the hearths was fed for a hundred years. Coal, coke, three shifts a day, the foundry never cold from one decade's end to the next, because letting the hearths die meant days of relighting and the owners would not pay for days. So the warmth was never once allowed to learn what ending felt like. And things that are never allowed to end can forget that ending is what they are for.

It is not a creature, probably. It has no edges that the conger or the shrimp or the weeds defer to, no den, no body — only the plumes, which are less limbs than *exhalations*. The fat silver bubbles that wobble up the chimneys every few minutes are its breath in the most literal sense the word will bear down here: gas from far inside, regular, untroubled, the respiration of something deeply asleep and dreaming of work. The deep irregular *tonk* from the foundry is it settling, the way a banked fire settles, except that it has been settling for longer than fires settle, and the intervals, if you stayed long enough to chart them — the shrimp may have; consult their ledger if you can read it — are not cooling intervals. They do not lengthen. It is not a pot cooling. It is a pot *holding*, at exactly the temperature required to keep the gardens riotous and the engine limber and the town warm enough to be tended.

It pays, in other words, the wages. Warmth is the only currency in Lowmost and every inhabitant from the moss to the keeper of Foundry Lane draws a salary from the hearths, and the hearths draw on something that should have been spent generations ago and was not, and the kindest reading — the town invites kind readings — is gratitude. It was fed for a hundred years. It is feeding back. The unkinder reading is only this: that the rule about the plumes exists, and rules in Lowmost are not decorative, and what twitches in the warm water when the stroke completes is paying attention, and you are advised not to be the thing it pays attention to. It is asleep. The whole settlement is arranged, perhaps, around the desirability of its remaining so. The engine keeps the hour. The hour keeps the sleep.

**The Shift**

Last, the resident you will not see, because it has no body to see and never did, even when the town was dry.

The people are gone — properly gone, gone in the gentle total way the dissolved ink on the time-cards is gone, every recorded hour returned letter by letter to the general water. There are no ghosts in Lowmost, no faces at the furred windows, no footsteps. What remained when the people left was not them. It was what they *made between them*, daily, for a hundred years: the Shift. The habit of the place. The shape work makes when it is done by many hands in the same hollow at the same hours for generations, which is a real shape, with weight and momentum, and which — this is the thing Lowmost knows that the dry world does not — does not need the hands once it is old enough.

The Shift is what the shrimp are doing. It is what the ferns are doing in the hallways with the clogs. It is the conger's beat and the engine's hour and the gauges' patient creep, none of which any of those inhabitants invented; each of them arrived in Lowmost — drifted in, spored in, hatched in the warm water — and found a position open, and filled it. The town hires. It has always hired. It hired farm boys off the hill country once and trained them to the dials; now it hires shrimp, and trains them to the dials, and the dials do not mind the change of staff because the dials never knew the staff by name to begin with.

This is the true inhabitant, if the word has to settle somewhere: a workforce-shaped patience, distributed through brick and weed and gland and gauge, that received no order to stop and has filled the vacancy of orders with the one order it can generate itself — *continue gently*. Everything that lives in Lowmost lives inside it the way the inhabitants of any company town lived inside the company: fed by it, paced by it, measured by it, and granted, in exchange, the deep unfashionable comfort of a position.

You may feel it begin to consider you, if you stay. It is not a haunting. It is an interview. The shrimp have already cleaned a gauge in your vicinity rather more often than the algae strictly requires. The conger has taken your pressure. The light is the light of a room at four in the afternoon, and somewhere under the moss the flywheel is coming up on dead center, and the whole town is about to lean into the stroke together — and there is a moment, just there, every hour, when the leaning has room in it for one more.

Take nothing that is still being measured. And before you swim up through the brown-gold and the olive and the green, be certain — be quite certain — that nothing down there has started measuring you in earnest.
