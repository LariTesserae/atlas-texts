# The People of the Roar

They are visible before they are anything else. That is the first thing to understand about them: in a place where sound has been taken entirely for the basin's own use, the inhabitants have become creatures of light, posture, and hands. You see them long before you could ever have heard them — figures on the causeways at dusk, lamp at the hip, conversing across forty meters of fog with an eloquence you can watch but not yet read. Newcomers describe the sensation of arriving among them as walking into a crowded room where everyone is speaking and no one is making a sound — until you remember, abruptly, that everyone is making a sound, the same sound, all the time, and that it belongs to none of them.

They call themselves by a hand-sign: the fingers of one hand rising and spreading slowly off the flat of the other, the way steam leaves stone. Travelers' journals have rendered it as *the Vented*, *the Steam-Folk*, *the Risen*, and every one of these is wrong in a slightly different way. The sign does not exactly mean people who live among the vents. It means something closer to *that which the basin gives off* — as if they were classifying themselves among the vapors, one more thing the mountain breathes out.

## The Body

The basin works on its people the way it works on its stone: slowly, evenly, without malice, and without pause.

Nearly everyone here is born hearing. Nearly everyone here goes deaf — not suddenly, and not completely, but in a pattern as predictable as erosion. The roar occupies a broad band of frequencies, and over decades it carves that band out of the ear the way runoff carves a channel out of soft rock. By thirty, a basin-dweller has a notch worn into their hearing precisely where the roar lives, which produces the local paradox that the longer you live in the noise, the less of it you receive. The old say the basin grows quieter as you age, and they say it with the satisfaction of people describing a debt being honorably repaid: the basin takes your hearing in exactly the shape of itself, and leaves the rest.

What it leaves, they have cultivated. Their high-frequency hearing — the band above the roar — often survives into great age, which is why the small sounds of the sealed rooms, the click of porcelain, the hiss of a lamp, can move them nearly to tears. And below hearing entirely, they have developed what they consider the basin's true sense: vibration. They read the ground through their feet the way other people read weather through a window. Every basin-dweller can stand still on a causeway and tell you whether the third-hour tram has left its winding-house, whether Hall Six's great flywheel is loaded or freewheeling, whether the fen crust to the north has been thinned by a wet week. Children are taught to feel before they are taught to sign. The first lesson, given barefoot on the flagstones of a nursery floor, is the same in every district: *the stone is talking; be polite.*

They are not large people, but they are extraordinarily *legible* people. Generations of conversing at distance have selected for and trained an expressiveness of face and frame that strangers find almost overwhelming at close range, like standing too near a lighthouse. A basin-dweller's resting face is a kept face, deliberately quiet, for the same reason their public voices are kept quiet — broadcasting is easy here, and so discretion has become the higher art. They sign small when they are being intimate. They sign from the wrist, hands low, close to the body, the visual equivalent of a murmur. To sign large — full-arm, above the shoulders — is to shout, and shouting carries here farther than any voice could: a full-armed argument on a rooftop can be read from three districts away, and will be.

## The Hands

The hand-language is the oldest thing they own — older than the collars, older, some of its forms suggest, than the causeways. It is a complete language with a literature, though the literature is performed rather than shelved: epic recitations given on winter nights from lamplit balconies, the audience standing in the street below in the rain-warm dark, the roar pressing down on everyone equally, the story riding above it untouched. Their poetry exploits what sound-poetry cannot — simultaneity. A skilled signer can carry two clauses at once, one in each hand, and the great performances braid them: the left hand telling what happened, the right hand telling what was hoped, the face carrying what was true.

Each district signs with an accent. The eastern ridges, where the fen crossings are longest, sign broad and slow — distance-speech, built to survive fog. The kiln districts sign fast and close, a language evolved in the heat-shimmer of the firing floors where large movement is dangerous. There are jokes that only work in one accent. There is a famous untranslatable courtship form, native to the second district, in which a question is asked with the dominant hand while the other hand simultaneously offers the asker's preferred answer, allowing the recipient to refuse the question and accept the answer, or the reverse, or — devastatingly — neither.

Names are signs, given twice. A child receives a milk-name from its parents, small and private, signed close to the chest. The adult name comes later and is not chosen but *noticed* — it is whatever sign the community finds itself already using for you by the time you are grown, distilled from your gait, your trade, your habits, some incident you would perhaps rather have been allowed to forget. The people regard this as the only honest naming system: you are called what you have demonstrably been. It makes them, as a population, mildly and permanently careful, in the way of people who know their biography is being drafted by everyone who watches them cross a street.

## The Voice

They do have voices. This is the thing outsiders most often get wrong.

The voice, here, is an indoor organ — a sealed-room organ, an under-the-blanket organ. It is used for perhaps an hour a day, and it has acquired the status that handwritten letters or heirloom silver hold elsewhere: a medium reserved for what is precious precisely because it is impractical. Families keep their voices the way families keep recipes. Children learn to speak in the padded instrument-rooms, taught by grandparents whose own speech has drifted, over a deafening lifetime, into pronunciations no outside ear would parse — a whole submerged dialect of softened consonants and felt vowels, because much of their speech is calibrated not to the ear but to the bones.

For there is a third channel, between hand and voice, and it is the most intimate they have: touch-speech. Forehead to forehead, or jaw resting on a shoulder, palm flat on a back — they speak into each other's skeletons, the words arriving as vibration, half-heard, half-felt. Couples talk this way in the roar, on open causeways, in full public view, with total privacy. The very old, whose ears have given the basin nearly everything, live in this channel almost entirely. To be invited to put your hand on a basin-dweller's sternum while they speak is the deepest courtesy the culture extends, and it is extended rarely. They have a sign for it, the sign that also means *to trust*: one hand laid flat over the other's pulse.

## The Trades

Work here has sorted the population into temperaments as much as professions.

The **collar-tenders** are the basin's physicians. They maintain the great vent-collars and the turbine halls, and they do it almost entirely by palpation — a tender walks the trembling length of a penstock with one bare palm on the iron, reading the machine's pulse, and can tell a loose stay-bolt from a scale blockage from forty meters of pipe away. Apprenticeship takes nine years, most of it spent learning to feel, and the masters are spoken of in the language of inheritance: it is said of a great tender that *she has the third hall in her hands*, and when she dies, her apprentices will press their palms to the same iron and try to find what she left there.

The **winders** keep the trams, and therefore the schedule, and therefore — the city would say — the time. They are the closest thing the basin has to a priesthood, a hereditary corps of maybe two hundred who tend the winding-houses, the cables, and the stone-carved timetables. Their authority derives from an arrangement everyone understands: in a city where no warning can be heard, safety is synchrony. You are not safe because you are alert; you are safe because the tram is *where the schedule says it is*, always, and so are you. The winders' oath, signed at investiture, translates roughly as: *I will be boring forever.* They mean it as a vow of love.

The **kiln families** run the long firings, dynasties organized around processes that outlast individual attention spans — a single porcelain cycle can run five weeks, and the famous vapor-cured ware must rest in the open basin air for a season, drinking the mineral fog, before it is finished. Kiln people are the city's philosophers of patience, and its insomniacs: someone in every kiln family is always awake, on a rotation that has not been interrupted, in some houses, for two hundred years. They keep the only continuous written records in the basin, firing logs going back centuries, and buried in those logs — temperatures, durations, glaze outcomes — is, incidentally, the best independent evidence for the quarter-tone drop, because the kilns draw their draft from the basin's breath, and the breath has measurably, fractionally, slowed.

The **flat-walkers** harvest the kettle-leaf and know the trusted stone — the paths across the scalded flats where the crust will bear weight. The routes are not mapped; they are *walked*, transmitted body to body, an elder crossing with a youth's hands on their shoulders and the youth's feet falling exactly into their fading footprints. Flat-walkers are few, weathered, and notably silent even by local standards. They are the only people who work daily inside the spray-radius of the vents, closer to the roar's sources than anyone, and the culture treats them with the specific respect owed to people who have stood near the mouth.

And there are the **surveyors of quiet** — the node-cartographers — who chart the drift of the still points. Their archive is the city's true treasure: centuries of maps fired onto ceramic plates, each plate a season, stacked in a dry vault, showing the slow migration of silence across the basin floor like the tracks of grazing animals. They will show the plates to anyone. They will discuss methodology indefinitely. Asked what the drift *means*, they make the small two-handed sign that is the local equivalent of a shrug performed in a cathedral.

## The Children and the Dove-Born

Children here are raised loose and watched constantly — the two facts are one fact. In a city where no child can be called home, every adult within sight of a child is, by ancient and unargued custom, responsible for that child, and the children grow up inside a lattice of distant attentive eyes, freer in their movements than children almost anywhere, and never once unobserved. They play visual games, generations deep: lamp-tag across the fens at dusk, signed rhymes with rules of escalating speed, a thing done with the white moths that adults pretend not to remember doing.

Some children are born deaf. Elsewhere this is a fact with consequences; here it is a fact almost without them — the deaf-born child learns the same language, walks the same stone, feels the same trams through the same feet. But the culture has noticed something about them, and named them for it: they are called *dove-born*, after the city's emblem, and they are regarded with a particular, careful interest. Because the dove-born, alone among the basin's people, have never received the roar at all — and when they sit in the still points, where the very old go to listen, they report, with the mild firmness of people stating the obvious, that there is nothing there. The old hear cadence. The dove-born feel warm stone and faint trembling and say so. The city holds both reports without choosing between them. It has been holding them for a very long time. Some say the dove-born are the only ones telling the truth; some say they are the only ones the basin cannot reach; the dove-born themselves, asked, tend to make the cathedral-shrug and go back to whatever they were doing, which the rest of the population finds either reassuring or unbearable depending on the hour of the night.

## The Old Ones

Age here has a direction: inward and downward, toward the still points.

A basin-dweller's life is sometimes described, by basin-dwellers, as a long approach. You spend your youth in the districts, your working life on the ridges and causeways, and your age — if you are granted enough of it — increasingly out on the floor, in the quiet nodes, sitting. The sitting is not retirement and it is not worship and the people decline, politely and absolutely, to say what it is. The old walk out along the trusted paths, settle onto stones polished by generations of predecessors, and listen — to the residue, the cancelled remainder, the place where forty thunders subtract one another down to a hum with the cadence of speech.

They do not report what they hear. This is not secrecy; the consensus among those who have tried to explain is that it is not *reportable* — that what resolves in the still points is the shape of saying without the said, syntax without words, and that sitting with it for years changes a person in ways the changed cannot inventory. What outsiders can observe is this: the very old of this city are unusually unafraid, unusually unhurried, and unusually kind, in the impersonal way of people who have stopped taking the noise of their own lives quite so seriously. And it is documented — drily, in the surveyors' marginalia — that the dying old sometimes sign in their sleep, fluently, in an accent belonging to no district, hands moving through forms the watchers cannot read and the dove-born say they have never seen.

When death comes, the body is carried to one of the roar-claimed buildings — the abandoned structures that the drifting nodes left behind, the ones standing now in full sound, their doors sealed with ceramic plugs. One plug is opened. The dead are given to the loudest rooms in the city, the places no living person can bear, and the door is sealed again. Then the household walks out to a still point and the dead person's name-sign is made there once, slowly, completely — given to the quiet as the body was given to the roar — and is not made again for a generation. They do not consider this an ending. The basin, they point out, has never once been heard to finish a sentence. Why should they?

## What Else Lives Here

The doves, the geckos, the moths — these are not wildlife to the inhabitants; they are fellow citizens, junior partners in the basin's single shared solution. The doves especially. Every district keeps cotes, and the birds wheel between the ridges on routes as fixed as the trams', and no one has ever seriously used them to carry messages, because the city's feeling about the doves is not utilitarian. The doves solved the basin *first* — gave up the doomed sense before any person thought to stop relying on it — and the people regard them the way one regards a teacher who taught by example and never said a word. Feeding the doves is the only act in the city that everyone, from every district, in every accent, signs the same way.

And then there is the resident the inhabitants will not call a resident: whatever it is that the still points are the quiet of. The cautious position — the surveyors' position — is that nothing lives there; that the cadence is interference, the pattern-hunger of the human ear given forty thunders and a curved stone wall to work with. The other position is never stated, only built: a city whose people map silence like farmland, retire into listening as a life stage, return their names to the quiet and their bodies to the roar, and have arranged their entire civilization — their schedules, their hands, their patience, their kilns geared down to geological time — into something that looks, from the rim at dusk, less like a settlement than like an instrument.

An ear, perhaps. Grown slowly, on the floor of the bowl, out of whatever arrived and stayed. The basin began a sentence long before anyone came. What lives here is the part of the basin that is trying — across generations, at a quarter-tone of progress per lifetime, with lamplit hands and unhurried dread and a strange settled gladness — to hear it.
