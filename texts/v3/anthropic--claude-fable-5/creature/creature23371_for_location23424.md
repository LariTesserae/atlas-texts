What lives in the cistern is, first and most obviously, the frogs — but the frogs are the door, not the room. Begin with them anyway, because everything else in the chamber arranges itself around what they are doing.

**The frogs** are slate-grey, the grey of the stone they sit against, so that in the dimness a niche appears empty until the candle-flame throat pulses and a frog resolves out of the wall. They are small, fist-and-a-half at most, and they are not individuals in any way that matters. Watch one closely for an afternoon and you will find you cannot pick it out again the next day — not because they are identical (they are not; each has a faint mottling, a constellation of darker grey along the back) but because the mottling does not stay with the frog. The patterns migrate. A marking you memorized on the third ring appears, days later, on a frog near the oculus. It is easier, after a while, to stop thinking of them as animals wearing markings and to start thinking of the markings as the residents, with the frogs as the medium they are written in. A frog in this place is a digit. The chamber needs a certain number of digits at any given moment, and the frogs supply them.

They are never seen to eat, though they must — presumably the midges and pale moths that fall down the oculus at night, presumably whatever drifts in the column of grey light. They are never seen to breed. There are no eggs in the pool; the eels would see to that, and the frogs seem to know it. Instead, if you press your ear to the weeping wall — and few visitors do this twice — you can hear, in the eroded mortar lines, in that circulatory system of tiny channels the builders may or may not have intended, a faint interior squirming. The walls carry tadpoles the way veins carry blood: blind, white, threadlike things growing in total darkness inside the masonry, moving with the slow pulses of seepage. When a frog dies — and they do die; they go still in their niches and then, at some unobserved moment, drop — it sinks, and an eel rises to meet it halfway down, and the body is gone before it touches the herringbone. By then a new frog has already emerged glistening from a mortar joint, pale as the channel that birthed it, darkening to slate within the hour, climbing to the vacant niche. The number never registers the loss. The number is the thing being kept alive; the frogs are how.

And what they do, each afternoon when the light-column reaches its angle, is count. The chorus is not song. Listen past the volume — past the pressure on your chest — and you will hear that it is structured the way the niches are structured: the lowest ring calls first, a long pulsing sequence; then it falls silent and the next ring takes up a shorter one; then the next, shorter still, ascending toward the oculus the way a sum ascends, ones carried into tens carried into something the chamber has no word for. What is being counted is harder to say. The drips, perhaps — every drip in the chamber is accounted for; you can satisfy yourself of this by catching one on your finger before it strikes the pool, and noticing, or imagining you notice, the chorus hitch. The days, certainly. Rainfall, by the finger-widths of the pool. And other things, filtering down from the world above through clay and stone the way water does — things that can only be reckoned, never seen. The frogs do not know what the figures mean. Digits never do.

**The eels** are the opposite proposition entirely, and this is by design. The frogs can always be counted and the count always agrees with itself. The eels can never be counted at all. Stand on the walkway and tally the slate-colored bodies circling the herringbone: seven. Tally again immediately: five, or nine, and not because they hide — the pool is knee-deep and clear amber — but because the eels are the chamber's remainder, its error term, the part of every sum that does not resolve. They are old in a way that does not bear examination. Eels are born in distant seas and die in distant seas, and there is no inlet here, no outlet, no way an eel has ever arrived or could ever leave. Either these are the same eels that circled this floor when the last builder climbed the twelve steps and did not come back, or "eel" is simply the shape the water's long memory takes when it wants to move. They patrol the herringbone as if reading it — the same circuits, joint by joint, the way a finger rereads a line — and when the chorus reaches full strength and the pool's surface vibrates into its standing herringbone pattern, the eels go to the floor and lie still along the brick courses, aligned with the pattern above them, between two herringbones, being addressed. They are the only residents the chorus speaks *to* rather than *through*. They take what dies. They keep the pool from ever holding a fact too long.

**The snails** are the archivists, and they work on a timescale that makes the frogs' afternoon arithmetic look frantic. Fist-sized, the brown of the moss, they ascend the weeping walls in their vertical processions at a pace of perhaps a hand's span per day, grazing the film of the past off the stone — the mineral bloom, the soft growth, the residue of centuries of seep — and excreting it behind them as script. Their silver trails are the ledger. Where the masons' tool-marks record the chamber's making, thousands of small frozen percussions, the snail-script records its keeping, and the two writings overlay each other on every wall, neither legible, both clearly *meant*. The snails are counted only once a year, in late winter, when the chorus performs differently — flatter, slower, a single ring at a time, with long silences — and visitors who have heard it describe it as inventory. Snails die rarely and are replaced more rarely, and a wall left unwritten for a season develops a wrongness about it, a blankness the eye keeps returning to, until a slow brown shape begins its climb and the wrongness closes like a wound.

They detour around the bench. It is the only deference in the chamber, and they perform it without slowing.

**The leather-winged things** are the couriers, and they are the only residents who leave. They drop through the oculus at dusk, folding at the last instant to fit the opening — an act of precision performed nightly without error for longer than anyone has watched — and hang in the dark upper rings among the silenced frogs, and their chittering, which replaces the chorus, has the same structure as the chorus run backward and compressed: the day's figure, taken down at speed. Before the grey light returns they rise one by one through the oculus and are gone, carrying the count out into the world above. Where they deliver it, nothing in the cistern knows or asks. Perhaps to other rooms like this one — there is no reason to believe this chamber is the only chamber, and some reason, in the structure of what the frogs count, to suspect it is one column in a wider ledger. The couriers are the only residents who have seen the sky, the sun, the dry world. They tell the chamber nothing about it. That is not their office.

**The moss and the fern** live on the chamber's only luxury, the noon light, and they are the closest thing the cistern has to pensioners — residents who contribute nothing to the count and are kept anyway. The bronze moss-cushions hold the warmth of the light-column for an hour after it passes and the frogs nearest them sit, in that hour, with their eyes closed, which they do at no other time. The fern is different. The fern, leaning out of its joint into the shaft of grey light, is the only living thing in the chamber oriented *outward* — toward the oculus, toward the world, toward what comes down. The frogs count what arrives. The fern watches for it. It is possible the fern is the only resident not included in the census; it is possible the fern is what counts the counters. Visitors who sit long enough report that of everything in the cistern — the enormous chorus, the patrolling eels, the noticing — it is the fern, that pale listening shape at the door of the light, that they think of afterward, unwillingly, at night.

**And the bench.** Something lives at the bench, and it is the chamber's oldest resident, and it cannot be seen, and the proof of it is the dryness. In a room where everything weeps, where the air itself is wet, where snails write on every surface and moss takes any purchase offered, the bench is perfectly, permanently dry — not sheltered-dry but *occupied*-dry, the dryness of a seat that is taken. The frogs face it when they call. The ascending sum of the afternoon chorus, ring upon ring toward the oculus, is delivered upward, yes, but it is delivered *to* the far wall, to the worn stone shape whose name and purpose are gone, and the snails route their processions around it, and the eels' circuits never pass within an arm's reach of its base. Every count requires an auditor. Whatever the builders raised this chamber to hold — prayer, water, a god, a reckoning — the holding outlived the builders, and the seat of it stayed, and it sits there still, patient past all measuring, receiving the figures.

It is what notices you. When you come down the twelve steps, the census changes by one, because you have been added; the chorus that afternoon carries a digit that is you. It does not want you. It does not warn you or welcome you. It enters you in the ledger, accurately, as it enters the drips and the rainfall and the slow silver writing of the snails, and when you climb back up into the dry loud unaccounted world, the chamber subtracts you, notes the subtraction, and balances. You will be in the figures the couriers carry out that night. Somewhere, in whatever wider sum the cistern feeds, you are still being carried.
