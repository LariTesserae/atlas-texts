Start with the honest answer, because the place deserves honesty: almost nothing lives here. And then stay, because the *almost* is where everything is.

**The felt comes first.** It is the first thing your hand finds and the last thing you stop thinking about. The black-red weed that covers the pavement, finger-deep, soft as soaked wool — it is tempting to call it a carpet of plants, a population, a species among species. But you can sample it at one terrace and again a thousand kilometers along the same drowned causeway, and the two samples are not similar; they are *identical*, in the way the two ends of one rope are identical. No one has ever found a seam in it. No edge where one individual gives way to the next, no margin, no frontier — just the felt, continuous across provinces, knitting the joints of masonry laid by hands ten thousand years apart. It may be the case — and the place will never confirm it, because the place confirms nothing — that the felt is a single organism. One living thing, continent-wide, paving the pavement, the second great act of surfacing performed on this floor and the first one done without hands.

If it is one thing, it is the largest living thing that has ever existed, and the slowest, and its entire life is indistinguishable from maintenance. It does not spread anymore; there is nowhere left to spread. It does not compete; there is nothing to compete with. What it does is *hold*. It holds the flagstones against the patient pull of the current. It holds the crumbling edges of the doorless doorways. Where a brick begins, after eighty centuries, to consider becoming two bricks, the felt is already in the crack, gripping both halves, voting against. The builders mortared their world together and the mortar failed, as mortar does, and the felt arrived afterward and took up the same office and has never once relinquished it. It is possible to think of it as the floor's last employee. It is possible to think of it as the floor's widow.

It is also a reader, in the only sense reading survives down here. The inscriptions — the ones worn to mere variations of smoothness, the ones your fingertips almost resolve and don't — those shallow textures hold a fraction more nutriment, a trace more purchase, and the felt grows in them a hair denser, a shade darker. Which means that across the whole drowned continent, the writing of the builders is still legible — not to a hand, not to an eye, but as a pattern of slightly deeper life. Every glyph is now a sentence of weed. Whatever was carved — names, tallies, warnings, prayers, the ordinary administration of an unimaginable patience — is being read continuously, by occupancy, by the only literate thing left, which understands none of it and keeps every word.

**Above the felt, the grasses, and above the grasses, the elders.** The ribbon-plants of the canopy are not a crowd; the dark will not support a crowd. They are individuals, and they are countable, in the way old trees in an old country are countable, and if there were anyone here to name them they would have names. Each one stands rooted in a piece of the architecture — a post-hole, a door-socket, a quarry seam — because the architecture offers the only true purchase on a floor otherwise sheathed in felt, and so the forest, without intending anything, uses the city *as it was meant to be used*. The sockets that once held timber posts now hold stalks. The drains still drain, channeling the faint current in the directions their makers chose, and the plants lean along those choices, fed by an engineering nine thousand years out of warranty and still, in its small way, working. The forest did not conquer the city. The forest moved in. It is the second tenant, and it found the fittings serviceable, and it has kept them all.

An elder's biography is simple and very long. It begins with an accident — a frond, broken perhaps once a century along a given terrace, drifting on the current for weeks, settling at last into some socket or seam and deciding, over a decade, to stay. Then four or five hundred years of standing. It records everything: each year's lean in its growth rings, the decade-long dimming when a turbid drift passed over, the season a cold seep crossed the plain and the great slow eddy's once-in-a-generation instruction to face another way. It flexes in the current and creaks, and that creak — the rope-taking-strain sound you hear from very far away — is the sound of a specific individual, having a specific moment in a life otherwise without events. When an elder dies, it dies the way the floor does everything: by thinning, over centuries, frond by frond, until only the holdfast remains, gripping its socket like a hand that has forgotten what it was holding and refuses, on principle, to let go. The holdfast outlasts the plant by another long while. Then the felt takes the socket back, and holds it, and waits for the next frond to drift in.

**Below the felt, below the pavement, below the third and fourth pavements down, the natives.** Because there is older life here than the forest, and it does not photosynthesize, and it has never seen even the memory of light at the ceiling of the water. In the sealed chambers — the cisterns, the granaries, the buried rooms whose doors were closed before the flood and have not been opened since, except by the millimeter — there are films on the walls. Blind, anaerobic, microbial, living on trapped gas and old residues and the patient chemistry of clay dissolving into cold water. They are, in all likelihood, the descendants of whatever lived alongside the builders: the cultures that soured their stores, sweetened their fermentations, lined their drains. Domesticates gone feral in the dark; or never feral, exactly — just continuing, in the pantries of a household that stepped out one geologic afternoon and did not come back. When a pocket of ancient gas finds a seam and bleeds upward through the weed in that soft, prolonged sigh, what is escaping is partly the breath of those chambers and partly the exhalation of their inhabitants, the only lineage on the Drowned Floor that knew the builders personally and stayed.

**And then there are the residents who are not alive, and the residents who are made of water, and the place declines to draw a firm line through either.**

The eddies first. In a world with no animals, the eddies are the closest thing — and the resemblance is not whimsical, it is functional. They move, and nothing else here moves of its own accord. They have lifespans: a slow gyre hundreds of kilometers wide is born from some shift in the deep circulation, lives for decades or centuries, grazes the canopy of an entire province — combing it patiently around to face a new direction — and then weakens, and dies, and is succeeded. They have territories. They have, recorded in the lean of ten thousand elders, something very like a biography. If you hung motionless in the dark for a human lifetime, the only creature that would ever pass you would be made entirely of the cold water itself, and it would take years to pass, and the forest would bow to it the whole way.

And the hands. The builders do not live here; nothing so soft survives. No bones, no tools, no names that a hand can read. But there is a sense — and the place insists on it quietly, in every brick — in which they have not entirely moved out. The thumb-trough dragged through wet clay before the firing is a one-second gesture preserved for ten thousand years, and the felt grows in it, denser there, alive in the exact channel of a particular thumb on a particular distant working day. Multiply that by trillions. Every gesture on the Drowned Floor is inhabited. Life has filled the handwork the way water fills a mold: the pecked tool marks, the tired irregular coursing of an end-of-day crew, the troughs and seams and sockets — all of it occupied, all of it held, all of it kept warm in the only sense of warmth this place permits, which is occupancy. The builders' residence here is not memory and not ghost. It is tenancy by proxy. Something lives in their fingerprints.

So this is who lives on the Drowned Floor, finally, when you let the answer finish arriving: a single vast holder of stones that may be one creature and may be the idea of one creature; a sparse aristocracy of standing elders, each in its inherited socket, each creaking its one note a few times a century; a sealed underworld of blind films keeping house in the closed rooms, the last domestic animals of a vanished species, still in the pantry; a fauna of slow water, the only things here that hurry, at the speed of years; and underneath and through it all, the gestures — inhabited, occupied, held.

Everything that lives here lives by gripping. The felt grips the stones, the holdfasts grip the sockets, the films grip the chamber walls, the weed grips the writing. The whole population of the place is one long act of holding on, performed in the cold and the near-dark, at a hand's-breadth per thousand years, by tenants who have never met their landlords and will keep the property in order anyway — patiently, indefinitely, with no one to thank them and nothing left to let go of first.
