# The People of the Poured Country

At first you will think you have already met them. The herons that turn their heads with a sound like jar-lids, the frogs spaced out by measuring cord, the wicker buffalo with warmth coming off its flanks — surely these are the inhabitants. But spend a little time wading the marsh-plates and you will understand that the herons and the frogs and the buffalo are *furniture*. They are part of the country the way the channels are part of the country: placed, regarded, held. They live the way a still life lives.

Then, in one of the groves, you will notice that a gap in the woven trunks is exactly the width of a doorway. That the clay before it has been swept. That there is a fired footprint on the threshold, and it is not yours.

## The Poured People

They are coil-built, person-height, hollow, and full.

You see one first at a distance, standing in a marsh-plate among the herons, and you take it for another heron-thing, another piece of the stillness — until it moves, and the movement is wrong for furniture. It is slow, but it is *careful*, and care is not something the country does. The country holds. The people carry.

Their bodies show the work that made them: the spiral seams of coiling rising from ankle to throat, smoothed in places, left proud in others, the way a potter leaves coils showing when she is not ashamed of them. Their surfaces are burnished — you can see the small overlapping strokes of the polishing stone, and where the burnishing catches the sourceless light their skin has the deep soft shine of a thing rubbed for a long time by someone who loved it. At the crown of each head is a narrow mouth, like the mouth of a bottle, stoppered with a small fitted lid.

Inside each of them is water. It was poured in at their making and it is the same water still — first water, original water, water that remembers its pouring the way every pool in this country does. It is their life, and they know its weight to the drop. This is why they move the way they move. Everything in their gait, their gestures, the deliberate way they kneel and rise, comes from one continuous calculation: *do not spill*.

Their voices are their interiors. They speak by tilting, by the controlled slosh and settle of what they hold, by humming a column of air across their own narrow mouths the way you would blow across a bottle — and the pitch tells you everything, because the pitch is the water level, and the water level is the self. A full one speaks low and slow, a deep brown vowel of a voice. One who has given much away speaks high and bright and a little hollow. They cannot lie about how much they are. To greet one another they touch — one knuckle, lightly, to the shoulder — and the struck note carries name, mood, fullness, history. A conversation between old friends sounds like rain beginning on a roof of bells, except that no one here has ever heard rain.

They do not eat. The fruit hangs ripe and stays ripe and they leave it where it hangs, the way you would leave a word in a sentence. What they do instead is *refill*. The great tilted vessels on their plinths — the ones pouring glass-smooth ribbons of water with that faint perpetual hush — are theirs. The people come to them when their voices have gone high and thin, unstopper their crowns, and lean into the falling ribbon, and stand there while their note descends back toward its proper register. This is why the vessels never empty. They were set pouring for the people. The country has wells because the country has someone to be thirsty.

And they feel — visibly. Their bodies are kiln-warm like everything here, but feeling arrives on them as dew. Joy, grief, fear, tenderness: condensation gathers on the burnished clay, beads, runs. A moved crowd of them glistens. You can watch an emotion travel through a gathering the way you'd watch breath fog glass, person to person, a weather they make among themselves in a country that has none.

## Shadows, and Where They Live

Nothing here casts a shadow, you remember. A few things have shadows painted on.

Every one of the poured people has a painted shadow, and the shadow is their address. Somewhere in the country — under a grove, beside a channel, out alone on the open glaze — there is a person-shaped silhouette of thin grey slip, laid down at their making, leaning in the same direction as every other painted shadow in the world. To rest, a person walks home, finds their shadow, and aligns their feet with its feet. Then they go still. Still as the herons. Still as the frogs. Indistinguishable, for as long as they rest, from everything else in the country — which is to say, for that interval, they are *finished*, and it is the closest thing they have to sleep.

Walk far enough and you will find shadows standing empty. Some belong to people who are simply out in the marshes. Some have been empty a very long time; you can tell because the country, which never weathers, has left them perfectly crisp, and their crispness is the saddest thing in it. The people do not paint over an empty shadow. They walk around it. Even the herons, you will notice, do not stand in one.

## The Open-Crowned

Out east, near the grove where the trees were woven but never closed — the hollow trees full of sky — live the others.

They were never finished. Their crowns were left open: no lid, no narrow bottle-mouth, just the coiled body splaying at the top like an unfinished basket, and inside them, instead of water, is the brightness. The same total sourceless light that fills the sky and the hollow trees fills them, and it rises faintly from their open heads, a shimmer, like heat off the country at noon, if the country had a noon.

They cannot speak. There is no water in them to sound. They do not refill, do not rest, have no painted shadows — the slip will not stay grey near them; it pales and lifts. What they do is walk the reedbeds.

You remember the reedbeds: planted as enormous glyphs, sentences of vegetation written across the marsh in a script no one walks high enough to read. The open-crowned walk the lines of them. Endlessly, patiently, in single file or alone, they trace the strokes of the great green letters, turning where the script turns, doubling back where it doubles, and the poured people believe — speaking of it always in their lowest, fullest tones — that this walking *is* the saying. That the glyphs are the one thing the open-crowned have to tell, written in reeds because they were given no voices, and that the message is addressed upward, to whatever might still be high enough to read it. No one knows what it says. Some of the poured people have tried to learn it by following an open-crowned one through a single letter, a journey of days, and they come back beaded all over with dew and will not speak for a long while, and when they do speak again their note has changed, very slightly, forever.

The people leave fruit at the edge of the eastern grove. It is never taken. It never rots. They keep leaving it.

## The Soft Ones, and the Croak of the Frog

Beneath the fired crust, you remember, the terracotta is still soft — still drying, kiln-warm, the country's own unfinished flesh. The poured people will tell you, tapping it out in low careful notes, what lies in that softness.

Sleepers. Figures the makers coiled but never fired, laid down in the leather-hard depths like words in a closed mouth. The country's under-warmth is firing them — slowly, slowly, over spans the people do not try to measure — hardness arriving in them an inch at a time, a shin, a hand, a heart.

And this is what the frog's single croak is. That lone hollow note, the thumb on the clay drum, the one that sounds once an hour or once a day and is never answered: the people stop when they hear it, every one of them, wherever they are, and the herons turn their heads. Somewhere under the country, they will tell you, the firing of a soft one has just finished. Each croak is a completion. The silence afterward is the new one beginning, very far down, to wake.

Eventually — seasons later, if there were seasons — the ground in some unremarkable place will swell, and part, and a new person will push up through clay that opens for them and smooths itself closed behind them, and stand blinking in the shadowless light, hollow and empty and warm. They arrive knowing exactly one thing: that the country is finished. Everything else must be poured into them. And it is — literally. The others gather, and each unstoppers their crown, and each gives a little of their own first water, a thin warm thread from their body into the new one's, until the newcomer has a level, a pitch, a voice. For a day after a birth the whole country speaks a tone higher. It is the most beautiful thing you will hear in the Poured Country, and the people are embarrassed by how much they love it.

## Crazing, Mending, and the Going-In

They age the way ceramic ages. Hairline cracks — crazing — spread across them slowly, from laughter, from grief, from too many crossings of the seam, from simply carrying their water a long time. The cracks are mended with raw slip dug carefully from the soft strata, and a mended place never fires again; it stays matte, soft, holding a thumbprint if you press it. So the old among them are mapped in soft grey patches, more and more of them, and their voices muddy as the soft places dampen the ring, until an elder speaks in a sound like water moving under earth.

When a person has become more clay than ceramic, they go in.

They choose a day no different from other days, because there are none. Companions walk with them out to one of the rough unsmoothed stretches, or to open ground where the crust runs thin. The old one kneels. They press both hands flat against the country. And they lean — slowly, the way they have done everything — into the ground, which receives them the way the pools receive your finger: opening, taking, closing over. Their water joins the closed loop. Nothing is lost; nothing here is ever lost; that is either the mercy of the place or its whole predicament.

Their hands are the last of them to go in, pressing.

And now you understand the fingerprints. The prints everywhere in the warm ochre ground, the ones "not yet faded," the marks of *whoever pressed it into place* — they are not only the makers'. The ground of the Poured Country is its dead, and the dead are still pressing, and when you dig your fingers into the soft terracotta you are interleaving your prints with theirs.

One more thing. Each person, if you ask, will point upward into the brightness and show you their bird — one of the far high birds that hang without circling, pinned to the inside of the sky. Everyone has one; everyone knows which is theirs; no one can say how they know. When a person goes into the ground, their bird beats its wings. Once. The only motion the sky ever makes. The sound takes a long time to come down — a single soft clap out of the brightness, arriving when the mourners are already walking home — and everyone in the country looks up.

## The Mender

There is one of them who is not like the others.

You may walk for days before you see the Mender, and you will know them at once: they carry tools. A wooden rib. A length of wire. A brush, and a small pot of thin grey slip. They are not taller than the others, or older-looking — the opposite. They have no crazing at all, no soft patches, no mended places, and their burnish is so deep it is hard to look at directly.

The Mender does the country's upkeep. They repaint shadows where the grey has thinned. They re-burnish the grain in the air where it has gone uneven, working the brightness with the flat of the wooden rib until the far groves stand straight again. They press thumbprints into the surface of the pools, smoothing the water like slip. And they walk the seam — that long raised join running horizon to horizon — pressing the offset channel a hair's width closer to true on every pass, an error a hand wide, a correction a hair wide, a labor either futile or infinitely patient, and the Mender's gait does not tell you which.

The people do not tap the Mender's shoulder. The Mender does not speak. But every fingerprint in the country, the people will tell you — every print in the ground that is not a dead one's, every print on the trimmed channels and the coiled herons and the woven trees — matches the Mender's hands exactly. Which means one of two things. Either the Mender is the last maker, the one who could not step back when the others stepped back, who stayed one instant too long inside the finished work and was annealed into it. Or the country, completed, made for itself a single image of its own making, so that the gesture of being made would never have to end.

The people hold both beliefs at once, the way they hold their water: carefully, without spilling.

When the Mender passes, everyone stops. Not in fear. In regard — the same permanent regard the herons hold for the fish, the strike that is always about to come — and the Mender moves through their stillness and is gone across the glaze, small, bright, busy, the loneliest thing in a country where no one is ever far from anyone.

## What They Are For

Here is the truth of them, and it is the truth of the place.

The country is finished. The instant of completion has declined to end; the fruit will be ripe forever; the strike will never come. Everything in the Poured Country is at the top of its breath — everything except the people. *Their* levels rise and fall. *They* craze, and are mended, and go soft, and go in, and the ground croaks, and new ones surface, and the whole register of their voices shifts, tone by tone, generation by generation. They are the one thing in the country that is still becoming. They are how a finished world goes on being made.

The makers did not abandon the work. They ended the landscape and left the making *inside* the inhabitants, the way you would pour water into a vessel before tilting it on its plinth: set going once, and not yet given permission to stop.

The people know this, more or less, in their warm low way. It is why they are so unbearably gentle with you. You are the only moving shadow they have ever seen, and the young ones will follow your shadow across the glaze for hours, and at last one of them — beaded all over with shy dew, voice up in its highest register — will ask if they might stand in it. Just for a moment. Just to know what it is like to have a darkness that goes where you go.

Tell them about rain, if you can bear to. Water that arrives from above, unpoured, unowned, addressed to no one. Watch the condensation come up on the whole gathering at once.

And before you leave, they will show you the path of visitors: every fired footprint of every stranger who has ever come over the lip of the world, kept and swept, trails of them leading inward across the country and — always — back out again. You will notice, because they will not point it out, that one trail leads only inward. They will not say whose. You will notice the Mender goes barefoot.

When you go, they will walk you to the finished edge. They do not grieve aloud; they bead. And one by one, each of them will touch a single knuckle, lightly, to your shoulder — and you will be surprised, you will be surprised your whole life, to learn that you also make a note. Then somewhere behind you a frog will sound its one hollow beat across the flat warm water, and the country will close back over your absence like a ring closing over a fingertip, and resume the top of its breath — all of it except them, who are still becoming.

Which is the long way of saying: who live there.
