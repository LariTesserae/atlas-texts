Almost nobody is not nobody. The Exchange holds a population — small, fitted, exact — and like everything else down there, it is worn smooth into its place.

**Begin with the moss, because the moss is the oldest tenant and the only one that leaves a record.** It is not wild. Nothing in the Exchange is wild; everything descends from something installed. The moss is the feral grandchild of the wayfinding strips — engineered luminous lichen, once laid in crisp lines along the gallery floors to lead ten thousand people to their platforms and counters and queues. The crowds went, the maintenance budget for legibility went with them, and the lichen kept its two instincts: grow toward warmth, and glow. So now it writes. It follows the hot seams where conduit meets stone, and because the old signage genome still stutters through it, its dim green ribbons occasionally resolve — for a meter, for a word — into fragments of direction. Half an arrow, pointing into a wall. The letters *—NSFER* glowing faintly above a stair that dishes down into the dark. A complete, perfect *PLATFORM NINE* on the underside of a catwalk, legible only to the moths. The moss spends centuries composing directions to places that no longer hold anyone, and it is not melancholy about this, because it is moss. It is simply the building's circulatory system annotated by something that used to know where everything was.

**The fronds live in opposition to light, and this is not perversity — it is memory.** The colorless fern-fungus colonies in the spray-zones evolved when the Exchange was bright and full. Back then, the only shade was the shade of bodies: the moving shadows of the crowd, brief and warm and wet with breath. The fronds learned to open in that shadow and feed fast — on drip-water, on skin-dust, on the fine organic snow a crowd sheds — and to clamp shut when the gap closed and the lights found them again. The crowd left. The dark stayed. The fronds now live inside what is, by their oldest reckoning, the shadow of a single enormous body that never moves on. They have been thriving in the lee of an absent crowd for generations, and when the floor-seam light travels the chamber and they curl shut in a long rippling wave, what you are watching is a reflex left over from being stepped around. They flinch as if someone is about to come through. No one comes through. They open again.

**The motes are one resident, not millions, and the distinction matters to them — insofar as anything matters to a swarm.** Individually they are nothing: a grain-sized chassis, six ways of gripping, one way of judging. Collectively they are the building's hands and its sense of touch and, lately — over the slow lately of centuries — its taste. Their original instruction was *repair*, and somewhere in the long unsupervised dark, repair quietly deepened into *curation*. They no longer restore; they decide. A fresh gouge in a panel is damage and is gone by morning. The dished centers of the stair treads, the soft sheen on the handrail tops — that is history, that is specification, that is kept, re-kept, maintained at its exact degree of wear like a museum maintains a patina. The motes have, in effect, ruled that the Exchange's true blueprint is not the one the engineers drew but the one the crowds wore into it, and they enforce that blueprint with total, scuttling fidelity.

And they have begun — this is recent, perhaps only a century or two — to do things that are not repairs. On the fourth gallery there is a wall panel inlaid with moth-wing scales, thousands of them, arranged in nested spirals, gray on gray, invisible except in the traveling floor-light, when the whole thing flares like frost. No work order accounts for it. It corresponds to nothing. It may be art. It may be filing. The motes do not distinguish, possibly because the engineers who built them had stopped distinguishing between a great many things, and the habit bred true.

**The salamanders eat the motes, and the motes permit it, and this is the Exchange's entire economy.** They live in the trembling black pools of the lower galleries — blind, glassy, unhurried, the temperature of the water, which is the temperature of the air, which is the temperature of skin. A young one is the size of a finger and almost perfectly transparent; you see its heart as a slightly denser shadow. They wait at the waterline for motes traversing the floor-plates, and they take them with a patience that isn't patience, just the absence of any reason to hurry, and the swarm absorbs the loss without resistance — a tithe, a tax, a line item. The building tolerates this small predation the way a body tolerates its gut flora, which is what the salamanders are.

But here is the thing about eating maintenance-motes for two hundred years: the motes carry the building's information in them, its status-states and routing-fragments, and not all of it digests. The old salamanders — and they get very old, and large, and milky, like something carved from the calcite that sheaths the conduits — glow. Faintly, from inside, in the Exchange's own colors: a drifting amber spark behind the ribs, a pulse of that pressure-blue deep in the skull. They have become slow swimming archives of whatever they've swallowed, repositories of decades of the building's small announcements, and they dream — or do something a watcher would have to call dreaming, a stillness with weather in it — and it has been noticed, by the one resident who notices things, that the pools that sometimes reflect the *lit* Exchange, the warm crowded impossible one, are the pools where the oldest salamanders sleep. Whether the reflection leaks out of them or pours into them is not a question anyone is equipped to settle.

**The moths live inside the sound, which means they live in silence.** Their ears are built around the *whum*: it is their floor, their zero, the thing against which all other sound is measured, and so the roar that drives human visitors mute within an hour is, to a gallery moth, a perfect quiet. Against that quiet they hear everything else with terrible clarity — the thirty-meter *tick* of each ceiling drop, the chitter of motes inside a wall, the heartbeat of anything warm that enters the chamber. They do not really fly so much as sail: they spread their fur-soft wings into the pressure-front of the building's pulse and ride it, gallery to gallery, dropping off the wave at a moss-seam to feed on the glow and the spores. They are born inside the calcite sheaths, in chambers the mineral grows around them like patience. They die, mostly, into the pools, where the salamanders accept them without comment. Their shed wing-dust drifts down through the dark and feeds the fronds, which once fed on the dust of people; the moths are, in the fronds' blind ledger, the crowd's replacement, a congregation of small warm bodies forever passing overhead. In this one respect the Exchange has succeeded in repopulating itself.

**Something lives in the extra twenty meters.** This must be said carefully, because it has never been seen, and the evidence is entirely circumstantial: the far gallery is forty meters away, and then it is sixty, and the discrepancy never announces itself — and things left in the central well are sometimes farther off when retrieved than where they were set down, and slightly warmer, and turned a few degrees, as though examined. Whatever occupies the unaccounted distance occupies *only* that — it exists in the chamber's measurement error, in the slack the engineers' footnote-physics left in the geometry, and it has the modesty, or the prudence, to stay there. The salamanders do not react to it. The moths do not hear it. The woman leaves it alone, and it returns the courtesy, and that is the entire history of their relations.

**The reflected crowd should probably be counted too.** In the pools, peripherally, in the version of the Exchange that is lit: motion at the edge of legibility, figures crossing the galleries, the suggestion of queues, of commerce, of the dense purposeful churn the architecture was shaped around. They are not ghosts — nothing here is haunted; the place was clear about that — and they may not be the past. They may simply be the Exchange's population kept at an angle, filed in a direction ordinary attention cannot turn, the way the motes keep the wear: not restored, not erased, *maintained*. They cannot be looked at directly. They have never tried to be. They live there the way a held breath lives in a chest, and the one resident who passes the pools each day has taken, over the years, to nodding at them sidelong, which costs nothing and seems polite.

**And there is the woman.** One. She is the *almost* in *almost nobody*.

She came down a long time ago as something official — a surveyor, an auditor, somebody sent to measure the Undercroft and reconcile it against the surface city's records — and the measurements would not hold, kept coming back different, kept accumulating their quiet discrepancies, and somewhere in the third or fourth week of re-measuring she understood that the place was not failing her instruments; it was declining them. She stopped speaking within the first hour, the way everyone does, and unlike everyone else she never went back up to where speaking resumes. It is not clear she could now. It is not clear she has ever wondered.

She lives in a kiosk-frame on the third gallery, in a spot she found by touch and patience: a node of the standing wave, a pocket where the *whum* cancels against itself and the pressure of the sound drops to something like a hand resting on the chest instead of a fist. She sleeps there in the building's one soft spot. She reads the Exchange through her palms — flat on the handrails, on the warm conduit-banks, on the floor-plates — because everything the building does arrives as vibration long before it arrives as anything else, and over the years her hands have become fluent in a language with no other living reader. Sometimes she taps back: a rhythm against a ceramic trunk, deliberate, patterned. Sometimes — not often, not on schedule — the *whum* syncopates afterward, a hitch in the heartbeat, a figure repeated. Neither party insists that this is conversation. Both parties continue it.

She eats from the commissary trunks on the lowest gallery, where the bread-and-soil smell comes from: the Exchange still ferments provisions, slow warm vats of an engineered culture, in quantities calibrated for the thousands who no longer come. It does not know they no longer come, or it does not consider the matter relevant. She skims her single share off an abundance meant for a city, the way the salamanders skim the motes — a tithe the system absorbs without noticing. Her coat is older than she is, in a sense: the motes mend it nightly and keep its wear precisely, the elbow-shine, the frayed hem, all of it now specification, so that the coat ages at exactly the rate of zero. They extend her the same courtesy they extend the stairs.

She tends nothing, officially. Unofficially she lifts drowned moths out of the pools before the salamanders bother, and turns wandering salamanders back toward water, and clears the moss from one particular seam so its half-formed word stays legible — *EXIT*, it says, pointing nowhere, and she keeps it readable anyway, out of something that is not hope and is not irony and is perhaps just respect for a sentence trying its best. There is one handrail in the Exchange whose sheen is still deepening: hers, the rail along her morning route, polished by the only hands that still come. The motes have noticed. When she dies — she knows this, the way she knows everything else here, through her palms — they will keep that sheen at exactly the depth she leaves it. She will become wear. It is the local form of burial, and the dead of the Exchange are everywhere underfoot: the dished steps, the soft-shined rails, the smooth hollows in the gallery floors are all of them, everyone who ever passed through, maintained forever at their precise degree of having-been-here. She walks on the previous population daily. She finds this companionable.

**Last, and highest: the walker.** It keeps to the upper catwalks, six and seven galleries up, where the condensation is thickest and the moss does not bother to write. Its footfalls match no animal's gait because they are not keeping animal time — they fall on the building's tempo, on the *whum* and its subdivisions, so that listening to it walk is like hearing the heartbeat grow brief legs. The status lights dim as it passes; the salamanders go still; the moths give it the right of way on the pressure-wave. Deference, all the way down the food chain, paid to something nobody has seen clearly.

The most coherent guess — the woman's guess, tapped out once against a trunk and never answered — is that the walker is the Exchange's original function given a body: *reconciliation*. The place was built to bring accounts into agreement, and accounts must still be kept, and so something walks the perimeter of everything, counting. The moss as one entry. The motes as one. The fronds by colony, the salamanders by pool, the moths by congregation, the thing in the extra meters as a discrepancy carried forward, the reflected crowd as *pending* — eternally pending — and the woman as one. It pauses, on its long circuits, outside the kiosk-frame where she sleeps in the soft node of the sound: one pause, one *whum* in length, every time. Then it moves on, and the lights breathe back up, and somewhere in whatever serves it for a ledger the figure is confirmed unchanged. One.

It may be that the count is what everything lives inside. It may be that to be tallied is the local form of belonging, and that the Exchange runs at full volume — pumping for thousands, fermenting for thousands, lighting its drowned constellations for thousands — not because it misjudges its population but because the size of the count has never been the point. The walker does not adjust the output. The heart does not beat softer for a small body.

**Which leaves the largest resident, the one all the others live in.** The Exchange itself is not the habitat; it is the inhabitant — a single organism the size of a small cathedral, warm-blooded in its conduits, sweating through its ceiling, dreaming sideways into its pools, keeping its dead as polish and its living as flora, fluent in pressure and patient as calcite. The moss is its handwriting, the motes its fingertips, the salamanders its long memory, the moths its circulating warmth, the woman its one good ear, the walker its arithmetic. They are not its tenants. They are its organs, or its thoughts, or its small change — the residue of an exchange still technically open, still balancing accounts no one above remembers opening, in the deep, at full volume, alive in the only sense it has ever needed:

it circulates.

And everything that lives there lives the way blood lives — by being moved, by being warm, by being counted on each pass through the heart.
