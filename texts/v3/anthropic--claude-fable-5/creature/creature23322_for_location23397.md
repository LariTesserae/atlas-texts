# The People of the Understory

Begin with their hands, because that is where the city begins on them.

Everyone here has carpenter's hands by their tenth year — not calloused so much as *finished*, the skin gone smooth and dense from a lifetime of dry air and handled wood, the fingertips faintly amber from resin that no amount of washing (there is no washing; there is wiping, with waxed cloth and powdered talc) ever fully lifts. They touch things constantly, the way other people glance. A tender walking a trestle-road will trail one hand along the rotating shaft without thinking, reading its speed and temper through the palm. Greeting here involves pressing the back of your hand briefly to the other person's — not gripping, never gripping; gripping is for tools — and the gesture carries information, because a hand tells you what someone has been working: loom-fiber leaves its own polish, splint-resin its own stain, the silvery light-trunk tissue a faint mineral dust that catches in the whorls of the fingerprints like frost.

They are a dry-cured people. The dryness has gotten into them the way it gets into everything: their hair grows pale early, ash-blond and silver-fawn even in the young, lank and soft and faintly luminous in the light-columns, and they oil it with pressed seed-wax that smells like the inside of the city, which is to say like the inside of a violin. Their skin seasons rather than ages — an elder's face is not wrinkled the way surface faces wrinkle but *grained*, finely lined in patterns that follow the muscles like the figure in old wood, and the people find these faces beautiful and say so, and have forty words for the kinds of grain a face can show.

Their voices are the first thing a visitor remarks on and the last thing a visitor stops noticing. They speak at the volume of someone reading aloud to a sleeping person. This is not timidity. The acoustics raised them — dry wood swallows sound, so a voice does not need to be large, and a large voice marks you as someone whose ears were trained somewhere wet. Their language is full of clicks and soft plosives and long held vowels, sounds that carry cleanly through cellulose, and they supplement it constantly with knocking: a knuckle-code rapped on any surface, since every surface is the same surface, and a message knocked on a doorframe in the loom district arrives, faint but legible, in the kitchens a quarter-mile away. Lovers in different towers talk all night this way. The elderly complain that the young knock too fast.

## What They Do

There are no jobs here exactly; there are *tendencies*, and a person drifts into theirs the way water would find a level, if water were permitted.

**The bandagers** are the closest thing to an aristocracy, and they would be horrified to hear it said. They are the ones who splint the growing districts — who wrap the soft junction-points of future buildings in resin-soaked fiber and hold the city to its intentions. The work requires a strange double patience: the bandage set today determines a doorway in forty years, so a bandager must hold the finished building in mind while handling shoots no thicker than a wrist, and must hold it *accurately*, because the city remembers errors longer than it remembers people. Bandagers apprentice for fifteen years. The first five are spent doing nothing but watching wood grow — sitting with the nub-fields at the cavity edges, learning to see motion in something that moves slower than shadow. They say a finished bandager can stand in an empty quarter and tell you, from the angle of a knee-high shoot, what the room it becomes will smell like.

**The tick-readers** are fewer, older, and stranger. Every district produces them eventually: people whose ears have organized themselves around the city's growth-sounds, the pinging and settling, until the noise resolves into report. They sit at junctions of the trestle-roads in the dusk between light-columns, very still, and once a day they walk to the engine-trunk substations and tell the tenders what they heard — which quarter is setting well, which scaffold is fusing crooked, where the lignin came in thin overnight. No instrument has ever outperformed them. Visitors assume mysticism; the tick-readers themselves are brusque about it and compare the skill, when pressed, to telling your children's footsteps apart in the dark. The city is loud, they say, if you were born inside it. Everything down here is the dark, and everything down here is footsteps.

**The pressers, the loom-hands, the gear-wrights, the lamp-keepers** — the industrial folk — are the bulk of the population and the most ordinary, in the way that the people who run any vast slow machine are ordinary: practical, unhurried, given to long technical arguments conducted at a murmur over shared gourds of resin-water. They have a deep guild humor built on the one joke the city permits, which is the joke about speed. Everything funny here is funny because it is fast. A loom-hand who drops a shuttle and catches it before it lands will be teased about it for a season. *Where's the fire,* they say to anyone hurrying, and it is the blackest thing they say, and they say it grinning.

**The beetle-followers** live differently — half-feral, affectionate, smelling permanently of grafting resin. They move with the splice-beetle herds from scaffold to scaffold, sleeping in fiber hammocks slung from the green bones of unfinished halls, and their actual work is diplomatic: the beetles graft what the beetles approve of, so the followers' craft is *persuasion* — adjusting a shoot's angle by a degree, scenting a junction with pressed attractant, arranging for the herd to discover, as if by accident, the joint the bandagers need fused. They speak of individual beetles by name and dispute their characters. They are the only people in the city who laugh loudly, out at the growing edges where there is nothing finished yet to absorb the sound or be offended by it.

**The bat-masters** keep the roost-poles and run the post, and are, by long tradition, the city's gossips, since every letter passes under their poles. **The grazer-watch** is not a profession anyone chooses; it is a duty rotated through the districts, a season at a time, of walking the floors at the dark end of the range with a capacitor-lamp and a knocking-staff, keeping count of the slate-colored shapes, and not asking the question everyone on grazer-watch eventually stops asking.

And **the well-walkers** climb. The light-trunks must be tended from inside — the silvery tissue brushed free of dust, the rare blockage cleared — and so a small hereditary handful of families spend their working lives ascending hollow verticals five hundred feet and more, on grown rung-galls, toward a coin of actual sky. They are the only people of the Understory who see the surface regularly, and they are changed by it in a way the others find faintly tragic. Well-walkers squint. Well-walkers know what weather is. Well-walkers come back down smelling of *outside* — green, wet, chaotic — and stand in the decontamination galleries letting the dryness take them back, and some of them weep there, and nobody asks whether it is for the sky or for the relief of losing it.

## The Children

Children here are raised on the floor — literally; the great empty quarters between districts are the commons of childhood, and small figures are always crossing them, running the only running the city sees, their footfalls swallowed to nothing so that they seem to fly silently across the pale grain like the shadows of birds that do not exist down here.

They are taught the rules before they are taught their names, or rather the rules *are* names: the first words most children speak are the warning-words, *wet* and *green* and *spark*, and a toddler who points at a sealed gourd and says *wet* solemnly is being a good citizen and is praised for it. They learn the knock-code before writing. They learn to walk softly not because anyone hushes them — nothing down here can hear them anyway — but because softness is the shape of every adult they will ever imitate.

Their games are the city's curriculum in disguise. They play *settling*, where one child holds a posture as long as growing wood holds a shape and the others try to catch the motion. They play postal games with the loft-bats, who tolerate children with the weary patience of all working animals. They adopt individual nub-towers at the growing edges — a child of six will claim a knee-high blunt shoot as theirs, visit it, talk to it, measure it against their body — and since the tower will take fifty years to finish, the claim is understood by everyone as what it is: the city's gentlest lesson, that you will not see the end of what you love, and that this is not sad, or is sad in a way you are expected to carry lightly, like everything else here, on account of the fuel.

## The Old, and the Dead

The old do not retire; they *season*. An elder's work narrows rather than ends — a bandager of ninety no longer wraps joints but sits where apprentices can find her, and is consulted the way the tick-readers consult the walls. The grained faces gather in the amber light of the cultivated windows in the oldest quarters, and the city's whole sense of itself pools around them, because they are the only ones who have personally watched a district go from nub to tower, and so they are the only ones for whom the city's patience is not faith but memory.

The dead do not rot. That is the founding fact, applied to people. The dead are wrapped in the city's own fiber and carried — quietly, by knock-code procession, the message *one of us, going up* rippling ahead through the walls — to the seasoning galleries: long dry chambers in the oldest cured root-mass, where the wrapped forms rest on grown shelves and slowly, over decades, become light, fragrant, woodlike. The people do not find this morbid. They visit. The galleries smell stronger of violin than anywhere else in the city, and the saying is that the smell of the whole Understory, the smell you noticed in your first minute of descent, is partly *them* — the ancestors, distributed through the air, seasoning still. To live here is to breathe your great-grandparents, faintly, and to find that companionable.

## The Stock, Closer Up

**The splice-beetles**, seen near, are beautiful in the way of all serious tools: their carapaces are the oxblood of heartwood, glossed like french polish, and they move with a deliberation that makes their size unalarming. They are not clever. They are something better than clever — *certain*. A beetle at a junction works with the absolute absence of doubt of an organism doing the one thing it is for, and the followers say you can calm yourself by watching one, and visitors who try it agree. The herds hum, very low, when a graft is taking well: a sound felt in the sternum more than heard, the city's only music, and the followers fall asleep to it.

**The loft-bats** are pale as the wood and nearly weightless, and they are the only inhabitants who routinely cross between the worlds, pouring up the light-trunks at dusk-shift to forage the canopy half a mile overhead and pouring back down before the surface dawn, dusted with pollen from a sky the people will never see. They are, in consequence, mildly sacred and thoroughly taken for granted, like all postmen.

**The floor-grazers** deserve their reputation, which is to say their double reputation. In the lit quarters they are placid municipal animals, rasping the floors clean, ignoring everyone, their small eyes mild. The grazer-watch will tell you, if you walk a shift with them and keep quiet long enough, the thing they have all noticed and agreed not to make much of: that at the dark end of the range the grazers do not graze. They stand among the new nubs, motionless, sometimes for hours, in arrangements — circles, lines, spaced intervals — that look less like feeding than like *attendance*. The watch's working theory, held loosely, the way they hold everything: the grazers are not cleaning the city. The grazers are part of how the city decides. What the dust they eat tells them, and what they tell the nubs they stand among, is not the watch's business, and the watch likes it that way, and walks on.

**The predator** has no agreed name, which in a city of careful namers is itself a kind of name. It is heard as silence — a longer gap in the ticking — and seen as speed, which here is the most foreign thing there is. The people's relationship to it is the most surprising thing about them: they are not afraid, or their fear has cured, like everything else, into something seasoned and useful. The herds need moving; the bats need thinning; the city keeps what it needs. Children are taught to stand still when the silence lengthens, and they do, and nothing has taken a still child in living memory, and the people decline to find this mysterious. *It knows what's stock,* the elders say, and leave the rest of the sentence in the dark where it lives.

## The Other Inhabitant

And then there is the resident you have been walking through.

The people will not call the plant a god, and will not call it a landlord, and become quietly slippery when visitors press for the right word, because they suspect — correctly — that their language has a word and other languages don't. The word they use translates poorly as *host*, in both the senses a visitor might fear, and they use it without fear.

What can be said: it is alive at a tempo that makes the people, in its frame, something like weather — a fast, warm, busy phenomenon moving through its galleries, useful the way rain is useful elsewhere. It has never spoken. It has never needed to. Its communications are architectural and take decades to read: a district grown wrong after a wound, doorways narrowing toward an old offense, or — this is the part the people hold closest — doorways *widening*, light-trunks descending precisely where a growing neighborhood will need them in thirty years, before anyone has planned the neighborhood. The bandagers keep records, generations deep, of these anticipations. The records suggest, without ever insisting, that the city has been expecting its people since before there were people in it. That the cavities are not hollows the plant happens to have, but rooms.

The people return the attention in kind, at their own small fast scale: the softness, the carefulness, the dryness kept, the fires unlit, the green wood uncut. Whether this is worship, or husbandry, or simply the manners of a long marriage, they decline to say. They will say only the thing every child knows, the thing that is on the lintels of the oldest towers in worn knock-code, the closest the Understory has to a creed:

*It is growing us a city. Be worth the wait.*

And underneath everything — under the looms' whisper and the herds' hum and the post-bats and the silent children flying across the pale floors — the ticking goes on, expansion, lignin setting, new wood declaring itself, the host's one continuous utterance. The people fall asleep inside it every night of their lives. Most of them, asked what it sounds like, will give you the same word.

It sounds, they say, like *listening*.
