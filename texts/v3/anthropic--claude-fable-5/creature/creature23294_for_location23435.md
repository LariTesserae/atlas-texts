# Those Who Keep House on the Body

Ask a band, in sign, what lives on the back of the green, and the answer always begins the same way: a flat palm pressed slowly downward, toward the warm bark. *The ground lives here.* Everything else — shrill and warden, ribbon and wing, human and child of human — is named afterward, in descending order of tenure. The people of the plain do not consider themselves the inhabitants of the place. They consider themselves late guests in an occupied house, and most of what they are follows from that.

So begin where they begin.

## The Host

Whether the great body *lives here* or simply *is here* — whether a place can be its own inhabitant — is not a question the bands ask, but a traveler standing on the hot bronze plates will ask it, and will not settle it.

The evidence accumulates without ever quite concluding. The body eats lightning on purpose. It shows tomorrow's sky in its rain-pools, casually, the way a large animal lets small ones shelter in its shadow without ever acknowledging them. Its chord changes key three days before weather arrives, which is either physiology or speech, depending on how charitable you are feeling. Once in a generation, somewhere on the plain, a band wakes to find that a sap-eye has opened overnight directly beside their cone-house — a new eye, where the maps show none for a hundred miles — and that it flows for exactly as long as the band stays, and seals when they move on. The wall-carvings record perhaps thirty such openings across all remembered time. The notation for them translates, roughly, as *it noticed*.

The bands hold all of this lightly. Their working theory, insofar as they have one, is that the body is awake the way a sleeper is awake — that it knows things without attending to them, and that its kindnesses are the kindnesses of a dreamer who shifts to make room without waking. They do not pray to it. Prayer would be presumptuous, like a flea petitioning the dog. What they do instead is *tend*: they keep the swallow-cairns standing, they clear dead matter from the minor pores, they thank the ground after reading a mirror, in a small fixed sign, the way one thanks a person who has passed the salt — not because the ground requires it, but because they would be diminished by omitting it.

## The Singers

Close up, a plate-shrill is a disturbing animal, because it is only partly an animal.

The front half is legible: six hooked legs, a jaw built for grazing the soft tissue of the seams, two long forelimbs that bow across a ridged plate of shell, producing the note. The back half is where it gets strange. The abdomen of a shrill is not chitin but *bark* — the same bronze-green plated hide as the plain itself, in miniature, warm to the touch with the same metabolic heat. Cut one open (the bands do, at the borders, where the law permits harvest) and it bleeds clear sap, faintly sweet, indistinguishable from what wells in the great eyes. The oldest carvings are blunt about what this means. The shrills are not creatures that came to live on the body. They are pieces of the body that learned to walk — organs that budded free in some unrecorded age, kept their function, and never left home. The screaming chorus is not a population of tenants making noise. It is the circulation of the host, performed by its own emancipated flesh.

A shrill lives perhaps two years. It spends the first as a pale grub in the deep seams, tended by the wingless seam-mothers — vast, soft, immobile matriarchs the size of barrels, wedged into the warm channels, each one the heart of a colony of millions, each one fed and cleaned by relays of tenders and never seen by daylight in her life. In its second year the shrill comes up, hardens, takes its place in the singing front, and begins to bow. It synchronizes not by hearing — the chorus is far too loud for hearing to survive in it — but through its feet, reading the standing wave in the bark itself, adding its note at the precise phase the wave requires. It sings through heat and rain and the descents of the flocks. It sings, in the end, through its own death: an old shrill's forelimbs wear down to stubs, its note flattens and falls out of phase, and within a day the ribbon-swarms find it. The seams take the husk. Nothing is wasted. A shrill is sap briefly organized into sound, and then sap again.

The bands harvest them only at the province borders, in the zones of beating dissonance where two singing-fronts interfere. Their reasoning is exact: a border shrill is already singing badly, already out of true, already lost to the circulation. Taking it harms nothing. Taking from the heart of a healthy province, where every note is load-bearing, would be — the sign for it is the same as the sign for opening a vein — *bleeding the ground*. No band does it. There is no enforcement and no memory of enforcement. Some laws are old enough to have become anatomy.

## The Eaters in the Seams

The ribbon-swarms are the plain's only real predators, and they have shaped everything else by a single elegant pressure: they are blind, deaf in the ordinary sense, and they hunt *silence*.

A ribbon is a flat, glassy, segmented thing as long as an arm, and a swarm is hundreds of them moving as one through the seam-channels, reading the bark's vibration through their whole undersides. What they perceive is the chorus — the vast lawful roar of synchronized life — and what they strike at is any hole in it. A shrill that falls out of phase. A seam-grazer gone still with sickness. A sleeping thing that has stopped moving in a place where everything moves. The swarm flows toward the silence, surrounds it, and the silence becomes permanent and then becomes ribbons.

The consequence, worked out over evolutionary ages, is a fauna that survives by *advertising*. Nothing on the back of the green hides. The glass-snails that graze the dawn condensation chime as they drag their mineral shells; the seam-hoppers drum; the night-walking pad-lizards keep up a continuous low chuckle in their throats even in sleep, a watch-fire of sound. Camouflage here is not stillness but rhythm — being so regular, so lawful, so woven into the chorus that the blind hunters read you as part of the circulation and pass through you like water through a grate. The swarms, in this sense, are not the chorus's enemy but its discipline. They cull whatever falls out of the music. The bands understand this perfectly and treat the ribbons with the respectful distaste one accords a strict and useful official: they sleep in shifts, they keep their chimes ringing, and they teach their children, before almost anything else, that on the back of the green it is the quiet ones who are eaten.

## The Wardens of the Eyes

At every great sap-eye lives a small herd — never more than a dozen — of the animals the bands call *eye-wardens*, and the law that gives the eyes to the animals is, in practice, the law that gives the eyes to them.

A warden is the largest thing that walks the plain: dome-backed, gray-green, slow as drifting weather, somewhere between a tortoise and a hill. It does almost nothing. It stands at its eye, lowers a long pleated snout into the welling sap, and drinks — and the drinking is the point, because the eyes, left alone, silt closed with their own sugars within a season. The wardens' patient suction keeps them open. They are not guests at the well; they are the well's maintenance, and have been for so long that the mineral rings around the oldest eyes show their tooth-marks layer by layer, going down through centuries like a signature repeated until it becomes a groove.

They are also the only silent things the ribbons never touch. The bands debated this for generations — the carvings preserve three theories — before the listeners settled it: a warden is not silent. Its body hums, far below hearing, at the fundamental of the vane-chord itself. Lying against a warden's flank, the deaf elders say, is like lying against the plain, only softer. To a ribbon-swarm reading the bark, a warden does not register as an animal at all. It registers as ground. Whether this is mimicry or kinship — whether the wardens, like the shrills, are walking pieces of the host — the bands decline to decide. They simply extend the courtesy both readings demand. A human may drink at a great eye only after the wardens have drunk, only from the downstream lip, and only with the flat-palm sign given first. The wardens, for their part, tolerate humans at a distance of about ten paces, have done so for all of remembered time, and have never once shortened it.

## The Travelers Overhead

The flocks deserve their own accounting, briefly, because the bands account for them constantly. The four-winged brass-voiced things that cross in hour-long fronts are not residents — they nest somewhere off the world's edge, in lands no band has seen — but their passage is so regular, so enormous, and so consequential that the plain's whole economy is organized around digestion of their visits: the gleaning of dropped feathers (which make the best fletching and the warmest bedding on the continent), of cracked eggs, of the fallen young, of the rich droppings that the bark absorbs within a day but that can be gathered within the hour and burned like resin.

A few winged things do stay. The tower-fronds, alone in all the plain, host residents in their high unfurling crowns: the *spark-keepers*, small soot-black fliers that nest in the lightning-scars and ride out every strike a few feet from the channel, for reasons no one has established, emerging afterward with their feathers crackling and standing on end, apparently exhilarated. The bands regard them as slightly mad and faintly holy, and will not harvest copper from a scar where spark-keepers nest until the birds have moved on.

## The People of the Circuits

And then the humans — thirty to a band, three or four eyes to a circuit, a few hundred bands to the whole continental back of the thing, thin as a film of dew and just as persistent.

Their bodies are the plain's first text. Dark, lean, long-limbed, built to shed heat; soles thickened and polished by the glassy trails until foot and path are nearly the same material; a gait tuned to the rise and dip of the cupped plates, so that a band on the move seems to flow rather than walk. Their hearing describes the arc of a life: children hear everything and flinch at nothing; adults hear the chorus as weather, present but unattended; and the elders, by sixty, have gone deaf to the roar entirely and have crossed over into the other sense — they hear with their bones. The most respected person in any band is a *listener*: an old man or woman who lies face-down on the bare bark for an hour at a time, sternum to the plain, reading the chord and its harmonics directly through the skeleton, and who rises to report the body's condition in sign — *flow strong in the south channels; a province faltering two days east; rain in four; all well*. Deafness here is not loss. It is graduation.

They keep two languages and the division between them organizes everything. The day-tongue is sign: rapid, spatial, gorgeous, capable of carrying argument, navigation, comedy, and grief across forty feet of standing roar. It is the public language, and a person's public name is a sign-shape, a gesture. The dawn-tongue is voice — used only in the muffled dark of the cone-houses and in the one hushed hour before sunrise when the seams run black and the chorus rests — and it is the language of intimacy and archive: lullabies, genealogies, the recitation of the wall-carvings, the spoken name that each person also carries and that perhaps five people living will ever hear. To speak someone's voice-name aloud is roughly what an embrace is elsewhere. The bands say that sign is for living and voice is for keeping, and a marriage is made, formally, by the exchange of voice-names in the pre-dawn quiet, witnessed by whoever shares the cone.

Courtship, though — courtship belongs to the *still-lines*. Where two shrill provinces border, their interference produces, here and there along the line, moving nodes of near-perfect cancellation: pockets a few dozen paces wide where the roar collapses into an eerie, pressurized hush. These are the plain's commons. Bands whose circuits adjoin meet at the still-lines to trade copper and amber and feathers, to settle disputes (judgment must be spoken aloud, in the open air, which on most of the plain is impossible — so justice, like marriage, has an acoustic geography), and to let their young people stand together in the strange quiet and hear one another's actual voices for the first time. The nodes drift as the provinces shift their cycles, sometimes miles in a decade, and tracking them is a recognized craft; the carvings devote whole walls to the slow migration of the meeting-places, so that a band returning after twenty years can calculate where the silence has gone.

Their work is what the original account suggests — tapping, gleaning, cold-hammered copper, the patient keeping of the cone-walls by carvers who serve the wall the way listeners serve the chord — but it is worth adding what they do not do. They do not store beyond need; the body provides on schedule, and hoarding is regarded as a failure of attention, like packing food for a walk across your own kitchen. They do not fight other bands; the plain is too large and the eyes too generous for the usual arithmetic of war, and the few recorded killings in the carvings are each followed by the same notation, the sign for *swallow*, meaning the killer was given alive to a soft place — a punishment so total and so old that it now functions purely as a story.

Which brings their dead. A band's dead go to the swallows: carried, wrapped in dropped flock-feathers, to the nearest softening basin, laid in its yielding center with the voice-name spoken once over the body — the last time it will ever be said — and left for the slow absorption, the going-down. The bands do not say the dead are destroyed. They say the dead are *kept*, the way the body keeps everything, drawn into the warm interior, into the mile and a half of living dark beneath the plates. They do not say — not in the carvings, not in the day-tongue, not officially in any tongue — what they think this has to do with the second chorus, the faint singing that rises past the rim from the forest of trunks below. But it is noticed that the dawn-tongue's word for the underside and its word for *the kept* are the same word with a softened ending. It is noticed that no band will travel within a day of the rim in the season after a death. And it is noticed that the Edge-walkers — the one band whose circuit runs along the rim itself, a strange, gentle, slightly formal people whom the other bands treat with the deference owed to those who live near a door — hang copper chimes out over the brink on long frond-rib poles, tuned to no flock and no weather, and that when asked in sign what the chimes are for, they answer with the gesture that means *answering*.

## The Other Chorus

Of what actually lives below — on the underside, among the trunks, in the hot dark the great body stands on — honesty permits only this much:

It sings. The Edge-walkers, whose listeners lie at the rim with their bones against the curving-over bark, report that the under-chorus is not an echo of the upper one. It is structured differently — slower, deeper, pitched to the trunk-wood rather than the plate-hide — and it moves in long phrases that the upper chorus does not contain. But the two are not strangers. The rim-listeners are unanimous, across all their generations of carvings, on one observation: when the upper chorus falters — when a province sickens, when the plague-silences open in the singing — the under-chorus *swells toward the gap*, from below, as if pressing fluid up into a failing vessel. Two choruses, a mile and a half apart, sustaining one circulation from either side, like the systole and diastole of something too large to call a heart.

Whether what makes that lower music has hands, or eyes, or names; whether it tends the trunks the way the bands tend the pores; whether it keeps carvings of its own that decline, in their turn, to mention the bright loud country overhead — no one on the back of the green can say, and no one goes down to ask. Not from fear, the Edge-walkers insist, and not from law. From the same instinct that keeps a guest, however long-settled, out of the rooms that were never offered. The body is large. It keeps two surfaces the way a sleeper keeps two hands, and it has, in ten thousand years, given no sign that it wishes them to meet.

So the people of the circuits live on the one hand offered to them, in the heat and the light and the enormous lawful roar, tapping and gleaning and signing across the noise, lying down at last in the soft places to be kept — and the plain carries them, as it carries everything, the way a sleeping animal carries the small lives in its fur: without effort, without acknowledgment, and without, so far as anyone can tell, the slightest intention of waking.
