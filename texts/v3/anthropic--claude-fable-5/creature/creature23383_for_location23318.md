**The census problem**

The honest first answer is that nothing lives here, and the honest second answer is that the first answer is a failure of instrumentation. Your eye is a shutter that stays open for hours. The residents of the Anvil Plateau require a shutter that stays open for decades. They are not hidden — nothing on this plateau is hidden; there is nowhere to hide, no soil, no shade that lasts, no water to sink into. They are simply *slow*, and slowness, past a certain threshold, is a better camouflage than any coloration, because it does not fool the eye. It fools the part of the mind that decides what counts as a thing happening.

So take the census patiently, the way everything here would want it taken.

The millworts count first, and they count as more than vegetation. Each one is a resident in the fullest sense: it occupies an address, holds it for centuries, keeps a schedule, produces, banks its production in the apron at its feet, and participates — as you'll see — in an economy and a correspondence. A millwort's biography is not eventful, but it is not empty. It knows the sun's angle to a fineness no instrument you carry could match, because the sun's angle is its payroll. It knows the warmth coming up through the rock beneath its root-collar, and it knows when that warmth varies by a thousandth of a degree, because — and this is the first thread to pull — the warmth *carries traffic*.

But the millworts were never the mystery. The mystery was the aprons. The pellets that are sometimes gone, *though nothing is ever seen taking them*.

Something takes them.

---

**The porters**

Go back to the great rhomboid plates, the slabs the size of city blocks, tilted at the angles they froze in. Most of them are exactly what they look like: poured stone, dead as an anvil. But walk the plateau long enough — months, which is a heartbeat here — laying your palm flat on slab after slab, and eventually you will find one that is warm. Not Caldron-warm, not formation-heat. *Body*-warm, a degree and a half above the bedrock around it, with the warmth concentrated low along one edge, the way heat pools in a sleeping animal's belly.

Someone will eventually call them porters. The plateau, as usual, declines to confirm.

A porter is between ten and forty feet long, low, slab-backed, its upper surface indistinguishable from the country rock down to the conchoidal gloss on its edges — which it *maintains*, refreshing its sham fracture-faces from beneath as they would otherwise dull, the single piece of vanity in its entire existence. The living part is the underside: a dense plantation of glassy feet, thousands of them, each one a piston the size of a finger, each one taking roughly a week to complete a step. A porter in full stride covers between two and four yards a year. There is no faster gait. There is no need for one. Nothing here is going anywhere, except the porters, who are going everywhere, at a pace below the threshold of witness.

They are the logistics layer of the plateau. They graze the feedstock aprons — drawing the pellets up through seams in their undersides, pellet by pellet, over months — and they carry. A time-lapse of a single century, if anyone could mount one, would show the plateau transformed: the empty forty-mile vista revealed as a *harbor*, slabs in slow procession along routes the belt-vines have surveyed and pegged out for them, freight moving between millworts, between terraces, and — note this, it matters — preferentially *inward*. Map the aprons sometime. The ones near the rim stay full for decades. The ones within a few miles of the Caldron empty almost as fast as they are laid down. The whole economy of the plateau drains gently toward its center, like water finding a level in a place that has never had water.

Whether the porters eat any of what they carry, or are paid in some fraction of it, or are themselves a kind of cargo that has learned to move itself, the plateau does not say. They have no eyes, no forward end, no apparent senses but heat and pressure. They navigate by the veins.

---

**What runs in the veins**

The pale lamellar mineral — the not-quite-quartz, the mica that decided to become metal — is not ornament. It conducts heat the way copper conducts current: fast, directionally, with almost no loss. The veins are the plateau's wiring, and the wiring carries signal.

Here is how you confirm it, if you have the patience. Find a millwort rooted on a vein outcrop — nearly all of them are; map the millworts from a terrace lip and you are mapping the vein network, the way streetlights map a road at night. Put your ear to the trunk and listen to the ticking-over. Then walk a quarter mile along the same vein to the next millwort and listen again. The rhythms are not identical. But they are *related* — phrases of the first turning up in the second, hours later, transposed, answered. The ticking is not only mechanism. Some fraction of it is read and write: pulses of warmth pressed into the vein at the root-collar, micro-degrees riding the mineral at the speed of conduction, arriving at the neighbors over hours, at the far terraces over weeks, at the rim from the center over the better part of a year.

There is, in other words, a nervous system on the Anvil Plateau after all. It is simply not made of nerve, and its thoughts are forty miles wide and take seasons to complete. The moss-hollows are part of it — those ten-thousand-fold enamel mirrors turning in silent unison are not each tracking the sun independently; they are *synchronized*, drilled, receiving their angle from below and executing it together, repeater stations dressed as ground cover. The belt-vines lay their catenary lines along buried veins almost without exception, the surface circulation shadowing the deep one. The porters steer by vein-warmth through their bellies, reading the traffic as they cross it, and the traffic — one suspects — reads *them*: notes the freight, the heading, the schedule.

Every signal path on the plateau, traced far enough, runs to one place.

---

**The resident of the Caldron**

You can hold several theories about the Caldron, and the plateau will not help you choose between them, so hold them all for a while.

It is a heat source, and the millworts ring it facing inward because they are heat-tropic. That is the modest theory, and it is true as far as it goes, and it does not explain why the feedstock flows toward it, or why the vein network converges on it like longitude on a pole, or why the hexagonal cracks across its surface are not random the way cooling joints are random but graded — finer toward the crown, coarser at the skirts, the patterning of a *carapace*, or a faceted compound surface, or a face.

It is the foundry. The plateau was poured, and the Caldron is the crucible it was poured from, still warm, still rising as the last of the melt comes up; the millworts face it the way castings would face their mold, if castings faced anything. A genealogy, not a devotion.

Or — and this is the theory that becomes harder to put down the longer you stand on the rim of that mile-wide dome, feeling it warmer through your boots than anywhere else on the plateau — it is the inhabitant. The one inhabitant. A fraction of an inch a year, the crust rises. Run the arithmetic and the emergence, if emergence is what this is, completes on the order of a hundred million years from now: the longest birth in the world, attended the whole way. Because that is what the rest of the plateau resolves into, once you have seen the porters and heard the veins: *attendance*. The millworts fix carbon out of the air into solid stock. The porters haul the stock inward. The veins carry the schedule outward. The aprons near the center empty because the center is consuming — or accumulating, or being *built*, course by course, from the inside, out of sunlight converted patiently into substance and walked to the site a pellet at a time across centuries.

A factory floor the size of a province, in production forever, output unknown — except the output was never going to ship anywhere. The output is at the center, under the hexagons, getting larger by a fraction of an inch a year.

---

**Population: one**

So the final census reads strangely. You can count it as thousands — every millwort a citizen, every porter a teamster, the moss-hollows a choir, the belt-vines a survey corps. Or you can count it as *one*, and ask whether the millworts live on the plateau the way you live in a town, or the way your hands live in your house. The veins make the second reading hard to escape. Nothing here is independent. Nothing here even has the privacy to be independent; everything is on the network, ticking its accounts inward, taking its angles from below. The plateau is not a habitat with residents. It is a resident with a habitat-shaped body, two and a half miles up, lying in the sun, slowly finishing itself.

Which leaves you, the visitor, in an odd position in the record. You are, almost certainly, the only animal in the plateau's history — the only nervous system that has ever crossed it at nerve speed. Your heartbeat, conducted faintly down through your boot soles into the vein you have been standing on without knowing it, is the highest-frequency signal the network has ever carried: a flutter, a brightness, seventy events a minute in a place where one revolution per hour is haste. The pulse of your visit is in the wiring now, propagating inward at the speed of warm stone, and it will arrive at the center years after you have climbed back down through the gap in the rim-rock and forgotten which year you came.

Nothing here is lost. The place runs in perfect tolerance, and everything that enters the schedule stays in the schedule. Somewhere under the hexagons, at a tempo that makes geology look fidgety, the Anvil Plateau will spend a very long time deciding what you were.
