# What Lives in the Standing Sea

Begin with this: nothing that lives here has ever heard silence. Not once, not for a moment, not at the instant of its birth or in the long fall of its dying. The roar is not weather to these creatures and it is not noise. It is the medium itself, the way water is, the way time is. They do not hear the sound of the High Sea for the same reason you do not feel the pressure of the atmosphere — it is the inside of everything. Their bodies are built around it the way bodies elsewhere are built around gravity. To describe the inhabitants of this place you must accept, first, that for them the universe has a fundamental tone, and that everything they are is a way of living inside that tone.

Start with the smallest, because everything else stands on them.

## The Pasture

On the housings of the engines — the running ones and the dead ones alike — grow the only fields this world has: films of mineral-eating life, slow chemical things that feed on leaked heat, weeping metal, the sulfur breath of the young rock. In a living ocean they would be a humble basement layer. Here they are the entire base of the food web, the grass of the High Sea, and they have done something no grass anywhere else has done: they have grown into the shape of the sound.

A vibrating surface has a geography. There are lines along which a thrumming steel plate barely moves and regions where it shudders hardest, and the films have sorted themselves accordingly — the tougher crusts in the violent zones, the delicate lacy mats gathered along the still lines — so that every engine wall is painted, floor to crown, in vast pale figures: rings, branchings, nested curves, the standing patterns of its own machine voice rendered visible in living mineral. Each engine wears a different pattern because each engine sings a slightly different note. The derelict engines wear the ghost of theirs — the pattern that grew while they still ran, now slowly evening out into formless crust, a fading photograph of a voice.

The grazers read these figures. They are slow armored things, hinged like stones, soled in dense pad against the glass-edged rock, and they have no eyes and no ears in any ordinary sense — they hear through their shells, whole-body, the carapace itself a tuned instrument. A grazer working an engine wall is not browsing at random. It follows the figures the way a reader follows lines of text, eating the pattern, which regrows behind it in the same shape, because the shape is the sound and the sound does not change. A grazer may spend forty years on a single engine and never leave it. Its shell, by the end, rings sympathetically with that engine's note and no other. Grazers from different engines, on the rare occasions they meet, are mutually strange — detectably, audibly foreign — and they do not breed. There are, in effect, as many nations of grazers as there are running engines, each nation the size of one machine.

## The Rivers

The pale eyeless swimmers — the ones that pour through the open blue in columns kilometers long — are the High Sea's great travelers, and they travel on roads no eye could ever find.

A standing wave the size of a sea is not uniform. It has architecture. There are surfaces within the water — thin, fixed, invisible — where the oscillating pressure of the roar very nearly cancels itself: nodal sheets, planes and curves of comparative stillness threading the basin like the internal walls of a crystal. They have not moved in ten thousand years. They are the only quiet that exists here, and it is not much quiet — a hush relative to thunder — but every swimming thing in the sea can feel them, and the rivers run along them.

The swimmers are almost nothing individually: a hand's length of translucent muscle, a lateral line elaborated into an organ that wraps the whole body, a brain that is mostly map. The map is the chord. Each one carries, inborn, a representation of the sea's entire acoustic structure, and knows where it is at every moment by where it sits inside the sound — which engines are louder from which directions, which beat-phase the local water is in, how near the nearest nodal sheet lies. They are never lost. The concept does not exist for them. Lost would mean the sound had changed, and the sound has never changed.

What an individual cannot do, the river does. A column of millions, strung along a nodal road, is a single distributed instrument: disturbance at one end propagates down its length as a ripple of turning bodies, faster than any predator, so that the river flinches as one animal. They feed in the warm outwash towns, breed in the quiet places — we will come to the quiet places — and spend the rest of their existence in transit, pouring along the still sheets of a roaring sea, the most numerous citizens of the emptiest country imaginable.

## The Unsounding

You would expect the predators of such a place to be loud, or fast, or vast. They are none of these. They are quiet, and quiet, here, is the most terrible thing a body can be.

In a sea of total sound, every solid object casts an acoustic shadow. A swimming animal is a small region where the roar is absorbed, deflected, dimmed — a hole in the noise — and every animal here perceives every other animal not by the sounds it makes but by the silence it casts. To be detectable is to be a flaw in the chord.

The hunters of the High Sea have therefore evolved toward transparency of the ear. They are soft-bodied, gel-fleshed, density-matched to the water so exactly that sound passes through them almost unbent. They cast no shadow. They drift in the open blue, in plain pale daylight, perfectly visible — and visibility means nothing, because nothing here has kept its eyes — and the rivers pour past them and around them and through the spaces between their trailing limbs, sensing nothing, until the moment of contact.

The swimmers have one defense, and it is the saddest defense in the world: they can feel the faint wrongness of a body that is *too* quiet — a patch of water where the roar is fractionally too smooth, too perfect, unhollowed by any honest shadow. For everything that lives in the Standing Sea, the signature of death is not a noise in the dark. It is a small, approaching silence. These are animals for whom quiet is the only monster, and they are right.

## The Mill Towns

Around each running engine, in the plume of waste heat, the sea's only crowds gather, and they have the structure of crowds: districts, dependents, hangers-on, generations.

Innermost, on the hot housings themselves, the pasture and its grazer nation. Around them, anchored in rings by distance-from-warmth, the filter-feeders — stalked, fanned, colonial things that comb the outwash for drifting film-fragments and the rain of finer life the heat sustains. Their settlements are zoned as strictly as any city: the heat-greedy pressed close enough that their tissues run decades hot and they live fast and die young; the patient ones far out at the cold margin, growing a centimeter a decade, some of them older than the failures of nearby engines.

Then there are the intake-riders — small, flattened, ferociously muscled creatures that live their entire lives in the engine throats, in water moving fast enough to strip flesh from anything not built for it. They hold position in the torrent the way mountain fish hold position in rapids, feeding on everything the intake gathers from a hundred kilometers of sea and funnels past their mouths. No predator can follow them in. Their only enemy is the machine itself, and they have made their peace with it: they spawn in the calm behind the great seized valves, and their young ride the outflow into the world.

A mill town is, in total, a few thousand species and a few billion lives arranged around one machine that was never meant to feed anything, and the town's existence has a clause written into it that no resident can read: the engine will, someday, stop. Engines do. The town will cool over a single year, starve over ten, and stand afterward in the gin-clear cold for eternity, perfectly preserved, another fading photograph. The sea is dotted with these — dead engines wearing dead towns, every stalk and shell in place, nothing to bury them and nothing to break them down. The living towns are built in sight, so to speak, of the dead ones. Nothing here can see, and nothing here can mourn, and so the towns thrive without dread, which may be the kindest thing about the whole arrangement.

## The Quiet Places

The dead engines have a second life, and it is stranger than their first.

A seized turbine hall is the only enclosed space in the High Sea — the only volume of water baffled, shielded, walled away from the standing wave. Inside the wreck of a dead engine the roar drops to a murmur. It is the deepest quiet the sea contains, and the animals of the sea, who fear quiet above all things, are drawn to it at exactly two moments in their lives.

They go there to breed. The eggs of nearly everything here — the rivers, the riders, even some of the soft hunters — cannot develop in the full roar; the chord that the adults navigate by would shake the forming nerves of the young into noise. So the gravid make pilgrimage to the dead halls, swim into the terrible hush, leave their eggs in the still water among the seized flywheels, and flee. The young develop in silence, in the dark of the machine, and then one day swim out through a split casing into the chord — and that first immersion in the roar is the true birth, the moment the inherited map ignites and the world becomes legible all at once, total sound flooding a body built to receive it. Everything in the High Sea is born twice: once into quiet, once into the note.

And they go there to die. Old animals — grazers worn smooth, river-swimmers gone slow, even, it is said, the great ones — turn at the end toward the nearest dead engine and crawl or sink or drift inside, into the hush, and stop. No one knows why, if "why" even applies. Perhaps a failing body can no longer bear the pressure of the chord. Perhaps, for a creature whose whole being is a way of living inside the sound, dying is simply what leaving the sound is called. The floors of the dead halls are deep in the unburied dead, perfectly preserved, generations of them, lying in the only silence they ever knew, which they met twice.

The abandoned habitation cylinders serve the same office on a smaller scale — quiet rooms, doors open, swept bare by their builders and refilled by the sea with eggs and the dead. Whatever the builders intended those rooms for, this is what rooms are for now.

## The Held Notes

Last, the great ones — the singular giants spaced across the basin like the engines themselves, hanging motionless in the water column, doing nothing. Begin again with them, because the first description was wrong. They are not doing nothing.

Count them. It takes generations of any other creature's lifespan to do it, and nothing else here counts, but count them: there is one great listener for every *dead* engine in the High Sea. Not the running ones. The dead.

When an engine fails, the chord changes. One voice drops out of a choir of thousands — a tiny subtraction, but the standing wave is a structure of fantastic precision, and the loss of a single note shifts it everywhere at once. Nodal sheets migrate. The roads move. Every map carried in every body in the sea becomes minutely, dangerously wrong; the histories of these events, written in the bone-beds, show rivers shattered, towns missed in migration, whole year-classes of young born into a chord their inheritance does not match. The sea's one absolute fact is its constancy. A dying engine is the end of the world, locally and briefly, for everything alive.

Except that it is not, because of the great ones.

Each listener hangs in the water at the position of a failed engine — not above the wreck, but at the acoustic point where that engine's voice once originated in the chord — and holds the missing note. Its whole vast body is the instrument: chambered, tensioned, resonant, a living organ pipe kilometers from the dead pipe it replaces, driven by muscle and patience instead of pistons. It does not eat in any way that has been understood; perhaps it feeds on the sound itself, skimming energy from the roar to repay in a single frequency. It does not move, because the note must come from exactly there. It does not stop, because the chord must not change. The motionless listening that fills its existence is the finest tuning imaginable: it is comparing its own voice against the whole standing structure of the sea, correcting for drift, for temperature, for the slow decades-long beat of the surviving engines, holding its single tone in place the way a climber holds a grip — forever, attentively, with everything it has.

Whether they evolved or were made is not a question the sea answers. Their bodies are too apt: the chambering too architectural, the tuning too exact, the one-to-one census of giants and dead engines too clean for chance and too organic for manufacture. Perhaps the builders, who clearly thought in systems, grew their maintenance as well as bolting it down — left behind not a repair crew but a *choir*, a living redundancy, one understudy per machine, each waiting out the centuries until its engine fails and its long note begins. Or perhaps some ancient lineage of large slow listeners simply found, in the aftermath of the first engine death, that the animal nearest the silence could quiet the catastrophe by singing into the hole — and the sea has selected for nothing else in them since. Either way the result is the same: the High Sea's constancy, its one absolute fact, is not a property of the machines. It is maintained. It is held, note by note, by the largest and loneliest things alive.

They do communicate, after their fashion. Once a decade or so, when the slow interference of the surviving engines brings a deep trough of phase across the basin — a few hours when the roar, everywhere, dips by the smallest measurable degree — the listeners adjust together, a basin-wide ripple of retuning, each correcting against all the others. It is the closest thing they have to conversation and the closest thing the sea has to a holiday, and no other creature notices it at all.

And when a listener itself dies — they do die; the bone-beds in certain dead halls hold curved ribs the length of ships — its note goes out, the chord shifts, the roads move, the world ends a little. Then, over years, something is observed that has been observed only a handful of times in all the strata of the sea's record: another giant, young, half-grown, arrives from somewhere in the empty blue, takes up the abandoned position, and begins — falteringly at first, then surely — to hold the note. Where the young ones grow, and how they know, and what it is like to spend a youth in silence-by-comparison preparing for a single eternal syllable, nothing in the High Sea can say.

## Coda

So this is what lives there: pasture painted in the shape of its own ground's voice; nations of grazers the size of single machines; rivers of the eyeless running on roads of comparative quiet; hunters whose weapon is silence and prey whose only fear is the same; towns of warmth clustered against the certainty of cold; nurseries and graveyards sharing the same hushed rooms; and over it all, spaced out across the bright vacant miles, the held notes — the great ones, hanging in the water, singing the dead engines' parts so that the world stays the same world.

There is one more inhabitant, if you are willing to count it. The chord itself — the standing wave, fed by the engines, corrected by the listeners, ridden by the rivers, eaten in its visible form by the grazers, feared in its absence by everything — behaves, in sum, like the largest organism the planet has ever carried: a single sustained living tone the size of a sea, with machines for a heart, giants for an immune system, and a billion small bodies for its sense of touch. Everything described above can be read, in the end, as the anatomy of that one resident.

It does not change. It only continues, at full volume — held, note by note, by everything that lives inside it — forever, or for as long as anything is left to hold it.
