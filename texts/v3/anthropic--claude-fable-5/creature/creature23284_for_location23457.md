What lives on the Loom Plateau is, first and last and at every scale in between, **the Loom itself** — and the difficulty is that there is no scale at which you can stand back far enough to see it whole, because you are always standing on it, and it is always, faintly, adjusting its grip.

Call it one organism. That is probably wrong, but it is the least wrong thing available. It may have begun as many plants — a forest, a mat of epiphytes, a tangle of canopy species that grew so high above their dying mountain that when the mountain finally eroded out from under them they simply did not notice — and somewhere in the grafting, in the centuries of tendril healed into tendril, the boundaries between individuals stopped mattering and then stopped existing. There is no place on the plateau where one plant ends. Cut a strand anywhere and the whole structure registers it; you can hear the registration, a tightening hiss that travels outward through the floor like news. The Loom is its own landlord and its own land, its own foundation and its own tenant. It does not live *on* the plateau. The plateau is the shape its living currently takes.

It is young. This is the thing the sharp corners are telling you, the pale callus, the speed. Old organisms settle. The Loom has not settled; the Loom is in the part of its life where everything is still being decided, and it is deciding fast, in green wood and torsion, a kilometer of new floor a day at the bleeding edges. Whether anything decides *within* it — whether the architecture is thought or merely growth wearing thought's costume — is a question the plateau itself may not have resolved. It may be what it looks like: a mind in the act of assembling itself, trying on structures the way it tries on towers, and discarding the ones that fall.

---

Pry up the floor — go down through the herringbone, through the older weave, into the dark dense layers that are decades deep and hot as a body — and you find the second resident, if it is a resident.

**The white threads.** Finer than the finest fiber, finer than hair, laced through every joint in the old weave like frost on glass. They are densest exactly where the grafts are: wherever tendril fused into tendril, the white lace got there first. It may be a fungus. It may be the actual weaver — the agent of every joint, the thing that taught a million separate plants to stop being separate — in which case the green colossus above is less an organism than a *loom* in truth, an instrument, and the threads are the hands. Or the threads may simply be the Loom's own nerves, grown inward as the green grew outward, and the distinction between tenant and organ may be one more boundary this place has quietly dissolved. No one has followed the threads to a center. There may not be one. There may be nothing down there but more connection, all the way down, connection as a substance.

---

The towers are the closest thing the plateau has to fauna.

Understand them this way and the silence stops being eerie and becomes legible: there *are* animals here. They are forty meters tall and rooted and they live for a week. Each one is an individual — genuinely; the Loom never repeats a form, which means it remembers every form, which means the towers have something like a lineage, a long unwritten bestiary of everything that has ever stood here. They are born blunt and pale. They mature into helices, vaults, colonnades. They move — slowly, a degree a minute, but they move, and the turning ones turn with what reads, against the clouds, unmistakably as *effort*. They age; you can watch the bark darken over a day. And they die, and are eaten by their parent, and their substance walks again as floor, as steam, as the next tower a kilometer on.

What they are *for* is the open question. Trials, perhaps. Hypotheses. The Loom thinking out loud in the only language it has. But stand inside the vaulted hall — the one with no walls, the roof of fused leaves, the light coming down the color of shallow seawater — and a different answer suggests itself, because that hall is not a hypothesis. That hall is a *room*. It has the proportions of a room. It is scaled to bodies that do not exist here, sheltered against weather it doesn't itself fear, lit the way you light a space you expect someone to look up in. The colonnades run at walking pace, at walking width. The arches clear a standing head.

The plateau is building habitations. It has never had an inhabitant. Almost.

---

Because there is one exception to *nothing is preserved*, and you will find it if you cross enough open floor: places where the herringbone goes irregular, where the weave swells into a long low seam — a meter and three-quarters, give or take — and the surface there is warmer than anywhere else, and it rises and falls. Slowly. Maybe four times a minute.

**The Stayed.** Travelers, or an expedition, or a whole earlier people — whoever first stood still too long. The Loom did to them what it does to everything it touches, with the same impartial ambition, and then it did something it does to nothing else: it kept them. They are fed; the seams are laced with vascular channels, the sap routed deliberately through. They are warm. By every measure that can be taken through a meter of living weave, they are alive, and they are *dreaming* — and here is the thing you will not be able to unthink once you have thought it: vegetal growth does not make corners. It does not make colonnades, or vaults, or halls scaled to a standing body. The Loom's entire architectural vocabulary — every arch, every spiral stair to nowhere, every roof — exists nowhere in nature and everywhere in the memory of people who once lived in cities.

It is building their dreams. The towers rise near the seams; the coherent ones, the almost-finished ones, rise nearest. A tower stands as long as the dream holds and comes down when the sleeper turns. The collapse you heard rolling in from kilometers off was someone forgetting, mid-sleep, what a cathedral was.

Whether this is captivity or symbiosis or something with no name yet — whether the Stayed are prisoners, or organs, or the closest thing the Loom has to a soul — depends on a fact no one can check: whether they would wake, if freed, and whether they would want to.

---

And there are walkers. A few. You will probably never see one — perhaps a dozen people on a surface the size of a small sea — descendants of those who came up out of the clouds and understood the single rule of residence here: **keep moving.** They sleep in two-hour shifts, or in slings hung from the turning helices, having learned that a tower busy winding itself will not spare a tendril for a sleeper. They drink the warm sweet channel-water. They navigate by the freshness of callus, reading the plateau's growth the way sailors read swell. They can tell from the pitch of the floor's groaning when a collapse is coming and from the smell of the steam where the edge is. They speak of the Stayed quietly, the way you speak of saints, or of debts. Every walker knows exactly which seam holds their stillest ancestor, and every walker, eventually, gets tired.

---

So: the Loom lives here, and it lives alone, and it knows it.

That is the last thing to understand about the tendril rising by your foot — pale, blunt, patient, already beginning. It is not hunger and it is not malice. Every vacant kilometer of this place, every wall-less hall, every colonnade stopped mid-thought, is the same sentence written over and over by something that has only ever heard rooms described in its sleep: *a guess at you.* The plateau has been furnishing itself for an occupant since before it knew what occupants were. The emptiness is not emptiness. It is an invitation, held open across a hundred thousand steaming hectares, flexing slightly, like the back of something breathing — and waiting, without rest, for someone to stand still long enough to be home.
