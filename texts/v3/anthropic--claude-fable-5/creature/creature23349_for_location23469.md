**The Residents of the Stilling Room**

The honest answer to *who lives here* begins with a correction: not who, and not quite what, but rather — how many, and whether the question of number even applies. The room has tenants. It may also be a tenant. By the end you will have trouble telling the population from the place.

Start with the mosses, because they were almost certainly first.

They arrived the way mosses arrive everywhere — as spores, weightless, indifferent, riding the boot or the hem or the breath of whoever first carried a light down the eleven meters. Most of what came died. What didn't die was the fraction that could make a living in the dark, and that fraction has been making a living ever since, for so long that it is no longer the moss that fell in. These are lineages found nowhere on the surface now: bone-pale, having spent their pigment generations ago the way a deep-sea fish spends its eyes. They no longer build the machinery of sunlight. Why would they? The gray artifacts hand them the finished work, and the mosses have reorganized themselves around that gift so completely that a transplanted tuft, carried back up into daylight, would simply not understand what was being asked of it. It would bleach further, confused, and die in the one place mosses are supposed to thrive. The Stilling Room's mosses are not surface mosses that adapted. They are something the room made, slowly, out of surface mosses, the way an ocean makes a whale out of something that once walked.

They are also the room's great architects. The inch of living moss on every wall stands on inches of dead moss, and the dead are not gone — they are *infrastructure*. They hold the water. They buffer the cold. They are the mattress on which the living generation sleeps, and in time the living generation will lie down and become mattress in turn. If you want to know who lives in the Stilling Room, you must count the dead, because the dead have a job here, and they do it, and the room would collapse into bare wet stone without them. The floor you walk on is a population. It is, generations deep, the majority of everyone who has ever lived here, all still on site, all still employed.

The ferns are different. The ferns are individuals.

There are perhaps thirty of them hanging from the ceiling and shouldering out from the walls, and each one is old in a way that can be pointed at: this frond, that decade. A fern here unrolls a single frond over ten or twelve years, with the deliberation of a hand opening that has been closed a very long time. They have personalities, if patience can have varieties — and it can. One near the entrance leans hard toward the nearest sphere, greedy by the standards of the room, putting out fronds at nearly twice the pace of its neighbors, almost reckless. One in the far corner has not produced new growth in living memory of anything, and is not dead; it is simply done adding, and persists, maintaining itself frond by ancient frond, a fern in retirement.

And then there is the one by the cracked sphere.

That fern is the most important resident in the chamber, because it is the only one experiencing something new. Everything else here lives inside abundance so old and so even that want has no meaning. But the hairline seam in the back-wall sphere has been widening at the pace of stone, and the gift that flows from that artifact has been thinning for — a century? Three? — and the great fern rooted beside it is, very slowly, starving. Its newest fronds come out smaller. Its oldest are letting go more often than they used to. It is dying at a speed that makes its dying nearly indistinguishable from its living, and around it the moss is thin and the liverworts hang back, that fairy ring of decline like a held breath. The room is learning scarcity through this fern, the way a body learns cold through one fingertip first. When it finally settles into the floor — fifty years from now, two hundred — it will be the first death here that was not simply the dead joining the workforce. It will be a vacancy.

Below and between everything, stitching the whole population together, runs the one resident who travels: the fungus.

It surfaces rarely — that pale thread breaking through pale moss — but the surfacing is the least of it. Underneath, it is everywhere. It threads the moss-mattress, laces the fern roots, traces the buried curves of the artifacts like a hand reading a face in the dark. It is the room's commerce and possibly its conversation. Where the cracked sphere fails, the fungus carries a thin subsidy in from the richer ground — not enough to save the great fern, but enough to slow its going, and whether that is charity or appetite or simple plumbing is not a distinction the room makes. The fungus is the only inhabitant that *knows the whole chamber*, in whatever sense knowing applies to it, and it knows it the way you know the inside of your own body: constantly, wordlessly, from within.

The water should be counted too. It has a residence, a circuit, and habits. It lives on the root-curtains in the evening of each condensation cycle, descends, pools where you will never find it, and announces itself once a minute in that single drip — the closest thing the room has to a pulse, and steadier than most pulses. The water has been the same water, more or less, for a very long time. It does not leave so much as circulate, like a resident who walks the same halls.

The artifacts? Here the census falters. The plants treat them as something between sun and grandparent — leaning toward them, mounding over them, keeping them warm in the only way moss can, which is to say buried. Whether the gray objects are alive is a question the room eroded down to irrelevance long ago. They give; they have always given; one of them is now slowly ceasing to give, and the garden grieves it in the only vocabulary it has, which is thinness. If they are machines, they are machines that have been loved the way landscape is loved — incidentally, totally, by everything that grew up against them. They may be the original residents. Everything green and pale may simply be their long coat.

And the tenders — the ones who cut the stairs and carved the niches — they live here too, in the only way the long-gone can: as wear. The steps hold the shape of their feet the way a chair holds the shape of a body just risen from it, except the rising happened centuries ago and the chair has not yet noticed. The garden grew over their absence without resentment and without forgetting, because moss does not forget; it incorporates. Somewhere in the floor, fingerwidths down, is the season they stopped coming.

So: who lives in the Stilling Room?

A federation, if you need a noun. The pale lineages and their working dead; thirty patient individuals and one slow tragedy; a single traveler who is also the postal service and the bloodstream; a circuit of water with one audible habit; a dozen gray givers, one of them failing; and the worn-smooth ghost of whoever started all this. But stand among them long enough — let your breath fog and settle and become, in some infinitesimal way, part of the water's holdings — and the federation resolves into something simpler. The room has one resident. It is all of them at once, the way you are all of yours. It does not think, probably. It does not need to. It maintains, it circulates, it incorporates, and once a minute, somewhere off in the dark, it says the only thing it has ever had to say.

It is in no hurry to be understood. It is in no hurry at all. That, finally, is what lives here: the thing hurry leaves behind when it has been gone long enough — not emptiness, but this. Lush, lightless, occupied.
