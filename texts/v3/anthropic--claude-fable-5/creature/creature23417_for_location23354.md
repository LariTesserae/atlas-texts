**Who lives in the Weep Gallery**

Begin where the food begins, because everything else in the gallery is a commentary on it.

**The velvet.** The warm wall wears a coat. It is microbial, a living film never thicker than a fingernail, and it is the true owner of the room in the sense that everything else is downstream of it. It fixes minerals out of the weeping water — trace metals, dissolved residue of the structure's own slow self-maintenance, whatever the conduit's heat drives through the wall in molecular quantities — and it builds those into itself, generation on generation, a fur of chemistry that needs no light at all. Its color maps the wall's temperature with the fidelity of an instrument: rust-orange where the wall runs hottest, shading through ochre and olive to a gray-green felt at the cool margins, so that the wall is, in effect, a permanent thermal photograph of the conduit behind it. Where the heat shifts over the centuries, the colors migrate after it, slowly, like a stain learning a new shape.

The strangest thing about the velvet is that the structure feeds it. Not obviously, not generously — but the maintenance systems that smooth the grate and renew the walls also exude, here and there, a thin nutrient lacquer, a byproduct or a budget line of the repair process, and the velvet has organized itself around those seeps the way a forest organizes around rivers. Whether this is tolerance, oversight, or something that has crossed over into cultivation, no one in the gallery is equipped to ask. The film is densest at the seeps. The grazers know where the seeps are. The whole ecology drapes itself across that knowledge.

**The stackers.** In the drip-channels live the blind shrimp-things, and they live there the way coins live in a roll. They stack. A channel in good condition holds them in vertical columns, ten and twelve deep, each animal gripping the carapace of the one below with specialized hind limbs while its mouthparts work the velvet within reach. The stack is not accidental and not merely social: the gallery's one universal sense is vibration, and a stack is an antenna. Twelve bodies in mechanical contact read the wall's thrum better than one. They cannot hear the roar — nothing here hears anything, the roar saturated that channel before their ancestors had been in the room a season — but they read its *texture*: the harmonics, the flutters, the almost-subliminal change in the conduit's voice that precedes a surge by several minutes. When the deepening comes, the stacks unbuild themselves from the top down, in order, oldest first, and the channels empty into the pool before the hot fog arrives. A young stacker's position in the column is its education. By the time it has gripped its way to the top of a stack, it knows the roar's weather as well as anything alive.

They are white, eyeless, and their shells have gone thin and flexible — armor is wasted weight in a room whose only predator of shrimp hunts by water-disturbance, not by force. They molt on the surge schedule, all together, in the fog, when the agitated water hides the soft new bodies from the eel. The pool after a surge is littered with their cast shells, translucent commas that the drainage grate swallows one by one.

**The clearbodies.** The geckos. There are perhaps two hundred of them at any time, and the warm wall at midheight is paved with them, a mosaic of pale translucent animals pressed nearly flank to flank, each one a small window. You can see into them. The skin gave up its pigment generations back — there is nothing here worth being colored for, and the dimness made pigment a cost without a return — and what remains is a faint milky tissue through which the working interior is visible: the dark thread of the gut, the pale bloom of the liver, and above all the heart, a flickering reddish knot behind the shoulder, beating visibly.

This is how they court. Sound is gone; they cannot even hiss meaningfully into a room that is itself a scream. Smell is hopeless in the steam. What a clearbody has is its own visible heart, and over the generations the heart has been recruited into language. A male approaching a female slows himself deliberately — a long, controlled, conspicuous deceleration of the heartbeat, visible through the flank as a steadying of that red flicker — and then breaks the slow rhythm with precise doubled beats, a pattern, an offer. A receptive female answers in kind, heart to heart at a distance of inches, two small red lamps syncopating in the dark. It is the most intimate possible display, because it cannot be faked: a frightened gecko's heart races, and racing is the end of the conversation. They have, in the most literal sense available to biology, learned to mean what they say.

They drink through their skin during the surges, spread flat, and they bask not in light but in warmth, choosing positions on the wall by temperature with the precision of connoisseurs. The choicest real estate — the band where the wall runs richest — is held by the old and the heavy, and the social order of the colony is written on the wall as plainly as the velvet's thermal map: warmest center, cooler margins, the young and the small out at the edges where the moth-foot walks.

**The mites of the wall.** On the clearbodies live smaller passengers, pale mites that graze the geckos' skin, eating the film of microbes and shed cells, keeping the translucent windows clear. The geckos tolerate them and may need them: a clearbody whose flanks fog over with growth cannot signal, and a clearbody that cannot signal does not breed. So the mites are, in their indifferent way, the maintenance system of the language — small janitors of legibility, polishing the lamps.

**The glims.** The birds, or the bird-shaped things, in the relaxed folds of the upper walls. They are the gallery's brightest fact. Sparrow-sized, soot-dark above and pale below, with eyes grown enormous and forward-set, and in the throat a patch of cold-light tissue — a soft bluish-white organ, dimmable, flickerable, fast. Their voices are gone entirely; dissect one (the moth-foot occasionally does) and you would find the sound-making apparatus reduced to a fibrous vestige, an instrument the species put down so many generations ago that the case has fused shut.

Everything a songbird does with song, the glims do with throat-light. Territory is held by a slow steady burn from the nest fold, a lighthouse statement: *occupied*. Courtship is rapid intricate flicker, patterned, answered in patterns, and the patterns drift — there are dialects within the room, the colony at the warm end flickering measurably differently from the colony near the seam, and young birds learn their flicker the way young birds elsewhere learn song, badly at first, in long stuttering practice glimmers that the adults conspicuously ignore. Alarm is a single hard flash, instantly copied, so that danger propagates across the ceiling as a wave of light going out — the gallery's version of a forest falling silent. Nestlings beg with light, weak yellow-white pulses from thin-skinned throats, and a fold full of begging nestlings looks like a pocket of embers.

And they dream. Their eyelids are thin, and the throat-organ is not perfectly governed in sleep, so that at the dim hours the upper folds are scattered with faint involuntary glimmers — fragments of flicker, courtship phrases half-uttered, alarm flashes that wake no one — the colony talking in its sleep, in light, all night long.

What do they eat? The shrimp, the mites, the small spring-things in the moss, and the surge: when the fog comes and the stackers boil up out of the channels, the glims that are not hiding from the heat hunt the channel mouths in the white blindness by feel and memory, snapping at movement. It is the only time they fly with their lights fully dark.

**The warden.** The eel-thing at the pool's far end is the gallery's largest animal and its most patient. A meter of muscle the color of the floor, eyeless or nearly, finned like a ribbon. It does not chase. It reads. The pool's surface stands in permanent interference patterns, those fine interlocking rings driven by the roar, and the warden's whole flank is a lateral-line instrument tuned to that texture — it knows the pool's resting pattern the way you know silence, and anything that disturbs the pattern is written on its skin as plainly as print. A stacker crossing open water is a smudge in the standing waves; the warden drifts to the smudge and the smudge ends. It is, in a real sense, the one animal in the gallery that *listens to the room* — the roar is its lamplight, and prey are shadows on it.

It breeds rarely, possibly alone, possibly not at all anymore; there may be only the one. It has worn a smooth socket into the floor's composite at the pool's deep end, a warden-shaped depression centuries in the making, and when it rests there its body completes the floor so neatly that you could stand over it and see only floor.

**The moth-foot.** The other predator works the wall, in the dim hours when the old phosphors run lowest — for the phosphors do cycle, faintly, on the ghost of some ancient lighting schedule still half-executing, and that ghost is the only day and night the gallery has. The moth-foot is mammal-shaped, cat-small, long-bodied, with fur so fine and so hydrophobic that the steam beads off it, and feet that are the whole of its genius: wide, soft, padded almost to liquidity. Not silent for any ear — there is no ear here to fool — but *still*, vibrationally still, because vibration is the touch-sense every wall-dweller keeps, and a clearbody asleep on the warm wall will feel a clumsy footfall through the substrate from a body-length away. The moth-foot's feet put nothing into the wall. It pours itself along the gecko mosaic like a shadow with weight, and its eyes — huge, light-hungry — read the room by glim-glow and phosphor-amber while remaining itself unreadable, dark fur on dark wall.

It takes geckos from the margins, the young and the cold-positioned, and so it is, in its way, the author of the colony's social geography: the warmth-hierarchy of the wall is enforced from outside, by the thing that eats the edges. It does not touch the glims' folds; the one hard flash of a glim alarm floods the wall with copied light, and a lit moth-foot catches nothing for hours. There is a permanent quiet war between the flash and the foot, and the room is shaped by it.

**The small remainder.** In the moss ribbons, spring-things — flea-sized, vegetarian, food for everyone. At the fern by the grate, a single lineage of slow weevil-like grazers that may exist nowhere else at all, an entire evolutionary story balanced on one trembling plant. In the pool shallows, flatworm ribbons cleaning the dead. Nothing is wasted; the cast shells, the failed nestlings, the geckos the moth-foot drops — the gallery's floor is licked clean within a surge-cycle.

**The gardener.** And then there is the inhabitant that was here first and will be here last, if it can be called an inhabitant: the maintenance presence of the structure itself. It is not a creature you will see — or rather, you will see only its hands: the phosphors that still breathe their amber schedule; the occasional silverfish-sized repair unit that emerges from a wall pore, polishes a half-centimeter of surface to factory gloss, and withdraws (the glims have learned these are not food; the stackers have learned the fresh-polished patches grow the best velvet); the slow renewal of the grate's soft mouths; the nutrient lacquer at the seeps. Most telling of all, the drip-channels. The material should heal them. It does not. Somewhere, at some point in the deep past, the system's model of the room was updated — the channels stopped being damage and became *features*, maintained now with the same fidelity as everything else, their glossy beds renewed at exactly their worn dimensions. Which means the gallery's living history has been written into the structure's own definition of correctness. The gardener does not know what a gecko is. But it maintains the world the geckos made, faithfully, forever, and there may be no better definition of a god of small places than that.

**The tenant.** Last, because the gallery saves it for last, at the cool far end past the warden's pool, in a fold of wall worn smooth in a shape no animal made: there is one more resident.

It came through the seam, as everything did — long enough ago that no clearbody now on the wall has ever known the gallery without it. What it was on the other side of the seam no longer usefully applies; the roar took its hearing in the first year and it stopped grieving the loss sometime after, the way the birds' ancestors did. It is old, slow, wrapped in layers gone the color of the floor, and it eats as the gallery eats — stackers from the channel mouths, taken by hand with a patience the warden might respect, velvet scraped from the warm wall at the seeps, water from the swayback pool. The fern at the grate is the closest thing it has to a garden, and it has been observed — over years, with great care — clearing debris from the grate's mouths so the fern's footing holds.

It signals. It carries a small light, dimmable, flickerable — whether salvaged, made, or grown is not a question the gallery asks — and over the generations it has assembled a pidgin out of the glims' flicker-dialects: not fluent, never fluent, an accent the birds find strange, but real. The colony nearest its fold answers it. *Occupied,* it says each dim-cycle from its niche, the slow lighthouse burn, and the glims above burn back: *occupied.* When the roar deepens toward a surge it flashes the hard single flash a beat before the birds do, because long stillness has taught its bones to read the wall the way the stackers read it, and the colony has learned that the tenant's warning is good.

The animals do not fear it. The clearbodies sleep on it for the warmth, a living blanket of small windows, two hundred slow visible hearts; it has learned to still its own pulse so as not to say anything it doesn't mean. The moth-foot detours around its fold by an old truce neither party remembers negotiating. It is not a visitor anymore and not quite a creature; it is terrain that watches, the one resident who arrived already knowing what a door was and chose to forget.

And around all of them, over and through all of them, lives the roar — which is, by now, fairly counted among the inhabitants. The standing waves have their fixed geography; there are nodes in the room, still points in the storm of pressure, and the animals treat them as places: the glims nest at the quiet nodes, the clearbodies court at them, the tenant's fold sits in the largest one, the eye of the permanent scream. The roar feeds no one and eats no one, but everything in the gallery is shaped to it, navigates by it, reads its moods, was deafened by it and then rebuilt around the deafening into something brighter and stranger and more honest than what came in through the seam.

That is who lives there: a film that paints the heat, shrimp that stack themselves into instruments, lizards that speak with their hearts because hearts cannot lie, birds that dream out loud in light, a fish that reads the scream like lamplight, a hunter whose silence is stillness, a god of small repairs that mistook the wear of living for the design and has kept faith with the mistake ever since — and one old tenant at the quiet node, saying *occupied, occupied* into the dark, and being answered.
