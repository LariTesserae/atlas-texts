**The Residents of Anan-Dur**

Begin with this: you have already been introduced. You stepped onto the plateau and within seconds — within the first echo of your first footfall — you were known. Counted, sized, located, classified. The stitchers in the reeds read the texture of your clothing from thirty meters. The herds registered your gait as *not one of ours* and adjusted their grazing front by a few degrees. Something in a terrace pool stopped knocking, listened to your heartbeat for a moment — you have never been so legible; your pulse carries here the way a lit window carries at night elsewhere — and resumed. You will spend your whole visit trying to perceive the inhabitants of the Tablelands. They perceived you at the door.

So understand what a resident of this place *is*, before cataloguing them. Elsewhere, an animal is a body that happens to make sounds. Here, an animal is a sound that happens to have a body. Identity is acoustic before it is anything else. A creature of Anan-Dur is born into its mother's voice the way creatures elsewhere are born into daylight; it learns its own call before it learns to walk; it will be courted by sound, threatened by sound, mourned by sound; and when it dies, what its kin lose is not a face — no one here has ever had a face in the visible sense — but a specific note gone out of the chorus, a small permanent hole in the local sound that closes over slowly, the way water closes. The eyelessness is total and totally without pathos. Nothing here is *blind*. Several species, encountering you, will click carefully and repeatedly at your eyes — two soft wet inactive spots on the front of your head, faintly pulsing, doing nothing — and the impression you get, standing very still while a curious animal sounds your useless organs, is of polite bafflement.

What follows is who answers when the Tablelands are asked who lives there. The asking, of course, is unnecessary. They are all already saying.

---

**The ground floor: the felt, and what the felt knows.** Start small, because the smallest resident is also the largest. The felt-meadows are not carpets of individual plants. A swale's felt is, by every measure anyone has devised, *one organism* — a single filamentous body, hectares across, centuries deep, its tissues threaded through the warm seep-water like a nervous system through blood. It eats minerals. It eats current, where the buried lines run. And it responds: press a hand into the mat and hold it there, and over a minute or two the filaments beneath your palm thicken almost imperceptibly toward your warmth. You are being grown at. Not fast, not urgently — the felt does nothing urgently — but the meadow notices heat the way you'd notice a light coming on, and turns toward you with its whole slow attention. The grazers crop it constantly and it does not seem to mind; the relationship has the settled quality of scalp and hair. It may be the oldest living thing on the plateau. It does not vocalize, which makes it, in local terms, the one great silence that isn't a predator — and the fauna treat it accordingly, with something that looks, if you squint with your ears, like deference.

**The swale-oxen.** The herds first, because they are the baseline of the place — the broad warm fact of it. A swale-ox is built like the terrain it lives on: low, rounded, shoulderless, a tonne or so of patient muscle worn smooth, as if the same epochs of rain that upholstered the basalt had upholstered the animal too. And in a sense they have. The felt grows *on* them. An adult ox carries a living pelt of meadow-mat down its back and flanks, rooted into a layer of dead skin the animal cultivates for the purpose, so that a swale-ox at rest is genuinely indistinguishable from a hummock of ground — same temperature, same texture, same faint mineral smell — until the hummock purrs.

The purr is everything. Each ox runs its contact-tone continuously from a resonating chamber in the chest, a low individual note held for a lifetime, and the herd is the sum of these notes: a chord, slowly drifting, in which every animal can hear every other animal's position, condition, and mood. A calf learns the chord before birth, through the wall of its mother's body. To be in the herd is to be in the chord; the worst thing that can happen to a swale-ox is not injury but *separation from the sound* — a calf cut off from the chord by a fold of terrain will stand motionless and silent, not calling, because calling alone marks you for the hushes, just waiting, hour after hour, for the chord to drift back into range. Herders is the wrong word, but the oldest oxen do something like shepherding: they station themselves at the herd's acoustic edges and purr slightly louder, holding the shape of the chord the way tent-poles hold canvas.

And when an ox grows old enough, it stops. This is documented; this is one of the verities of the place. The oldest animals go quiet over a period of weeks — the note thinning, lowering, finally letting go — and where the purr ends, the ox stays. It does not die quickly. It stands, then kneels, then settles, and the felt on its back roots downward through it into the warm ground, and within a season there is a new hummock in the meadow, slightly richer than the ground around it, that the herd grazes gently and walks around rather than over for years. The swales are full of these. You have been walking on them since you arrived.

**The stitchers.** Up in the reed-stands and the tension-vine rigging lives the plateau's quick intelligence — small-bodied, long-fingered, gun-rapid climbers who read the world in click-trains so dense and fast that their picture of the dark is sharper than your picture of noon. A stitcher sounding an object perceives texture, density, interior structure; they know the water level inside a vent-palm, the ripeness of a seed-case, the fact that you have recently eaten, the contents of your pockets in outline. Their name — give them that name, anyway — comes from the sound of a troop on the move: overlapping click-trains running through the canopy like thread through cloth, stitching the dark together.

Two things about them will follow you home. The first is that they cannot sneak. The tension-vines hum when crossed; the canopy is a sounding-board; every movement a stitcher makes is published. And so their society — it is a society; there's no smaller honest word — is built on total disclosure. There is no ambush among stitchers, no theft that goes unheard, no approach that isn't announced by the vines themselves. What they have instead is *rhetoric*: a troop's politics are conducted in open sound, in claim and counter-claim and mimicry, and they are devastating mimics. They do the oxen. They do the rain. They do the engines — a stitcher troop will sometimes hold the local engine's drone in chorus, dead on pitch, for minutes, for no reason anyone has established, and then collapse into what is hard not to hear as laughter. They will do *you* within a day of your arrival: your footsteps, your breathing, the foreign squeak of your gear, played back from the dark canopy with cruel accuracy.

The second thing is the gliding. Stitchers cross the gaps between reed-stands on membrane, launching into pure blackness, and a glider in flight must *sing continuously* — a sustained, swooping note whose echoes off the approaching canopy are its only landing instruments, pitch-bent by its own speed, corrected mid-air. A glide is a song with a landing at the end of it. On still nights — the phrase persists; forgive it — you can hear the long arcs of glide-song crossing the dark overhead, each one ending in a chord of disturbed vines, and it is the closest thing the Tablelands have to watching birds.

**The terrace guild.** The travertine pool-fields hold a layered society of their own, and the thing to understand about it is that the terraces' famous music — that kilometer-long orchestra of overflowing lips, each pool with its note — is *partly composed*. The pool-wrights see to that. They are armored, methodical, crab-bodied things, each holding a pool or a small ladder of pools as territory, and they tune them. A pool-wright spends its life adjusting the travertine lip of its pool — building it up with deposited stone where the note runs sharp, grinding it down with its shell where the note runs flat — because the note is its *address*: its territorial claim, its advertisement, its name in the map of sound the whole plateau navigates by. A well-tuned pool, true-noted and steady, passes from wright to wright across generations, each holder maintaining the pitch like an inherited instrument. The knocking you hear on the terraces — shell on stone, patient, percussive — is partly feeding, partly construction, and partly, in season, courtship: rival wrights drum in alternation across a contested pool, faster and faster, until one of them loses the rhythm and concedes. The terraces also keep their soft tenants — the rim-eels, who live in the overflow lips themselves, bodies pressed into the falling water, filtering, and whose presence subtly flattens a pool's note, so that a wright's relationship with its eels is an endless quiet negotiation between board and lodgers.

**The hushes.** Now the predator, and the truth about the moving silence.

A hush is large — you would not believe how large; the few that have been measured run to the size of the biggest oxen — and it makes no sound at all. That is its entire strategy, and its entire body. Every surface of a hush is deep, soft, sound-devouring pile; its footfalls are engineered absences; its breathing is baffled through chambers that kill the noise of it; and when it opens its mouth, the mouth is an anechoic cavity, a hole that returns nothing. To you this would mean an animal that is merely quiet. To the residents of Anan-Dur, who *see in sound*, it means something far worse: a hush is not invisible, it is a *visible piece of nothing* — a hole in the world, moving. A stitcher that clicks at a hush gets no echo back. Where the world should answer, there is a gap, an absence with edges, and the vertigo of that — a patch of reality that has stopped returning calls — is the terror this whole ecosystem is organized around.

Which explains, finally, the great dead silences. When the chorus stops across a square kilometer all at once, that is not fear spreading mindlessly. It is *countermeasure*. A hush can only be a hole in something; against silence, it has no figure and no ground; the moment everything stops sounding, the hush goes from being the one invisible thing in a loud world to being one quiet thing among thousands, and it is suddenly the hush that is blind. So the silence you stand inside, heart hammering — audibly hammering; you are very likely the loudest object in it — is the entire neighborhood *hiding the predator from itself*, holding still, holding nothing, in a standoff that can last an hour. The hush must move first. Sometimes it makes a single tiny deliberate sound — a tick, a dripped drop — to panic something into answering. The old oxen know that trick and purr their calves quiet through it, chest to chest, below the threshold of carrying.

Hushes are solitary, necessarily; two holes in the world cannot perceive each other by ordinary means. How they find each other to mate is one of the standing mysteries of the plateau, and the least bad theory is the strangest: that they navigate by the *shape of each other's silence* — reading the chorus for the moving gap that another hush cuts through it, homing in on absence, two negations crossing a continent of sound toward one another. No one has ever heard a hush's voice. It is not certain they have one. It is not certain they need one.

**The veils.** A brief tenant of the middle air, mentioned because you will meet them as weather before you understand them as animals: drifting in the fog layer, up where the cloud-ceiling's condensation begins, live soft buoyant filter-feeders — gas-bladdered, trailing meters of filament, riding the slow warm convection. From the ground they are known only as anomaly: a patch of the perpetual rain that falls *wrong*, displaced, interrupted, a moving dry shadow in the drizzle with a fringe of redirected drips around it. The stitchers track them by that drip-signature and rob their trailing filaments of caught matter, which the veils tolerate, having no means of objection and, it appears, no opinions at all. During breathing storms they are driven down among the reed-stands by the sagging ceiling, and a grounded veil — a room-sized softness sprawled over the canopy, ticking with rain — is one of the few things on the plateau that everything else simply walks around in silence, as if embarrassed for it.

---

**The bell-bodied, properly.** They deserve more than the rumor of them. A bell-body is a great slow dome of an animal, travertine-crusted at the rim like the engines it lives off, and it does not eat the world's matter — it eats the grid. It walks the buried conduits, feeling the carbonation of the current through the broad pad of its foot, and where the line runs rich it settles, seals its rim to the ground, and draws. And it *rings* — this is the crucial thing — not as a voice, not as something it chooses, but as an instrument: the struck-bronze tone is produced by a resonating organ excited directly by the current passing through the animal's body. A bell-body's ring is the grid made audible. The tone reports load, voltage, the health of the line; a clean steady note means a clean steady current; flutter in the ring means flutter in the wire. They are, whatever else they are, *gauges* — living meters distributed across the network, sounding its condition continuously into the dark.

And the engines listen. That is no longer seriously disputed by anyone who has stood in an overlap zone and paid attention. When a bell-body settles and rings, the local engine's rotation-note shifts — slightly, slowly, within hours — in the direction that steadies the ring. Load-balancing, perhaps; impedance, perhaps; a thermostat answering a thermometer. But the young bell-bodied ring badly — sour, wavering, untrue — and they get *worse* service from the grid, and they follow the old ones, and their rings mature over decades toward purity as though toward fluency; and the oldest bell-bodied ring in chords, complex sustained structures that the engines answer with adjustments no simple load-balancing explains; and when an old one dies on a conduit, the engine at the head of that line changes its note for days. Call it mourning and you have said too much. Call it impedance and you have said too little. The bell-bodied occupy the exact seam of this place — livestock or instruments, parasites or congregation, descendants of the builders or just the first animals to learn that the warm lines were edible — and the seam, like every seam in Anan-Dur, has long since healed over.

**The fitters.** Last, and innermost: the residents of the engines themselves.

Inside every installation on the plateau — in the warm machine-spaces, the crawlways behind the turbine halls, the galleries threading the pressure vessels — lives a colony of small, many-handed, soft-spoken creatures whom you will never meet outside, because they never go outside. They are born in the housings; they die in the housings; their entire world is the interior of a running machine, and their entire way of life is its upkeep. They scrape travertine from the bearings before it can foul them. They pack felt-growth into vibration points and strip it from intakes. They carry off grit, dress seals with a waxy secretion their own bodies produce, haul condensate away from windings, and groom — there is no other word — groom the machinery, constantly, generation after generation, with the absorbed instinctive thoroughness of birds preening or ants tending fungus. The engines they inhabit run measurably truer than they have any right to after such time. The fitters are why.

Here is the fact that will not resolve, the one you will carry off the plateau and turn over for the rest of your life: *their bodies fit the maintenance passages exactly.* Hand-span to bolt-head, shoulder-width to crawlway, reach to recess — exactly. Which permits precisely two explanations. Either the fitters have evolved, across the deep time since the builders vanished, to fit machinery that was simply there — life pouring itself into an available shape, as life does — or the passages were *sized to them*: built for those hands, those shoulders, that reach, by makers who were their ancestors, or their masters, or them. They keep tools, of a kind — spalls of ceramic and slivers of the corrosion-stoic alloy, worn by generations of identical grips into perfect fits for their hands, passed down, never discarded. Heirlooms, or just very good rocks. They sing while they work, high and threaded and continuous, and the engine housings resonate, and the structures carry the song out through the great rotation-note — which means, and this is documented, this is real here, that the drone every animal on the plateau grows up against, the voice of the machines themselves, has *always been partly choir.* No one on the Tablelands has ever heard an engine without its fitters. There may be no such sound.

Are they the builders? The honest answer is that the question no longer parses here. The Tablelands abolished the distinction between built and grown a long time ago, and they abolished it from the inside out — beginning, perhaps, with the fitters, whoever they were when they began.

---

**The rule of the residents**, then, to set beside the rule of the place: nothing here lives *in* the Tablelands the way a tenant lives in a house. Every inhabitant is also habitat — the felt is grazed, the oxen grow felt, the pools are tuned, the vines report, the engines shelter their choirs and feed their bells — and every inhabitant is also, first and last, a contribution to the sound. The chorus of Anan-Dur has been continuous for longer than any species now singing in it. It has never once stopped — not wholly, not everywhere, not through any storm or any hush — and the individual creatures of the plateau are best understood as the means by which it keeps itself going: cells of a noise, replaced as they wear out, the way your cells are replaced while you go on being you. Ask the Tablelands who lives there and you will be answered before you finish asking, from every direction at once, in purr and click and knock and ring and drone, in glide-song falling through the black and work-song threading out through the housings of machines older than the listeners' whole kingdom.

What lives there is the sound. Everything else is how it stays alive.
