Eleven people live in the Pumphouse Quarter. Or twelve, or thirteen — the count depends on whether you include the coal-punter, who sleeps within the boundary perhaps forty nights a year, and on whether you have started counting the child the chandler's granddaughter is carrying, which the quarter has, because the quarter counts slowly and far in advance, the way it does everything.

Eleven, then, plus the provisional two. Plus the geckos, the eels, the frogs. Plus the figs, which everyone here would include in the census without hesitation if a census were ever taken, and plus the engine, which is the oldest resident by any measure that matters, and which everyone speaks of — quietly, close to the ear — the way you speak of a grandfather sleeping in the next room.

Start with the people, since they are the ones who would answer if you called.

---

**The Engineman.** He has a name — Casson — but it gets used about as often as the engine's pressure gauge gets read, which is to say almost never, and for the same reason: the information is constant. He is the third Casson to hold the post, or the fourth; the records are in a ledger whose pages have softened together into a single damp block, and he has never felt the need to pry them apart. He learned the work the way the figs learned the chimneys, by long contact. He cannot hear the engine anymore, in the way that you cannot hear your own pulse, but he *feels* it, and the feeling has colonized him completely: he walks in two-stroke time, he chews in it, and when he sleeps — in a hammock slung in the boiler hall's warm upper dark, among the pipes — his breathing locks to the beam's rhythm within three breaths of closing his eyes. Once, eleven years ago, the engine missed half a stroke. A valve hesitated, the *whump* arrived late by perhaps a quarter second, and every person in the quarter woke at the same instant, sitting upright in the dark, hearts going. Casson was on the gallery with a lamp before the next stroke landed. Nothing was wrong. He stayed up there four days anyway, sleeping in twenty-minute fractions, one hand resting on the cylinder casing like a hand on a fevered forehead, and nobody thought this excessive.

His hands are the softest in the quarter, oddly — kept supple by oil and steam — and the strongest. He oils sixty-one points on a circuit he walks every day, and the circuit takes him along three root-bridges and through one terrace house whose interior is now a single fig buttress with a door at each end, and he greets the trees on the way through. Not sentimentally. The way you greet a colleague.

**The Pruner.** Her name is Edith Brail and she is the closest thing the quarter has to a priest. The terms between the engine and the green — never written, possibly never said — are enforced, on the green's side, mostly by the green itself, which is the strange and central fact of the place. But growth is growth, and twice a year something errs: a root noses toward a bearing, a laurel shoot rises into the path of a governor's spinning balls. Then Brail is sent for, and she comes with her tools rolled in oilcloth — saws fine as surgical instruments, a curved knife, pots of tar and wax — and she studies the trespass sometimes for a full day before touching it. She talks to herself while she works, or possibly not to herself. She makes the smallest cut that will serve. She seals every wound. She takes the cuttings away and burns them in the boiler, so that nothing is wasted and, she says, *so the tree gets it back as warmth*, which is either metaphysics or composting depending on your mood.

Her masterpiece — the thing visitors are taken to see, the thing the children show off — is not a cut at all. In the boiler hall there is a fig root, thick as a man's thigh, that descends toward the great flywheel and stops, one hand's breadth short of the rim, and then *curves*, following the wheel's sweep through a quarter of its arc before diving into the floor. The clearance is constant the whole way: a hand's breadth, no more, no less, the root's inner face polished smooth by ninety years of the wheel's wind. Brail never touched it. The tree did that itself. She keeps it pruned of side shoots, the way you would keep a treaty document free of mildew, and when she dies — she is past seventy — the question of who inherits the oilcloth roll is the only piece of quarter politics anyone can name.

**The Lampwright.** A narrow, blinking, indoor sort of man named Tace, who lives in the old chandlery — fittingly, he says, since the chandlery always was the light trade — and who is responsible for the dynamos, the sagging wires, and the lamps that burn day and night inside their cauls of moss. He is the one who almost knows which switch is which. He has a diagram, inherited, annotated in four hands, contradicting itself in two places, and his life's work is the slow, terrified verification of that diagram, conducted at the rate of roughly one switch per year, always at noon, always with the whole quarter warned in advance, always with his heart in his mouth. Three lamps have been lost in his tenure. He grieves them specifically. He can tell you where each dead lamp is and what used to live on it — because the lamps are habitats, and Tace, by profession, is also the quarter's herder of geckos.

**The Geckos** belong here, in his entry, because they belong to the lamps. They are pale to the point of translucence — you can watch a moth descend through one, dimming as it goes — and they have never in their lineage known darkness, because the lamps never go out. They are born on warm glass, hunt on warm glass, and press their splayed hands against it in the small hours with an expression, the children insist, of contentment. There are nine. Each has a name and a tally-column. When Tace kills a switch and a lamp dies, the geckos of that lamp must emigrate, and this is a public event: the whole quarter turns out to watch the displaced gecko make its slow, affronted journey along the wires to a neighboring light, where it will be received by the residents with a certain amount of hissing and then, within a week, tolerated. The children record these wars in full.

**The Chandler's Granddaughter,** Verey, who renders tallow no longer but has kept the family habit of making things in small batches with great patience: candles still, for the punts and for the superstitious; a wax-and-resin compound that Brail swears by for sealing prunings; and a moss-felt, pressed and dried over the steam lines, from which most of the quarter's bedding is made. She is the one who is carrying the provisional thirteenth resident, and she is the one who, every year on the same day, poles her punt to the foundry pool and measures the giant water-lily colony against a notched stick her grandmother notched before her. The colony has gained, in three generations of notches, a little under two pads' width. Verey records this with complete seriousness. It is the quarter's fastest-moving statistic.

**The Grandmother.** There is exactly one, and she is everyone's, regardless of blood: old Mrs. Hale, ninety-something, who lives in the upper room of a terrace house whose lower room is now underwater and full of duckweed and, she maintains, exactly as her mother left it. She is the quarter's memory, which in a place that changes too slowly for any single life to catch in the act makes her something close to its only witness. She remembers the lily pool one pad smaller. She remembers a lamp that no longer exists. She remembers — and this is her great holding, produced rarely, like a coin too valuable to spend — the engine being *stopped*, once, for nine hours, when she was a girl, for the replacing of a bearing. She remembers the silence. She says the silence was the loudest thing she has ever heard, that the frogs went mad in it, that the whole quarter stood about in the channels not speaking, shoulders up around their ears, until the beam took its first stroke again and everyone, all at once, without meaning to, sat down.

**The Children.** Two of them certainly — Pim and Aldous Casson, the engineman's grand-niece and grand-nephew, eight and eleven — and a third, Verey's daughter Lin, who at fourteen has half-left childhood and now keeps her tallies privately. The children own the quarter in the way children own anywhere: completely, in a layer adults cannot see. They know which root-bridges flex pleasantly and which valve wheels can still be turned a quarter-turn and back (this is forbidden; this is done). They keep the censuses that matter — geckos by name, frog appearances by date and location, eel sightings above all. An eel sighting is the supreme event of a season. There may be two eels in the foundry pool, or one; the children's records, going back through several childhoods now in a tin box, suggest a single eel of impossible age, and Mrs. Hale, consulted, says only that the eel was there when she was their size and that it was *not small then either*. They have named it. They will not tell adults the name. It is the one secret the quarter contains.

**The Frogs.** The metronome frogs deserve their own standing, because they are the quarter's other clock. They call only in the engine's silences — in the held beat between *whump* and *whump* — so that their song is the exact negative of the works, a sound made entirely of the gaps in another sound. Nobody knows if this is fear or opportunity or something the frogs would not have a word for either. The effect, on a warm night, is of a single interleaved music with two authors, neither of whom acknowledges the other, both of whom have kept perfect time together for a hundred years.

**The Coal-Punter,** Marsh-side Anna, the only resident who is also a stranger. She comes in along the wide channel six or eight times a year, low in the water with sacks, and she is the quarter's whole foreign policy: its news, its salt, its needles, its outside opinion. She says — and this is repeated within the boundary with a pleasure nobody examines too closely — that you can smell the quarter before you can hear it, and hear it before you can see it, and that the moment her punt crosses the boundary into the works' breath, blood-warm, something in her chest unclenches that she had not known was clenched. She sleeps better here than anywhere, she says, and leaves anyway, every time, because somebody has to be the part of the quarter that goes away and comes back. The figs are fed her coal's ash. She considers this, correctly, a transaction in which she is a party.

---

And then the residents who would not answer if you called, and who are not least for that.

**The Figs** are the senior population, and the quarter's true civic body. It was they who negotiated the terms, if negotiation is what the slow century of mutual accommodation amounts to — they who learned the chimneys, learned the leaks, learned, somehow, in whatever way wood learns, the exact swept volume of every moving part in the boiler hall and grew around those volumes the way a careful hand cups a flame. They drink the condensate. They breathe the steam. Their hollow trunks vent the smoke, so that the quarter's exhalation rises through living wood and the trees are warm to lean against, always, like the flanks of large patient animals. Whether they should be called tame, or the engine should be called grown, is a question the residents do not ask, because the answer is obviously *both*, and obvious answers are not discussed here, only lived in.

**The Engine** is the eldest, the deepest, the one the others are arranged around like organs around a heart, and if you ask who lives in the Pumphouse Quarter the honest answer eventually arrives here and stops. The people feed it; it warms them. The figs shelter it; it breathes for them. The frogs sing in its silences, the geckos hunt by its light, the eel grows old in its leaked heat, and the water — the original tenant, the reason for everything — is lifted by it, patiently, stroke by stroke, and sent elsewhere, as it has been sent for longer than anyone living can witness.

So: what lives in the Pumphouse Quarter is, finally, an agreement. Eleven people, nine geckos, one eel or two, a polity of trees, a hundred-year-old machine, and the terms between them — never written, possibly never said, kept anyway, kept absolutely, renewed with every oiling and every pruning and every coal sack and every held silence into which a frog drops its note. The agreement is the resident. Everything else is how it gets around.

Pim and Aldous would tell you it's the eel. They may also be right.
