You will be in the city for some time before you are sure anyone lives there at all.

This is not because the people hide. It is because they have been tuned, over many generations, to the same key as everything else. They wear grey-green, not as uniform but as convergence — wool waxed against the wet, dyed with the city's own lichens because lichen dye is what there is, so that a crew at work on a rooftop reads at any distance as a patch of moss that happens to be moving. They speak below the threshold at which fog carries a voice as a voice rather than as a texture. They move along the terraces with the unhurried continuousness of the trams. Your first day, you will see the city working everywhere and people nowhere, and then something will resolve — a face under a hood, three paces off, watching you with mild interest — and after that you will see them constantly, the way you suddenly see the salamanders once you've spotted the first one, and you will wonder how a hundred thousand people were invisible to you an hour ago.

Call them what they call themselves, which is nothing in particular. They have a word that outsiders translate as "the maintainers," but it translates just as well as "the residents" or "those who keep on," because in their language the verb for *to live somewhere* and the verb for *to keep something in repair* are the same verb, and have been for so long that they are puzzled when a lowlander asks which sense is meant.

---

**Their bodies first**, because the city has written itself into them.

They are a pale people, but not the pallor of sickness — the even, luminous pallor of skin that has never in its life been struck by direct sun. Many of them have never seen the sun at all, except as the brightening of the overcast, and the few who have descended below the cloud line report the experience the way other people report fever: *the light had an edge to it, you could feel where it stopped, my shadow followed me around like something asking for money.* They do not envy it. Their eyes are adapted to the grey — large-pupiled, quick at distinguishing the forty shades of dim that make up their visible world, weak and watering in glare. They can read a face across a courtyard at dusk. They can tell, by the quality of the whiteness alone, whether the cloud is thickening or about to tear.

Their hands are the hands of people who handle wet stone and cold metal all their lives: thick-palmed, seamed, the fingertips slightly splayed and astonishingly sensitive. A panel-wright runs a thumb along a mortar line and knows its age within a decade. A mesh-tender pinches a strand of fog-fence wire and can feel the corrosion inside the zinc coating, the way you might feel a bruise under skin. They touch their city constantly, habitually, the way other people glance at clocks — a flat palm against a wall in passing, reading its dampness, its temperature, the faint deep hum of the turbines through it. Watch any resident walk any street and you will see this: the trailing hand, the brief contacts, a continuous low-grade conversation between the person and the masonry. They are not aware of doing it. Children do it before they can talk.

They are long-lived, or seem so; nobody is entirely sure, because their relationship to age is strange (more on this later). They are not large. They are very strong in the specific ways their work requires — grip, back, the patient strength of holding a load steady rather than the explosive strength of throwing one — and they have, almost universally, a quality of physical *deliberateness* that visitors mistake at first for slowness and learn, usually by trying to keep up with a roof crew, is nothing of the kind. They simply waste no motion. On wet stone, at altitude, in fog, four hundred feet above a drop you cannot see the bottom of, wasted motion is what kills you, and the city has been removing it from their bloodline for half a millennium.

And they are quiet. This must be insisted on, because it is the thing about them that outsiders never fully adjust to. Their resting volume is a murmur. Their argument volume is a slightly faster murmur. A mother calling a child in from the terrace edge produces a sound you would not hear from ten feet away — but the child hears it from forty, because their hearing has gone the way their speech has gone, down into the fine grain of the muffled world. They hear the city the way the city sounds: as texture, as undertone. A resident can stand in an interior room and tell you, from the tick-pattern of droplets off the eaves, which way the wind has shifted. They can hear a gutter beginning to fail — the runnel's note changing — a season before it fails. Their language is full of words for kinds of dripping. It is nearly empty of words for kinds of loudness, and the words it has are all pejorative, and all of them are also the words for *carelessness*.

---

**They are organized the way the water is organized**, which is to say: in circuits.

There is no center to the city's life because there is no center to its problem. The damp eats everything everywhere at once, so the people are distributed everywhere at once, in crews, and the crew — not the family, not the district, not any guild or office — is the fundamental unit of the place. A crew is anywhere from four to forty people bound to a *round*: a circuit of the city's fabric that they walk and work and rework continuously, arriving back at their starting point just as it needs them again. A moss crew's round might take three months. A gutter crew's, a year. The deep-masonry crews, who renew the great retaining walls and the vaulted foundations, work rounds that take longer than a working life, so that a mason spends her whole career on one slow lap of the city and hands her position in the circuit to a successor the way a runner hands off in a relay — and the crews keep logs, wall-logs, scratched and leaded into the masonry itself, so that a mason opening up a foundation course reads the marks of the woman who closed it eighty years before, and knows her name, and her crew, and what she found there, and leaves her own marks for the one who will come in eighty more. The deepest walls carry twenty generations of this. The residents say, without any apparent sense of saying something remarkable, that they work alongside the dead. The dead were just earlier in the round.

Membership in a crew is the answer to the question "who are you." Children are born into a household but they are raised, from about age seven, half inside a crew — apprenticed not to a trade exactly but to a *circuit*, walking the round with the adults, learning it the way you learn a long poem, until the city's fabric along that route is installed in them street by street, joint by joint, weep-hole by weep-hole. By twelve, a child can be sent alone along the crew's round to check it, and will come back with a report of preposterous precision: this panel browning at the northwest corner, this channel running a half-note low, salamanders moved from the third runnel to the fifth, which means the third runnel is going acid, which means the pipe above it has started leaching, look to the pipe. The adults take these reports with complete seriousness. They are usually right.

There is governance, of a kind — allocation councils that meet to argue, in murmurs, over where the grinding houses' output goes, which districts' rounds get shortened when a hard winter accelerates the rot — but it is thin, procedural, almost reluctant. The real law of the place is the round itself. The worst thing a resident can be is not violent or dishonest (both are rare, and dealt with quietly); the worst thing a resident can be is *behind*. To let your section of the circuit decay past the point where the next pass can recover it — to leave the people downstream of you, in time, a worse city than you were handed — is the one transgression for which they have real, cold anger. Their profanity is mostly about negligence. Their highest compliment, applied equally to masons, cooks, parents, and the dead, translates roughly as *nothing of theirs ever surprised anyone*.

---

**Their relationship to time is the strangest thing about them**, and it takes longest to see.

They do not, in any way a lowlander would recognize, believe in permanence — they live inside the proof that nothing lasts; they have watched every wall they love be eaten and replaced, sometimes twice. And they do not believe in loss, either, for the same reason: they have watched every wall they love be eaten and replaced, and it is still the wall. A resident will show you a building and tell you it is six hundred years old, and tell you in the next breath that no stone in it is older than ninety, and feel no contradiction, because to them the building was never the stones. The building is the *round* — the pattern of care moving through the material, the shape that the replacement keeps reproducing. The stones are just what the building is currently wearing.

And they extend this, without fuss, to themselves. They do not exactly think of themselves as aging; they think of themselves as *being maintained* — fed by the terraces, watered by the fences, patched by their physicians (who are organized in crews, on rounds, like everyone else, and whose wall-logs are the medical records, kept in lead and scratch in the clinic walls). And they do not exactly think of themselves as dying. A person who dies has *gone out of the round*, and the round continues, and the person's marks are in the walls, and their successor walks their circuit, and in what sense, the residents will ask you — gently, genuinely puzzled — in what sense is that an ending? Their funerals are short and nearly silent. The body goes to the composting houses on the north spur, with the leaf-litter and the spent moss panels, and becomes growing medium, and goes into the terrace troughs, and the residents do not find this grim because they do not find it figurative: the dead feed the green, the green feeds the living, the living keep the walls, the walls hold the dead's marks. Everyone is still here. Everyone is just earlier, or later, in the round.

This is also why their memory works the way it does. They are indifferent historians in the lowland sense — they keep almost no chronicles, no annals, no archive of events — and they are historians of terrifying depth in their own sense, because the wall-logs record not what *happened* but what was *found and done*, joint by joint, for five hundred years, and a resident can stand at any point in the city and tell you everything that surface has ever needed. Ask them about wars, famines, the city's founding, and you get shrugs and contradictions. Ask them about the southeast retaining wall of the third terrace and you get an unbroken account going back to before the lowland kingdoms existed.

---

**What they love, they love quietly and specifically.**

The salamanders, first. No resident will articulate a belief about the salamanders, because the residents do not really articulate beliefs about anything, but watch the behavior: the pause in the work, the careful cupped hands, the moment of attention to the small cold gold-flecked body before it is set down in the next runnel over. The salamanders are the city's instruments — they live in the water, so they *are* the water's testimony; where they thrive, the circuit is healthy, and where they leave, something upstream has gone wrong — and the residents have folded this usefulness into something that is not worship but is not nothing. A crew whose runnels are full of salamanders is a crew that is not behind. People keep none as pets. People know individual ones by their markings and will mention, in passing, in a murmur, that the big one with the broken-ring pattern has moved up two channels, the way you'd mention a neighbor's news.

The bird, second — the small grey-brown weep-hole bird with the two-note call. They call it, approximately, *the confirmer*, because its dawn call is the city's only natural clock, and a morning when it doesn't call is a morning the residents go quiet in a different register, an attentive register, until it does. When the oldest walls come up for renewal, the masons schedule the work around the nesting, and build the new weep-holes to the old dimensions, exactly, because the birds are particular, and a wall the birds won't nest in is, by general unspoken consent, a wall built wrong, whatever the survey says.

The pale cistern fish they regard with something more complicated — a kind of formal respect at arm's length. The fish live in the deepest water, the oldest water, the water that has been longest out of the sky, and nobody knows where they came from, and the residents, who can account for every other living thing on the ridge down to the moss species in each panel frame, are quietly unsettled by the one inhabitant they cannot log. Cistern crews lower their lamps and their voices both. Children dare each other to look into the deep cisterns and almost never actually do.

And they love — this surprises visitors — the cloud itself. Not as scenery; they barely have the concept. They love it as a lowland farmer loves rain, with an attention so practical it has worn through into tenderness. They have dozens of names for its conditions. They read its speed off the singing of the mesh, its wetness off the bead-rate on a fence wire, its coming changes off the salamanders and the gutters and the feel of their own coats. On the rare evenings it tears open and the lowlands appear below — the distant sunlit country, hard-edged, shadowed, dry — they come out onto the terraces and look at it the way you might look at the sea floor through clear water: with interest, without longing, glad of the glass between.

---

**A few of them leave.** Every generation, a handful go down the switchbacks and out under the cloud line and do not come back, and the residents speak of them without bitterness, in the same tone they'd use for a gutter rerouted: the water went another way; water does that. More come up than go down — drifters, debtors, the burnt-out, people from the loud dry world below who heard about a city in the sky where the work never ends and the noise never starts, and climbed toward it for reasons they could not have stated. The residents take them in without ceremony and without enthusiasm, assign them to a crew, and wait. Most last a season. The damp gets into them, or the silence does, or the discovery that the city's peace is not rest but *pace* — that the stillness they climbed toward is made, droplet by droplet, panel by panel, out of unceasing work — sends them back down.

But some stay. And the ones who stay say the same thing, near enough, when you ask them what kept them, murmuring it to you under a shaded amber lamp with the tick of the eaves all around and a crew passing silent on the rooftop opposite, lowering the day's spent panels, raising the new:

That down below, everything they built was meant to last, and nothing did, and every loss was a defeat. And that up here nothing is meant to last — and so nothing is ever lost. The wall is eaten and the wall remains. The people die and the people remain. You take your place in the round, and the round was going before you and will go on after, and your whole life is one pass of your hand along a surface that ten thousand hands have passed along and ten thousand will — and somewhere in your first year, on some grey indistinguishable afternoon, with cold water running past your boots toward its turbine and its cistern and its terrace and its long fall back into the fog, it stops feeling like working on the city.

It starts feeling like being the city's way of staying.

Which is, they will tell you — quietly, because everything carries and nothing carries far — what living somewhere *is*. They have one verb for it. They have never needed two.
