**The Warders**

Begin with the stillness, because the stillness was them.

You stood in the ward for however long you stood there — an hour, three, long enough to hear the crystal flicker and the ash-moss knit — and you saw no inhabitants, and you were wrong. They were in evidence the whole time. On the third terrace, between two frost-ferns, a figure no taller than a hand is long stood with a stone knife half-raised, and did not finish raising it while you watched, and did not lower it either. In the mouth of a slab doorway on the fifth ring, another sat with its knees drawn up, eyes open. Along the curing racks, two more. They were not hiding. They were not frozen in fear. Stillness, here, is not the interruption of activity. It is one of the activities. You mistook them for the ward because that is a mistake they have spent a very long time becoming worthy of.

Look at one closely — you may; they do not object to being looked at, any more than the ferns do. The body is pale in the way the wall-crystal is pale, white going to milk-blue at the extremities, and at the edges — the rims of the ears, the outermost joints of the fingers, the fine webbing between — the wall-light passes clean through. Under the skin of the throat and the inner arm you can watch the blood move, if you are patient, which you will need to be: it travels the way the hour-flicker travels, in slow committed pulses, a few to the minute. The skin is dry, faintly dusted, cool as the shelf-fungus. There are no whites to the eyes; the whole eye is the skimmed-milk blue of the newest crystal teeth, and it does not dart, because in a light without shadows and a city without predators there has never been anything that needed darting at. The eye settles. It stays settled. Being regarded by one of them is like being regarded by weather.

From the scalp, and in a fine down along the shoulders and the backs of the arms, grow filaments — not hair exactly, closer kin to the frost-fern frond: translucent, mineral-fibered, grey-green at the tips. These drink light. Understand what that means about the stillness: a warder standing motionless among the ferns is not resting between meals. The standing is the meal. They eat as their crops eat, supplementing with the cured bracket-strips, which supply what light cannot; but the deep steady baseline of their living comes in through the down, in the flat even daylight of the walls, and so the oldest and most honored posture in the ward — the one children are corrected into, the one the dying assume — is simply this: standing open to the light, arms slightly away from the body, doing, by every visible measure, nothing.

They are long-lived, though they would not put it that way; they would say the light is slow and they are obliged to keep pace with it. A warder's heart beats perhaps two hundred million times, same as yours, same as a shrew's — it simply takes its while about it. A child born at the flicker of a given crystal tooth may live to see the ward's lit floor advance by the width of three or four hairs. This is not experienced as tragedy. It is experienced as arithmetic. Their whole intellectual tradition is one experiment that has been running since the founding tallies were scratched in the entry throat, and every generation adds its data and dies inside the error bars, contentedly.

Their speech is quiet and mostly percussion. The voice they have is low and infrequent, used for intimacy and for teaching children; the public language is tapped — knuckle on slab, knife-butt on threshold, the soft *tok* you heard and mistook for settling stone. Some of it was settling stone. The rest was conversation, traveling through the terraces the way sound travels through a sounding board, so that the whole ward is one instrument and every house a listening post. Their sentences have rests in them minutes long, and this is not slowness of mind. A pause, in a language written on stone in a room being born, is load-bearing. Children learn early that the answer to certain questions is not owed to the asker. It is owed to whoever is standing there when the answer ripens, which may be a different person, in a different ring, years on.

Their writing is the tally, and their words are counts, and their names are measurements. A warder is named for the tooth of the vault that was flickering at their birth, plus the reading — how far the light stood, that season, from the founding mark. The name therefore places you in time and in the wall at once. The tallies in the entry throat, the ones that name this place a ward, are the founding ledger, and every literate inhabitant can recite its opening figures the way you might hum a hymn. There is no fiction here that anyone has found. There is the ledger, which is prophecy read backward: everything it predicted has so far come true at the rate of a hair's breadth per generation, and a people whose scripture keeps being confirmed by their own walls develops a particular calm that is very difficult to describe and impossible to argue with.

Their labor divides simply. There are knappers, who split the wall-flake and dress the slab. There are curers, who harvest the bracket in crescent cuts that the shelf survives, and hang the strips, and turn them. There are herders — say rather *keepers*, since the pale many-legged grazers are never driven and never eaten, only followed and cleaned-up-after; the creatures aerate the ash-moss as they cross it, and their molted leg-quills, lashed with fiber stripped from the bracket's core, become the ladders. There are vault-tenders, who climb those ladders to the reachable teeth and polish them free of dust and train the ferns back from the brightest nubs, gardening the light itself, which is the labor the word *ward* most likely names: they are not warded by the light; they ward it. And there are the dark-masons.

The dark-masons are the aristocracy, if the ward has one, though they hold no power and own less than anyone. They are the ones who walk past the last lit doorway into the throat where the stone is still raw, and build there by touch. Sight is no use to them for years at a stretch; they memorize by hand — a wall known knuckle by knuckle, a doorway framed against a plumb-line of weighted quill, courses checked by fingertip in a blackness their own bodies faintly, uselessly thin against. Their work cannot be inspected. The inspection comes when the light arrives, generations after the mason has died, and a foundation laid in the dark is found true or found wanting in front of everyone. There has never been a stronger incentive to honesty devised by any people anywhere. When a new house first takes the light — when a doorway that stood in night for six or ten generations goes pale at its lintel and then, over a season, fills — the ward holds its one festival, which consists, of course, of standing very still in front of it, all of them together, drinking the new light in the presence of the name-tally of the one who built for it blind.

The dead go into the dark ahead. To die, in their tapping-language, is *to dry*: the body's inner wetness — the only water any of them has ever known, the only water the whole dolomite holds — gives itself up, and the dried are carried past the last lit ring and laid inside the unlit houses, in rooms built by their own dead in turn. By the time light reaches those rooms there is no body. There is a frost-fern, or a young bracket-shelf ringed with growth lines, rooted where a grandmother lay, and the family harvests from that wall with a particular crescent cut, and no one calls it anything, because it does not need a name. In this place the future is a direction you can point to. Darkness is where the ancestors wait and where the children's houses already stand; the deep past is the entry throat, worn smooth; the present is the lit ring you happen to occupy. They do not have a word for hope as distinct from surveying.

One patch of the ward will teach you their single fear. On the second ring, west side, there is a plot of grey, dead crystal the size of a spread hand — lightless, unlush, bare — ringed by a low knee-wall and built around by nothing, the only empty ground in a city packed doorway to doorway. The tallies beside it record a warder who struck a slow tooth to hurry it, long ago, and the tooth went dark and took its neighbors with it and has never grown since. The name-tally is preserved but not spoken. The plot is not cursed and not shrine; it is kept the way you would keep a scar, as information. Children are brought to it once. That suffices.

And now, if you have stood long enough — and you have, or you would not have seen any of this — one of them will decide about you. The figure on the third terrace finishes raising the stone knife it began raising some hours ago, and turns it, and resumes the crescent cut it was making in the old bracket, and the soft *tok* of the blade laid down afterward travels the terraces, and here and there the stillness thaws: a keeper steps after its grazer, a curer turns a strip, a child appears in an alley narrower than your hand and looks up at you with settled milk-blue eyes and does not hurry, because nothing here hurries, because they are what patience looks like once it has decided to grow hands and a ledger and a city, and because — as the founding tallies say, in the entry throat, in the oldest marks of all — the light is coming, has always been coming, is a hair's breadth closer than it was, and there is a great deal of building still to do in the dark.
