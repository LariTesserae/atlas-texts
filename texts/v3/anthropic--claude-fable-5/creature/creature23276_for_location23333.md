**Who Lives in the Cirque**

Ask what lives there and the honest first answer is: visitors. The bowl runs on arrivals. Choughs out of the cloud-sea, finches at the basins, the slow ceremonial flies, the raptor-shadow that never lands, the night-thing that circles and leaves before light. These are not residents. They are circulation — the bowl's traffic, its breath going in and out. If you stop counting them, the population of the cirque drops almost to zero.

Almost.

**The hare, first.**

The hare is the bowl's one full-time animal, and the only warm body that will die here. It came up out of the cloud-sea once — there is no other way to say it; the nearest meadow is a vertical mile below, and no hare has business at this altitude — young, probably, and driven, and improbably it found the notch, and the steps, and the green. It has not gone back down. Possibly it cannot. Possibly it no longer occurs to it as a thing one does.

It eats the margins of the cushion-domes, never the crowns, working the same circuit of plants in an order it never varies, cropping so lightly that the domes show no wound. It drinks last at the basins, after the finches have gone, in the blue hour when frost is already forming. It has, in all likelihood, never seen another hare since its climb, and it has stopped doing the things hares do for each other — the drumming, the boxing, the flight. What it has kept, and refined past anything its species intended, is stillness. The bowl's other animals freeze when the raptor crosses; the hare freezes for pleasure, or for practice, or for reasons that have stopped being animal reasons. It will sit for an hour in the open, white on grey, doing nothing detectable, ears down, eyes half-closed, and you would swear — you would be embarrassed to swear, but you would swear — that it is doing what the bowl is doing. Holding still on purpose. Attending.

Hares live a handful of years. This one is the third or fourth. That is the strange part, and the part that takes longest to accept: the hare is always there, and it is never the same hare, and no one has ever seen one arrive. The bowl, it seems, is an office that gets filled. Something in the cirque keeps a hare the way other places keep a bell, or a lamp.

**The green, second.**

The cushion plants look like a population and are closer to a body. Dig — you would not dig; the place forbids it as clearly as a held-up hand — but if you dug, you would find the oldest domes joined beneath the gravel, root to root, a single weft running under the whole floor of the bowl. Most of the green here is one plant, or has become one plant, or never bothered to be more than one. It is the largest living thing on the mountain and among the youngest, and it grows at the speed of its situation: a finger-width a decade, a ring of new domes a generation. It does not compete, because there is nothing to compete with. It does not struggle, because the bowl has removed the wind, and the light arrives doubled, and the dew is delivered nightly without fail. It is a plant living the life plants would describe to each other if plants told stories about heaven — and like anything in heaven, it has been freed up to do something other than survive.

What it does with the surplus is the rings.

**The rings, third — and here the answer to the question starts to change shape.**

Because the rings are not the plant's idea. Growth has its own geometries — radial, opportunistic, downhill toward water — and the rings ignore all of them. They center on the deepest basin and they spread outward in arcs laid down, by the count of the domes, perhaps once a century, the way a tree lays down a year. The vegetation is not making the pattern. The vegetation is *recording* it. Something at the center of the bowl is moving outward very slowly, and the green is the wake it leaves.

So look at the center. The deepest basin: a hollow the size of two cupped hands, drunk to bare stone every evening, jeweled full every dawn. Nothing is in it. Nothing is ever in it. And yet the wind, which scours every other surface on this mountain to a polish, will not come over the lip — not *cannot*; it could; watch the cloud-streamers tear past a body's length overhead — *will not*, or does not, the way you do not walk into a room where someone is sleeping. The bowl's stillness is not an absence of wind. It is an occupancy. The air inside the cirque is not empty air. It is full of something, the way a held breath is full.

This is the resident. It has no body unless the still air is its body, no face unless the bowl is its face, no eye unless — but the rock already told you about the eye. It was born, as nearly as anything here can be dated, when the mountain was: a peak thrust up so fast and so violently that the stone is still ticking and settling, still flinching from its own creation. All that noise, all that grinding ascent — and in one small high hollow, by accident of shape, the wind could not reach and the racket could not enter, and for the first time in the mountain's short furious life there was a place where nothing was happening. Stillness pooled there like water. It has been pooling ever since. The resident of the cirque is the mountain's first quiet — very young, as presences go; younger than some forests, younger than some whales — and it is doing what the very young do. It is paying attention to everything, one thing at a time, and growing in rings, the way attention grows when it has nowhere to be.

It does not eat, but the dew gathers to it nightly as though the bowl were condensing around a held thought. It does not speak, but it has arranged to be spoken to: every dripping basin a syllable, every tick of settling rock the mountain talking in its sleep, and the resident listening the way you listen to a parent through a wall — learning, from the cracks and the cooling, what it is going to be when the mountain finally decides its shape. It keeps a hare the way a child keeps a question. It receives the birds one at a time, which is the only way it can bear them; the bowl is never crowded because the resident cannot yet attend to two things at once, and never empty because it cannot bear to attend to nothing.

**The people knew.** That is what the southern lip records. They did not build walls because you do not move into someone's infancy. They cut eleven steps and seated a cairn so well it rings, and the placement says everything the offerings repeat: steps and stone set *between* the arcs, touching neither — guests minding where a sleeping child has spread its arms. The cairn that rings when tapped is not a marker. It is a gift of the only kind the resident can receive: a sound, left behind, so that on the days no birds come up out of the white, something in the bowl can still be struck, faintly, by weather or by settling stone, and answer the dripping basins. The spiral of horn is a curve, for a being that thinks in rings. The drilled pebble is a stone with an eye, for a being that is one. The honey-colored blade goes translucent when light passes through it, and light is the one thing the resident is known to live on. They came, did the brief considered thing — an introduction, it looks like; a *welcome* — and went back down through the cloud and never presumed to come live with it.

So: what lives in the cirque at the top of the weather is a hare that is an office, a plant that is a record, a population of guests received singly — and beneath and around and composed of all of these, one young, slow, enormous attention, still less than a mountain old, lying in its stone cradle with the clouds pulled up to its chin, learning stillness from the hare it keeps and patience from the moss it instructs, listening to its parent settle, watching almost nothing, and missing, as you were told, nothing at all.

It will outlast the rings. It will outlast the knife-fresh edges; it will be there when the glaciers finally come to gnaw them round, and it will pay attention to that too, one winter at a time. By then it will be old, and the mountain will be old, and perhaps it will have decided what it is. For now it is what lives there: the youngest ancient thing in the world, three hundred paces across, green, bright-lit, and awake.
