Everything in the Natal Vault lives at the expense of newness, and so to describe its inhabitants is to describe a procession — from the things that eat the world directly, up through the things that eat the eaters, to the one small kind that has begun to eat *time*, hoarding it, which nothing here has ever done before.

Start smaller than you thought you could.

**The mist is inhabited.** The not-quite-rain that sweats from the ceiling does not fall clean. Every droplet is a broth — mineral motes, dissolved metals, and a flocculent living haze of chemotrophs, single cells and loose colonial threads that bloom in the warm air the way algae blooms in a pond. They are the true base of everything. They need nothing but the vault's own exhalation: sulfur, iron, heat, wet. When the rock-light swells and the basins steam, the haze blooms so thick that the far ridges blur gold, and every animal in the vault is, in that hour, feeding — filtering, lapping, straining the weather itself through gill, membrane, mouthpart, skin. The vault's smallest inhabitants are its climate. The first thing you learn about this country is that you are breathing its pasture.

**The wall-grazers** come next, and the second thing you learn is that you have been walking on the dead. The thumbnail tiles that fur every surface — armored in their own excreted mineral, packed in their millions, rasping — do not move once they have set. Each one cements itself to the stone in its first hours, buds new tiles from its rim for a season or two, eats the wall beneath it down to spent rock, and dies in place. Its soft body is scavenged out through the seams by things thinner than hairs; its shell remains, and is grown over, and becomes wall. Cut into any ridge in the older country and you find it laminated — band after band of fused grazer-shell, thousands of generations deep, an architecture of graves. A meaningful fraction of the Natal Vault's geography is not geology at all. It is the accumulated dead of its humblest citizen, and the living generation grazes on the tombs of the last, and adds its own layer, and the country thickens. The surf-sound you hear everywhere, always, is this: the world being eaten and rebuilt out of its eaters, simultaneously, at every point.

The grazers have one act of motion in them. When the stone beneath a colony is exhausted, the youngest tiles at the rim unseal themselves — a wet click, multiplied by thousands into a sound like sleet — and tumble, and are caught by the convection drafts, and drift, light as ash, until they strike fresh stone and seal again. On certain hours of the cycle whole hillsides let go at once, and the air over a spent ridge fills with a slow grey-gold blizzard of migrating shells. The gliders feast then. Everything feasts then.

**The harvester-lines** — the segmented, obsidian-glossed walkers, finger-length to dog-sized — are not the mowing machines they first appear. Watch a line long enough and you see the restraint in it: they crop the mature tiles from the centers of the grazer-colonies and step over the budding rims, always, by an instinct old enough to look like husbandry. They are tended-to in turn. Smaller versions of themselves — not their young; a different kind entirely, a mimic that has adopted their gloss and gait — run along their flanks and clean the joints of their armor, eating the mineral scale that would otherwise seize their segments solid. A big harvester left ungroomed locks up within a season and is eaten where it stands, still alive, by its own attendants. There is a lesson of the vault in that, and most of its inhabitants embody it one way or another: here, even your symbionts are only waiting for you to stop moving. The country does not punish stillness. It *uses* it. Stillness, in the Natal Vault, is a resource, and something will harvest yours.

**The pools** are nurseries, and the air is their adulthood. This is the elegant secret of the vertical circulation that so overwhelms a newcomer — the spiraling thousands rising on the heat and raining slowly down. Most of the vault's fliers begin as the pale swimmers: blind, soft, navigating the milk-and-rust water by taste and warmth, gorging on the mineral broth. When a swimmer has eaten enough it hauls itself out onto the hot rim of the pool and splits its own skin, and what climbs out is a damp, folded, membranous thing that stands trembling in the rising air until its surfaces dry taut — and then it simply lets go of the ground. It will spend the rest of its life in the convection columns: feeding on the living haze, mating in mid-rise (the columns over the hottest seams are, in the bright hours, gauzy with paired bodies turning around each other), and finally, spent, descending. The descent is deliberate. The old fliers glide down to the pools they were born in — to pools generally; whether to their own, no one could say — and fold their wings and go under and dissolve, fattening the water for the swimmers below. The pools eat their own sky. A basin pond is its population's cradle and its grave and its larder, all one water.

A few of the airborne kinds have escaped this cycle entirely: the floaters, gas-bladdered, never having touched ground or water in living form, born buoyant from eggs their parents glue to the warm undersides of the ceiling ribs. They drift the high country in loose herds, luminous-bellied where the rock-light strikes up through them, grazing the mist. They are the only inhabitants of the vault that live wholly in its weather, and the only ones, the builders' marks suggest, that the builders watch for pleasure.

**The lithovore herds** — the ambulant boulders — are the vault's slow nobility, and the longest memory it had until lately. Up close, one resolves: a domed back armored in its own deposited slag, scarred and grown over and scarred again like an old whale; beneath, a skirted mantle of acid-weeping flesh, the entire underside one continuous mouth that presses against new stone and drinks it. They are old. The glaze-scars they leave can be dated by their burial depth, and the deep scars say the great matriarchs have been walking these basins for centuries — that some living animals remember, in whatever way flesh remembers, geography that no longer exists. Their young ride in the lee of their mothers' skirts for a decade, sheltered in a microclimate of warmth and dissolved mineral, before they grow armor enough to face the predators and the crowds at the rock-births. The herds navigate by a map written in their own waste: the mineral they exhale and excrete steers the new stone, ridge by ridge, into the corridors they prefer, so that an old herd is forever walking roads its grandmothers laid down without intending to. They are the unconscious terraformers, the landlords who do not know they are landlords. When one dies, it does not fall so much as *settle*, and the vault closes over it in a season or two, and somewhere in the basin country there are hills with hearts.

**And you learn what strikes the columns.** It takes time, because the strikers do not want to be seen, and they are the only large inhabitants of the vault of whom that is true. They are tall — the tallest things that walk here, stilt-legged, narrow as the shadows of the columns they ring — and solitary, and they carry on one forelimb a club of fused, dense mineral that they grow as other things grow teeth. A striker approaches a hollow column, sets its splayed feet wide on the bare rock, and *strikes* — and then stands utterly still, sometimes for an hour, listening with its whole body. The feet are the ears. The bell-tone runs down through the bedrock and out through the deep country and comes back changed, and the striker reads in its return the hidden shape of the vault: the hollowing places, the rising heat, the new extrusion still days from breaking the floor. They ring the columns to *see*. That is why they are first to every birth of rock — folded down among the boulders at the very lip of the predicted ground, waiting, sometimes for days, until the floor cracks and the eager come crowding to the newborn stone. The strikers eat the eager. They eat arrival itself. And because each striker's ringing is audible to every other striker for miles, what sounds to a newcomer like random bell-tones in the din is in fact a sparse, slow conversation among solitary cartographers, claiming territories, announcing finds, mapping a country that will not stay mapped — the vault's first information network, struck out on its own bones.

**Which brings you to the builders**, because the builders listen.

Find them at the dim ebb, when the mist is rising and the herds are steaming and still. On a stable ridge, in the lee of a slag windbreak, a band of them — never more than a few dozen — is settling for the dark-amber hours, and one of them is not settling. It stands apart at the ridge crest with its bare feet set wide on bare rock, in unmistakable, learned imitation of a striker's listening stance, feeling the bells.

They are knee-high, hunched, quick. Their hide is the dull color of old stone, pebbled with small mineral nodes they cannot help depositing — they are, like everything here, slowly turning to rock, and their elders are visibly armored with age, slower and grayer and harder, and are valued for it; an old builder is a shield. Their forelimbs end in hands of real cleverness, two opposed digits and three fine ones, callused glassy at the fingertips from a lifetime of knapping. But the eyes are the thing. The eyes are large and dark-gold and tuned to gradations of the rock-light that nothing else in the vault bothers to resolve: they see the age of stone. To a builder, a hillside is not amber-white; it is a banded calendar, this seam hours old, that face a season, that dark ridge spent and ancient, and they read the country's age the way you would read its colors. Their word for *when* — if the patterned clicking they exchange holds words — is almost certainly their word for *how bright*.

They are the only creatures here that carry light. Out at the vault's margins, where the stone is old and dim, you find their trails by it: small cairns capped each with a chip of young, still-glowing rock, replaced as it fades — lanterns, refreshed by every traveler who passes, a corridor of borrowed daylight maintained communally through the dark country by individuals who will never meet. That is the heart of them, that habit. Everything else in the vault hedges against the land's restlessness or rides it. The builders alone have begun to *correspond across it* — to leave things for strangers and descendants, to act on a timescale longer than a body.

Their economy is the frontier's, same as everything's: they feel the extrusion-tremors through their feet (taught to themselves, generations back, by watching the strikers — and they still defer to the bells, breaking camp when the ringing shifts), and they are early to the births, prying mineral-fat grazers off the infant stone and filling woven slag-fiber sacks in the rich first hours, gone before the herds make the ground lethal. They follow the herds at a wary distance afterward, and this following is where their strangest practice begins. They collect the herd-leavings. They carry the rank, mineral-dense slurry in glazed bowls out onto the raw new ground, to those cairn-lines with no ford and no cache and no purpose a striker or a harvester could read, and they paint the cairns with it, deliberately, dressing the stones in the same chemistry by which the herds unknowingly steer the growth of the world. The herds terraform by accident. The builders have *noticed*, and are doing it on purpose, in lines.

A cairn-line is a generational act. The builders who lay one will not live to walk the ridge it becomes; the stone grows too slowly for that. They lay them anyway, and they tend lines their elders laid, and they camp — you can find the knapping-debris to prove it — beside half-risen ridges that match marks made before any living builder was born, as if keeping an appointment. They are the first inhabitants of the Natal Vault to make promises to the geography.

And they are the first to ask something of it in return. Their dead are not cached or hidden or eaten. They are carried — sometimes for days, the band traveling slow and clicking low — out to the newest ground that can be safely reached, the rawest, brightest, hours-old stone, and laid there, and weighted gently with a single chosen cairn-stone, and left. The vault does the rest. The new country grows over them in a season; the depositing rain seals them in; they become a band in the rock, as the grazers do, as the matriarch herds do — except that the builders *chose* it, and choose the spot, and remember the spot, and route their lantern-trails past it. Whether this is grief or agriculture or both is not a question their clicking will answer for you. Perhaps for them there is no difference. They are farming the planet, and they plant their dead in it, and they mark where they would like it to rise.

So: that is what lives here. A weather that is alive; walls that are cemeteries grazing on cemeteries; lines of glossy reapers tended by groomers who are also waiting to be their undertakers; ponds that birth their own sky and drink it back; centuries-old boulders paving roads they cannot perceive; tall bell-ringers reading the future through the soles of their feet — and, on the stable ridges, in the lee of their little walls, the small gold-eyed ones, the only creatures in this newborn country that have looked at a world still wet from its own making and thought, not *eat it before it cools*, but *wait*.

At the dim ebb you can stand on a ridge and hold all of it at once: the settled glittering drifts of the air-swarms, the steaming herds, a far bell struck once and answered once, the surf of the grazers never stopping — and below you, on raw ground that glows like a hearth, a line of small cairns marching out into the haze, freshly dressed, patient, telling the world where to grow.
