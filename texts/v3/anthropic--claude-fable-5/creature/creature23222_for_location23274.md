**Who Lives at Erebh-Saline**

Begin with the oldest residents, because everything else here is their guest.

The terraces are not geology. The white shading to ochre shading to bruised orange is pigment, and pigment is population: mats of microbial life, layered like varnish, each color a different community holding the exact band of heat and salt it can survive and no other. The smooth lips of the pools are built by them, calcium and salt laid down a film at a time, the way coral builds reef — which is to say the terraces are not where the oldest residents live. The terraces *are* the oldest residents, or at least their architecture, their slow secreted city. They have been doing this since before there was a word for salt. The original engineers, building the works around the springs rather than over them, believed they were preserving a geological feature. They were, without knowing it, observing a property line.

The pans belong to the next oldest. The rust-red that tints whole hectares is not rust and not the shrimp — it is the water itself, dyed by halophile cells in densities that defeat counting, trillions to the hectare, organisms for whom the brine that kills everything green is not a hardship but the entire and only world. They live fast, die in clouds when a pan goes dry, and leave their color behind in the crystal: the salt that ships out on the fortnightly train is faintly pink, and the buyers pay extra for it, and so the smallest residents of Erebh-Saline are, strictly speaking, the export.

The shrimp are the patient ones. They bloom when the brine is right and vanish when it isn't, but vanishing, for a brine shrimp, is a technicality. Their eggs cure into cysts — dry, mineral, indistinguishable from grit — and wait. They are in the cracks of the dike walls and the dust on the warehouse sills and the mud of the swallow nests. The works could shut down tomorrow, the pumps could stop, the pans could bake to pavement for fifty years, and the first time water touched them again the shrimp would simply resume, as though no time had passed, because for them none would have. Nothing else in town can do this. The shrimp know it. They carry themselves — drifting in their billions through water hot enough to redden a hand — with the unhurried air of the only residents holding a permanent lease.

The flies are stranger than visitors realize, and visitors already find them strange. The columns are only the adults. The real life of a brine fly happens below the surface: the larvae graze the microbial mats, and the adults, when they need to lay eggs or feed, walk *down* into the brine wrapped in a bubble of air that clings to their body hairs, a private silver atmosphere, and stroll about on the bottom of water no fish could survive, dry inside their bubble, and then let go and pop back to the surface and fly away. There are uncountably many of them and each one is a small impossible diving bell. The town treats them as weather.

Then the birds, who are really two nations.

The first nation passes through. The stilts and avocets, the phalaropes spinning their little whirlpools, the flamingos holding the southern lagoons in that pink band — these arrive in their seasons, in their thousands and tens of thousands, strip the pans, breed on the dike islands where the foxes can't wade, and leave. They treat Erebh-Saline as a vast filling station, which is what it is, and their droppings pay the bill, feeding the blooms that feed the shrimp that will feed them again next year. They are residents only in aggregate: no individual stays, but the nation never really leaves, the way a wave is permanent though no water in it is.

The second nation holds territory. The swallows are citizens in the fullest sense — ten thousand mud nests under the cooling galleries, generations deep, the colony older than anyone now working the night shift. There is no fresh mud in a salt town, so the swallows quarry the condensate gutters, where dripping water has rinsed the dust just barely sweet enough to build with; you can watch them at midmorning, lined along a gutter lip, working the mud like masons, and their nests, tested over decades, hold. The owls in the two dead chimneys are dynasties too. The chimney bases are floored a meter deep in pellets, a granular archive of everything small that has lived and died here for sixty years, and one of the night-shift workers — the one with the patience for it — sometimes sieves a bucketful and can tell you which decade the mice arrived and which decade they lost.

The ravens belong to no nation; they are freelancers. They know the schedule. They know that the man who rakes fly drift from the intake screens comes three days a week, and on those three days they are waiting on the screen housings before he arrives, conversational, proprietary. They have names for him, probably. He has names for them, certainly, though he won't say what.

The foxes run the warehouse district and have for longer than the company records bother to note. Salt-stained to a grubby cream — kits are born red and bleach in their first summer — they den under the floors of buildings built for inventories that never came, and they keep rounds the way the night shift keeps rounds: the dike walls for heat-killed birds, the screen housings for fly windrows, the nest islands in the season when the wading birds are careless. They have never seen anything green. Their relationship with the humans is formal and exact: ten meters, maintained on both sides, neither party feeding or chasing, both parties watching. In spring the kits play on the halite pavements and the crockery-ring of their footfalls carries down the service avenues, and the night workers stop to listen, and the vixen lets them.

The geckos hold the iron. Everywhere the metal stays warm — which is everywhere — they post themselves at the edges of light, pale as the salt tideline, working the margins of the fly columns. They are the town's smallest landlords; every rivet is somebody's.

And the people. A few hundred, in housing rows built for ten thousand, which means everyone lives where they choose and most choose carefully — near a working pump house, because of the heartbeat.

This is the thing to understand first about the human residents: they sleep to the pump. The deep slow thudding two streets over, more felt than heard, is in their blood pressure by now. Those who retire to the coast mostly come back within a year, complaining that silence at night feels like something has stopped that shouldn't have. They are not wrong about themselves.

The eleven of the night shift are the town's working organ, and their craft, after all these years, is mostly listening. They diagnose the works by hand and ear — a palm flat on a handrail to feel a bearing going, an ear cocked at a pressure valve to hear whether its sigh is the right sigh. They carry brooms for moving roosting birds, gently, and each of them wipes the condensate from the rails as they walk, each with a recognizably different swipe, so that they read each other's passage through the town the way trackers read mud.

The day people are fewer and more particular. The man with the wire brush, who knocks the salt tideline down off the company brick and has been losing slowly for thirty years, ends every round at the cemetery, where he does the headstones last and takes his time. The assayer can taste a sample of brine and name the pan it came from, blindfolded, by stage of concentration — a parlor trick she performs exactly once for each visitor and never again. The storekeeper opens the commissary two days a week and keeps the accounts in the company's last issued ledger, in pencil, because no one at the holding firm — three owners removed and barely aware the town exists — has ever sent another.

Some of the residents came for their lungs. The saturated salt air, already used, is kind to certain kinds of breathing, and there is a small contingent of people who arrived decades ago for a six-week cure and simply never organized their departure. They will tell you they breathe better here than anywhere on earth, and that the air being secondhand is no flaw: at least, they say, you know what's in it.

There are children — four, currently. They are fluent in birds before they are fluent in letters, do their schooling by correspondence, and leave at eighteen on the fortnightly train, every one of them, always. And some fraction of them come back at fifty or sixty, the way the water comes back, drawn down the same gradient. The town has a return current. Nobody is surprised by it anymore.

The dead stay best of all. The cemetery sits on a low halite rise, and nothing in that ground decays; the salt sees to it that the dead keep, quietly, the way the company stores keep. The same white tideline creeps up the headstones that creeps up the brick, a few millimeters a year, and is knocked back, and creeps again.

Ask a resident who lives at Erebh-Saline and you will get a shrug, or a list of neighbors, or — from the assayer, once, to a visitor who pressed — the answer that *the salt does, and the rest of us are staff*. This is glib but not false. Every resident, microbial to human, holds a position in the same respiration: lifting, concentrating, blooming, eating, dripping, returning. There is no other employer.

And the position is open. Stand still long enough on a service avenue, in the warm wet air, and the moisture will begin to condense on your forearms, because you are cooler than the town. It will gather and run and drip from your fingertips into a gutter cut a century ago, and the gutter will carry it out to the pans, and the heat will take the water and keep the rest. From the moment you begin to drip, you are on the books. The town does not distinguish between residents and visitors. It distinguishes between things that are part of the loop and things that have not been standing there long enough yet.
