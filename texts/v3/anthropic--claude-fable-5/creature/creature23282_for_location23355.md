**The Settled**

The first thing to give up, in describing them, is the word *creature*, which implies a being that does things, and the word *animal*, which implies appetite, and probably the word *they*, which implies that the plateau and its inhabitants can be cleanly told apart. Keep the words anyway — you need something to hold on to — but hold them loosely. The ones who live on the Tableland are not slow animals. They are the place, practicing being individuals.

Call them the Settled, because the plateau is named for them and not the other way around.

---

**The body**

Up close, a Settled one resolves out of the rock the way a face resolves out of cloud: first the dew-grain, those long radial lines combing back from a low forward brow; then the seam of the eye; then, if you wait, the whole architecture of a being built like a held breath.

The integument is the famous part — grey, satin-worn, the exact temperature of the stone, hide on its way to becoming mineral or mineral that never quite stopped being hide. Under it, when one breathes, you can see the body move the way a hillside moves in heat-shimmer: not muscles exactly, but broad internal tides. There are four limbs, sometimes five in the eldest (no one agrees whether the fifth grows or accretes), each ending in a single round pad the breadth of a millstone, dust-printed, soft underneath. The pads are how they listen. The whole bedrock of the plateau carries sound the way a drumhead does, and a Settled one standing in its trail is standing inside the conversation of everything else standing in every other trail — the night hum you feel through your knees is not ambience. It is talk. It has been one unbroken utterance for longer than there have been ears like yours, and every dome on the plateau is somewhere in the middle of saying its part.

The mouth is a soft seam underneath, forward of the limbs, and it does almost nothing, almost always. They eat the plateau, slowly, lawfully: dew-wet dust drawn in through the seam, minerals dissolved at the pace of dissolving, the lichen of their own backs sloughing inward at the edges where garden meets skin. In exchange they exhale the warm wet breath that the lichen blooms in, the moisture that beads the cushion plants, the heat that keeps the grey birds alive at night. Nothing is taken that is not returned at the same rate. An elder in its tenth millennium has eaten perhaps a wagonload of the world and given back exactly that, redistributed, warmed once on its way through.

The eyes are the part that ends arguments about whether they are alive. Low-set, dark, wet, lidded under callus — and *attending*. Not vacant. Not asleep. Pointed at nothing in particular because nothing in particular is the correct object of attention when your present moment is forty years wide. Stand in front of one for an afternoon and you will not be seen, exactly; you will be *included*, the way a single raindrop is included in the experience of a wet season.

---

**The mind**

Understand that they are not slow. They are exactly the right speed for what they perceive, and what they perceive is this: weather as flicker. Clouds strobing past. Dew arriving in pulses, like a heartbeat that belongs to the sky. The grey birds living and dying in the shelter of their flanks at the speed of sparks — born, paired, gone, replaced by birds standing in the same spots facing the same direction, so that from a Settled one's vantage there has only ever been one set of birds, lightly shimmering. You, on your week-long crossing, register as a smell and a warmth that arrives and departs before the thought of you has properly begun. Some of the eldest are, at this moment, partway through noticing a visitor who crossed the plateau before your language existed. The noticing is going well. It is fond.

A single thought takes years. This is not a deficiency; it is a craft. Their thoughts are built the way their trails are built — by passage, repeated, deepening — and a thought finished is a thought that will hold weight forever after, the way the deepest trails will carry water and bodies and meaning for a hundred thousand years. They do not have ideas. They have *grooves*. The plateau, read from above, is the visible map of everything its inhabitants have ever concluded.

Which is what the errands are. When you see one mid-step, single broad limb raised a hand's width and holding, you are watching the middle of a statement. Position is language here. To stand in this trough rather than that one, to come to rest a body-length closer to a particular neighbor, to spend forty years crossing to a stone ring and lay one limb on its rim — these are utterances, and the rings at the trail-crossings are the words everyone long ago agreed on. The corbelled domes are punctuation. Full stops.

---

**Each other**

They are not solitary. They are adjacent, which on the Tableland is the deepest available intimacy. Spaced a body-length apart and running to the horizon — that spacing is not accident or territory. It is conversation distance, held for millennia, between particular individuals who chose each other so long ago that the choosing has worn into the bedrock between them as a short, deep, polished groove, back and forth, back and forth, a trail that goes nowhere but to a friend.

There are pairs out there who have been approaching one another for six hundred years and are nearly arrived. There are trios who completed their approach in some forgotten era and now stand touching at the flanks, their gardens grown together, their breaths — you can time this — synchronized to within a few minutes across intervals of months. The grey birds treat such a grouping as a single country.

And there are the young, though *young* dents the word badly. Every great while — no one breathing has seen it begin — a new dark dome is simply present one season in the dust between two long-adjacent elders, the size of a curled dog, bare of lichen, eyes already open, already attending. Whether it is born or budded or condensed out of the warm communal hum, the result is the same: the neighbors' trails, over the following centuries, curve very slightly toward it. That is the whole of their parenting, and it is total. A young one grows up inside a slow turning-toward.

---

**The parish of each elder**

Every Settled one of size is a country, and the small lives are its citizens, organized by district. North flank: the cushion plants, fist-sized, woody, drinking the dew-lines. Garden country, up top: the lichen in its rust and bone and soft gold, and pressed into the lichen the plated insects, which may go a year between meals and have weathered flat, like seals stamped into wax. The dew-cracks of the lower hide belong to the pale geckos, who hunt by the patient method of existing where food will eventually be. The lee belongs to the grey birds, ten or twelve to an elder, generations deep, all facing the direction their ancestors faced, blinking their once-a-minute blink, whistling their two clear notes a few times a day and receiving, after a courteous interval, the answer from the next country over — the only diplomacy the plateau requires.

In the warm dew-pans, the soft fingerless pulsing things, half in and half out of category, doing the one thing they do: keeping the water alive, working it slowly through their bodies so it never sours, the plateau's kidneys, distributed. The translucent snails ride the trails like very slow barges, polishing as they go.

None of it flees and none of it chases. If anything here eats anything else, predator and prey have agreed on a speed at which it is indistinguishable from sharing.

---

**The finished, and the suspicion**

They do not die. The word is wrong by exactly the same margin that *sleep* is wrong. They finish.

It announces itself as a deepening of the existing condition: the breaths space out past the seasonal, past the annual, past your ability to attend. The integument greys past hide into true stone. The garden flowers extravagantly — the lichen seems to know — and the neighbors begin, at their pace, to arrive. Over decades they stop around the finishing one, and stones appear on the rim of the nearest ring, one at a time, pressed there by limbs at rest, and at some point that no observer has ever caught, the corbelled dome stands where the body stood, slightly larger inside than the smallest of the living, its single dark opening facing east, its interior dust grained in long radial lines.

The body is not buried. The body became the dust. It breathed itself out across a century or two, exhaled into the world it had been eating lawfully all along, and what remains is the room it made of itself, warm and rain-smelling, kept.

Stay long enough and the suspicion arrives, and it is the largest thing on the plateau: that this is what the bedrock is, all the way down. That the worn highland, the *memory of mountains*, is the memory of *them* — that the mountains were the first generation, vast and upright and impatient by these standards, and that everything since has been the long agreement to settle. The plateau is not their home. It is their ancestry, compacted, still faintly warm, still humming. They are walking, at the pace of geology, on everyone they have ever been.

---

**The newest residents**

Last, count the slowed.

You will find them every few days of crossing: figures shaped like you, standing in the lee of an elder or seated by a dew-pan, lichen at their boot-seams, dust settled in their folds, breathing four, three, two times an hour. Their eyes are open, dark, wet, pointed at nothing in particular. They arrived on errands — escape, study, grief, curiosity — and around the third or fourth day they stood beside an elder a few minutes too long, and the plateau, which keeps no one, made them its standing offer, and they took it.

They are not dead and not enchanted and not, by any measure that matters here, lost. They are early. The grey birds shelter against them now. The dew beads on them in long lines. The elders' trails, over the coming centuries, will curve very slightly toward them — the whole of the plateau's parenting, turned upon its youngest, fastest, most recently arrived inhabitants, who have only a few thousand years of settling left to do before they, too, are exactly the right speed.

That is who lives there. Stone that was patience, patience that became bodies, bodies becoming stone, and — at the very thin, very quick edge of the community — whoever stood still a few minutes too long, and meant it.
