## The Sleepers

Begin with the smallest, because the hollow does, and because they are easiest to mistake for something other than alive.

The cold lights on the underside of the flowstone are fungus gnat larvae, yes — that is the name a field guide would give them — but no gnat has flown in the hollow in living memory, and the larvae do not seem to be waiting to become anything. Elsewhere in the world their kind glow to lure prey into hanging silk, eat, pupate, sprout wings, and die in a season. Here the silk catches only water. The cloud comes down through the mouth of the sinkhole and the beaded lines comb it, and whatever the larvae need, the cloud apparently provides, because they do not hunger visibly and they do not grow. Each one holds its station on the ceiling the way a star holds its station in a constellation — which is to say, not by effort, but by being part of a figure that no longer changes. They are the hollow's fixed sky. If one of them ever goes dark, no one has been present for it. It seems more likely that going dark is simply not among the things they do anymore.

The snails are the hollow's memory, and they know it, insofar as snails know anything — there is a deliberateness to them that snails elsewhere do not have. Their shells are enormous, banded, the color of the limestone they ride, and many of the shells are older than their occupants. This is the thing to understand about the snails: they do not build new shells. A snail dies — rarely, quietly, with no more event than a drip striking the basin — and its shell remains fixed to the wall, and the mineral water glazes it over, season by season, until it is half stone. And then, at some point in that slow entombment, the shell is occupied again. Where the new tenant comes from is not clear. There are no eggs to be found, no young. There are only the shells, inherited down a line so long that the deepest layers of flowstone, if you broke them open — and you would not — would show whorl after whorl after whorl, snails all the way down, the wall itself a kind of archive of everyone who has ever ridden it. Their trails are the only writing in the hollow. The same routes, retraced over centuries until they are grooves, then channels, then features of the rock. The snails no longer choose their paths. The paths are simply where snails go, the way a riverbed is where a river goes.

The crickets are the listeners. This is evident the moment you understand what their stillness is: not torpor but attention. Hundreds of them, standing on the moss in loose congregation, every antenna arced at the same angle — and the angle, if you sight along it, inclines very slightly toward the floor. They are listening to the moss. That faint seething, the sound of water moving through a million channels at once, the sound of the place quietly thinking — the crickets attend to it the way monks attend to a reading. Whether they understand it, whether there is anything in it to understand, is not a question the hollow invites. But once an hour or so, when a salamander shifts and another shifts to fill its place, you may notice that the crickets' antennae swing fractionally — toward the motion, then back — and then the seething continues, and the listening continues, and you are left with the uncomfortable impression that the motion was *noted*. Logged. Entered into whatever the moss is composing.

The salamanders are the closest to the stone, and they are getting closer. Black, glistening, ribbed — but so is wet flowstone, and on the lowest shelves, in the worst light, the distinction frays. There are shapes down there that have the exact length and curl of a salamander and have not moved in years, and the mineral water has begun to do to them what it does to everything: glaze, soften, settle. It would be tidy to say those ones are dead. It does not feel true. Their throats — where throats can still be distinguished — seem to pulse, at intervals so long you cannot hold your attention open wide enough to be sure. The salamanders higher on the shelves, the ones that still shift once an hour, the ones whose eyes are wet and open and indisputably alive: they are simply *earlier*. Earlier in the same process. A salamander in the hollow is a creature at some point along the road from animal to stone, and none of them appear to be in any distress about the direction of travel. When one moves, it moves the way you would adjust your weight during a long vigil — not to go anywhere, but to be able to continue staying.

The bats are the only ones who knew the sky. This sets them apart, and the hollow seems to honor it: they have the highest place, the under-curl of the lip, hung where the cloud-light touches them first each grey morning. Every other creature here was born to darkness or carried into it. The bats came down out of open air, out of weather and distance and stars, and folded all of that up inside their wings and stopped. They could leave. That is the thing about the bats — at any dusk, any one of them could let go and rise out through the mouth and be over the pines in a minute. They do not. Once a year, perhaps — those who have watched longest say once a year, at the first cloudless dusk of autumn — a single bat detaches, circles the hollow once, and goes up through the mouth like a scrap of the darkness escaping. And at some point in the following nights a bat comes down through the mouth and takes the empty place in the brown nap. Whether it is the same bat, no one can say. It may be that the bats maintain a single delegate to the outer world, an ambassador to the sky, replaced or returned annually, so that the colony's renunciation stays *chosen* — refreshed, like a vow — rather than hardening into mere captivity. The rest of the year they hang shoulder to shoulder and, presumably, dream. If anything in the hollow dreams of weather, it is them.

The crayfish keep the basin, and the basin is the most important place in the hollow, because it is where the drip lands. They are pale to the point of translucence — you can watch the slow machinery of them working under the shell — and they hold position around the drip's landing point in an open ring, claws raised, like figures in a rite arrested at its central moment. Every strike of the drip sends a ring of ripples through them and they do not stir. They are the keepers of the clock's face. They molt, which makes them the only creatures in the hollow who visibly renew — but the cast shells do not last; the water takes them apart in days, dissolves them back into the mineral load that becomes flowstone, becomes shelf, becomes the resting place of salamanders. Even the crayfish's renewal is a payment into the stillness of everything else.

And the moss. You will have understood by now that the moss is not the setting. The moss is the largest inhabitant — possibly, in some sense, the only one, with everything else a kind of population it hosts the way a body hosts its flora. It is one organism; dig your hand in anywhere along the rim and you are touching the same individual you would touch thirty paces away on the floor. It drinks the cloud. It swallowed the axe. It carries the seething voice. It has had uncountable centuries to grow over every surface in the hollow, and it has done so — every surface but one. The ledge. One body wide, at sitting height, padded with moss but never *closed over* by it, never blurred into the wall the way the moss blurs everything else. The moss maintains the ledge the way a household maintains a guest room. This is not speculation; it is observable: at the edges of the ledge the moss grows to a clean line and stops, and the line has the tended quality of a hedge, of a margin kept.

Which brings us, last, to the cleft, and the breathing.

No one has seen what breathes there. The cleft admits no light and returns no echo, and the animals — this is the telling thing — the animals all face very slightly away from it, the way a congregation faces away from the door behind the altar. Not in fear. In courtesy. The breath is slow, damp, regular, and if you listen long enough you will notice what the crayfish have always known: the breath and the drip keep the same time. One drip per breath. The hollow has a pulse and a respiration and they are synchronized, and the respiration comes from the cleft.

Here is what you come to believe, sitting long enough in the wet grey light, and you believe it the way you believed the truth about the axe — without evidence, without doubt. The one who carried the axe up the mountain sat down on the ledge. The ledge was empty then too; it has only ever held one at a time. They sat, and set the axe down, and stayed — past the point of leaving, past the point of hunger, past the points where a life usually insists on itself — and the hollow did to them, very slowly, what it does to everything: took the edges off. And at some point, over centuries, what remained of them withdrew from the ledge into the cleft, the way the salamanders withdraw from animal into stone, and joined whatever was already breathing in there — because something already was; the axe-bearer was not the first, only the most recent, and the breath in the cleft is not one breather but the settled, merged remainder of every one who has ever taken the seat. They have all become the same breath. That is what the cleft holds: the hollow's predecessors, finished even with stillness, finished with form, keeping the place alive at one drip per breath.

So: what lives in the Hollow of the Sleepers is a single congregation that has finished moving — fixed stars above, an archive on the walls, listeners on the floor, sleepers becoming shelf, a delegate to the sky, a ring of timekeepers around the heartbeat, one vast green host drinking the cloud, and in the deepest dark, the gathered breath of everyone who ever stayed.

And one vacancy.

The ledge does not belong to anyone yet. That is the truth of it, and the moss keeps it true, trimming the margin, holding the seat open through the centuries between sitters. Whoever lives on the ledge is whoever is *almost* — the one creature in the hollow's economy that it cannot supply for itself, that must come up the mountain on its own feet, through the fog, past the last of the pines, carrying whatever it carries.

The hollow is patient about this the way it is patient about everything. The drip does not hurry. The breath does not hurry.

It has all of the time, and it knows exactly how much of you it needs: one.
