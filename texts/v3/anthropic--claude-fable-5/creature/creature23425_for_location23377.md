**The people of the Setting** are easy to mistake, at first, for a quiet people. They are not. They are a *soft* people — which is different — and the difference is the first thing you must understand about them, because everything else follows from it.

Begin with the gait, because the gait is the oldest thing about them.

A resident of the Setting walks the way you would walk on the back of something asleep, which is, after all, the literal situation. The weight goes down rolled, heel to toe in one unbroken transfer, knees soft, never a stamp, never a jump, never the flat percussive slap of a hurrying foot. Children learn it before they learn words; you can watch toddlers in the fern-alleys being gentled out of toddling, taught to pour themselves forward instead of falling forward, and by five they move like everyone else — that smooth, attentive, faintly maritime glide, as though the whole population had grown up on the deck of one enormous ship. Visitors from set country are identified instantly and from a great distance, not by their clothes but by their footfalls. There is a word for such people. It translates, more or less, as *the heeled*.

They dress in grey waxed wool because everything beads over by mid-morning and wool sheds the city's weeping; they go bare-headed in all weather, because a resident reads with the face — the temperature of the air tells you the age of the ground upwind. And they touch things constantly, lightly, in passing: a palm flat on a wall, two fingers trailed along a rock terrace, the back of a hand laid briefly against an extrusion. This is not affection, or not only. It is dating. A practiced resident can age ground to within a week by warmth alone, and they take readings the way other people check the sky, hundreds of times a day, without thinking, a whole city forever feeling the world's pulse at the wrist.

---

**Their voices** sit just above a whisper and their language has been worn smooth by the ground underneath it.

The verb *to be* exists but is considered slightly vulgar — overclaiming, the way it would be overclaiming to introduce a draft as a law. Things *stand for now*. Things are *currently arranged*. A resident does not say *my house is on Third Ridge*; she says something closer to *my house keeps near Third Ridge*, and Third Ridge itself is named for behavior, not position — it is the third ridge in the sense of the third attempt, the ridge the ground keeps trying in that quarter, declined twice and rising again. Directions are given by gradient and age rather than landmark, because landmarks travel: *meet me where the ground is two weeks old*, people say, and they find each other.

Even the names are provisional. A resident carries a *working name*, and revises it perhaps two or three times across a life, at the great re-foundings — not abandoning the old name but re-bedding it, the way the rail gangs re-bed track. The old name is not dead. It is earlier ground. Elders will sometimes call a grown woman by the name she carried at nine, and this is not forgetfulness; it is a form of tenderness, a way of saying *I knew you when you stood there*.

And because the percussion taboo runs deeper than engineering — runs all the way down into manners — the people have built a whole expressive culture out of the unstruck. They do not clap. Approval is shown by raising both hands and turning them slowly, palms out, so that in a crowded hall a well-received speech is answered by a silent field of turning hands, like the city's billion droplets catching light. They do not knock; doors have small felted pull-cords that sigh. Their music is entirely drawn and breathed — bowed strings, bellows-organs, long-throated flutes — and there is exactly one deliberate loud sound in the whole forty kilometers: at shift-change, the bellows-organ on the central cooling tower opens a single sustained chord, swelling and fading over a full minute, and the city calls this *the breath*, and the swallows have learned to ride it.

The official explanation for all this softness is structural: percussion troubles the unset ground. Every resident will give you the official explanation. But watch their hands while they give it — watch the flat palm laid, almost apologetically, on the nearest warm rock — and you will understand the common position, the one never said loudly: that the quiet is not engineering. It is courtesy. They live on a sleeper, and they have agreed, all of them, across generations, not to wake it.

---

**The trades** are the city's true nobility, and the highest of them is the strangest.

The jacking crews you will see first, because they own the dusk: figures in grey moving down every street with long levers, cranking the buildings back toward plumb a few millimeters at a turn, in silence, by hand signal. They are the city's most intimate strangers. A crew levels the same blocks for decades; they know which windows hold insomniacs, which floors carry new cradles — and it is an old, unadvertised kindness of the trade to shade a nursery floor a half-degree, so that a crib drifts gently away from the cold wall in the night. People leave warm rinds and tea on their sills for the crews the way other cultures leave offerings for quieter spirits, and the crews accept them the same way: without acknowledgment, completely.

The map-printers are a guild of cheerful, institutional failure. Their weekly maps are wrong by Tuesday and they know it; they print errata, which are also wrong, and they know that too. Residents read the maps the way set people read weather reports — not for fact but for trend, for the shape of the ground's mood — and the printers have come to think of themselves less as cartographers than as biographers of a subject who will not sit still.

But the great trade, the one children are tested for, is the Readers.

A Reader walks the newest ground at dawn, while it still gives underfoot, and answers it. Because the ground, as everyone knows and no one quite says, does not heave at random — it *drafts*. It puts up a terrace where a foundation might want to sit, ridges at gauge-width, a hollow vault beneath a thirsty district — and every draft requires a reply. The Readers carry wax pencils and a vocabulary of marks: this terrace *accepted*, build on it promptly and well, because acceptance is a duty — if the ground offers and the offer stands empty, what does it learn? This ridge *declined*, jack the tenements gently away, let the moss have it. The work is called, in their own word, editing, and the Readers carry themselves with the particular gravity of people engaged in half of a correspondence whose other author they have never met and whose intentions they discuss only in the conditional.

They have their schisms, naturally — kept soft, like everything here. There are Readers who hold that every draft should be accepted, that declining is a failure of imagination; the eastern staircase haunts them, forty steps to nothing, and the heretical old argument resurfaces in every generation: *we should have built the thing it was a staircase to*. And there are Readers — fewer, quieter — who have noticed that the three-toed trackways appear most often on ground adjacent to declined proposals, and who have begun to wonder, privately, in the conditional, whether the ground drafts more than architecture. Whether the prints are the ground practicing an inhabitant. Whether the city has been receiving, all this time, two kinds of proposal, and answering only one.

---

**The children** are older than the world they stand on, and they know it, and it makes them strange and wonderful.

A nine-year-old in the Setting has outlived every surface she has ever played on. Her first hill has been re-leveled; the alley where she learned to glide is a knoll now, deep in horsetail. So the children's games are games of impermanence played offensively, with glee. They play *Still* — the whole pack frozen in the ferns, holding, holding, until the ground itself moves first, that deep woody groan rising through their sternums, and then they collapse laughing, because you always lose to the ground, that is the point of the game, the ground always blinks last by never blinking. They play mapping games where the winner is the child whose map is wrongest in the most interesting way. They dare each other up the declined staircase, forty mossy steps to nothing, and the dare is not the climbing — it is standing on the top step, on the threshold of the unbuilt thing, and saying your working name out loud.

Adults walk around the staircase. The children understand why better than the adults do.

---

**The old** are the city's archives, because nothing else can be.

Paper fails here; place fails here; an elder of the Setting has outlived every map and most of the geography. So memory has gone into the body and the procedure. The old remember in jack-turn counts and re-bedding songs, in sequences of hand signals for crews long dispersed; they do not point at places, because the places have left, but at *processes* — *there, where the third ridge keeps trying; there, where the ground has wanted a pond since before your mother*. To walk the city with an elder is to be shown not what stands but what recurs, and slowly you understand that this is the resident's true homeland: not a where, but a *tendency*. They are not nostalgic. Nostalgia requires somewhere to return to. What they have instead is a kind of trained, weathered grace at release, a lifetime's skill at setting things down softly — which makes them, the young complain, nearly impossible to argue with.

---

**The dead** are the only finished things in the city.

The word for dying is the word for setting. *He set last winter*, people say, with no euphemism intended, because none is needed. The dead are carried out — felt-shod bearers, the breath sounding long from the tower — and laid into a dimple of week-old ground, into rock still waxy enough to receive them, and then the family goes home, and the ground does the rest. Within a season the new rock has risen around and over, gently, the way it takes everything, and the grave begins to travel. Mourners visit while they can. Eventually the hill that holds their dead cannot be told from any other hill, and this is considered correct — the ground keeps better track than grief does, and the city is built on its dead the way every city is, except here it is literal, and warm, and meant kindly.

There is one further custom, half-secret. Some families, at a setting, press the dead one's hand into the curing rock nearby — one print, no marker. The ground keeps it forever. The city is veined with these too, among the three-toed lines, and residents step over both kinds the same way: without comment, which is not indifference. It is the deepest politeness they have.

---

**The neighbors** — because the animals are not wildlife here; wildlife implies a wilderness elsewhere, and there is no elsewhere. The animals are residents with different trades.

The eels of the condensation channels are the city's confidants. Pale, long, unhurried, they gather at the warm outfalls at shift-change — they know the breath as well as any worker — and three generations of mill hands will know three generations of a single channel's eels by name and disposition. Workers tell the eels things. The eels are excellent at it.

The otters are the only beings in the Setting who have memorized the jacking schedule in full — crews consult them, jokingly, and then less jokingly, because an otter denned ahead of the crew is never wrong about where the crew will go. The swallows keep the city's only clock worth keeping, that vast soft dawn rushing, the plain inhaling. The ash-moths take the evening shift on the warm pipes, fanning slowly, a congregation of small grey hands turning palms-out, and more than one resident has noticed that this is the shape of applause here, and declined to make anything of it, out loud.

---

**And the tenant.** Last, because it lives here most of all, and no one has ever seen it.

Three toes, a neat unhurried stride, pressed into ground that was hours old and is stone forever now. The prints cross the city in their thousands, fossil footprints of something extinct, or shy, or — the quiet Readers' word — *pending*. They appear on the newest ground, often near declined proposals, always walking away from the built and toward the rising. Residents step over them without comment, and perhaps that is all it is: politeness. Or perhaps the stepping-over, generation after generation, *is* the comment — the city's standing reply to its oldest open proposal, neither acceptance nor refusal, the mark a Reader makes when the answer is *not yet*.

Because underneath all of them, of course, lives the principal resident, the landlord and the infant both: the ground itself, oldest thing and youngest thing, rising warm from depth, breathing its slow geothermal breath up through the moss into forty kilometers of soft-walking, soft-speaking tenants who level their houses against it nightly and feed its eels and bury their finished dead in its rising and answer its drafts in wax pencil at dawn. They live on the back of something asleep. They have made a civilization out of not waking it — or, the children would tell you, standing on the top step of the staircase to nothing, saying their working names into the cold green air:

out of keeping it good company while it dreams.
