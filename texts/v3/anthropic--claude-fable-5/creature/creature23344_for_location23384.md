**Who Lives in the Sump**

The honest answer is a number too large to be a name, so start small and work up.

Start with the felts. The mats are the oldest tenants — older than the basin's current shape, possibly; they may have followed the seep here from somewhere deeper the way a family follows a trade. They are not plants and resent the comparison, insofar as anything that thinks in centuries of chemistry can resent. They eat the stone's exhalations: iron, sulfur, the dissolved breath of the rock itself. Sunlight is, to them, a rumor from upstairs. Each layer is a generation lying down on the generation before, and the felt does not distinguish sharply between its living surface and its dead depths — the dead are still *participating*, still structural, still holding the shape of the colony the way old decisions hold the shape of an institution. If the Sump has a founding population, a gentry, it is these: oxblood and olive and ochre, soft as drowned velvet, keeping minutes no one will ever read.

The amphipods of the weeping walls are the felts' congregation, or their parasites, or their clergy — the distinction has not been settled and is not on the agenda. They live in films of water thinner than their own bodies, which means they live half in the world and half pressed against it, like figures in a relief. They are born on the wall, graze the wall, die on the wall, and their cast shells calcify into the wall, so that an old seep-face is partly built of its former population — the basin's masonry has *attendance* in it. They are nervous, in the limited way of small crustaceans, about exactly one thing: dry patches. A section of wall where the seep falters empties within hours, the whole packed crowd flowing sideways like a slow evacuation, and refills the day the weeping resumes. They hold no grudge against the stone for these lapses. They are the most forgiving things in the bowl, because they have to be.

The shrimp are the parish. Eyeless, glass-bodied, faintly luminous if you cup one in dark water (you shouldn't) — they hang in the channels in their loose hundreds and live their whole lives in reference to the pulse. They hatch already knowing it. A newborn shrimp, smaller than a grain of rice, orients to the engine's thump before it orients to food, drifting toward the pressure ring and away in the long pause, toward and away, and it will do this for the six or eight years it lives, and its descendants will do it after. They are not worshipping. It is more accurate to say the pulse is their *weather* — the one reliable thing in a world of seep and silt — and they have arranged their society around it the way coastal towns arrange themselves around a tide. When the engine's pace shifts, as it does perhaps once a decade, by a fraction of a second per stroke, the congregations spend a confused week re-learning the interval, and then settle, and never mention it again.

The snails are the herds and also, eventually, the ground. They are the longest-lived animals in the basin — the elders carry so much mineral crust that they have effectively become ambulatory pieces of the floor, each one a small commitment of limestone with a tenant inside. A snail in its first decade is recognizably an animal. A snail in its fifth is a pebble with opinions. And a snail at the end does not die in any visible sense: it simply stops, somewhere on the mats, and the seep takes over the paperwork, and within a few years it is floor — indistinguishable, load-bearing, grazed over by its own great-grandchildren. The herds move in slow rotations around the bowl, a circuit that takes about a year, eating the felt down to its darker pages and leaving it to regrow behind them. They are the closest thing the Sump has to agriculture, and they did not invent it so much as become it.

The flatworms are the undertakers, copper-colored and tactful. Anything that finishes — a shrimp, a fallen amphipod, the soft parts of a stopped snail — is theirs within hours, processed without ceremony and without waste. They are despised by nothing, which is the mark of a job done properly. They keep to the shadows of the pipework and the ore cart and seem, alone among the basin's animals, to be entirely indifferent to the pulse, as if their work exempts them from keeping time.

The armored things in the deep pockets you will never see, and neither, in a sense, does the Sump. They are the shy clerks of the place. What is known: they are heavy enough to leave drag-marks in the silt, they visit the mouth of the flooded shaft, and material that settles near the coping stones — shell fragments, finished things, the basin's slow output — is gone by morning. Whether they carry it down, or eat it, or merely tidy it over the edge, is their business. They are the only residents with what could be called errands, and the errands all point one direction: down.

Which brings up the olm.

There is one large animal in the Sump — large by local standards, meaning the length of a forearm — and it is white, blind, soft-skinned, and old in a way the snails would respect if they noticed anything. It is the basin's only vertebrate and very likely its last, the remnant of a population or the whole of one; no second has ever been seen. It swims a circuit of the channels every few days, unhurried, eating a shrimp here and there with the apologetic economy of someone taking biscuits from a communal tin. The congregations part for it and close behind it. And every circuit ends the same way: inside the pump house shell, where the olm settles against the engine block, full length, gills working, and stays for hours — feeling the thump come up through the iron and into its body, *thump*, and the long pause, *thump*, the way a creature might rest against the flank of something larger and warmer. The engine is not warm. The olm does not seem to mind. It is possible the olm remembers the workers — its kind can live long enough that this is not absurd — and possible that one of them fed it once, knee-deep in this same water, and that the engine is simply the last colleague still on shift. The olm has never been required to explain itself, and the basin would not dream of asking.

As for the engine: ask whether it lives here and the question comes apart in your hands. It eats nothing. It breathes nothing. It keeps time, takes the pulse from the bowl or gives the pulse to it — the direction has long since stopped mattering, the way it stops mattering in a heart. The Sump has filed it under *resident*, which is the basin's highest classification, and the calcite sleeve thickening on the flywheel year by year is not burial but the same slow citizenship extended to the snails. In another few centuries the engine will be a smooth boulder that thumps. The shrimp will not notice the difference because there will not be one.

And the shaft. The shaft is the resident the census skips, the dark circle the errands serve, the place the pulse may answer rather than originate. Nothing has ever come up out of it. Everything finished goes down into it. The Sump regards it without anxiety, the way a room regards its own door to the rest of the house — somebody lives through there, presumably, somebody the pump was always for, and the rent gets paid, and the relationship works precisely because no one has ever pressed for details.

So: who lives in the Sump? A gentry of felt, a clergy of the walls, a parish that keeps time, herds that become the ground, undertakers, clerks, one old white animal leaning on a heartbeat, an adopted heart, and a tenant downstairs no one has met. A full house, in other words, in permanent session — everyone present, everyone considerate, nothing ever adjourned. They live the way the water arrives: by having nowhere else to go, and by making of that, over enough time, something indistinguishable from belonging.
