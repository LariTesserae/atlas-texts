Start with the paviors, because they are what you are standing among, and because for the first hour you will mistake them for part of the deck.

That mistake is not a failure of attention. A pavior's shell is cast in the same pale gray as the alloy beneath it — not camouflage, since nothing here hunts by sight, but convergence: the deck is the only surface this world has ever offered as a model, and so the deck is what its animals have become. Each one is a low oval dome, plated in overlapping scutes, edges rounded the way the drainage channels are rounded, as if the same engineer specified both. Underneath is the whole animal: a skirt of muscle ringed with thousands of fine rasping teeth, working the nutrient film off the metal in slow rotary strokes. The dorsal surface carries a line of breathing slits that open in rhythm with the falling condensate, so that a basin of ten thousand paviors seems to inhale each time a drop lands.

They never collide. Their spacing is maintained by pressure sense along the skirt — each one knows its neighbors as a faint constant touch, and the pavement flexes and redistributes around any disturbance the way water does, without alarm, without haste. Put your hand on a shell and nothing happens, or almost nothing: a slight settling, the weight accepted, filed, equalized. There is no flight response because there has never been anything to flee that fleeing would help with. You will not find an old pavior — no chipped scute, no clouded slit — and for a long time you will not find a young one either, until you learn to look at the basin rims at the moment a caller sounds, when the soft, coin-sized new ones emerge from brood-grooves in the overflow channels and harden over a few days into pavement. Growth is the only motion they are permitted in excess. Everything else is held.

What a pavior knows, as far as you can tell, is this: the film, the warmth, the touch of its neighbors' edges, and the call. When a caller's two-tone note crosses a basin, the whole pavement stops rasping for the duration of exactly one drip-fall, and then resumes. That pause is the closest thing they have to an event.

The laddered live differently, in lines. These are the climbers on the provision stacks — long, low-slung bodies, six to ten gripping limbs depending on district, fur the color of condensation and always faintly wet with it. Their eyes are the largest of any animal here, dark, lidless, and they are the only creatures on the Provision Floor that will look at you. The look contains no question. It is the look of someone confirming that you exist and finding the matter closed.

Their queues are the famous mystery. Nose to tail up the weeping channels, each animal drinking in turn, no jostling, no enforcement — and if you lift one out of line and set it on the deck a hundred meters away, it will walk a slow, unbothered circuit and rejoin at exactly the position it left, as though its place in the queue were a location, a home address written somewhere in its body. They give back as much as they take. Their fur carries oils that polish the stack metal; their grooming clears the syrup channels of mineral crust; the stacks shine brightest exactly where the laddered drink. They are the orchard's gardeners, and they have no idea, and they do not need one.

Above all this, the turners ride. Broad-winged, nearly silent, translucent at the trailing edge, each one circling a fixed point on the deck's warm thermals — a gyre it holds for life and may inherit, since young turners appear already circling, slotted into the family column like a new thought into an old habit. They descend one at a time, in an order no one assigns, to take ribbon-stuff from the extruders; the tearing of the ribbon is the only sound they make. They sleep aloft, half the brain at a time. Their shadows are the only moving darkness in this world, and the paviors track those shadows with their breathing slits — the closest thing the Provision Floor has to weather.

In the deep channels lie the braid: eel-bodied swimmers packed and aligned with the flow, feeding on what the water carries — dissolved sugars, sloughed film, the fine particulate exhaust of everyone else's eating. They are the world's kidneys. When a basin's water clouds, the braid moves to it as a single body, a kilometer of muscle traveling like one rope drawn through the channels, and within a day the water is clear again and the rope has redistributed itself into stillness.

Then there are the takers, and the takers require honesty.

One per district, roughly. Tall, stilted, patient, standing motionless among the paviors sometimes for days, the gray pavement rasping on around their legs. The taking itself you have seen described: a grazer detaches, walks to the stalker, is enclosed — quickly, totally, no blood reaching the water, the thousands around it not pausing. What the description omits is the selection, because the selection cannot be seen. Nothing passes between taker and pavior — no gaze, no scent your instruments find, no sound above or below your hearing. And yet the right one rises, every time, never two, never none. You will develop hypotheses. The deck signals through the alloy. The taker emits something through its stilts. The choice is made elsewhere, below, and both animals are merely the choice's hands. You will not be able to rule any of these out, and after enough months you will stop trying, which is itself a way of becoming local.

You never see a taker die. But occasionally you find a patch of deck being burnished with unusual attention, and you suspect.

The burnishers are the explanation for the world's impossible youth, and almost no one who passes through ever notices them. Finger-length, hard-cased, slow, traveling the deck in loose herds with mouthparts like jeweler's tools, they eat oxide, scuff, shed fur, the calcium of broken brood-shells, the microscopic record of everything that touches the alloy — and they redeposit it, metal in layers thinner than light, edges restored, weld seams re-brightened. The deck is not unworn. It is continuously re-newed. The Provision Floor's freshness is not a state; it is a verb, conjugated forever in the present tense by the least-regarded animals in the world. Nothing eats the burnishers. Possibly nothing can.

And the callers. You may walk a week between sightings. Upright, solitary, throats like bells of loose skin, and the call you already know: two tones, carrying for kilometers across the flat warm water, answered once. What you learn with time is that the first tone never varies — the same frequency in every district, a carrier, a signature — while the second tone shifts in small precise increments. The most defensible hypothesis is census: each call a number, a local count, a flow confirmed; each answer the ledger balancing. The least defensible hypothesis is prayer. At this distance from the builders, you will find, the two hypotheses are not distinguishable, and the callers themselves would not understand the question.

None of these animals fears. This is the thing you must sit with longest. Fear is a loan taken out against a future, and here the future is identical to the present, so the loan was never issued. Their calm is not stupidity — the laddered solve their queues, the turners navigate three dimensions in their sleep, the takers wait with what looks unmistakably like attention. It is that every problem intelligence evolved to solve has been solved on their behalf, permanently, by the warm machinery under their feet. What remains inside them might be contentment. It might be absence. From outside, the two are indistinguishable, and that indistinguishability is the true resident strangeness of the Provision Floor — more than the takings, more than the hum.

As for who lives below: press your ear to the deck long enough, past the pumps and the synthesizers, and you will catch something slower underneath them — a rhythm with a period of hours, district-wide, like breathing at the scale of geography. It may be machinery cycling. It may be the deepest inhabitant. Three beliefs are available, and long-term observers tend to hold all three at once. That the builders are down there, sealed and sleeping, and the surface is their garden turned inside out — the animals their external organs, fed and counted on their behalf. That the builders are the animals — that they edited themselves down from wanting into simply being, and the paviors are people who finally received the thing everyone claims to want, which is enough. Or that the builders dissolved into the system entirely, and the largest living thing here is the equilibrium itself: a creature with a continent for a body, whose cells are species, whose heartbeat is the hum, whose voice is the callers, whose metabolism is the takings, whose skin is kept young by the burnishers, and whose thoughts — if it has them — take the only form available to it, which is populations holding perfectly steady.

Which leaves the last inhabitant, the provisional one: you.

You are the only thing on the Provision Floor that accumulates. Your boot-scuffs are the only history the deck has carried in living memory, and a herd of burnishers is already following you, erasing it at a respectful distance. Within a day of your arrival, a provision stack somewhere near your path will quietly change its output — a new ribbon, denser, savory, extruded at the height of your hand, in flavors that converge over a week toward whatever your body keeps choosing. The ledger has opened a line for you. Stay, and the line stays open: fed, watered, warmed, counted. One day a caller's second tone will shift by an increment too small for your ear, and that will be you, entered.

Whether the deck has a plan for the other end of your line — whether a taker will someday come to stand near you on its patient stilts, and whether something in you, after enough years of warm water and white light, will rise and walk to it the way the paviors do, without alarm, without being asked twice — is the one question the place declines to answer.

Nothing that lives here has ever needed to ask it.
