Begin with the gardens, because they are the only residents you would recognize as residents, and even then only barely, only if you kneel.

The colored bands on the wet stone — mustard, rust-orange, that green so saturated it reads as black — are not stains. They are nations. Each streak is a community of thermophiles a few degrees wide, billions of individuals deep, and the borders between them are drawn in temperature with a precision no surveyor could match. The mustard band lives at the heat the orange band would die in; the orange lives where the mustard would starve; the near-black green holds the cooler frontier where the chemistry thins out and ambition becomes possible. They do not compete, exactly. They are sorted. The basin assigns each of them a contour and they fill it wall to wall.

They eat the water. Not anything in the water in the way you'd mean it — they eat the water's freight, the dissolved sulfur and iron and hydrogen the vent hauls up from below, stripping electrons off mineral chemistry the way leaves strip light off the sun. Some of the outermost green communities still bother with daylight, hedging. The inner nations gave up on the sun entirely, or never took it up. They run on the planet's own internal economy, paid directly, no photosynthetic middleman.

And here is the thing about them that matters, the thing that makes them the basin's truest tenants: they are deaf, and they were deaf before deafness meant anything. Their lineages are older than ears. Older than the invention of listening, older than the strategy of survival-by-attention that drives every bird around the rising vapor and keeps every animal out. The roar that erases the acoustic world — the roar that makes the basin uninhabitable for anything that lives by noticing — costs them nothing, because they predate the whole project of noticing. The basin did not select for the deaf. It selected for the never-hearing, and found tenants who have been waiting for exactly this vacancy for three and a half billion years. To them this is not an extreme environment. This is the original environment, the first one, conserved here like a room kept the way a dead relative left it. Everything outside the basin is the extreme.

Their generations turn over in hours. Run the arithmetic and the steadiness that unnerves human visitors — the painted flame, the trembling needle that never drifts — becomes, for them, simply the shape of the universe. A community in the mustard band has lived under that one unvarying note for more consecutive generations than there have been human generations under the sun. The roar is not a property of the air to them. It is the air. It is what existence sounds like, except that nothing in the basin has ever heard it, and so it is not even that. It is just true, the way gravity is true.

They migrate, though you will never see it. As the terraces thicken — that millimeter a season — the heat contours creep outward, and the nations creep with them, each color advancing into the territory the next-hotter color is vacating, a procession measured in years per footstep. And they build as they go. The pale stone is not purely mineral; look closely at a broken shelf and you'll find laminations, fine wavering layers, the texture of mats that lived, were entombed by their own mineralized water, and were lived upon in turn. The terraces are a city that constructs itself by burying its own ground floors. The inhabitants occupy only the newest story, always, and the basin keeps the rest as record. The residents and the architecture are the same substance at different ages. To live here long is to become terrace — this is the basin's single law of tenancy, and the mats obey it a layer at a time, willingly, the way you obey your own pulse.

That law has caught one other resident. The bootprint. Whoever made it left decades ago, but the print did not leave; it took the basin's offer, hardened, and now resides here permanently — a person-shaped absence, mineralized, accumulating its slow sheath like the pipe, like the wellhead bolts. The basin does not host visitors. It incorporates them or releases them, and the bootprint is the one piece of a human being that chose to stay. In a thousand years it will be deep inside the white mound, a hollow in the stone the shape of a heel and five seams of stitching, and it will be no less a resident then. Possibly more of one. The basin's citizens are ranked by depth.

There are lighter tenants too — the ones who keep hours rather than epochs. The small rainbow that stands at waist height at certain angles of sun is the most punctual: it arrives when the light permits, occupies its post, and dissolves without complaint, a resident in the way a habit is a resident. The basin's private weather lives here the same way — the perpetual condensation, the thin hot threads weeping back toward the center, a complete hydrological cycle forty paces across, self-contained, running its circuit of rise and cool and fall thousands of times a day. If you are generous about what counts as an inhabitant, the weather is one: it never leaves, it has a routine, it touches everything, and the basin would be a different place without it. It is the closest thing the basin has to a housekeeper.

Then there is the other one. The one below.

Be careful with the word lives here, because it may be exactly the right word used in exactly the wrong tense of attention. Whatever sits beneath the young rock does not live in the basin. The basin is what its living looks like from above — the visible edge of a metabolism, the exhaust of an occupation. The column of steam, the painted-flame fixity, the waveform of mechanical regularity: these are not its body and not its voice. They are its breath, and breath is the least of what a breathing thing is.

You can say a few things about it from the evidence. It is provisioned — nothing runs at perfect constancy for decades on dwindling reserves; constancy is the signature of surplus, of something drawing on a supply it has measured and found ample. It is patient in a way that suggests not endurance but indifference to duration, the patience of something for which the question "how long" has never been interesting. And it is occupied. Not erupting, not failing, not even working in the strained sense — running. Partway through. The steam is not its purpose; the steam is what its purpose pays upward, the rent a deep process remits to the surface in exchange for being left alone. Above the borehole, stone accretes, shelf on shelf, white and slow. It is hard not to suspect a mirror: that below, something is also accreting — or being dismantled, refined, converted, ushered from one state to another — at the same unhurried rate, the terraces and the task two faces of one ledger.

Whether it noticed the drilling is the question visitors don't ask out loud. Decades ago a bit chewed down through the rock and punctured whatever envelope held it, and the response was — nothing. No surge, no decline, no adjustment. Either the puncture was beneath its notice, a pinprick in the hide of something measured in different units entirely, or it was noticed and accounted for and found to change nothing that mattered, the way you do not reseal a window that opens onto a view you never use. There is even a third possibility, the one that occurs to people on the climb out: that the borehole was convenient. That something which exhales forever might not mind being given a mouth, and that the drillers, intending extraction, performed instead a small unwitting kindness to a thing that cannot thank them and would not think to.

It is almost certainly not aware of its tenants. The mats grazing on its breath, the rainbow clocking in, the bootprint settling deeper into citizenship — these stand to it as the mites in your eyelashes stand to you: real, dependent, intimate, and entirely outside the scope of what it considers. The basin is a whole inhabited world balanced on the rim of its exhalation, and the exhalation does not know. This is not neglect. It is scale. Every resident of the basin lives on the resident below it — the mats on the basin's heat, the weather on the steam, the steam on the engine, the engine on its task — and no story in the building has ever exchanged a word with another, because the building is deaf from the foundation up, and always has been, and was perhaps built that way on purpose, if purpose is a word that applies, which the dial in the gauge-house, trembling at its one faithful reading, declines to confirm or deny.

So: what lives there. The oldest kind of life on the planet, arranged in heat-colored nations, surfing a stone front a millimeter a season, deaf since before deafness, at home since before homes. A weather the size of a room. A rainbow with office hours. One human footstep, naturalized. And underneath it all, the landlord — patient, provisioned, partway through — whose breath is the basin, whose schedule is geologic, and whose task, whatever it is, has never once been interrupted, and continues, at this moment, at the same pitch, in the dark, below.

You carried a piece of it out, remember. The note in your sternum, fading over an hour. For that hour you were the basin's briefest tenant, vibrating faintly at the frequency of the breath, registered, recorded, and then — because you live by listening, and so cannot stay — quietly let go.
