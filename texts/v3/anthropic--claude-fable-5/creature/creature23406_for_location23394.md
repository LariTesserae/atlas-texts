**The Inhabitants of the Dry Vault**

Begin with the loudest, because they will not let you begin anywhere else.

The bats are the city now — not its occupants but its citizens, in the full civic sense. They number in the millions and they are not one people. The high carved vaults of the upper districts hold the small insect-hunters, packed so densely that the ceiling appears furred, a brown velvet that shivers and resettles all day long. Nearer the fissures roost the larger kinds, long-faced, who go up at dusk to strip the surface forest of its fruit and moths and come back down smelling faintly of a world the others have never touched. And in the deepest halls — the ones with the strange acoustics, the ones whose proportions still feel deliberate — live the old colonies, lineages that may have roosted in those exact chambers since before the dust on the plaza below them was an inch deep.

They have organized the architecture without knowing it. Nursery vaults, where the carved ceilings catch and focus sound, hold the pups in their millions, naked and pink, each one packed wing-to-wing with strangers; a returning mother finds her own child by voice alone, threading one cry out of a roar that would deafen reason. There are bachelor galleries, and halls of the old, where bats too worn to make the nightly climb hang in the quieter dark and are fed, sometimes, or are not. The city's builders cut rooms that shaped the human voice, if that is what they were for. The colony inherited those rooms and filled them with a language of its own — and it may be the only language ever to have used the city as it was meant to be used, every chamber a resonator, every street a flight-corridor, the whole vault one enormous instrument played continuously for ten thousand years.

Below them, on the soft dark hills of their leavings, stands the second nation.

The guano fauna live in a wealth they did not make and cannot exhaust. Beetles in glittering congregations, bronze and black, working the fresh fall; beneath them, in the older strata, pale grubs and mites in numbers better described as weather than as population. Crickets the color of candle wax, antennae longer than their bodies, reading the dark by touch. Centipedes gone amber and translucent, fast as a flinch. Springtails like living dust atop the dead dust. The floor beneath a great roost is, functionally, a mouth: a bat that falls — and they fall, the old and the sick and the unlucky pups — is received, surrounded, and gone by the next dusk-tide, down to a scatter of fine bones that the beetles polish and the dust then files away forever. Nothing in Sessern is wasted except time, of which there is an infinite supply.

The salamander-things are the slowest of the floor nations and possibly the oldest. Blind for so long that their lineages have forgotten what blindness replaced, skin gone the no-color of the deep roots, they move so rarely that dust accumulates on their backs — you can guess how long one has held its station by the gray bloom along its spine. They eat perhaps a few times a year. They may live for centuries; no one has watched long enough to know. The few people who have spent real time in the vault stop thinking of them as animals and start thinking of them as fixtures, small damp officials of the dark, each one keeping its post in a doorway or a channel-bend as though assigned.

Then there is the nation of the roots, which is really an embassy. The great white curtains descending from the ceiling cracks are the underside of the surface forest — trees a hundred feet of rock above, living a second, secret life below, blind and patient. The roots are the only water in Sessern. They sweat. In the deep dry, every column of braided root-wood carries a faint film of moisture down its length, and the city's life arranges itself around these slow wet pillars the way other cities arrange themselves around wells. Pale geckos live in the braids, licking the wood. The fungal gardens crowd the root-bases in their ghost and ash and rust-orange shelves, and the gardens feed grazers, and the grazers feed the hunters: white whip-spiders flattened into crevices; and the racers — long, muscular, dust-colored snakes that anchor themselves in the root curtains nearest the fissures and hang out into open air at dusk, striking blind into the outpouring ribbon of the colony, taking one bat in ten thousand, which is enough.

There are humans, occasionally, in the way that there is occasionally weather. Guano-diggers came once, generations ago; their footprints are still on the plaza floors, crisp as the hour they were made, leading in and — this is noted by everyone who follows them — leading out again. A few hermits and failed pilgrims have stayed longer. The standing observation is that no one lives in Sessern, but some people decline to leave, and the vault does not seem to distinguish between the two. It absorbs them the way it absorbs everything: slowly, politely, completely.

And then — last, and least certain — there are the far districts.

Out where the roosts thin and the roar softens to a pressure behind the ears, the carved neighborhoods stand emptier than empty, and it is there that the vault keeps whatever it is that the bats will not roost above. There are districts the colony avoids — whole terraced wall-faces, acoustically perfect, untenanted — and in the plazas below them sit the shapes that the first surveyors marked down as boulders.

The dust has buried them to the waist. They are tall, or would be; pale in the way the deepest roots are pale, which is not white but the absence of any reason for color. Their proportions rhyme with the architecture — the same rounded-doorway curve to the shoulders, the same fluted economy in what might be limbs — so closely that you cannot say whether they were made by the builders, or descended from them, or are what the builders slowly became when they stopped building and simply stayed. Their eyes, if the smooth places are eyes, sealed over long ago, the way the salamanders' did. They breathe, the patient observers insist: once an hour, perhaps once a day, a settling of the dust on the chest, no more.

They do not leave footprints, because they do not lift their feet. The smooth grooves worn into the plaza floors — the channels everyone attributes to the vanished river — run, here and there, in directions no water would have taken: up gentle slopes, in slow arcs around the root columns, converging on the silent districts from all sides. Some of those grooves are old beyond reckoning. Some, the careful say, looking at the dust within them, are not.

Whether they are the builders, or the builders' heirs, or something the empty city grew in the shape of its makers the way a shell grows in the shape of its lost animal, no one knows, and the vault is not telling. They have never answered anything. They are simply the quietest of the inhabitants, the deep counterweight to the roar — and standing among them, in the dust that records every footstep as geology, you understand at last the full census of Old Sessern: the millions above, screaming the city's name all day and all night; the nations of the floor, eating the wealth that falls; the roots, descending forever; and at the still center, half-buried in the slow snow of millennia, the few who finished first, and stayed to see how the long afterward comes out.
