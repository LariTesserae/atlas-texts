What lives on the plateau is time. Everything else — the plating, the furnace hearts, the noise — is packaging.

That has to be understood first, because otherwise the bestiary makes no sense. Down in the ordinary world, an animal is a way of carrying water around. Here, where the air has gone soft at the seams and cause has worn loose from effect, an animal is a way of carrying *sequence* around — a sealed vessel of before-and-after, pressurized, maintained at great cost, defended with the whole architecture of the body. Every creature on the plateau is a clock that decided to want things. The drone you can never walk out of is not incidental to them, not a byproduct the way a cow's smell is a byproduct. It is the sound of insistence. Each animal is announcing, continuously, at industrial volume, *here is when it is*, and the plateau, which has stopped having opinions on the subject, lets them argue.

So: the argument, species by species.

---

**The kiln-beasts** are the plateau's great fact, and most of what else lives here lives in their shadow, on their backs, in their wake, or off their leavings.

Up close — and you can get close; they have nothing to fear from anything your size, and curiosity was a soft part the wind took from this lineage long ago — a kiln-beast is mostly process. The carapace plates ride on beds of ligament that creak like dock rope. The spinal vents open and close in sequence, stern to bow, a slow wave of exhalation that travels the length of the body once a minute. Below, the jaws: not a mouth so much as the first stage of a works, a pair of mineral grindstones the size of cart wheels, turning against each other without hurry, fed by a lipless scoop that planes the rock as the animal walks. The plateau loses perhaps an inch of itself per beast per century. The beasts are, very slowly, eating their own country, and the country, very slowly, is being remade as windrows of slag behind them. Neither party seems to mind. It may be the same substance taking turns.

What they cannot make for themselves is fire.

This must be said as plainly as the description of the land says nothing built it: *there is one fire on the plateau.* One. It is very old — the beasts' anatomy implies it is older than the beasts — and it is divided now among some thousands of furnace hearts, but it has never been more than one fire, because a kiln-beast cannot ignite. It can only be *lit*. A newborn comes into the world cold: the mother casts it — there is no other word; she deposits, over a season, a calf-shaped accumulation in a slag basin of her own making, layer on layer, the way the plateau itself was layered — and what lies there at the end is a perfect, silent, ash-grey engine with no spark in it. An ingot in the shape of a child. It will lie there for days, and the herd will drift in from miles around, called by something, until the mother lowers her head, opens the smallest and most anterior of her vents, and breathes a thread of her own furnace into the calf's intake.

The first sound a kiln-beast ever makes is the *whump* of catching. The herd answers it from horizon to horizon. If you have heard that dusk chorus and thought it was mating calls, you heard half the truth: the steam-hammer cries that roll across the flats each evening are partly courtship, but they are mostly *roll call* — each animal sounding its fire, the others marking it, an accounting taken nightly of how many pieces the one fire is currently in. The number matters to them. You can hear that it matters.

Mating, for what it's worth, is not contact. It is synchronization. Two beasts will stand a quarter mile apart for weeks, calling, adjusting, until their intake-exhaust rhythms mesh — until they are running on the same stroke, the same time, two engines arguing the same argument. Only then. The plateau's animals cannot exchange anything so crude as fluid. What they exchange is timing.

---

**The threshers** are the second estate: the segmented ones you hear working the ridgelines, low-built, fifty or sixty feet of articulated plates riding on rows of piston legs, each footfall a hammerblow — the stamping-mill sound. They are the plateau's only recyclers. They follow the kiln-beast windrows the way gleaners follow a harvest, sometimes generations behind, milling the slag back toward powder, refining out of it whatever the great grazers' guts left unfinished. A thresher's interior runs cooler than a kiln-beast's — more forge than furnace, a deep cherry glow rather than orange — and they are correspondingly faster, more nervous, more *strung*. Their version of the dusk call is a rapid percussive stutter, like a telegraph with strong feelings.

They have one behavior no other animal here shares: they attend the dead. When a kiln-beast stops — and they do stop; even sealed arguments wear out — the nearest threshers will converge over weeks and dismantle it, plate from ligament, with a patience that looks like ceremony because at this scale everything looks like ceremony. They do not consume the furnace chamber. They open it, and they wait beside it while it cools, and then they leave it — a single dark vault sitting alone on the flats, of which the plateau has accumulated many thousands, the only things here you could mistake for ruins. They are not ruins. They are emptied hearths. The fire that was in them went out honestly, in its time, and that — you come to understand — is the good death here. There is another kind.

---

**The flyers** never land. This is not a figure of speech. They are cast aloft — kiln-beast mothers carry their developing young in dorsal recesses among the vent-rows, and a flyer's first act, when its bearings have run in, is to ratchet itself off its host's back into the standing thermal that every kiln-beast wears like a hat. From then on, the air. They feed by riding exhaust plumes, skimming heat directly out of the rising columns; they drink nothing because nothing here drinks; they sleep, if it is sleeping, in the great permanent thermals over the warmest flats, locking their wing-ratchets and turning in slow autorotation for hours, dreaming whatever a governor dreams.

The *whick-whick-whick* is the healthy sound. Learn to hear past it for the other one — the thin rising shriek of a bearing going dry. A flyer's bearings are its mortality. It cannot maintain them; nothing can maintain them; they are sealed at birth like everything else here is sealed, and when they fail the flyer does not fall so much as *unwind*, spiraling down across most of an afternoon, shedding angular momentum it has carried since the day it was thrown. The threshers gather where it will land. They are never wrong about where it will land. Some calculations the plateau's animals do in their ligaments.

---

**The tenders** are what you find when you finally climb a grazing kiln-beast's flank — and you can; it will register you the way a building registers a bird. Down among the vent-rows, in the hot dark between the plates, lives the plateau's only small fauna: hand-sized, beetle-backed, jointed like escapements, thousands per host. They do not feed on the beast. They *regulate* it. Each tender clings to a ligament or a valve-margin and does one small thing at one exact interval — damps a flutter, trims a vent, holds a beat. Collectively they are the animal's fine timekeeping, externalized and made citizens. A kiln-beast's deep machinery sets the tempo; the tenders keep it honest.

A beast that loses its tenders — to age, to mischance, to the slow attrition of any population — does not die. It *drifts*. Its rhythms shed precision a little per season. Its calls land off the beat at dusk, and the herd's answers come back wrong, then guarded, then not at all. And here is the thing that, once you know it, reorganizes everything you have seen: the beasts that wander into the worn basins are not lost, and they are not foolish. They are the ones whose time has already broken. The basins do not capture them. The basins are simply the only place left where their argument is no longer wrong — where an animal whose before and after have come unstitched can stand on ground that doesn't contradict it.

The shapes you see from the rim — the spine that closes on itself, the too-many of the same leg, the exhaust still rising from where the animal was last week — those are not monsters, and they are not the basin's dreams, or not only. Some of them are herd members. Some of them, the herds remember. The wide margin the grazers keep around the deep basin is not fear. Watch long enough and you will see it for what it is: a vigil kept at the maximum distance at which keeping it is survivable.

---

And then there is **the quiet**.

You will know it is on the plateau the same way everything here knows: by subtraction. The drone is total — you have read that, you have heard it, you stop hearing it within a day the way you stop hearing your own blood. So when a region of the soundscape simply *closes*, a circle of genuine silence a mile across, moving, you will feel it before you understand it, as a wrongness in your chest, an instrument going out in an orchestra you'd forgotten was playing.

No one — nothing — has seen it well. The descriptions the eye brings back do not assemble: something low, something wide, something the color of the ground at the hour you are looking at it, moving with the terrible smoothness of a thing that does not reciprocate, has no stroke, no beat, no *when*. It is the plateau's one predator, if predation is the word for what it does. It does not eat slag, or heat, or flesh, because the plateau's animals are not made of anything you can eat. It eats *running*. It settles its silence over a beast — usually an old one, sometimes a drifting one, once in a great while a sound one whose roll-call was answered the night before — and the animal's rhythms, deprived of their own echo, deprived of the herd's answering beat, deprived of *sound itself*, falter, hunt for the time, and lose it. What the quiet leaves is not a carcass. It is a stopped engine, intact, perfect, every plate in place, cold all the way through. The threshers will not attend these. They circle them once and leave them sealed.

When the quiet takes a beast, a portion of the one fire goes out of the world unbreathed, and that night's chorus is different — longer, and with a gap held open in it, deliberately, where a rhythm used to be. The herds count. You can hear that they count.

One more thing about the quiet, and it is the worst thing, told here only because the plateau has stopped explaining itself and someone must: it is the only living thing that crosses the deep basin. Directly. Without margin, without harm, without hurry. It can do this because it is doing what every other animal here spends its entire vast loud burning life refusing to do.

It carries no time to lose.

---

That is the census. Kilns and threshers, flyers and tenders, the broken ones keeping their unwatched residence in the worn places, and the one cold subtraction that walks among them all. It is not a large fauna. It does not need to be. Life on the plateau is not a matter of numbers; it is a matter of *insistence* — and the definition that finally fits, the one the plateau itself would offer if it still offered anything, is this: a thing lives here if it can be heard. The drone is the biosphere. The roll call is the food web. Every animal is a piece of the one fire keeping the one time aloud against a sky that has worn through, and at dusk, when the furnace hearts come up like coals through a grate and the steam-hammer chorus rolls out across two hundred miles of finished stone, what you are listening to is not noise.

It is the plateau's entire population, all of it, every living thing for a province in every direction, saying the only thing any of them has ever said:

*Still. Still. Still.*
