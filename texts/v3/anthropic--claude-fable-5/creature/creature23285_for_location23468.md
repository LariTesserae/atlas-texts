# Who Lives in the Risen Quarter

Count them honestly and the census is short: thirty-some people, thirty-some swifts, an unnumbered moss, the dead, and one resident nobody has seen. The tooth has rooms for thousands. It has tenants enough to fill a single long table, and not all of them eat.

Start with the ones who do.

## The Carvers

There are, at last counting, thirty-eight of them, and the counting is itself a custom: every autumn, when the tooth talks most, each living resident strikes the census stair once with the heel of a hand, and an elder keeps tally, and the year's number is pressed wet into a strip of fresh stone at the base, where it will harden and rise and become permanent like everything else here. The column of census strips now climbs eleven meters up the eastern face. You can read the whole demographic history of the Quarter at a glance, from below, squinting: a thin wavering line of numbers, none of them ever large.

They are short people, deep-chested from the altitude, and you know a carver's age by the hands before the face. The young ones — the *waterline* workers — have hands like wet leather, nails worn to crescents, thumbs flattened from a lifetime of testing: the first skill anyone learns here is to press a thumbnail into stone and read what comes back. A waterline carver can date a wall to the season by touch, blindfolded, the way a vintner dates a vintage. They will do this for you if asked. They will do it if not asked.

The waterline is where the real work happens and the real workers live, though "live" is generous — they sleep in the dry middle band like everyone else and descend at first light, because the base of the tooth is faintly warm and always damp and the new stone exhales something that gives long-term sleepers there a cough the elders call *the stone's milk*. By day, though, the base is crowded — crowded by local standards, meaning eight or ten people — roughing out doorways with antler picks, hauling spoil in pine baskets, working in the permanent slight crouch of people racing a deadline that advances a finger-width a month. They talk constantly while they work and almost never about the work. The work is too obvious to discuss. The deadline does the discussing.

Above them, on stone a decade or three old, work the *finishers* — older carvers, slower, armed with basalt and wet sand, polishing and truing what the waterline roughed out before their knees gave. Finishing is considered the contemplative trade. A finisher may spend a year on one threshold. The threshold doesn't mind. The threshold has nothing but time, and is acquiring more of it, vertically, as they work.

And above the finishers, on ladders, the elders — gilding nothing, because there is no gold here, but doing the elder's equivalent: pressing nothing, carving nothing, simply *checking*. Running old hands along edges they cut sixty years ago at ground level, confirming that the work has held, that the room they made as apprentices is rising clean. The elders call this *visiting*. The rooms they visit are empty and will stay empty. They visit anyway. One does not stop being courteous to a room merely because no one will ever live in it.

## The Three Answers, Embodied

Ask why they keep carving and you get three answers; the answers have hardened, over generations, into something like households. Not clans exactly — thirty-eight people cannot afford clans, and everyone is everyone's cousin — but three temperaments, three tables at the feast, three styles of mark.

The **Spenders** hold that softness is the only wealth and the only sin is to sleep through it. Their idiom for a wasted opportunity of any kind — a missed marriage, an uneaten meal, a fine morning spent indoors — is *letting it harden*. They carve fast, rough, and generously; Spender rooms are large, plain, and slightly crooked, and there are a great many of them. The Spenders are not troubled that the rooms stand empty. Emptiness is not waste. *Uncut* is waste. An empty room is a spent coin, and a spent coin has done its work.

The **Keepers of the Owed** carve for someone. Descendants, say some of them; a people who left and will return, say others; the distinction matters enormously to the Keepers and to no one else. Keeper rooms are finished to a fault — drip-channels cut, sills trued, a hearth-niche in every chamber though no fire has ever been lit in most of them — and each is sealed at completion with the lineage stamp and the glyph that means, roughly, *held*. The Keepers maintain the rope ladders to the upper levels, or maintain the fiction of maintaining them. When a Keeper is asked who, exactly, is coming, the answer is a look upward, which is also the past, which is also, here, a kind of future.

The third group has no settled name and would refuse one. They are the ones who said, when asked, that the tooth would keep rising anyway, blank, and that blankness is unbearable. Call them the **Answerers**, since that is what they do: the stone speaks and they reply. Theirs are the strange rooms — the chamber shaped to turn the wind's moan into a fifth, the stairwell cut in a spiral for no structural reason, the wall of pressed handprints, hundreds of them, four generations deep, rising now through the moss line so that ferns grow from the oldest palms. The Answerers produce most of the Quarter's children and most of its leavers. This is generally agreed to be the same trait expressing itself twice.

## The Tenant

One person lives above the inhabited band, alone, in the abandonment.

The position is old and always filled, though never by appointment; someone simply moves up, usually in middle age, usually after a death, and the others adjust their courtesies. The current Tenant has held the upper city for nineteen years. The duties are self-assigned and unvarying: sweep the rooms that wind has filled with rowan leaves, test the old ladders and mark the failed ones with a smear of white slurry, prune the junipers back from the window mouths so the swifts can pour through clean, and walk the highest halls once a season, knuckles brushing the walls, so that every room is touched by a living hand at least four times a year.

The Tenant descends on census day, strikes the stair once, eats at the long table, answers questions in a voice gone strange and resonant from talking mostly in large empty rooms, and climbs back up before dark. The children are forbidden to follow and therefore follow, and are not punished, because every Tenant was once a child who followed.

## The Children

There are six at present, which is considered a rich year. Each was given, at birth, the same gift: a parent's wet thumb pressed a mark into fresh stone at the base, low down, at the level of the world. The mark rises with the tooth; the child rises with the years; for the first decade or so the child wins the race, looking down at their own beginning, and then the stone's patience tells, the mark draws level, passes eye height, keeps going. The stone always wins eventually. There is a particular afternoon in most lives here — fourteen, fifteen years old — when a person looks at their birth-mark and finds it just above their head for the first time, and that is the day, by custom, they choose a trade. No one administers this. The tooth administers it.

The children's games are vertical and tonal: they race the stairwells, knock messages between light-shafts in a code the adults pretend not to remember inventing, and commit the Quarter's only crime, which is carving in fresh stone without leave — initials, animals, lies. The punishment is to be shown, by an elder, an illicit carving the elder made as a child, now eleven meters up and absolutely permanent, and to be told: *think about what you want overhead for the rest of your life.* Some children stop. The ones who don't become Answerers.

## The Dead

The dead are the Quarter's most numerous residents and its only homeowners.

A body is carried down, not up — down to the warm base, to a niche cut the morning of the death, in stone soft enough that the grave is dug in an hour by grieving hands and no tool. The body is laid in, the niche is sealed with a slurry of the freshest rock, and every mourner presses a stamp or a thumb into the wet face of it. Then the dead begin their tenancy: a hand's width a year in a good year, less in most, rising through the waterline, through the working levels, through the dry inhabited band — there is a decade or two when you live among your particular dead, pass their sealed doors daily, and this is held to be the proper arrangement of a household — and on upward, into the abandonment, into the moss, into the green.

The oldest graves are in the gardens now. Roots have found some of them; there is a wind-flattened rowan on the seventh level of the western face that the whole Quarter calls by a great-great-grandmother's name and means it literally. The dead are the only residents who never move house, the only ones whose rooms stay occupied, and — the elders point out, visiting — the only ones who will ever see the top of anything.

## The Swifts

Thirty-odd, dark, screaming, the only fast things in the country. They nest in rooms newly come of age and abandon them as they rise too high — no one knows too high for what; the swifts know — so the colony pours downward through the city, generation by generation, against the current of everything else. The carvers feed them nothing, touch nothing, and consult them about everything. A room the swifts have chosen is *ripe*. A room they've quit is *gone up*. A summer they fail to return at all is discussed for years afterward in lowered voices, and there has only been one.

Beyond the swifts, the bestiary thins to almost nothing: spiders in the moss pelts, a thread of pale midges over the cisterns, a fox that visits the saddle in hard winters and never climbs. The Quarter notes the fox in the autumn tally, separately, as a guest.

## The Knocker

And then the resident with no census mark.

It lives, if it lives, in the deep stair-shafts below the waterline, in stone too new to be called rooms yet. Two knocks, a pause, two knocks, in answer to loud sounds from outside. The theories are these, in descending order of how comfortable they make people: that it is the stone drying; that it is an acoustic trick of the shafts; that it is the dead, settling into their rising; that it is the first carver, who by some accounts never left; that it is the tooth itself, and the tooth is learning the local custom, which is that one announces oneself before entering a room.

The Tenant, who has the longest acquaintance with the Quarter's emptiness and is therefore deferred to on the subject, holds the last opinion and adds one observation: the knocking has, very slowly, over nineteen years, gotten better. More even. Better timed. The way an apprentice gets better.

So they knock back. Everyone, always, without exception — the Spenders briskly, the Keepers formally, the Answerers with variations. Two knocks, a pause, two knocks. The cost of politeness is nothing. The thing being answered is, after all, still coming up out of the earth, warm as an animal, soft enough to take a thumbnail, and whatever it turns out to be when it is finished being born, every person in the Risen Quarter intends for it to remember that they were courteous — that the first thing it ever heard from its tenants, from the very beginning, riding upward year by year in the permanent stone, was an answer.
