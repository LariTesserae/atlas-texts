**The Census**

At first you would say nothing lives here, and the plateau would let you say it. That is, in fact, the first thing the place does to a visitor: it presents its evidence — the unchewed moss, the untracked basins, the windows lit on empty rooms — and waits for you to file the report. *Uninhabited.* The report is wrong, but it takes days to see how, because you are counting the way counting is done elsewhere: looking for things that move, eat, breed, leave marks, die. Nothing on the plateau does any of those things, and so nothing registers. But the census of a place like this has to be taken differently. You have to count what is being done, and to whom, and by what, and for whom. Counted that way, the plateau turns out to be one of the most crowded places imaginable.

---

The first inhabitant is the one whose organs you have already walked through.

The holding-towns are not its houses. You sensed this — that they had the quality of organs rather than dwellings — and the sense was correct, just incomplete. They are heart-chambers, glands, lungs. The cable runs strung between them are nerve and tendon; the shafts cut into the living rock are its root system, going down to whatever it drinks; the fixed steam plumes are its exhalation, which is the same exhalation the whole plateau lives inside — that saturated half-degree-from-condensing breath is *its* breath, held at the top of the inhale for a geological age. It is one being. It has no outside edge anywhere on the tableland. It does not move because it has nowhere to move to; it is already at every place it could go.

Its metabolism is the strangest thing about it, and the easiest to misread as absence. It eats gradients. That is the whole of its diet. Every difference that tries to open in the world — a degree of cooling reaching toward frost, a millimeter of moss reaching toward longer, a grain of stone loosening toward round — it finds, consumes, and excretes as sameness. The continental roar is the sound of this digestion. People mistake the constancy for idleness, for a machine left running, but constancy at this scale is the opposite of idleness: it is a being at absolute full exertion, doing the hardest work there is so smoothly that the work reads as nothing happening. The sixty-cycle hum is its resting pulse, and it has no other kind.

It does not notice you. This takes a while to accept and longer to forgive. You walk through it for days, the only moving thing in its entire body, and it never turns toward you, because you are within tolerance. The heat you shed is repaid into the cold. Your footprints in the meter-deep moss are lifted out behind you, the pile restored, your passage refunded. Your voice, if you use it, is taken into the chord. You are a perturbation, and cancelling perturbations is what it is. It metabolizes your visit the way it metabolizes frost. You will leave no more trace on it than weather leaves on a heartbeat, and this is not contempt; it is simply that you are small enough to be part of its food.

---

The second population is much larger, and consists entirely of prisoners.

Frost lives here. This is the thing the edges were trying to tell you. Frost is present on the plateau — present at every weeping arris, in every film of water sliding down every blade of stone, half a degree away, fully formed in potential, leaning on the door of every droplet — and it is not permitted. Not absent: detained. The same is true of erosion, which inhabits every conchoidal face, pressing; of wind, which the dimpled tarns have never once been allowed to meet; of ripple, of thaw-and-freeze, of season, of succession, of rot. The entire ordinary staff of time is on the plateau, reporting for work continuously, and continuously refused.

And because the engines *repay* decay rather than preventing it, the prisoners include events as well as forces. Things on the plateau do happen — and are annulled. Somewhere in the green pelt, every second, a moss filament reaches the end of its life, and the death is held at the instant before its completion, refunded, returned to its starting balance. The lushness that has nothing to do with warmth is this: not a living carpet in the flowing sense, but a population of organisms each suspended at the top of its own verb. The stunted silver trees are mid-gesture and have been mid-gesture since the lifting. Every tarn contains, in custody, all the ice it has ever almost made. Every knife-edged ridge contains the rounded boulder it would by now have become — the plateau is densely inhabited by its own cancelled futures, each one present, each one paying rent in stillness. When you put your face close to the perfect moss, what you are looking at is a crowd.

---

The third inhabitant is harder to locate, because it does not live in space at all.

*The temperature here was decided once.* A decision implies a decider, and the decider is in residence — you can feel the residency in the quality of the gray light, which is the light of an occupied house — but you will not find it in the towns, because the towns are its instruments, not its rooms. It lives in time. It inhabits the one moment the plateau was lifted into, the way you inhabit a room: furnished it, closed the door, and stayed. The plateau is its address — not a *where* but a *when*, a single second appointed and upholstered and held at temperature, and the shadowless light that tells no time is simply the window-light of a tenant who has only one time and needs no others told.

You cannot meet it, and the reason you cannot meet it is the same reason you can move. Everything native to the plateau is at the chosen moment; you arrived carrying your own seconds with you, tracking them in like mud, and you cannot stop shedding them. Your motion is not vitality. Here, motion is just what being at the wrong time looks like. You are the anomaly — the one thing on the tableland that is displaced, dragging a wake of elsewhere-now behind you — and the place, with no malice whatsoever, tidies your seconds away as you pass. The tenant of the moment does not refuse to see you. It is simply never *when* you are.

---

And then there is the fourth resident, who is the reason for the other three, and who is made entirely of absence.

The one the plateau was finished for lives here. This sounds like wordplay until you have spent enough days in the holding-towns, and then it becomes the plainest fact about the place. No one has ever been more thoroughly in residence anywhere. Every property of an entire landmass has been set to their preference and kept there at the cost of a continental roar: the cold tuned, the wet tuned, the light tuned, the green tuned, the doors standing at angles that are not random — you become certain of this slowly — but *convenient*, hospitable to a particular gait; the lit windows at the height of particular eyes. The plateau is a mold shaped around a casting not yet poured. The expected one inhabits it the way a name inhabits a reserved seat, the way the guest of honor inhabits a table set before the door opens: exactly, completely, and not at all.

You will try to read their shape from the vacancy. Everyone does. You will measure the stair-rises in the gantries, the widths of the gridded streets, the height of the constant amber lamps, trying to triangulate the body all this accommodation implies — and every measurement will come back the same way: *you, nearly.* Which means either that the vacancy fits itself to whoever measures it, hospitality so total it has no fixed guest at all — or it means the other thing, the one you will think about on the long walk down off the tableland, into country where the edges are rounded and your footprints stay.

---

So the census, in the end, reads: one worker, the size of the world it works on, eating differences at full exertion forever; a multitude of prisoners, every force and future the place forbids, all present, all held; one tenant sealed inside a single furnished second; and one resident who is a person-shaped hollow in everything, kept in perfect condition, accommodated down to the angle of the doors.

Or — and this is the version that comes to you last, with the roar steady at your back like a planet's pulse, already fading from sound into a property of air you are leaving — the census reads: *one.* One inhabitant wearing four descriptions. The work and the held, the moment and the waiting, indistinguishable at the bottom, a single being whose body is the engines, whose dream is the moment, whose hunger is the vacancy, and whose breath is the whole wet green tableland — held, the way a held breath lives in a chest: enormous, effortful, perfectly still, and not, whatever it looks like, finished.
