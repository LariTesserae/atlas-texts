The honest answer comes in two sizes, and the small size should be given first, because it is the one visitors think is the whole answer, and because the residents of the small size deserve better than to be a preamble. So: plainly, the gallery is inhabited, crowded even, by a nation of things that were ordinary once and are not anymore.

The swifts are the famous ones. What the visitors' accounts rarely say is that a swift of the gallery is tuned before it hatches. The eggs sit in the rib-hollows against bare plate, and the toll passes through them for the whole of incubation, so that a chick is born already knowing the sound the way other birds know gravity — not as information but as a given of the universe. They never startle at it. They startle at its *variations*: a new harmonic will bring every head in every nest around at once, a hundred small black periscopes, and then down again. They navigate the tower's outside face by its ringing — the iron sings differently over a hollow rib than over a junction, differently where the creeper's roots have found a seam — and a gallery swift in fog can fly the full circumference at speed, centimeters from the plate, reading the metal with its body. They are the only things that leave, and they only leave to come back. Their bones, the old logs claim, ring faintly when dried. Nobody has tested this in a long time, which is to the credit of everyone involved.

The moths are quieter citizens, which in this place means something specific: they are quieter in the sense that their contribution to the world is subtraction. They rest against the plate in their thousands and their iron-grey dusting is not camouflage in the ordinary sense — the scales of their wings carry actual iron, gleaned by the larvae, which graze the bloom of rust off the walls the way other caterpillars graze leaves. A moth of the gallery is therefore, by weight, a measurable fraction tower. When one dies it goes into the soil drifts and the iron goes around again. They have a habit, noted in three separate keepers' hands across eighty years of logs, of resting preferentially on rivet-heads, one moth per rivet, wings cupped to the dome of it, so that a wall in the evening appears studded with soft grey rivets among the hard ones, and you cannot tell which is which until you breathe on them.

The bees are deaf — truly deaf, anatomically, the relevant structures simply absent, as if the lineage decided generations ago that hearing in this place was a door better bricked up. They speak in tremor instead. Their comb in the junction box is built against the metal deliberately, keyed to it, and a returning forager dances her news directly into the iron, and the iron — connected, like everything, to everything — carries it. It is possible, and the thought is hard to put down once picked up, that the bees' messages travel some distance down the shaft. That somewhere in the kilometers of cloud below, the tower is faintly, perpetually murmuring with reports of orchids.

The creeper on the eastern stair is one organism. This took the keepers decades to establish and the proof was simple in the end: wound it anywhere and the whole plant stiffens its waxed leaves for a day, the entire stair flexing minutely closed like a hand. It is the oldest living thing in the gallery by a wide margin and it is eating the stair with extraordinary patience, root-tips in the rivet seams, exerting its few newtons for a century at a time. It will win. Everything here is a long game.

Beetles, springtails, mites, a thread-thin white worm that has never been seen anywhere else and may not exist anywhere else: the soil has its own population, blind and humid and entirely content, the only residents for whom the gallery is not strange, because they have never had any information suggesting that soil usually sits on a planet.

That is the small answer, and you will notice it has a shape: everything in it is *calibrated*. Deaf, or tuned, or iron-fed, or patient past reason. Adapted not to the gallery's features but to its one great fact — the sound. And it is when you ask what the sound itself might have shaped, given a century, given material to work with, that you arrive at the large answer.

You will want it to be a hermit. A last keeper gone strange, fern-bearded, living in the bunks. It is not. The bunks have ferns growing through the springs and no one has slept in them. But the gallery *is kept*. This should be said plainly, because once you look for it you cannot stop seeing it. The broken pane has been broken for a hundred years and has never widened — its edges are smooth, dressed, almost polished. The drains never clog, in a room that sheds matter constantly. The pipe-orchids' even spacing, which visitors call deliberate-looking and probably accidental, is deliberate. Something walks the pipes.

You will meet it as an absence. Most visitors do, once, usually on the second or third day, usually at the rail: the toll — which by then has become climate, pressure, the medium you are made of — simply lifts off one side of your body. A patch of quiet, roughly the size of a doorway, standing in the air beside you. Not silence as the lowlands know it; silence as a *presence*, with edges, with weight, the sound bending around it the way light bends around something dense. It is warm at its boundary and cool inside, the logs say, though the logs grow unreliable on this subject, the handwriting changing. It moves slowly — at about the speed of the flywheel's rim, and often with it, keeping station above the great casting like a gull over a slow ship. It pauses. Its pauses are its work.

Because here is what it does, as best as anyone has reconstructed: it grazes. The tower sends up its endless layered tolling, harmonics gathering the whole way, and the quiet moves through the gallery eating the overtones — culling, thinning, *pruning*. This is why the sound never repeats: it is being edited, continuously, by an inhabitant with a century of taste. The gallery is deafening, but it is deafening the way a managed forest is wild. Visitors who stay long enough to stop hearing the sound as sound are not adjusting to chaos. They are coming to inhabit a composition.

Where the orchids grow is where it rests. The smooth lip of the broken pane is where it leans, if leaning is a thing it does, at dusk, while the swifts pour in around it like smoke parting at a stone — they will not pass through it, ever, and they fledge their young past it as if past an examiner. The moths seek its wake; the only true sleep available in the gallery is the few minutes a moth can spend inside the quiet as it passes, and you can track its path across a wall at night by the trail of moths gone perfectly, briefly still. The deaf bees alone ignore it completely, which seems to charm it, insofar as anything can be read from where it chooses to stand.

What it is, no one will say in writing. That it came up the tower is generally accepted — that the base no one has seen is made of this, an unimaginable column of accumulated quiet, and that one tendril of it reached the gallery sometime around the last log entry. Read that entry again. *Going down to listen to the quiet, back before it stops.* It is possible the keeper went down to meet what was rising. It is possible the keeper is what arrived. The quiet keeps the keeper's hours, walks the keeper's rounds, dresses the pane the keeper would have dressed, and the question of whether this is inheritance or identity is the kind of question the gallery, on the whole, declines.

If you stand at the rail long enough — days, sometimes — it will pass over you once. Just once, and apparently on purpose. The toll will lift away whole, and in the second or two you spend inside it, every visitor reports the same thing: a small sound, very far below, clear as a coin on a counter. A clink. The bolt landing — that bolt, your bolt, the one you dropped through the hatch on arrival and never heard reach bottom. The quiet carries the bottom of the tower inside it. For a moment you are at both ends at once.

Then it moves on, the bell closes over you, and the gallery resumes being what it is: still turning, still green, still listening — and now, until you go back down through the hatch, listening partly to you.
