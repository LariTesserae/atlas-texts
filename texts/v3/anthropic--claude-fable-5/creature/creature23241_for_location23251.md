**Who lives in the Weir**

Begin with the people, because they would want you to begin with the people, and because they are the easiest to see. They are not the oldest residents. They know this. It is the first thing they would tell you, if telling were something they did at a distance.

---

**The Held**

Outsiders call them Weirfolk, basin-people, the cloud-top stackers. They have no spoken name for themselves because they have almost no spoken anything. Their name for themselves is a grip: the right hand closing around the left forearm of another person, firm, held for the length of one slow breath, then released. Travelers who learn the pressure-language render it, inadequately, as *the Held*, or sometimes *those who are holding on*, and the people accept either translation with the particular patience they extend to all approximations.

They are not tall. Generations in the bowl have built them low and broad, deep-chested for the thin air, thick through the wrist and forearm — a Weir child of ten has the grip of a lowland mason, because the grip is not just labor here, it is literacy. Their hands are extraordinary and ruined in the way that all working hands are ruined: split-nailed, rope-callused, scarred white across the knuckles where the cold stone has taken its tithe. They keep them beautiful anyway. Hands are the face here. They oil them with rendered fat each night, the way other peoples comb their hair, and the worst insult in the basin — almost the only insult, in a culture with little use for them — is to speak to someone with a glove on.

Their faces carry the bowl on them. Every adult wears the same mask of weathering: skin gone wind-glazed and brown-grey except for two pale ovals around the eyes where the horn goggles sit, so that indoors, by lamplight, with the goggles off, they all look faintly startled, owlish, as if recently woken. Their eyes themselves are narrow by habit and by breeding, the lids heavy, the squint built in. Most are some shade of grey or watery blue. Elders go nearly white-eyed, not blind — cataract is strangely rare here, the goggles do their work — but bleached, the way the courses of stone all weather to the same wet grey within a season.

And they are quiet in the body, not only the voice. This is the thing visitors find hardest to describe afterward. The Held move the way the ice leaves the rock: a slip, a settling. They do not stamp, clatter, slam, or drum their fingers. There is no point — the bowl would eat it — and a habit pointless for ninety generations stops being a habit and becomes an anatomy. Watch a bucket-line of them passing stone in the white glare and you will see thirty people working flat-out in something that looks, from the cirque rim, like ceremony. It is not ceremony. It is just what effort looks like when it has given up on being heard.

**How they speak, and what that has made of them**

The pressure-language is spoken skin to skin, mostly palm to forearm, a vocabulary of squeezes, taps, draws, and holds — somewhere over a thousand root-signs, the scholars who have studied it guess, though the scholars who have studied it admit they were only ever shown the guest registers of the tongue, the words you give a stranger.

Understand what this means and you understand most of the people. To speak, you must touch. To converse, you must stand inside another person's reach and stay there. There is no shouting across a room, no muttering at someone's back, no addressing a crowd. Every utterance in the Weir is, structurally, an intimacy. Their word for *conversation* and their word for *embrace* differ by one finger.

The consequences run all the way down:

They cannot lie well, and know it. The forearm you speak on carries a pulse; the hand you speak with has a temperature, a tremor, a hesitation. The language is involuntarily honest the way a face is involuntarily honest, only more so, because you cannot turn a forearm away. So the Held do not prize honesty — it would be like prizing wetness — and instead prize *tact*, the art of which true thing to say. Their great social skill, the one they admire in elders and try to grow in children, is a kind of merciful selection.

They cannot argue at a distance. To quarrel, two people must hold each other, and most quarrels do not survive the holding. Those that do are taken seriously, because anything you can keep saying with your hand on someone's living arm, you mean.

They have almost no concept of eavesdropping and a vast, structural concept of privacy. Privacy is simply distance. Step back two paces and you are unreachable, sealed, alone in the glare. A person standing apart at a pool margin is understood to be in the equivalent of a locked room, and is left there. Grief is done this way, standing apart; so is decision; the basin at any hour has three or four figures stationed at its edges in dignified solitude, and the rest of the settlement flows around them like water around set stones — which is, of course, exactly the courtesy they learned it from.

And at distance, past the reach of hands, they have the ripple-telegraph: the signal pools, kept ice-free, and the flat slap of a paddle. The code of rings is simple — there are perhaps forty distance-signs, mostly verbs: *come, eat, water-moving, wall-down, dead, born* — and it is read with the eye, a silver circle widening across grey water. Children learn ripple before grip; a baby on a hip will turn its head to a signal-ring months before its arm can hold a word. The ripple-watchers, usually the old, the lame, and the heavily pregnant, sit shifts at the pools, and the office is honored the way other peoples honor bell-ringers, which is to say absolutely and without fuss.

They are not, as travelers sometimes report, deaf. Their ears work. It is just that hearing, in the bowl, has been reassigned. They hear through bone — teeth set lightly against a pry-bar, a palm flat on the rock — and they read the basin's health through their feet the way a sailor reads weather through a deck. An elder walking the terraces at dawn, pausing, pressing one bare sole to the cold stone, is taking the pulse of the neighbor. They sleep in heaps, family cells full of bodies in contact, partly for warmth, partly because for the Held a touching body is a speaking body even in sleep, and a night alone is a night spent, by their reckoning, in silence of the worse kind.

They count among themselves a few offices besides the ripple-watch. The *wall-memories* — usually two or three living at a time — carry the settlement's structural history in their hands: which courses were laid in which lifetimes, which joints failed and why, which stones (every large stone is known individually, and many have names) have proven trustworthy and which are sulkers that shed their ice badly and shoulder out of true. The *seep-tenders* keep the warm vents clear and grow the pale cress, a leaf of which is placed on the tongue at every birth, marriage, and death, and at no other time, so that the taste of it — faintly sweet, faintly mineral, like licked stone — is for every Weir person the taste of the hinges of their life. And there is, always, exactly one *wrack-reader*, and the office is held the way one holds something hot.

**The wrack-reader, and the dead**

Because the water arranges things. The lines, the rings, the famous feather-settlement laid out accurate to the doorways. Someone must look at these arrangements — it would be rude not to, says one school of thought; it is rude *to*, says the other — and so the compromise, old as the walls: one person looks, on behalf of everyone, so that no one else has to and no one else may. The wrack-reader walks the gravel flats after each migration of the pools, studies what the water has sorted, and reports in three signs or fewer. *Nothing. Lines again. East.* The economy is the point. A wrack-reader who reported at length, who interpreted, who *translated*, would be removed from office — it has happened twice in memory — because the settled theology of the Weir, insofar as it has one, is this: the neighbor is not writing to you. The neighbor is doing what the neighbor does, and if some of what it does looks like address, the mannerly thing is to notice without presuming. You may act on a gesture. You may not claim to have understood it.

Their dead go to the water, because there is nowhere else for them to go — no soil, no timber for burning, and the stone is needed. A body is washed, dressed in its own birdskin, given the cress-leaf, and laid at a pool margin at the basin's edge, and the family stands apart from it, each at their own distance, for as long as they choose. Then everyone goes back to work. Within days — never more than four, the Held will tell you, and they have been counting for ninety generations — a channel opens to the body, or a pool walks itself over, and the dead person is taken. No one watches this happen. It is the one event in the bowl that has, by absolute custom, no witnesses; when the water is seen to be coming near a laid-out body, the ripple-signal goes around — a slow double-ring, *turn away* — and the whole settlement works facing the southern wall until it is done.

The bones come back. Not soon — years, sometimes decades — but the wrack-readers' tallies are clear: the pale drift of feather and shell and casting that the water sorts into its lines carries, every so often, a human bone, water-polished, and the reader is obliged to report it like anything else. *Bone. Lines.* The Held do not find this distressing. The newts come back out of the ice; the molt comes back in rings; the grandmothers come back a knuckle at a time, folded into the neighbor's arrangements along with everything else the bowl has shed. There is a grip for it, taught to children early, a slow draw of four fingers down the forearm, which the travelers' word-lists translate as *returned*, and which the Held, pressed for nuance, will amend with a second sign that means, roughly, *still here, differently*.

**The other residents**

The white birds — the Held call them with a flutter of fingertips against the wrist, *the upstairs people* — live and die without ever leaving the bowl. No one has seen one below the cloud line. They mate in the water-fluted grooves of the cirque walls, lay one egg, and spend the nights glazed: the ice-skin forms over them as they roost and they let it, their hearts slowing to a beat every few seconds, and in the morning the colony sheds its glass with the rest of the rock, a thousand soundless slips, and rises. A bird that dies in the night stays glazed and does not rise, and the wall keeps it, a white shape under shine, sometimes for years, until the water decides otherwise. The Held take feathers and skins only from birds the water has already brought down and sorted into the wrack — to take from the wall itself is the second-worst thing a person can do here, after building a roof over a seep — and so every birdskin coat in the settlement has passed through the neighbor's hands first, and is understood to be on loan.

The pillars of midges over the warm seeps are permanent. Individual midges live nine days; the pillars are older than the walls. They hold their columns in air that never moves, dense grey standing smudges, and the birds thresh through them all day in rotating gyres, and the pillars close behind the birds like water behind a paddle. Children are told the pillars are the breath of the seeps, which is not what the Held believe but is what they tell children, distinction they maintain carefully.

The pools hold the wax-white crayfish, eyeless, which walk backward out of any pool the water is about to abandon — the Held watch for this and trust it more than any other sign. The black newts overwinter as ornaments, frozen mid-stride in the ice-skin of the margins, and in the thaw they finish the stride. A newt has been observed to complete, across one freeze, a single step begun the autumn before, and the Held have a grip derived from this — thumb pressed slow across the wrist-bone — meaning *patience*, or more exactly *the rest of the step*.

And the silver fish, which arrive in any new pool within a day, by no route. The Held have, over generations, idly tested this — a pool opened on a high dry terrace, ringed with watchers; a pool opened inside a cell, under a roof, in a basin carried up by hand. Fish by morning, both times. They no longer test it. The conclusion they settled on is the one they apply to everything here: the fish are not traveling to the water. They are part of what the water *is*, kept folded somewhere in its grammar, unpacked where needed, the way a sign is kept folded in a hand.

---

**The neighbor**

Which brings us to the oldest resident, and the real answer to the question of who lives in the Weir.

The Held do not say the water is alive. They are exact about this, in their compressed way; the grip glossed as *the neighbor* carries no animacy marker, and when travelers press them — *is it a spirit, a god, a beast?* — they answer with the sign for *wall*, then the sign for *not-wall*, then they stop, having said what they meant. It is not a god; you cannot worship something whose habits you have to mop out of your doorway. It is not a beast; it has never hungered, hunted, or fled. It is the other party. It holds the basin the way they hold each other's forearms: continuously, with pressure, saying things or not saying things — they decline to rule.

But live here a season and you will keep your own tally, as every traveler does. The bowl eats sound, yes — but pressure carries in it perfectly, which is to say the basin is deaf to everything except the kind of speech its people use, or its people use the only kind of speech the basin transmits, and which of those you write down says more about you than about the Weir. The signal pools sometimes ring without a paddle: a single circle, widening from the center, no source. The watchers note it and signal back the sign for *seen* — not because they believe they are being addressed, they will tell you, but because answering is cheap and rudeness is not. The water mounds, holds shapes, lets them go. It takes the dead unwitnessed, as if it shared the custom. It laid the settlement out in feathers, once, the day before it took the eastern cells — a gesture the Held refused then and refuse now to call a warning, and acted on anyway, which is perhaps the most precise description of their entire relationship.

And there is the matter of the village itself. Ninety generations; not one stone where it started; a third of the people rebuilding at any hour. Step back — stand at the cirque rim with your slit goggles on and watch the whole bowl at once — and the settlement stops looking like a thing the people maintain against the water and starts looking like a shape the bowl *keeps*, the way a migrating pool keeps its shape while every particle of it changes: dissolving at one edge, depositing at the other, walking itself slowly across the centuries with forty drystone cells held steady in the middle of the flow. The people are the mechanism. Their hands are how the basin holds a village, the way its cold is how it holds its pools and its grooves are how it holds its birds.

The Held know this reading exists. Travelers keep offering it to them, palm to forearm, in clumsy guest-signs: *you, the water's hands. The village, the water's shape. You, kept.* And the answer, when an elder bothers to give one, is always the same, and is the closest thing the Weir has to a creed. A slow grip on the questioner's forearm — the people's own name, *the Held* — followed by a small correcting pressure, almost gentle, that turns the word over and gives it back with its other face showing:

*Holding.*

Both, the elder means. The same grip from either side. That is what a neighbor is. That is who lives there: a basin of water doing whatever it is the water does, and a few hundred quiet, broad-handed people doing it back — touching, sorting, returning, rebuilding, neither one letting go — in the bright bowl above the clouds where nothing ever stops moving, and nothing, in ninety generations, has ever once let go first.
