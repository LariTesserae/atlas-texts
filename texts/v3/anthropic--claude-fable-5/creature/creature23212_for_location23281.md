# The Quartersfolk

Begin with the hands, because everyone here begins with the hands. They are a working people's hands — calloused, strong at the thumb — but they are stained in a way that no soap addresses and no one tries to: amber at the fingertips, deepening over the years to a polished tobacco-brown, the cumulative residue of resin handled daily for decades. You can read a person's trade in the stain. Trimmers carry it in a band across the palm where the shears ride. Pressroom workers have it to the wrist. The membrane growers, who handle the living panes only with the backs of their fingers so as not to scar them, have it strangest of all: their knuckles are amber and their fingertips are pale, as if their hands had been dipped wrong-way-up into the town.

They are slow people, and they would tell you so without embarrassment, because they do not mean by it what other towns mean. They mean *calibrated*. A Quartersperson walks at the pace of the engine — eight strokes a minute is, it turns out, very close to a comfortable amble — and they talk the same way, with pauses long enough that strangers often answer a question before it has finished being asked, which the Quartersfolk find rude in a mild, forgiving way, the way you forgive a sapling for growing crooked before anyone has staked it. Their conversations are full of weather that has not happened yet and grievances that are forty years old and entirely amicable. They do not interrupt. Interrupting, here, is the social equivalent of girdling a tree.

## How They Are With the Buildings

Each household stands in a relationship to its structure that outsiders persistently misname. It is not ownership — you cannot own something that heals. It is not quite stewardship either, which implies the building is helpless. The local word, untranslated, sits somewhere near *tenancy-with* — the way you might say you live *with* an elderly relative who still does her own cooking.

Practically, it means this: every inhabitant knows their building's tells. The mother of the family by the east common knows that when the doorframe sweats in dry weather, the cistern-gall needs draining. The old pressman knows his house dislikes the smell of lamp resin and so he leaves his work clothes in a shed his house grew for the purpose — grew it, the family insists, slightly faster than asked, with what they describe as pointedness. Children are taught the houses' moods before they are taught their letters, and a Quarter child of six can tell you, laying a small palm flat against a wall, whether the building is drinking or resting or annoyed, with the unselfconscious fluency of someone who has never imagined walls could be otherwise.

When someone is born, nothing happens to the house, and the Quartersfolk consider this correct: a birth is the family's business. When someone dies, the white leaves come, and the family does not sweep them when they fall. They are gathered by hand, pressed flat, and kept in the household's leaf-book — every loss the building has marked, going back in some homes three hundred years, pages of white leaves gone ivory, gone parchment, gone the color of the membrane windows themselves. Reading a family's leaf-book is the deepest intimacy the Quarter offers, and it is offered rarely, and accepting it obligates you in ways nobody has ever written down.

## The Trades

**The trimmers** are the youngest crews, because the work is ladders and weather, and they are the closest thing the town has to a rowdy element, which is to say they sometimes laugh loudly before noon. Trimming is not cutting; it is *negotiation conducted with shears*. A good trimmer prunes so that the building barely notices — takes growth the structure was finished with anyway, persuades a doorway to stay door-shaped by making door-shaped the easiest direction to grow. A bad trimmer leaves a wall sulking, and a sulking wall is a season of cloudy panes and stiff hinges, and the trimmer hears about it at dinner, every dinner, gently, forever.

**The listeners** walk the sap lines with their rods, and they are the town's diagnosticians, its priests of pressure. Most are older; the ear takes thirty years to train and improves until death. The senior listener — there is always exactly one, by no rule anyone can cite — is currently a small square woman who has heard the engine's parent trunk through eleven thousand mornings and claims, plausibly, to know its sound better than her own pulse, which she says has spent her whole life slowly synchronizing anyway. She is the one consulted when a building seals an entrance, when the bakery has a bad year, when the engine's ninth-day rest seems to want to become a tenth. Her verdicts are short and never argued with, partly out of respect and partly because arguing with her involves pauses so long that most people lose their nerve in the silence.

**The membrane growers** are the aristocracy, if eleven years of stooping in a damp grafting-house can be called aristocracy. Each pane is begun for a particular wall of a particular building, sometimes for a buyer not yet born — there are panes growing right now promised to grandchildren. The growers think in decades as a matter of professional necessity, and it deforms them socially in a way the town accommodates: they are terrible at small plans, forget appointments made for the same week, and are unfailingly, eerily reliable about anything ten years out.

**The engine-keepers** are the smallest trade and the strangest, because their work is mostly restraint. The engine cannot be hurried, so the keeper's craft is the craft of not-doing performed with total attention — oiling what asks, easing the governors through the seasons, and above all honoring the ninth-day rest, during which the keeper sits in the engine-house doing visibly nothing, on the theory, centuries settled, that the tree rests better attended.

## Temperament, Lifespan, Departure

They live long — the damp, the resin in the air, the impossibility of hurrying anywhere all seem to agree with the human frame — and they age the way their buildings do, thickening at the joints, going slowly amber, healing rather than mending. An old Quartersperson has the same swollen settled handsomeness as an old doorframe, and they know it, and the comparison is a compliment here.

A few leave, every generation. The town does not resent this; the wide commons remember that the place itself once decided not to be bigger, and the Quartersfolk extend the same right to their children. Those who go are said to *go to fast country*, and the ones who come back — most do, eventually, in their forties or fifties, worn shiny by speed — are received without ceremony. Their houses, the families report, put out a modest flush of new leaf over the door within the month. Green, not white. Unasked. Every time.

And in the afternoons, when the mist is long gone and the light comes down green-gold, you will see them at their full characteristic ease: a trimmer asleep against a warm wall that has grown a slight hollow exactly his shape; the listeners' apprentices lying flat on the engine-house roots to feel the strokes through their backs; the senior listener standing at the cistern's edge in something that is not quite conversation with the heron, both of them silent, both of them patient, neither of them in any doubt about which one is in charge.
