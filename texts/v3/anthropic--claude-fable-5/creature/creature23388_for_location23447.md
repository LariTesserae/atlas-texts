# Who Lives in the Hollow Crown

You hear them long before you see them. This is the rule for everything that lives here, and it is the first thing to understand about all of them: they are creatures of a world that arrives through the ears, and each of them has made a different bargain with sound. Some spend it lavishly. Some hoard it. One kind of resident has learned to spend it in another's currency, and they are the ones who interest us most — but they come last, because that is the order in which the dark gives things up.

---

## The Criers

The climbers on the walls — the people call them *criers*, or a two-syllable falling call that means the same thing — are the loudest animals in the world, and they can afford to be, because nothing hunts them where they live.

Seen close (you will not often see one close; they permit it rarely, and on their terms), a crier is mostly limb. The torso is a slight, keel-chested thing, smaller than a child, slung between four arms that are each longer than the whole body — pale grey-green, the exact non-color of structural fiber in wound-light, with a sparse pelt that traps condensation and channels it toward the mouth. They drink off their own forearms. The hands are the marvel: two true fingers and a hook, the hook a single curved claw of keratin grown so dense it rings faintly when tapped, and a crier at rest hangs from one hook with the whole body slack, swaying with the chamber's slow flex, asleep in the way of something that has never once in its evolutionary history worried about falling. They do fall, occasionally. They open their arms and the loose skin between limb and flank catches just enough air to turn a fall into a long descending swerve toward the nearest pillar. It is not gliding. It is an apology for falling.

The ears are a third of the animal. They move independently, constantly, two pale dishes turning through the dark, and a crier's face is mostly an architecture for holding them. The eyes are small, weak, tuned only to the blue-green of wound-glow — a crier perceives the world as blackness threaded with the faint luminous seams of healing tissue, which is to say a crier sees only *food and the routes to food*, and hears absolutely everything else.

They live in troops of eight to twenty, matrilineal, led by the oldest females — not the strongest but the ones with the largest call-repertoires, because rank among criers is knowledge, and knowledge among criers is *song*. A troop's territory is too vast to patrol; it is held acoustically. The matriarch's calls, returning off distant walls, tell her the shape of chambers she has not entered in years — a crier reads echoes the way we read print, and an old one can scream once into a void and know, from what comes back and when and how softened, that a new tear has opened four kilometers north and slightly down, that the floor below it is membrane and not yet safe, that another troop has already arrived because their bodies are blunting the reverberation along the east wall. The screaming that fills the crown at all hours is not noise. It is census, cartography, claim, and gossip, conducted at distances of kilometers, and the echoes that double and quadruple every call are not interference — crier language is *built* on its own echoes, calls shaped so that the returning sound completes them, statements that are only finished when the chamber has had its say. A crier transported to open ground, if such a thing could happen, would be struck mute mid-word forever.

They eat the new growth — the translucent rib-tips, the wet scar-tissue, the sweet structural sugars the plant pours into its wounds. This makes them the heralds of the world's expansion: criers always know first where the plant has torn itself open, and they always announce it, and everything else that lives here has learned to parse that announcement. When the criers scream the long rising sequence the people call *the news*, gliders converge, moth-clouds shift, and somewhere a human band quietly re-knots its route-cords and starts walking.

What the criers fear, they do not name. When the floor-skin of a chamber moves, a troop goes silent all at once, mid-chorus, hooks set deep, ears flat — twenty animals becoming part of the wall. The people learned this silence by watching them. It is the oldest piece of borrowed wisdom in the crown: *when the criers stop, you stop.*

## The Lure-Bearers

The gliders own the air the way the criers own the walls, but they have made the opposite bargain. They are nearly silent. Their voices, when they use them at all, sit below the range of human hearing — long infrasonic pulses you feel as unease, a pressure in the chest with no source. What they spend instead is light.

A large lure-bearer is the size of a fishing boat's sail and weighs less than a child: a frame of hollow bone, a body that is mostly flight muscle and a stomach, and the wings — membrane stretched over finger-struts, the leading edges studded with paired photophores, cold yellow points that the animal can kindle, dim, occlude, and pattern at will. The light is bait, but bait is the least of it. Lure-bearers *converse* in light. Courting pairs spiral around the great pillars trading slow patterns, ignition answering ignition through hundreds of meters of black, and the people, who watch this from their shaded platforms, say the patterns rhyme.

They hunt by deceit, and the deceits are local and learned. In one region of the crown, the lure-bearers drift with two points of light spaced and bobbing like a careless traveler's lamps; in another they hold a single steady glow at the pitch and steadiness of a wound-scar, and wait for grazers; in a third — and this is the one that cost the people most, generations ago — they learned the warm tone of tallow-light, and would hang in the dark above a camp like a friendly lamp descending, until they were close enough to take whatever looked up. Every human lamp in the crown is shaded now, hooded in gall-husk, throwing light only downward and only when needed, and the people's word for an unshaded light is the same as their word for *invitation*.

For all this, the lure-bearers are not the terror of the place. They take the unwary and the small; they do not pursue; a hunting pass that misses is not repeated, because a second pass announces itself. There is even a use for them. Their dung, which falls in slow scattered pellets through the chamber air, seeds the floor-folds with the spores of the only thing the people farm — a grey shelf-growth, dense and faintly sweet — and an old band knows the drift-lanes the gliders favor and camps beneath them the way fishing villages sit at river mouths.

The largest lure-bearers, the old ones, give up hunting altogether. Past a certain size they live entirely on the moth-swarms, drifting open-mouthed through the columns of small life that rise on the warm breath of the plant, lights doused, wings barely working, going around the great pillars for years. The people call these elders *the patient ones* and consider it good luck to feel one pass overhead — the soft enormous pressure-wave, the half-second of deeper dark — though no one can say what the luck is for.

## The Drift and the Pools

Below the notice of the great animals, the crown carries a fine continuous traffic of small life, and almost all of it is going up.

The moth-things — there are dozens of kinds, and the people name them by sound: *paper-wing, hiss-wing, the silent ones* — hatch in the warm wet vaults far below and spend their lives riding the plant's exhalation toward the cold. They drink condensation off the pillars, they are drawn helplessly to any light, and they exist, as far as the rest of the world is concerned, to be eaten. But they have one season of consequence. When a chamber flowers — and chambers do flower, rarely, once in a human generation for any given vault: the walls erupting in pale waxy blooms the size of rooms, the only abundant light most residents will ever see, a soft white luminance that makes travelers weep and squint and shade their eyes against what is, objectively, the light of an overcast dusk — it is the moth-drift that carries the pollen up-shaft from vault to vault, through seams and along wind-routes, so that the plant's far-separated blooms can find each other. Everything feasts during a flowering. Everything also, by ancient unspoken truce, lets enough of the drift through. Even the lure-bearers dim their lights for it. The people say the plant pays for its weddings in advance, and everyone takes the payment, and no one robs the wedding.

The black pools have their own residents, and they are the strangest small things in the crown: eyeless, colorless swimmers, finger-长, descended — the people's lore insists, and the anatomy agrees — from something that once lived in sunlit water at the mountain-plant's distant feet, carried up egg by egg on the legs of moth-things over ten thousand years. They live under the ice-rims in winter and rise in the brief indoor rains, and they sing. Each pool has a single note, a thin crystalline pulse produced by the swimmers in chorus through the still water and the floor-tissue beneath, and because each pool's note is fixed by its depth and its population, the pools are landmarks — the most reliable in the crown, steadier than the wind-voices, and the people's route-songs are tuned to them. A traveler in absolute darkness, hearing a faint pure tone through the soles of the feet, will press an ear to the warm floor and *know where they are* to within a hundred meters. The people do not eat the swimmers. Not from scarcity — from policy. You do not eat your map.

And everywhere, on every surface, the grazers: thumb-sized armored things, slow as growth itself, rasping the films of algae-like bloom that thrive in the wound-light. They are the floor's texture, the wall's faint constant whisper, the protein in every human stew, and the reason the criers' children survive their first year. Nothing in the crown is humble enough to escape being eaten, except the things below.

## The Things Below

Of the residents of the sealed chambers, the under-vaults, the spaces beneath the membrane floors, honesty requires that little be said, because little is known, and the people enforce the little fiercely.

This much: they are large. The dragging sounds, where the floors are thin enough to pass them, suggest bodies tens of meters long moving with no urgency at all. They are warm — a band camped above an under-vault on a cold night will feel the floor heat gently where something has paused below, and will move camp without discussion. They never surface, in the proper sense; what travelers have witnessed is the floor-skin lifting, a slow smooth bulge the size of a house rising a meter and holding and subsiding, as if something below were pressing its back against the ceiling of its world to feel the shape of the one above. The criers go silent for this. The people go silent for this. The silence is not superstition. It is the entire body of accumulated evidence about what happens to things that make noise above a bulge, which is that they are not seen again, and the floor where they stood is found afterward unbroken.

The people hold two theories and refuse to choose between them, on the principle that a wrong certainty is more dangerous than an open question. The first theory: the things below are animals like any others, old beyond reason, grown vast in the sealed dark on a diet no one can name, the crown's deep fauna as the criers are its shallow fauna. The second theory, the one told in the lowest voices: they are not animals at all. They are the plant's own — its hands, its housekeepers, the means by which a body the size of a mountain range moves matter through itself, clears its failed chambers, seals its wounds from beneath. The people note, in support of this, that the things below are never heard in dying chambers, only in living ones; that the great floor-collapses are always preceded by their departure; that they have never, in all the generations, been heard to make a voluntary sound.

The people have no word for them. This is deliberate and absolute. To name a thing here is to be able to speak of it, to speak is to make sound, and sound travels through the floor better than through the air. When reference cannot be avoided, the people press a flat palm briefly against the ground — a gesture, silent, that means *that* — and the conversation moves on.

## The People

And then there are the people, who are the strangest animal in the crown, because they are the only one not made for it, and the only one that knows it.

They came up. That is the whole of their origin story, and they tell it without ornament: long ago, for reasons the tellings disagree on — flood, war, famine, a debt, a calling; pick your lineage and you will hear a different reason — some hundreds of human beings climbed out of a world of light and into the body of the plant, carrying stone, seed, fire, and children, and kept climbing for three generations, and stopped where the plant was young, because the young plant tears itself open and the tears are food and light, and the crown has been their country ever since. There may be twelve hundred of them now, in perhaps forty bands, scattered through a habitat the size of a small nation. They will likely never be more numerous. The crown is not generous; it is merely sufficient, and they have shaped their whole existence to the width of that sufficiency.

**Their bodies** are recognizably, almost defiantly human, but generations of the dark have edited them at the margins. They are slight and long-limbed — heavy bodies fall through membrane floors — with broad feet, the toes long and slightly spread, because the feet are sense organs here: a person of the crown reads the floor through bare soles the way you would read with your fingertips, gauging membrane from true floor by the grain and give and warmth of it, feeling the deep structural groans arrive through bone before they arrive through air. Their skin has gone pale and faintly translucent at wrist and temple. Their eyes are large, dark-adapted to the point of pain — a flowering chamber dazzles them, an unshaded lamp at close range is rudeness verging on assault — but the eyes are the lesser sense and everyone knows it. The ears are where the people live. Not larger, particularly, but *trained*, from infancy, with a rigor no sighted culture spends on seeing. A child of the crown learns to click — a sharp palatal snap — and to read the return, and by ten years old can stand in a void and tell you the distance to the far wall, the presence of a pillar, the softness of old growth versus the ring of new rib, the wrongness of a space where the echo comes back chewed because something is hanging in it.

**Their voices** are the most beautiful thing about them, and the most disciplined. The crown punishes loudness and rewards pitch, so the language has poured itself into tone: a quiet, breathy, intricately melodic speech, words distinguished by contour more than consonant, perfectly suited to being hummed through closed lips at the threshold of audibility. They can speak below a whisper and be understood at arm's length; they can also, when distance demands it, abandon the voice entirely for the struck-fiber drums, whose deep notes ride the resonant walls for kilometers, and the drum-language is the same language — the same tones, stripped of breath — so that a message arriving from far off is not code to be deciphered but simply speech, heard late.

Their names are sounds, not words. Each person carries a short melodic figure, given in infancy and adjusted at adulthood, that *is* them — sung to summon, drummed to ask after, hummed by the dying to identify themselves to whoever finds them. The people's name for their own kind is a two-note call, low then high, and it is also their greeting, and it is also a complete sentence. It means, as nearly as it can be rendered: *here — and you?* Every meeting in the dark begins with it. Identity, in the crown, is not a face. It is an answer.

**Their knowledge** is sung, because it has to be. There is no writing — nothing to write on that the plant will not grow past, tilt, swallow — and the country itself will not hold still: a route mapped in fixed terms is wrong within a season. So the maps are songs, and the songs are not descriptions of the land but *duets with it*. A route-song is a sequence of pitches: the wind-voice of this chamber, the pool-note of that floor, the gourd-chime hung at the third pillar, the interval between the moaning shaft and the chamber that sings in fifths — and you walk the route by singing it under your breath and listening for the world to sing the next line back. When the plant shifts and a chamber's voice changes, the song must change, and the changing of a route-song is the most serious ordinary act in the people's life: it is done collectively, by the band's elders, tested by its best listeners, and then drummed outward to every band that shares the route, because a wrong map here does not inconvenience. It kills. The people's word for *liar* and their word for *one who sings a route carelessly* are the same word, and it is the worst thing you can call someone.

**Their economy** is condensation, scar-sugar, moth-net, grazer-shell, and patience. They drink the pillars' weeping, collected in gall-husk vessels hung along the ribs. They harvest the wound-glow scars for the sweet repair-tissue — always partially, always from the edges, never the center of a wound, and always with the small spoken formula that accompanies every taking, which is not a prayer exactly; it translates as something like *noted, and owed*. They net the moth-drift in the up-drafts, farm the grey shelf-growth beneath the glider-lanes, render tallow for the shaded lamps, and trade between bands at the flowering festivals — the only times the scattered people gather in numbers, walking for weeks along the route-songs to stand together in a blooming vault, in light, looking at each other's faces, which they otherwise know mostly as voices. Children conceived at flowerings are common and considered lucky. Marriages are made there; the dead of the past years are sung there; disputes between bands are settled there, in the light, on the theory that no one can sustain a grievance while squinting.

**Their dead** are given to the wounds. When a person dies, the band carries the body to the nearest fresh tear — sometimes days of travel — and sets it into the torn tissue, and within a season the plant has grown over it entirely, sealing it into the wall, and the scar above a grave glows, as all healing glows, a little longer and (the people swear) a little brighter than other scars. The graves are therefore lights. A band's territory is threaded with the faint blue-green of its ancestors, and a traveler navigating by wound-glow is, often without knowing it, navigating by the dead — which the people find neither morbid nor remarkable. *The plant keeps what it is given*, they say, *and gives back light*, and they leave it at that, in their way, declining the metaphysics.

**Their belief**, finally, such as it is. They do not worship the plant. They are clear on this, and a little impatient when the question is pressed. Worship implies distance, petition, a being who might refuse or relent, and the plant — their word for it is the same as their word for *world*, and the same as their word for *house*, and very close to their word for *while* in the sense of a long duration — does not refuse or relent. It grows. What they hold instead is a single quiet conviction, arrived at over generations of listening, which shapes everything from their architecture to their grammar: *they are its fauna.* Not its tenants, not its parasites, not its priests — its animals, like the criers and the lure-bearers and the things below, one more kind of small life distributed thin along the frame, kept the way a body keeps its flora, neither loved nor begrudged. Some of them go further, in the low-voiced way the people go further: they note that the plant pours sugar into the tears its animals follow; that the floors firm fastest along the routes most walked; that the wind-voices the route-songs depend on are made by seams the plant could heal and, somehow, mostly doesn't. They do not say *it knows us*. They say — and this is as far as they will go, palm flat on the warm floor — *it has not yet grown as if we weren't here.*

So they build to be abandoned, and sing their maps, and shade their lamps, and answer every call with *here — and you?*, and once in a generation they stand together in a flowering vault with light on their faces, and when a new chamber tears itself open in the heights with that long roar and the great cold inhalation, the people stop where they are and listen with everything else that lives in the dark — the criers screaming the news across the voids, the gliders turning toward it, the drift already rising — and they wait for the silence after, and then someone, somewhere, hums the first revision of the first route-song into the new emptiness, very quietly, to hear what the world will sing back.

---

Because that, in the end, is what lives in the Hollow Crown — under the screaming and the wing-rip and the pool-song and the breathy melodic speech: listeners. Every resident of the place, from the moth to the matriarch to the unnamed warmth below the floors, has been shaped by the same single pressure into the same single posture, ears open, body still, attending. The plant grows and the hollow rings and everything inside it leans into the sound, reading.

And if the second theory is right — if the things below are the plant's hands, and the sugar in the wounds is on purpose, and the seams are left singing because someone needs the song — then the crown has only one resident, really, one slow enormous life kilometers thick and mostly absence, and everything else, criers and gliders and swimmers and people, all the small attentive fauna of the frame, is simply what it keeps. The way a body keeps a heartbeat. The way a house, given long enough, keeps its echoes.

*Here — and you?*
