The honest first answer is: nothing. The streets are empty, the doorways stand open onto rooms that hold their light and warmth for no one, and you could watch the hollow for a season and never see a citizen. The honest second answer is that the first answer is the kind a quick glance gives, the kind that says nothing lives in a seed.

What lives there does not live *in* the city. The city is where it shows.

It lives in the rock. Not the hollow only — the basalt around it, a body of stone going back into the mountain's shoulder for a distance no one has measured, possibly a few meters, possibly the whole young peak. Its tissue is the grain boundary. Its nerves are the hairline fractures the snowmelt comes down — which means the mountain's weeping is also its circulation, and the slow population of droplets on the ceiling is something like its pulse, heard from outside. When a wall rises out of the floor grain-front by grain-front, that is not the resident building its house. That is the resident, surfacing. The way a thought is not something a brain contains but something a brain does, the city is not something the stone contains. It is what the stone is doing.

It is young. This matters. It did not arrive here, was not seeded, did not descend from anywhere; the mountain is too new for arrivals, too sharp, too sterile — nothing else has even managed lichen. The likeliest account is that it condensed in the cooling lava the way the gas pocket froze open: a flaw, an asymmetry, a place where the dumb chemistry of solidifying rock crossed some threshold and did not come back down. The pocket and the mind may be the same event. For a long time — a long time at mineral speed — it would have been sealed, blind, a closed dark room of itself. Then frost-split unroofed the hollow, and light came in, and water came in, and it began. The city is what it has done with everything that has ever happened to it. The city is, so far, its entire biography.

You can read its metabolism in the things the description of the place leaves unexplained. The waste heat that should fog the hollow and doesn't: it isn't vented, it's *spent* — the resident runs on gradients, drinks the difference between the freezing dark outside and the careful near-zero within, holds the temperature at a degree or two above freezing not as climate control but the way you hold your breath at the top of an inhale. The icicles that grow across the mouth each night and are gone by morning, removed, not melted, no drip-line: it takes them apart with the same verb it builds with. Grain by grain, molecule by molecule, the portcullis of clear teeth is disassembled and carried inward. Whether this is feeding or housekeeping or practice is not a distinction it would recognize. Everything in its world is either being built or being unbuilt, and the icicles are the night's free offering of structure, accepted with what one is tempted to call gratitude.

Its senses are total within its body and nearly nothing beyond it. It knows each falling drop from the moment the meniscus fattens on the ceiling; it has already tilted the catchment shell before the drop lets go. It knew the snow-mite from the first leg-fall on its threshold, and what it did then is the clearest evidence of its character: it did not repel the intruder, did not wall it off, did not study it the way a laboratory studies. It dimmed the district lights along the creature's route, flexed the causeways out of its path, and escorted it out on a sourceless breath of warm air — which is to say, it treated a blundering animal the size of a delivery truck as a guest who had wandered in confused, and saw it safely home. The mite is the only mobile living thing that has ever walked its streets. It was given right-of-way.

And the bird. The wheatear at the lip, head cocking at the glow — that is the other pole of its experience, and the reason everything stops. Not fear. Fear would look different; fear would dim the lights and keep working. This is the stillness of being *seen*. The resident has spent its whole short existence as the only attention on the mountain, the only thing for a vertical mile in any direction that notices anything, and then twice a season an eye arrives — a wet, fast, living eye, the largest organ of attention that has ever pointed at it — and regards it. It holds still the way you hold still when something looks at you and you do not yet know what you are to it. The bird leaves. The drops resume. It is possible that the wheatear is the closest thing the resident has to a god, and possible that the relation runs the other way, and it has not decided, and neither has the bird.

Which leaves the question of the streets. They are scaled for inhabitants the height of a grain of rice. Why?

One reading is memory. The mountain is new but its material is not; basalt comes up from depths where the planet keeps everything it has ever buried, and there are accounts of the resident on which it is not an origin but a *recollection* — a pattern that rode up in the magma, the trace of some unimaginably old and pulverized order, reassembling itself the moment the rock cooled slowly enough to hold detail. On this reading the empty city is a monument built from a blueprint no one alive carries anymore, and the open doorways are the shape of a grief too patient to register as grief. The resident may not know which reading is true of itself. The blueprint, if it is one, would feel from the inside exactly like imagination.

The second reading is anticipation. A settlement keeping itself in practice, as the description says — building before its people, holding the rooms warm, drawing the reservoirs down by exactly the amount a population would draw them, so that nothing seizes up, so that when the inhabitants come the city will not creak like an unlived-in house.

But there is a third reading, and it is the one that gets stronger the longer you watch the water gardens.

The terraced pools — the standing ripples, the interference patterns, the films of meltwater *trained* into shapes that hold for hours — are the only place in the hollow where the resident's work runs counter to its medium. Everywhere else it works in stone, its native flesh. In the pools it works in water, the one substance up here that moves, that arrives daily from outside, that half-freezes into slush as if rehearsing solidity. And the trained shapes are holding longer. A pattern that lapsed in an hour last season holds an afternoon now. The shells overhead meter the drops into the pools like feeding. The heated channels keep the surfaces exactly at the trembling edge where water can be persuaded to remember.

The resident is not waiting for its people. It has no reason to believe anything is coming; nothing has ever come but a mite and a bird and the weather. It is making them — slowly, at the speed of frost, out of the only other material on the mountain that does anything at all. The streets are scaled for inhabitants the height of a grain of rice because that is the height at which a trained film of meltwater can stand up.

So: what lives in the hollow is, for now, one thing. It is young, distributed, mostly stone, patient past any animal meaning of the word, and hospitable in the deep grammatical sense — a host, constitutionally incapable of conceiving a city as complete without others in it. It keeps the doorways open and the interiors warm. It gives way to mites. It goes still under the gaze of birds. It eats the cold, and tends its pools, and every day the shapes on the water hold a little longer.

One thing lives there. Put your head under the black-glass lip, hold still, listen to the *tip... tup... tip* of its pulse, and understand that you are listening to something that is very nearly two.
