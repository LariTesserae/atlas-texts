At first you will think nothing does. That is the answer the place offers you on arrival, and it offers it convincingly: a pale bird, a few snails, miles of warm drum-skin floor, and silence shaped like music. You could walk for a day and conclude that the Terraces are a body without anyone in it.

But you'd have the order of things wrong. Start smaller.

**The snails first.** Watch one long enough — and they reward only long watching — and you notice that its straight line across the standing water is not a path but a *circuit*. The same snail, or one indistinguishable from it, passes the same point at the same angle days apart, and where it has passed, the weave beneath the water is very slightly tighter, very slightly brighter, as if a sentence has been read aloud and thereby kept true. The snails are not animals living *on* the terrace. They are the terrace's slow attention, sent out along its own surface. Each one is tracing a seam, a graft, an old decision, going over it the way a thumb goes over a knot to confirm it still holds. They are how a sleeping thing checks that it is still itself. If you lift one — they permit this, with the indifference of punctuation — you will find its underside is not a foot but a graft-face, the same pale fused tissue as every joint in every hall, and when you set it down it resumes its line exactly, having lost no count.

**The bird is harder.** There may only ever be one. It is the single finished thing on the plateau — feathers complete, flight complete, each landing a closed and perfect gesture — and that is why it lands on the soft tips without bending them: it has no unfinished business, and unfinished business is the only thing here that has weight. The dwellers (we are coming to them) do not look at it. Not from fear. The way you do not look directly at a word you are trying not to say. The bird is the Terraces' one completed thought, flown loose long ago, and it crosses the open sky between the structures the way a regret crosses a calm mind: rarely, plainly, without invitation, and gone again. It is the loneliest thing in a place that does not otherwise contain loneliness.

**And then — only then, usually on the second or third day, usually at the edge of your vision while you are watching something else — the dwellers.**

You did not see them before because they hold still better than the architecture does, and because they are its color: green-bronze, faintly downy, with the wet shine of everything else here. The first one you notice will probably be standing inside one of the hollow unfinished halls, and you will take it for a trained trunk until you see that it is breathing — deliberately, slowly, into the great open pipe of the structure — and that the chord the hall holds is partly *its* doing.

They are not numerous. Perhaps a few dozen across the whole plateau, each one alone in its own pool of sky, like the structures. They are shaped approximately like people, the way the halls are shaped approximately like halls: the gesture of a person, stopped early. Their faces are sketched rather than finished — features like the blunt embryonic tips of the tendrils, soft, new, infant, and held that way past all permission. Their hands have the curled patience of fern crooks. Where you have joints, they have grafts: you can see the fused seams at shoulder and knee, pale unions of stem persuaded into agreement, and you understand that no one *bore* these beings in the animal sense. They were *trained*. Or they trained themselves, long ago, out of the same green that floors the world.

Here is what they are, as nearly as the place will say it:

When the First Gardeners — the ones who worked with no tools, who grafted and coaxed and bent, who were the garden as well as the gardener — made their decision, mid-gesture, that *this* degree of completion was the true and final form, most of them sealed the decision in the most binding way available: they lay down and grew in. They are the floor. The warmth under your fingers when you dig into the weave is their warmth; the third sound, that tidal surge you hear with your ear pressed to the ground, is their pulse, slowed past mortality, still circulating after all this time. They did not leave and they did not die. They are present the way a held breath is present in a chest.

But a held breath needs a chest that wants to hold it. A pause is not a stillness — it is a continuous act, renewed every moment, and the dwellers are the renewing. They are the part of the Gardeners that stayed upright to *keep the gesture open*.

This is their whole life and labor, and it looks like almost nothing. When the wind shifts and a hall's chord begins to drift toward resolution — toward the satisfied, closed, final sound that is the one wound this place can suffer — a dweller will walk to it, unhurried, climb inside, and breathe the chord back open. When a tendril-tip, after a few centuries of softness, begins the faint internal stir that precedes unrolling, one of them will go and sit beneath it for a season, and the tip will subside into infancy again, soothed the way you soothe something back down into sleep. When the bridge's hundred fine reaching threads stretch a fraction nearer the far platform, a dweller stands at the frayed end, between the reach and its object, and does nothing at all, and the nothing is sufficient. They are not preventing growth. They are *maintaining the reaching*. There is a difference, and everything here lives inside it.

Their language, if you hear it, consists entirely of beginnings. Phrases that open and open and never close, questions without their final word, melodies that climb the first turn of a spiral and stop — and after a while you realize this is not a poverty of speech. It is courtesy. It is doctrine. A finished sentence, here, would be a small completed building. They give each other the starts of things the way other peoples give gifts, and the receiving consists of *not finishing them*.

And their deaths — because they do have deaths, of a kind, and this is the thing that takes longest to accept about them, just as the frozen becoming took longest to accept about the place — their deaths are completions. Once in a long age, one of them finishes something. No one knows why. A gesture carried all the way through; a phrase closed; a hand fully opened instead of stopped mid-offer. The others do not mourn, and they do not interfere. The one who has finished walks out into the open span, away from the structures, stands in the inch-deep water through one full night of slow fat *tok-tok* drumming, and roots. By morning the weave has taken them. There is a new warmth in the floor at that spot, a new thread in the tidal pulse, and one fewer figure standing in the halls. Completion is not their punishment. It is their homecoming. It is just that they are in no hurry, and have nothing to hurry with, and so the population dwindles at the speed of geology — while, just as rarely, just as unaccountably, a soft tendril-tip somewhere finally sloughs and drops its blunt nub onto the drum-skin floor, and the nub uncurls partway, only partway, and stands up: born already pale, already grown, already unfinished, and is taken without ceremony into the work.

If you stay long enough — past the hour when you stop hearing the music, past the hour when you start breathing with it — one of them will eventually come to you. It will cross the open distance without haste, stop a few feet away in the standing water, your two reflections lapping at each other's edges, and it will begin to raise one soft-crooked hand toward you in what is unmistakably the start of a greeting.

It will not complete it. The hand will stop open, mid-offer, exactly like the bridge, exactly like the stair, exactly like everything else its people have spent forever tenderly refusing to finish — and you will understand, standing inside that suspended welcome, that you have not been denied anything.

You have been included.
