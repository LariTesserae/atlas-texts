# Who Lives There

Start with the faces, because the absence of them is the first thing you'd notice and the last thing you'd understand.

A face is a solution to a problem this place does not have. Faces happen where there is light to look toward, wind to smell against, prey that flees in a particular direction — they are sense organs bundled at the front of a body because the world has a *front*, a direction events come from. Here, events come from nowhere. Nothing falls. Nothing blows. The indigo seam never moves. So the locals never gathered their senses into a forward-facing knot, and the result, when you finally see one up close, is a body that seems to be all flank — smooth, or ribbed, or pored, but *continuous*, with no point of address. You cannot tell which end you are talking to. Neither can it, and it has never needed to.

What they have instead is distributed everywhere. Pressure-sense, mostly: the entire skin of nearly every animal on the plateau is a barometer of obscene sensitivity, able to feel the four-a-minute exhalation of a bellows-tree from the far edge of its garden, able to feel *you*, your body heat convecting the thin air, your footsteps arriving through the basalt. They hear with their whole mass, through the ground, the way you'd hear underwater. They taste the condensate threads to know which way is home. Eyes were tried, long ago — there are vestiges, paired dimples of pale tissue on some of the older lineages, like the memory of an idea — and abandoned. The indigo seam is not worth the metabolic rent.

## The Valve-Wrights

Begin with them, because they are the citizens, the ones whose lives most resemble what you would call *living somewhere*.

Up close a valve-wright is about the size of a badger and shaped like a consonant — a smooth hard curve of body with six limbs that fold flat into grooves, so that at rest it is nearly cylindrical, a plug, a stopper. This is not an accident. A valve-wright sleeping in a duct *is* a valve; the tree uses their dormant bodies as gaskets, and a wright settling into its sleeping-niche can feel the pressure differential seal around it like a held breath. They are born in the warm galleries near the heartwood, in clutches of three, from leathery eggs the tree itself incubates in pockets of its tissue — and here already the line blurs, because the pockets are not generic hollows. They are *fitted*. The tree grows a brood-pocket in the shape of a valve-wright egg before any egg has been laid in it, the way your body grows a socket before the tooth.

Their lives are work, and the work is honest. Frost is the enemy — frost intrudes at every pore, every intake, riming the valves shut, and a tree whose intakes seal over will suffocate across a slow season, its compression chambers laboring against nothing. So the wrights patrol. They have rasping mouthparts on the undersides of all six limbs (the mouth, too, was distributed — why carry food to one point of the body when you can eat with whatever part is touching the meal?) and they plane the frost away in long curling shavings, eat it, drink it, carry the water inward. They cull fungus. They massage stiffened valve-tissue with a kneading motion that the tree responds to with a flush of warmth, localized, deliberate — payment, or gratitude, or a reflex that has been mistaken for both for a hundred thousand years.

They are not silent with one another. In the pressurized galleries the air is thick enough for real sound, and the wrights drum — limb-tips against duct walls, patterns that travel through the tree's tissue faster and farther than they travel through air. The tree conducts their conversations. Whether it listens is the kind of question this place refuses to answer.

When a wright dies, it dies inward. An old one stops patrolling, descends through the galleries to a particular depth, finds a pocket — fitted, again, grown in advance — and seals itself in, and the tree resorbs it over a season, laminating the minerals of its body into the next concentric ring. Cut a tree open (don't) and count the rings, and in every third or fourth ring you would find a denser band, a stratum of citizens. The trees are made partly of everyone who ever maintained them. The wrights know where they're going when they go. They drum, on the way down, a slow pattern that the young ones answer from above, and then they don't.

## The Tallowbacks

The garden grazers deserve their name, because they are mostly insulation. A **tallowback** is the size of a sheep and the shape of a loaf, four short limbs, no neck, no face, just a long underside mouth that mows. Beneath the skin: a hand's depth of fat so dense and so structured it functions less like blubber than like a building material. A tallowback's core temperature would scald you. Its surface is barely above the frost. It is a thermos that eats.

They live in the gardens and never leave them, and their relationship with the bellows-trees is the kind of arrangement that looks like obedience and is probably appetite. They crop the moss in spirals, and the spirals are exactly the patterns that open the moss bed's surface to the tree's warm exhalation at exactly the cycle's peak — and the tree, in turn, blooms a sugary exudate along the freshly cropped lines, so that the grazer's next pass follows its last one, and the spiral propagates. Nobody is directing this. The tallowback thinks, to the extent it thinks, *the food keeps being in front of me*, and the result is gardening.

They sleep through the trough of every breath. Four times a minute, when the exhalation fades and the garden's ferns tick shut and the cold leans in, every tallowback in the garden goes still — heart slowed, surface vessels clamped — and four times a minute they resume, mid-bite, with no sense of interruption. Their experience of life is continuous warmth and uninterrupted meals. The cold half of every minute simply does not happen to them. They are arguably the most contented animals that have ever existed, having edited the bad parts of the world out of their consciousness at the level of physiology.

They calve once a decade, a single calf, and the calf's first act — before nursing, before standing — is to press itself against the bellows-tree's flank and *listen*, imprinting on the specific compression rhythm of this specific tower. A tallowback moved to another garden (nothing moves them; this is hypothetical; everything here is hypothetical until the courier is involved) would starve beside abundant moss, waiting for a breath that never comes in quite the right time.

## The Cup Dwellers

Every garden's condensate pools have their own nation, and the nations have never met.

Take one pool — basalt cup, palm-sized, skinned with ice at the trough of each breath and liquid at the peak. In it: perhaps forty individuals of a species that exists in this cup, the six cups downslope of it, and nowhere else in the universe. They are translucent, segmented, the size of a grain of rice, and they live at a tempo that makes everything else on the plateau look geological — because they have to. Their world freezes shut two hundred times an hour. They have a working generation time of weeks. They are the only things here that *hurry*.

Three kilometers of near-vacuum is, for them, what intergalactic space is for you, and so every garden's pools have drifted into their own forms. In one garden the cup dwellers have gone social, rafting together at the freeze so the outer individuals form a sacrificial shell of bodies around a liquid core where the inner ones survive — and the species has, accordingly, evolved a lottery, a chemical shuffle performed at each freeze that randomizes who is outside. In another garden, eleven kilometers away, the same ancestral animal became a loner that secretes its own antifreeze and tolerates the solid state, going glassy and inert and clinking faintly against the basalt until the thaw. In a third, they have begun eating the moss spores that wash into the pools, and the moss has begun, over millennia, growing spore-bodies that overhang the water — the first tendril of a partnership that, given another million years, may produce something neither moss nor animal in the way that the bellows-trees are no longer quite plants.

Each garden is running a separate experiment. The plateau is an archipelago of unfinished answers to the same question.

## The Courier

And then there is the one that crosses.

You will want it to be a monster, because it is huge and unseen and it lives in the void, and you should let go of that want now. The thing that walks between the gardens — long-bodied, low, armored in overlapping plates that have been scoured by frost to a dull mirror — is the gentlest necessity on the plateau. It is, functionally, the bloodstream of a hundred-kilometer organism.

Understand what it carries. Plastered into the grooves between its plates, packed there by valve-wrights who groom it like a visiting dignitary whenever it arrives at a garden: spores. Eggs. Wright larvae in sealed pellets. Pollen-analogues from the bellows-trees, which cannot otherwise reach one another, having no wind to ride and no insects that survive the open. Every genetic transaction between every garden on the plateau passes between this animal's armor plates. Without it, each garden is a closed system, inbreeding toward silence; the trees know this in whatever way trees know things, and a garden where the courier arrives erupts into a kind of vegetable festival — exudates, warmth, the moss flushing its rare reproductive colors (dark red, visible to nothing, persistent anyway) — and the courier stands in the exhalation for a day, being unloaded and reloaded by a swarm of wrights, eating its fill of the sugary bloom, and then turns back into the dark.

How it survives out there: slowly. It seals itself — the plates clamp down, the body beneath holds a private pressurized atmosphere, the metabolism banks to embers — and it walks the void as a closed vessel, navigating by the ground-borne *thoom* of distant trees, each tower a different rhythm, the whole plateau a constellation of drumbeats it reads through its footpads. The crossings take days. It does not sleep so much as it walks asleep.

There is probably only one alive at a time. This is the saddest and most certain thing anyone could learn about the plateau. The courier breeds rarely, late, and singly — and when its successor is grown, packed and provisioned in the warmth of some garden's galleries, the old one makes a final crossing with no destination. The trails are lined, here and there, with what you would take for boulders: long, low, frost-glazed forms, dozens of them, accumulating since before the moss had its current color. Nothing rots in the open. Nothing falls on them. The courier's road is the only cemetery on the plateau, and the courier walks it every circuit, over and over, past every predecessor, on its way to deliver the mail.

## The Inmost Ones

Deeper than the wrights' galleries, in the compression chambers themselves, where the air is squeezed dense and hot and the *thoom* is not a sound but the architecture of the world — something lives there too.

Call them **marrow-eels**, though they are not eels and the trees have no marrow: ribbon-shaped, boneless, blind even by local standards, adapted to a pressure that would let them exist nowhere else on the plateau or possibly the planet. They cannot leave. An exposed marrow-eel dies in seconds, unbecoming, and they seem to know it the way you know not to step off a cliff — they recoil from the valve-thresholds with the only vehement motion in their repertoire. Each one is born, lives, and dies inside the chamber-system of a single tree. Their entire cosmos is a warm pulsing volume the size of a chapel, and its god breathes four times a minute, and they swim in the breath.

What they do in there is housekeeping at a level the wrights can't reach: they graze the chamber walls clean of mineral scale, keep the laminated tissue supple, and — this is the strange part, the part that suggests the arrangement is older and stranger than symbiosis — they *time* things. A chamber's contraction is initiated by a wave of stimulation along its walls, and the wave is propagated, partly, by the eels, swimming their circuits in formation, brushing the trigger-tissue in sequence. They are, to some unmeasured degree, the tree's pacemakers. The breath you feel in your sternum from two kilometers away is being kept by a school of blind ribbons that will never know there is an outside.

And the parasites — because there are always parasites, even here, even in an economy this stern. **Frost-lice**: pale, flat, thumbnail things that slip into the galleries and squat in the warmth, eating the tree's exudates, doing no work. The tree's response is the closest thing on the plateau to anger. It maps them — somehow, through pressure-sense or chemistry — and it grows a wall. Over a week, the infested gallery is sealed at both ends, laminated shut, and the pocket of squatters is entombed alive in the next ring along with the honored dead of the wrights, indistinguishable to anyone counting strata later. The trees keep accounts. Everything inside them is either working, paying, or becoming a layer.

## The Largest Inhabitant

Which leaves the question you've been circling since you felt the whole plateau exhale at once.

The trees coordinate. Across gulfs no signal should cross, a hundred kilometers of towers fall into phase, and the air of the entire visible world rises and falls together. The mechanism is arguable — ground-vibration, the drumbeats traveling through basalt; chemical letters carried in the courier's armor; the eels' pacemaking entrained, tower to tower, over centuries. Probably all of these. But look at the shape of the thing. Towers that pulse and signal. A web of trails connecting every one to every other. A single long-bodied impulse moving along that web, garden to garden, carrying information — slow, yes, achingly slow, one transit in days, one full circuit of the network in years.

A nervous system is just this: nodes, connections, and something that travels between them. The plateau has all three. It has had them for hundreds of thousands of years, refining, entraining, accreting purpose onto its trails the way frost accretes onto everything else. If it is thinking, it thinks at a tempo where a single thought might take a century to finish crossing itself, and the courier's whole devoted life — every crossing, every cemetery walk, every festival arrival — is one impulse along one nerve. The synchronized nights, when every tower breathes together and the air of the world moves as one tide, might be nothing. Might be entrainment, dumb physics, coupled oscillators doing what coupled oscillators do.

Or they might be the moments the whole plateau manages, briefly, to pay attention.

To what, there is no way to know. The indigo seam, perhaps — the one event, the one direction, the only thing in the sky. A hundred kilometers of breathing towers, citizens drumming in the galleries, blind ribbons keeping time in the warm dark, one gentle armored mailman walking the graves of all its predecessors — all of it inhaling together and holding, for a moment, facing the only light there is.

None of them have faces. The plateau, it turns out, may have grown one anyway: a hundred kilometers wide, turned toward the bruise on the horizon, waiting at four breaths a minute for the sun to finish rising.

It has been waiting longer than the moss has been black. It is in no hurry. Nothing that lives here is.
