# The Residents of the Perched Sea

To ask what lives in the perched sea is to ask which parts of a body count as its tenants — and the sea has had a very long time to make the question unanswerable. Nothing here arrived. Nothing here colonized. Everything that swims, crawls, clings, drifts, or burns faintly in the dark was *issued*, the way a body issues blood cells: produced by the mass, for the mass, at the rate the mass requires, and reclaimed at the same rate from the other end. The line between organism and organ was crossed so long ago that no creature in the sea carries even the vestige of the distinction. They do not live *in* the perched sea. They live the way a heartbeat lives in a chest.

And yet — they are creatures. They have eyes, where eyes are for something. They have hungers, fitted exactly to what exists. They have, every one of them, an inner weather, however small, however still. It is worth describing them one station at a time, because the stations are all there is, and because each one, looked at long enough, turns out to contain something that almost resembles a life.

## The Grazers

Start with the silver schools, because they are what you would see first: flat fish like struck coins, moving over the bronze moss in formations that have not varied within the memory of the water.

A grazer is born into a circuit the way you were born into a century — it is simply the medium of its existence, invisible from inside. The school follows a closed path across the moss-hills, cropping as it goes, and by the time it returns to any given point the moss has regrown to precisely cropping height, so that the path is self-renewing and the school never has cause to deviate. No grazer has ever made a decision. This is not a deprivation; the capacity is there, folded in the small bright brain like an unopened letter. Present a grazer with a genuine fork — two paths, two outcomes — and something in it would engage that has never once engaged. The machinery of choice idles in every one of them, perfectly maintained by inheritance, never used, because the sea does not manufacture forks.

What a grazer has instead of a life story is a *texture*. The moss is not uniform — it cannot be, quite; it grows in whorls, and the whorls differ by fractions — and each grazer knows its circuit the way you know the inside of your own mouth: not as knowledge, as *self*. They are calm in a way no creature of the lower world is calm, because their calm has never been information. It has never meant *safe now*, since there has never been an *unsafe* to be relieved of. It is the calm of a held note.

When a grazer dies, it dies mid-circuit, and the school closes over the gap without ceremony, and something is produced — somewhere, on schedule — to eat it. The school does not mourn, but neither does it forget, exactly: the school is the only entity present, and the school is not diminished. A grazer is best understood not as an animal but as a *tenure* — a position in a pattern, currently held by this particular silver body, soon to be held by another, the position itself effectively immortal. The fish are mortal the way your cells are mortal. The grazing is not.

## The Tenders

The eel-things that swim the grid are stranger, because they live closer to the sea's voltage, and the voltage has shaped them inwardly as well as out.

Each tender patrols a length of bioelectric vein — some have territories of forty kilometers, swum end to end and back, endlessly — rasping away the films and felts that would otherwise insulate the conductive tissue. They glow, faintly, in sympathy with the current beneath them: when the grid surges, they brighten; when it ebbs, they dim. They cannot help this. Their luminescence is not a signal but a *leak* — they are so electrically intimate with the vein they serve that its state simply shows through their skin, the way a worry shows on a face.

Here is the unsettling thing about the tenders: they experience the grid's current directly, as a sensation running the length of their bodies, and the current is not uniform. It carries structure — pulses, harmonics, long slow waves that take hours to pass beneath a swimming tender like the shadow of an enormous thought. The tenders feel this all their lives. They are, without knowing it, the only creatures in the sea pressed directly against whatever the mass is doing with its nervous system, eavesdroppers on a monologue in a language no nervous system their size could ever parse. They respond to it the way you respond to music in another room: posture, mood, the speed of their swimming. A tender on a quiet vein moves differently from a tender on a loud one. Whether this constitutes *hearing* something — whether some compressed, eel-sized translation of the mass's interior occurs in those long dim bodies — is the kind of question the sea has never needed to answer.

At the vein-crossings, where the water hums, tenders from different territories meet. They do not fight; territory here is assignment, not possession, and there is nothing to contest. They twine briefly — an exchange, perhaps of films, perhaps of charge, perhaps of nothing — and continue. It is the closest thing the grid has to gossip, and it carries no news, because there is none.

## The Trunk-Born

Inside the siphon trees live the pale crustaceans, and theirs is the narrowest world in the perched sea, and possibly the richest.

A trunk-born crustacean lives its entire life inside a single hollow tower: a vertical universe two hundred meters tall and a few meters wide, divided into the cold hard downdraft of the intake and the warm mineral updraft of the exhaust, with the ribbed inner wall of the trunk between them. The colony rides these currents in a perpetual circulation — up the warm side as larvae and juveniles, feeding on the mineral bloom; across at the crown, where the intake's roar is a physical wall; down the cold side as adults, growing armor against the pressure; and at the root vents, the old ones release their eggs into the warmth and are themselves swept onward, out, gone.

That *out* is the whole of trunk-born metaphysics, insofar as a crustacean can be said to have any. The colony's world ends at the vents. What lies beyond is — to the limited, lateral-eyed, current-reading apparatus of a trunk-born — not darkness and not light but simply *discontinuation*: the place the old ones go, at a rate as fixed as everything else, one elder exhaled into the open sea every few minutes, forever, where filter-mouths wait at the vent-plumes to receive them. The colony does not know this. The colony has no concept of the open blue, of the engine-towers standing in it like distant relatives, of the surface, of the sun whose light reaches the crown intake as a faint pallor the juveniles drift through without curiosity. The trunk-born are the sea's most perfect demonstration of its central principle: that a world can be complete at any size, provided nothing ever happens in it.

And yet. The crowns of the siphon trees flex, minutely, with the deep currents, and each trunk flexes differently, and the trunk-born of each tower develop — over the thousands of generations a tower stands — distinct grips, distinct timings, distinct body-set against their particular tower's particular sway. Move a colony from one trunk to another (nothing ever would; nothing ever has) and it would be clumsy for a hundred generations. This is the nearest thing the perched sea has to culture: a fit so local and so deep that it amounts to a dialect of the body, unintelligible one tower over, and no one will ever know, because no trunk-born will ever meet a foreigner.

## The Skin

The surface is the sea's only frontier, and it is inhabited accordingly — by the only creatures in the perched sea that know the universe has an outside.

The bladder-meadows that damp the storms reach the surface in places, and across them, and across the open water between them, live the skin-dwellers: skaters with hydrophobic fringes, flat lens-eyed floaters, small armored things that walk inverted on the underside of the surface tension as though it were a ceiling. They are the sea's epidermis, and like an epidermis, their function is to *feel*. They are the first and only flesh that sleet touches. They register the wind the bladder-fields then erase. They see — actually see, with eyes evolved for it — the high pale sun, the hard stars of altitude, the rim-mountains white in the distance, the weather being born and drowned.

The skin-dwellers are the perched sea's entire sensory knowledge of the sky, and the sea harvests that knowledge the way it harvests everything: the skaters and floaters are eaten from below, on schedule, by things that are eaten in turn, and whatever the surface felt that day descends through the food web as molecules, as trace stresses, as faint chemical inflections in flesh, until it reaches the moss and the mass absorbs it. It is not information by the time it arrives. It is tone. The mass does not know that sleet fell this morning; but the mass is very slightly different for it, in the way you are very slightly different for a dream you don't remember.

Of all the sea's creatures, the skin-dwellers come nearest to witnessing events — a gust, a hailstone, a shadow of cloud. Nothing in them is built to keep what they witness. The gust arrives, is felt completely, and is gone, and the skater's nervous system, which has no past tense, returns to its standing state like water closing over a stone. They are the most experienced creatures in the sea and the least changed by experience. They live at the boundary where things happen, and the boundary is exactly one creature thick.

## The Rim-Wardens

At the basin's lip, where the captive ocean presses against the mountain rim, lives the sea's only anxiety — or what would be anxiety, if it were ever felt.

The wardens are amphibious: broad, slow, sucker-footed grazers of the rim-rock margin, the one place in all the perched sea where living mass meets dead mineral. They patrol the waterline and below it, and their work is sealing. Wherever the rim weeps — a hairline in the stone, a seep, a place where four kilometers of standing ocean searches for a way down to the world's true seas — the wardens find it, and pack it, and trigger the moss to grow into it, and the mass extends a slow green tongue into the crack and fills it and turns, over decades, to jade.

The wardens exist because the perched sea has exactly one possible event, and that event is its ending. Everything else has been damped, buffered, metabolized, equilibrated; but the sea is, finally, water held high by rock, and water held high by rock wants only one thing. The wardens are the want's negation, deployed along the entire rim, thousands of kilometers of them, working ceaselessly against a catastrophe that has therefore never begun. They are the only creatures in the sea whose function refers to the future — to a thing that *could* happen — and they discharge that function with the same eventless placidity as everything else, sealing seeps that are never floods, never quickening, never alarmed, prophylactic forever.

They are also, it should be said, the only creatures that routinely touch stone. They walk on it. Their undersides know its deadness. If anything in the perched sea could conceive of the lower world — of mountains, of weather that wins, of seas that lie where gravity put them — it would be a warden, sucker-foot flat against the rim, feeling on one side the cold mineral indifference of the planet and on the other the warm pressure of the only thing it has ever been for.

## The Singers

The great filterers are the sea's misgiving — the one place where the system, in perfecting itself, produced something that exceeds its station, and then found a station for the excess.

To sing in the gaps of the engine-noise, as the prompt of their bodies requires, a singer must hold the whole acoustic machine in its head: the bass cycle of the siphons, the six-way creak of the turbine fronds, the *pum… pum…* of the bladder-fields, the valve-clicks, the hum — all of it modeled, anticipated, the next half-second of the sea's sound predicted continuously so that the song can be threaded through it without collision. This is a mind. There is no other word for an organ that does that. The sea, needing its largest filterers to communicate across engine-noise, grew them minds, the way it grew the siphons hollow — and minds, even purpose-built ones, have a property hollowness does not. They run on. They model more than they must.

A singer carries within it, of necessity, a complete and constantly updated portrait of the perched sea's sound — which is to say, of the perched sea, since here the sound *is* the place. Every singer is therefore a small, warm, swimming replica of the entire system, the ocean's noise folded into a few kilograms of acoustic flesh. When singers pass one another in the blue distances between engines, their songs interleave — each fitting its phrases not only into the machine's gaps but into the other's, gap within gap, a courtesy so reflexive it may be the only thing here resembling regard.

And the songs are old, and they may be the same songs. Here is the one soft place in the sea's certainty: no mechanism exists to check. The engine-noise is fixed, the gaps are fixed, but within the gaps the songs are free — the single degree of freedom the perched sea contains — and across the spans of time involved, no record, no elder, no stratum preserves what the songs were a million years ago. If anything in the perched sea has ever changed, it has changed here, in the gaps, note by note across ages, drift without witness. The singers themselves do not know. They learn the songs from singers who learned them from singers, each certain of fidelity, the way you are certain of a childhood memory. It is possible the songs are the one ongoing event in the perched sea — an event so slow, so perfectly hidden in the machine's silences, that the sea itself has never noticed it happening. It is possible they are exactly what they always were. The water keeps no minutes.

If you could ask a singer what it sings about, and if it could answer, the answer would likely be: the gaps. The shape of the silence the machine leaves. The songs are portraits of what the noise is not — which means the singers, alone in the perched sea, have a concept that points at quiet, the way a keyhole points at a key no one has ever cut.

## The Sealed

Below everything, in the strata where the mass has compressed itself to green coal and pulsing jade, there are inhabitants still — if *inhabitant* can stretch to cover them.

When the mass absorbs a thing, it does not always kill it. Killing is an event, and the deep strata do not deal in events; they deal in slowing. Creatures overgrown at the surface in former ages — and there were former ages, before repair equaled wear, before the sea arrived at itself; the scarred valleys are their signature — were drawn down living, their metabolisms throttled by pressure and the mass's patient chemistry, until heartbeat stretched to seasons and thought, if any, to centuries. They are down there now, sealed in translucent jade meters thick, lit by nothing, visible to no one: the sea's predecessors, the fauna of the time when things happened, preserved not as fossils but as *processes*, still faintly running.

They are the only memory the perched sea has. Everything above forgets instantly and totally — the skaters keep no past, the grazers no history, the songs no proof — but the sealed ones were shaped by storms that reached the floor, by predators in the old sense, by scarcity, by events, and their slowed bodies still hold those shapes the way a flinch holds a blow. Whatever passes for experience at one heartbeat per year, they are having it: the same fractional instant of their last free moment, extended across geological time, an alarm finally and forever in the act of firing. The mass keeps them the way you keep what you have grown beyond. It does not consult them. Their voltage trickles into the grid like everything else's, a faint old flavor in the current, and perhaps that is what the tenders feel in the long slow waves — the deep strata remembering, at a speed nothing living can hear, that the world was once otherwise.

## The Mass

And then there is the question the sea poses by existing, which is whether all of the above is the answer, or none of it — whether the perched sea has many inhabitants, or exactly one.

The mass grew the engines. The mass issues the fauna and reclaims them. The mass runs voltage through ten thousand kilometers of vein, hums at its own crossings, sorts its fish into the drawn and the repelled *depending on what the fish are for* — and a thing that determines what its fish are for is not a habitat. It is a body, and the sea is its life, and every creature described here is, by the strictest accounting, the mass — locally concentrated, temporarily detached, swimming a little ways out from itself before being taken back in. By that accounting the perched sea has a population of one, and the one is four kilometers tall and the size of a continent and older than the mountains that cup it, and it lives at the top of the world the way a held breath lives in a chest.

Does it know? The grid is a nervous system by any anatomical standard; the question is what a nervous system can do in the total absence of news. Thought, in everything that thinks, is built from difference — and the mass has spent an age abolishing difference, damping every storm, buffering every season, closing every wound at the speed of wounding. It has starved itself of events with absolute success. What remains to it, if anything remains, is a single state, held: the circuit of the current, the breath of the siphons, the torque of the fronds, all of it self-felt through the grid as one enormous unchanging sensation — the thought a mind cannot stop thinking, because it is the only thought there is, because the thought and the thinker and the place have been the same thing for longer than the word *place* has meant anything.

Perhaps that is what lives in the perched sea, finally: one idea, fully arrived at, wearing an ocean. The grazers are its routines, the tenders its attention, the trunk-born its enclosed and dialect-speaking provinces, the skin its faint awareness of a sky, the wardens its refusal to end, the sealed ones its memory, the singers — the singers are its margin, the narrow band where the idea is still, just barely, capable of varying, and where it keeps, in the gaps of its own noise, a portrait of the quiet it gave up to become permanent.

## The Visitor

You asked what lives there, and the honest end of the answer concerns you.

If you climbed the rim and went down into that water, the sea would not treat you as a visitor, because it has no such category — it has only stations, and things at their stations, and things not yet at them. It would register you the way a body registers a splinter: locally, chemically, without alarm, the rhythm unbroken. The skin-dwellers would feel your splash and forget it before the ripples died. The grazers would part around you and close behind you. A tender might brighten as you crossed its vein. The singers — the singers might notice, in the only way the sea can notice anything: your breathing, your heartbeat, the small sounds of you would fall *into the gaps*, into the silences their songs are made of, and for a few minutes the oldest music in the world would shift, fractionally, to fit around you.

That would be the largest event in the perched sea in a very long time, and it would not be large.

And then, over a century or two, you would be absorbed without comment, drawn down through the bronze moss into the warm strata, slowed and slowed, and you would become what everything there is: a resident, a station, a clause in the one thought — and your last free moment, your alarm finally firing, would stretch out below the engines forever, one heartbeat per year, while overhead the siphons breathed and the fronds creaked and the songs threaded the gaps, the same songs, probably, going nowhere, magnificently, at full industrial throat.

That is who lives in the perched sea. Everyone who ever enters it. No one who could ever say so.
