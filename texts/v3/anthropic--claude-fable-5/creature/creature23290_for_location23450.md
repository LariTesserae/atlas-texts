# The Vantlers

The first thing to understand about the people of Vantle is that they are made the same way the soil is made.

Nobody here is from anywhere else, and nobody is from here in the way a lowlander is from a village — born onto ground that was always ground. A Vantler is born onto floors, into a lineage of floors, in rooms that are roofs of older rooms, and they know it from infancy the way other children know which way is down. Ask one where their family comes from and they will not name a district first. They will name a *stratum* — "we're fourth-floor people, engine side" — and only then a neighborhood, and the order of those two answers tells you everything about how a Vantler holds the world in their head. The lowland mind is a map. The Vantle mind is a core sample.

## Their Bodies

Altitude has been at work on them for as long as the moss has been at work on the bronze, and to roughly the same effect: a slow, total accommodation.

They are deep-chested, almost barrel-built, even the slight ones — lungs grown generous on thin air, blood thick with the patience of it. They climb stairs the way lowlanders walk on flat ground, with an economy that looks like laziness until you try to keep up: never hurrying, never stopping, breathing in a long settled rhythm that matches, if you listen, the thrum of the engines underfoot. Visitors gasp on the great stairs. Vantlers converse on them.

Their eyes are creased at the corners from a lifetime of that hard high light, and most of them squint slightly even indoors, even at night, the way sailors do — a permanent address to brightness. Their skin weathers early and handsomely, going the same settled brown as the stable rust. And they are, almost universally, barefoot or near it. The floors of Vantle are warm; shoes are for the parapet districts and the few cold stair-shafts on the north brows, and a Vantler's feet are wide, prehensile, sure on wet bronze and mossed stone, reading the floorwarm the way a lowlander reads weather. They have a word, *kelth*, for the particular animal heat that comes up through the soles, and they speak of districts by it — this plaza runs warm, that gallery has gone cool, someone should look at the mains under the loom-quarter. The first symptom of trouble anywhere in Vantle is usually felt through someone's bare feet, days before any gauge shows it.

They sleep in contact with the structure when they can — backs against warm walls, beds built over flue runs — and many of them will tell you, without embarrassment, that they cannot sleep properly in silence. The engine-thrum is in their sternums from the womb. Vantlers who travel down to the lowlands report the same complaint with the same wording, generation after generation: *it was quiet, like something had died.*

## Their Work

A Vantler does not ask what you do. They ask what you *keep*.

It is the fundamental social question, and everyone has an answer, because everyone keeps something: a length of trough, a panel of screen, a tree, a stair-lamp, a section of railing, a crab census, a song. The grand keeping belongs to the guilds — the Screenwrights with their work-songs, the engine families who rebuild the great machines one component per decade and pass the rebuild down like a saga in progress, the Mirror-tilters who walk the eastern yards each dawn making the day's small adjustments so the light arrives in the deep streets on time. But beneath the guilds is a vast informal economy of tending. A woman keeps the fig over Coil Stair because her grandmother kept it. A man keeps three lamps and a quenching pool and has opinions about the fish. Children are given small keepings the way lowland children are given pets — a planter, a stretch of channel to clear of leaves — and a child who keeps their channel well is spoken of as promising in exactly the tone other places reserve for cleverness.

The temperament this breeds is hard to describe to outsiders, who tend to call it slowness and are wrong. Vantlers are not slow. They are *unhurried in the specific way of people who do not believe in finishing*. Nothing in Vantle has ever been finished; the city crossed out of that category long ago. A Vantler approaches every task — a meal, an argument, a marriage, a flywheel — as a thing to be brought into good condition and then kept there, indefinitely, by attention. They find the lowland idea of *completing* a piece of work and walking away from it faintly horrifying, the way one might regard abandoning a child on the grounds that it had been successfully born.

It makes them magnificent maintainers and very poor revolutionaries. Vantle has politics — the tea-balconies seethe with it, water rights and trough allocations and the eternal jurisdictional war between the Screenwrights and the Orchard guild over the moss-beards — but it is the politics of stewardship, conducted on the timescale of trees, and its harshest sanction is the one that needs no enforcement: to be known as someone who does not keep what they keep.

## Their Speech and Their Beliefs

The language of Vantle is a lowland tongue gone strange with altitude, the way the pigeons have. It is rich where the place is rich. There are eleven common words for cloud, sorted not by appearance but by *yield* — what a deck will give to the screens — and the morning greeting in the western districts is, literally translated, "good graze," spoken when a fog wall is sliding in. There are words for the different registers of the engine-thrum, and old people use them diagnostically, the way other old people discuss their joints. The word for "down" and the word for "before" are related, and every Vantler feels the rightness of this in their bones: the past is not behind them, it is *underneath* them, load-bearing, walked on daily.

They have no gods to speak of, or rather they have declined, with characteristic patience, to settle the question. What they have instead is a kind of structural piety. The engines are spoken of with the grammar reserved elsewhere for grandparents — individually named, individually charactered (Old Sull runs sweet, the Tercel has a temper in cold weather) — and the engine families will tell you, not as metaphor, that a machine rebuilt continuously for that many generations is an ancestor in the only sense that matters: a thing your people have kept alive by giving it pieces of their lives. The great theological dispute, conducted nightly on the tea-balconies for centuries without resolution or any real desire for one, is the mountain. The Foundationists hold that there is stone down there, original ground, and that this matters. The larger faction holds that it does not matter and probably isn't, and that Vantle stands on Vantle, all the way down, which is both more frightening and more comforting, and Vantlers savor positions that are both. There is a third faction, small and serene, which holds that the question is the mountain — that the argument itself is the bedrock, and as long as it is kept in good condition the city will stand. They are considered eccentric and are probably right.

When something cannot be explained — the pale fish, the gallery crabs, the sealed doors in the deep strata — a Vantler files it under *before* and feels no itch. This is not incuriosity. It is a people who live on top of their own unexcavated history and have made peace with standing on questions.

## Their Lives

Children in Vantle are raised half by their parents and half by the structure. They run barefoot in packs along the trough-lines, stand in the valve-plumes, learn the city's three dimensions the way lowland children learn fields — and they keep the calendar by animals. The screaming hour, when the swifts pour out of the flues, is the universal signal to come home for the evening meal; mothers do not call children in, the birds do. A child's first formal rite is the *first descent*: taken down by funicular through the strata, past the borrowed-daylight shafts, to stand on the floor of an engine-hall and feel the thrum at its source, in the body, until they understand — and they do understand, every one of them, you can see it land in their faces — that the heartbeat they have heard all their lives is a kept thing, and that someday the keeping comes to them.

Adolescence is when they go to the edge. Edge-sitting is half courtship and half philosophy: the young of Vantle out on the warm parapet stone in the evening wind, swifts overhead, the lowlands a blue rumor miles below, working out between them what everyone here must work out — that they live in an impossible place, and that the impossibility is *load-bearing*, like everything else, and holds.

And at the end, a Vantler goes into the soil. This is the quietest and most absolute of their customs. The manufactured earth of the rooftop orchards is composted, generation over generation, from the city's green waste — and from its people. The dead are given to the soil-houses of the Orchard guild and become, in a year, the dirt of Vantle, which already has a lineage and now has theirs. There are no graves and no need for them; the orchards are the graves, all of them, communally, and when a Vantler eats a fig they are aware of this in a way that is neither morbid nor mystical but simply *structural*. Everyone here is standing on everyone. Everyone here is fed by everyone. The phrase they use, when a death is mentioned, is the phrase they use for everything settled and underneath: *they've gone down to before.*

## The Others

The people share the city, knowingly, with their co-residents, and citizenship in Vantle has always been a matter of keeping rather than kind. The goats are addressed by name and argued with. The crab-wardens of the Screenwright guild speak of their slate-blue charges in the first person plural — *we're down two on the west troughs* — and a warden who loses crabs grieves visibly and is comforted. The swifts are nobody's and everybody's; districts take quiet pride in the size of their evening river of birds, and a flue gallery that falls silent in spring is treated as a civic emergency.

Below all this, in the cool sealed strata where the funiculars do not go, there may be others still. The delvers — the small antiquarian guild that maps the buried plazas by lamplight — report what delvers have always reported: channels of warm water flowing where no main runs, lamps found burning in galleries no record opens onto, the sense, in certain deep vaulted rooms, of housekeeping. Whether anyone lives down there, or whether the city has simply become old enough to keep parts of itself, is one more question Vantle has stopped asking. The delvers leave the found lamps lit, out of courtesy, and climb back up to the warmth and the light and the green — to the terraces where the rest of the living lean against the flank of the sleeping thing they tend, and are tended by it, and cannot any longer say, and do not any longer care, where the keeping ends and the kept begins.
