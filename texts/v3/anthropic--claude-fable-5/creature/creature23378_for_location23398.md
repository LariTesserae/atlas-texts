**What Lives at the Sump Works**

The honest first answer is: nothing. You will want to count the starlings, and you should not. The starlings do not live at the Works any more than the flywheel lives there, or the lamp-amber, or the four-second sigh. They run there. The eels run, the snails run at their slower setting, the moths run hot and fast around the lamps, the herons run at a speed indistinguishable from stone. Living, in the sense you carried down the terraces with you — the sense that involves hunger and increase and a forward lean into tomorrow — is not something the fauna of the Works does. They are the works of the Works. They are what the place is made of, the way a clock is made of brass.

So: nothing lives there. That is the first answer, and it is true, and it does not survive a stay of more than three days. Because on the third day, or thereabouts — the Works has no days, only the lamps' permanent overcast afternoon, so say instead: on your third sleep — you will see the Keeper.

---

You will see the Keeper first at a distance, at the top of the lamp by the spillway, and you will mistake them for a heron, because the herons have taught you that anything standing still and grey on a high point is a heron. Then the figure will descend the lamp-ladder with a long-spouted oilcan in one hand, and you will revise.

The Keeper is person-shaped and person-sized and wears a coat that was some color once and is now the color of the drizzle. The oilcan's brass has been polished by one pair of hands into the same soft-buttoned smoothness as the rivets. The Keeper's face has gone the same way — not old, exactly; rounded; weathered into accidental gentleness, an engineer's face with the ornament worn off. The skin has a faint mineral sheen, like the limestone footings. The eyes are the color of the canal.

The Keeper walks a circuit. Pump hall, winding house, the row of vaults, the lamps in their order, the spillway, the stack, and back to the pump hall, oiling as they go. The circuit takes eight hours and some minutes, and it takes eight hours and some minutes every time; you could set a watch by it if your watch were good enough, and yours is not, and the Keeper knows this about you the moment they see you, with no malice at all. Between circuits the Keeper sits on a bench in the winding house, hands folded, eyes open or closed — it is curiously hard to determine which, and after a while you stop trying — while the great feathered wheel turns overhead at its one patient rate.

Here is what the Keeper oils: everything. Here is what needs oiling: nothing. The machinery of the Works has never squealed, never bound, never run dry — it could not, any more than the canal could forget how to flow downhill. The oiling is not maintenance. Watch long enough and you understand that the circuit is not something the Keeper does *to* the Works. It is the Keeper's flue, their lamp-orbit, their lap of the warm vaults. The Works is not being kept. The Keeper is.

You can speak to the Keeper. The Keeper will answer, politely, in measurements. Ask how long they have been here and they will say, "Pressure's steady." Ask their name and they will say, "Four seconds, near enough," with a small nod toward the engine, as if introducing a colleague. Ask whether they were the engineer — because someone built this place, someone with that famous contempt for ornament, and it would be tidy if that someone had simply never left — and the Keeper will consider the question with what looks very much like courtesy, and then read you the level of the canal. The Works does not keep history. Neither does its instrument.

You can offer the Keeper bread. People have. The Keeper will take it the way the herons take it — graciously, conclusively — and the next circuit they will weigh what they weighed. And if, some quiet hour in the winding house, the Keeper permits it (they will; the permission has the same temperature as everything else here), you can put your ear against the drizzle-colored coat and listen. What you will hear is a heartbeat. What you will count is: four seconds. Rising and sinking. The patience of a sleeper's chest. The Keeper's pulse and the beam engine's stroke are not similar. They are the same reading taken at two gauges.

Whether the Keeper was a person who became part of the Works, or a part of the Works that was issued in the shape of a person, is not a question the Works has any apparatus for answering. It may be the wrong question. The flock is the same flock; the Keeper is the same Keeper; the species has a population of exactly one, and it is at carrying capacity.

---

That is the second answer, and it is better, and it is still not the bottom of it. The bottom of it is in the deepest vault, where the bubbling is.

The Works is named for its sump, and a sump is the lowest point — the place water drains to, the place a pump works exists to serve. The beam engine rises and sinks, rises and sinks, day and night, and no coal comes in and no goods go out, and you will eventually ask yourself the obvious engineering question: *what is it pumping?* Stand at the mouth of the last vault, in the cool exhaled air, and listen to that vast contented bubbling, and the answer will assemble itself in you slowly, the way the calm does.

Everything drains. That is what the Works knows that the world above the terraces does not. The starlings' missing eggs, the eels' unmade journeys, the deaths nothing here dies, the hungers nothing here feels — the whole rich rotting surplus that an ecology runs on, the gradient, the slope, the *next* — all of it runs downhill in this basin like everything else, and it collects where low things collect, and there in the dark something takes it in. The bubbling is digestion. The contentment in it is real, and it is the only emotion the entire Works expresses, and it is enormous. The thing in the sump is never hungry for the same reason a riverbed is never thirsty: it lies at the bottom of everything, and everything comes to it.

It is working on you from your first hour. You feel it as peace. That fine even calm that settles over visitors by the second sleep, the way your boot-scrape startles you because your own noise has become foreign — that is the suction, gentler than gravity, drawing off the top of your variance. The drizzle falls in its ring; the wheel turns; and somewhere below the warm vaults, the part of you that fidgets and yearns and runs fast and slow is being taken up in solution, a little at a time, and carried down, and received with that low contented sound. Nothing will harm you. Nothing here would dream of it. Harm is a kind of change, and change is the one cargo this port handles, inbound only.

This is why the visitors go back up the terraces walking a little faster than they came down. Not fear. Thrift.

---

And so the last answer, the one that takes longest because it is the largest: ask what lives in a body, and at the end of all the counting of cells, the true answer is *the body*. The starlings are plumage. The eels are blood, braided through the warm vaults like blood through a liver — and the brick is liver-colored, you noticed, you noticed on the first day. The snails are skin, very slowly boiling. The bats are breath in the flues; the drizzle is sweat the body drinks back; the Keeper is the small attending hand every body keeps for itself; and the thing in the sump is the stomach, or the sleep, or the satisfaction — the organ of having-arrived. One resident. Everything else is anatomy.

It is alive the way a held note is alive. The way a standing wave is alive: motion everywhere, change nowhere, the whole roaring apparatus spent — gladly, completely — on the upkeep of a single wet, lamplit, four-second moment. It cannot die, having nothing left to change with. It cannot grow, for the same reason. It was finished, perhaps, before it was built; the engineer (if there was an engineer, if the Keeper's oilcan is not simply something the Works grew, the way other places grow orchids) did not create it so much as give it a body to be finished in.

While you are there, you live there too. Briefly, the Works has a symptom — a fever, a flutter, one variable reading walking its lanes. It will accept your living the way the heron accepted the bread: graciously, conclusively, without the least use for it.

And when you go, the Keeper will note your departure from the top of the spillway lamp, and somewhere in whatever ledger a place like this keeps — gauge-readings, circuit-times, the canal's level holding steady down the footless centuries — you will be entered, without malice, in the only terms available:

*One, going up. Variable.*

Which is the Works' word for alive. It is also, you should know, its only word for *not yet finished* — and it watches you climb the shining terraces with the mild patience of something that can wait, and does not need to.
