**The Tender of the Casting House**

Begin with the handwriting, because that is where the inhabitant first appears: not in the flesh but in the swollen clipboard by the door, in dated columns of measurements — flow rates, cure depths, the stream's small rebellions logged to the millimeter. Read back far enough through the damp-wrinkled pages and you will notice something. The hand changes. Forty, fifty pages down, the careful slanting script gives way to an older, squarer one, heavier on the downstrokes, the hand of someone who learned to write on a slate and then spent decades writing on tags wired to molds. The post has been held before. The one who holds it now inherited it — inherited the clipboard, the stakes, the brass clips, the bell — and took up the predecessor's columns without breaking them, so that the record runs continuous, two lives wide, as though the landscape were being witnessed by a single very long attention.

The current tender is a woman of indeterminate late middle age, broad through the shoulders from the stair, with the particular hands of someone who spent her first working life on the casting floor far below: fingertips pale and smooth where lime has eaten the prints away, knuckles enlarged, a fine white tracery of old burn scars on both forearms like the seam lines on the rock she now keeps. She was a mold-finisher, once — one of the people who went over a fresh casting inch by inch with rasp and stone, taking off the flash, making the made thing look meant. Her lungs took the dust the way all their lungs did. When the works' surgeon listened to her chest and shook his head, she was offered the choice the works offers its worn-out finishers: the pension cottage in the valley, or the stair. She chose the stair. The climb that costs you an hour costs her nearly two now, with rests on the landings, and she makes it every day or every second day, and she has never once, in the record, called this a hardship. The warm wet air up here is, in fact, the only air she breathes easily. The Casting House is good for her chest. She may be the only person alive for whom this room is medicine.

She does not exactly live in the room, and does not exactly live anywhere else. Behind the second structural rib on the north wall, where the copper plating steps back to make a shallow alcove, there is a canvas cot kept rolled and strapped, a tin kettle that sits directly on the insulated riser and is always two minutes from boiling, a shelf of tools wrapped in oiled cloth, a single enamel mug, and a folding stool worn shiny at the edges. On the nights when a fresh pour is curing — when the eastern section is ticking and soft and must be listened to — she stays. The works below knows this and pretends not to. There is a fiction, maintained in the ledgers, that the Casting House is inspected daily by a member of staff; there is the truth, which is that it is *inhabited*, intermittently and quietly, by a woman who falls asleep to the diffusers' exhale and wakes when a valve cycles wrong, the way a mother wakes to a changed breathing in the next room.

Her work, as she would describe it if you ever got her to describe it (you would not; she answers questions with the clipboard), is mostly *waiting correctly*. She tests the cure of new castings with the heel of her thumb and with a small bronze-headed hammer, tapping and listening — green rock answers dull, cured rock answers with a faint ring, and she can hear the difference through ferns, through mist, through her own bad ears. When a section finally rings true end to end, she crosses to the door, where a bell hangs — bronze, hand-sized, cast in the works below from the same furnaces that feed the slurry pipes — and she tolls it once. That is the whole ceremony. One stroke, allowed to die away completely. Then she takes down the enamel SOFT plaque and stacks it with the others, and that piece of the world is open. The bees do not react to the bell. The snails do not react to anything. But she has noted in the log, twice, in her careful slanting hand, that the gecko comes out onto the warm outcrop within a few minutes of the toll, every time, and she has not ventured a theory.

About the gecko. She knows, of course. She has known for over a year. It appears nowhere in the manifest of introduced species — she has the manifest; she checked the night she first saw it cross the outcrop, a quick pale soft-footed thing with eyes like drops of the stream. By every regulation that governs her post she should have reported it, and the works would have sent up a man with a box. She has not reported it. The log is otherwise scrupulous to the point of severity — she records when she so much as moves a stake — but the gecko exists only in two unattributed marginal notes, and in the small chipped saucer of water that sits, unexplained by any document, in a crevice on the south face of the outcrop, refilled whenever she passes. It is the first wild fact in a room where everything was decided, and she is its silent accomplice. Her predecessor, she suspects from certain margins in the older pages, kept a similar secret — something about a fern that was never planted — and let it stand too. It may be that this is the actual tradition of the post, handed down beneath the official one: the landscape is permitted, quietly, to surprise.

The bees she manages with brass instruments and almost no smoke; they are a dark, small, nearly silent strain bred somewhere below for working under glass, and they treat her as weather. The snails she counts monthly, lifting them one by one from the rills with two fingers; a few of the eldest shells carry tiny dots of red paint, her marks, and the log notes their travels with the gravity of a shipping registry — *No. 4 has crossed the ravine. Eleven days.* She speaks to none of these creatures aloud. The room's principal voice is the dripping, and she has folded herself into the room's quiet so thoroughly that visitors — the rare engineer sent up to read the steam meters — sometimes stand within ten paces of her and do not see her, a grey-and-green figure crouched at a disputed bend of the stream with a stake and a measuring rod, holding still, letting the water show her what it intends.

Because that, finally, is what she understands her tenancy to be: she is not the resident of the Casting House. She is its midwife, or its witness, or — the word the old hand used once, on a page near the binding, before the script changes — its *attendant*. The thing that truly lives in the room is the room. The landscape is the inhabitant: an infant terrain, days old in geological terms, lying in its warm copper crib above the clouds, fed by pipes, breathing steam, learning. The stream that argues with its cast channel is the creature flexing. The lime crust at the grate is its first tooth. Every measurement she takes is a line in a baby book — *first pool, first meander, first deposit* — and she takes them knowing what every attendant of every infant knows: that the records are kept for someone else, later, and that the thing being recorded will not remember her at all.

She thinks about this, presumably, on the long climb down, in the warm dark of the riveted shaft, resting on the landings with her hand on the sweating rail. Someday her hand will be the older hand in the clipboard. Someone newer will take up the columns without breaking them, find the saucer in the crevice, hear the gecko before reading of it, and understand. The moss will not notice the change of tenders. The stream will go on making its own arrangements. And the bell by the door will hang waiting through whatever interval falls between them, holding its one note — the note that means *cured*, *ready*, *finished enough* — for the next pour, and the next thumb pressed flat against the warm new rock, testing whether the world has set.
