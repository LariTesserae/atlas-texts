# The Inhabitants of the Terraces

Begin with the census problem, because everything here begins with a counting problem. The city's population is not a number. It is — like its streets, like its horizons — a *range*, and the range includes zero. There are hours, possibly whole years, when nothing lives in the Unbuilt City at all, and then there are the other hours, and the difficulty is that nobody has ever caught the place in the act of changing from one to the other. You do not see the inhabitants arrive. You notice, eventually, that the emptiness has texture, and then you start sorting the texture into kinds.

What follows is the sorting. It should be read the way the city should be walked: diagonally, lightly, without insisting.

## The Frost-Grazer

Start with the comfortable one, because everyone does.

The grey moth-thing is the only resident that obeys all the rules of lower altitudes except one. It has mass — set a hand near it and you feel the cold come off it, a degree colder than the wall. It has symmetry, of the ordinary bilateral kind. It has a shadow that lies exactly where its body says it should, attached at the feet, moving when it moves, and in a city of migrating shadows this makes the Grazer something like a saint, or a fossil: a remnant of the old physics, still keeping the old law in a parish where the law was repealed long ago.

The single rule it breaks is continuity. It does not cross the space between this wall and that wall. It is *on* this wall, attending to a bloom of frost-dust with slow mouthpart motions like someone reading braille — and then it is on that wall, mid-meal, as if its life were a film with frames removed. Watch it for a week (one observer did; her notes survive) and you begin to suspect that the Grazer experiences its life as continuous, and that it is the space between the walls that is intermittent. It does not skip. The distance does. The Grazer simply declines to be in the parts of the city that aren't reliably there, the way a careful animal declines thin ice, and what looks like teleportation is just very, very good footing.

It eats the frost-dust, or unmakes it, or escorts it — the dust is thinner wherever the Grazer has been, and accumulates thicker somewhere else within days, and whether this is digestion or transport no one can say. There is at least one Grazer. There has never been evidence of two at once. There has never been evidence of a young one, a dead one, a moulted skin. The pleasant hypothesis is that it is one animal, the same animal, and that it has been grazing the frost of the high quarters since before the lintels rounded — that it is the city's contemporary, its sole pet, its one ordinary thing, kept the way a person living alone keeps a clock for the sound.

The less pleasant hypothesis is the doorways. See below.

## The Tilewalker

The large pale thing of the lower terraces is harder to be calm about, and the literature is not calm about it, but the thing itself is the calmest entity anyone has described at this altitude.

It is seen only at the dim end of the light, when the shadow-weather has pooled and the colonnades go the colour of the inside of a shell. It is pale the way the frost-dust is pale — not white, but a grey that has given up on being a darker grey. It is the height of a tall door. It walks. That is nearly the whole of its observed behaviour: it walks the colonnades, the long lower galleries, the perimeter terraces, with the steady, unhurried, faintly nautical gait of something pacing a deck it knows will tilt.

The disagreement about its limbs — four, or six — has been resolved, to the extent anything here resolves, by the suggestion that the Tilewalker has as many limbs as the local geometry currently supports, and changes the count without noticing, the way you change your stride for a slope without thinking about your knees. It is not shaped like an animal. It is shaped like *walking*, with the minimum of body required to do it. Seen at any distance it could be a person in pale clothing. Seen closer — and two accounts claim closeness — it has no face, but it has a *front*: an orientation, an attention, a side of itself that it gives to the direction it is going, which is somehow more courteous and more final than a face would be.

It has never approached anyone. It has never avoided anyone, either, which is the unsettling part: it adjusts its path around a standing traveller by exactly the margin it adjusts around a fallen pillar, no more, granting you the precise standing of an obstacle. One climber stood in a colonnade and let it pass within arm's reach. He reported a sound like dry cloth, a temperature drop, and the strong, specific impression — his phrase — *of being walked past by a corridor*.

And then there are the prints. Mostly they are prints: pad-shaped depressions in the frost-dust, walker-spaced. But every so often, in a sequence of ordinary prints, one print is instead a doorway — a perfect, miniature, fully formed arch a few centimetres tall, pressed into the dust with the lintel-curve and threshold-sag of the city's own architecture, already weathered, already old, eroding over the following days exactly as the great doorways erode over millennia.

This is the detail on which the whole question of the Tilewalker turns. Because the city, remember, was never built; it *occurred*; it is what this stone does at this height. And here is a thing that paces the terraces nightly, and where it treads, sometimes, architecture occurs. The cautious reading is that the Tilewalker is a creature whose footprints sometimes misfire into the local vernacular, the way a footprint in volcanic mud picks up the texture of the mud. The other reading — and at altitude, after a few days, in the settling-groan hours, the other reading becomes very hard to put down — is that the Tilewalker is not an inhabitant of the city at all. It is the city's *growth*. It is the process, given just enough body to make rounds: the thing the mountain sends out at dusk to continue, at hand-span pace, at print scale, the work of becoming municipal. The city is not finished. Nothing about it suggests it ever intends to be. Something has to walk the next streets into the stone, and the Tilewalker walks.

If that is true, then the Frost-Grazer's diet acquires a grim elegance, because the frost-dust is the city's seed-stuff, its mineral pollen, and the Grazer thins it. One resident extends the city. One resident prunes it. Between them, the angle of repose.

## The Tenant of the One Room

The round chamber behind every door of that famous street has a single window facing south from all directions, and frost-dimmed footprints on the floor, and the footprints are sometimes yours and sometimes already there when you arrive, and they are also yours then.

The Tenant of the One Room is, in other words, you — but a *you* administered by the room rather than by yourself. The room appears to keep its occupants the way the stone keeps light: as a residue, a pallor, persisting after the source has gone elsewhere. Everyone who has ever stood in the chamber is still, in some thinned and footprint-shaped sense, standing in it, and when you enter you are added to the tenancy, and when you leave you do not entirely come out. Travellers describe the feeling exactly the same way, across accounts that cannot have read each other: a faint reluctance at the threshold on exiting, not their own reluctance, *light as a hand on the shoulder of a coat you've already taken off*.

The Tenant never appears. The Tenant is a population of one room rather than a person; its body is the sum of arrivals; it grows by visitation, at the rate of perhaps one visitor a decade, which makes it the slowest-growing living thing on the mountain, slower than the lichen-stains, slower than the lean of the towers. It is also — this should be said plainly — the only inhabitant of the city that is made of people. If you want to meet the locals, this is where they are. They are a smell of cold cloth, an averaging of footprints, a window that faces south for everyone equally. They are very gentle. They have learned the place's one law better than anything else here, because they persist by being almost nothing at all, and they keep the window facing south, all of them together, the way you'd keep a candle lit.

## The Stairwalkers

Now the hard one. Now the question the city itself asks, in its stone, on every grand stair: *who wore these?*

The treads are bellied. The handrail-grooves are polished. The thresholds sag in the middle the way thresholds sag under ten thousand years of crossing feet. And nothing crosses them. Nothing has ever been seen to cross them. The cheap answer is wind, and the wind here could not wear a snowflake. The honest answer is the footsteps — the soft displaced sounds in adjacent streets, the steps that are never there when investigated, that the literature calls echoes of nothing, or echoes of events not yet occurred, or sounds the city is only now finishing.

Take the city's broken geometry seriously, and follow it one floor down. Space here has a tidal range: a street is many lengths, a plaza many sizes, all of them genuine. Suppose time here has the same failure. Suppose the city's *now* is not a point but a range, and suppose its population lives in that range the way the Grazer lives on the reliable walls — distributed, unfixed, present across the whole tidal span of the moment but never concentrated at any instant of it. Then the city would be exactly as observed: thronged and empty at once. The stairs would wear, because they are walked constantly, by a people smeared so thin across duration that no single second contains enough of any one of them to see. The footsteps would arrive out of order, because order is a property of the kind of time the Stairwalkers don't live in. The shadow found two streets from its tower would not be a misplaced shadow; it would be a shadow *attending someone*, someone present at the plaza across the whole long day but at no o'clock in particular, and the great pooled darknesses migrating through the districts would be — this is the conclusion the longest-staying observer reached, in her last coherent notebook — *crowds*. Crowd-shade. The shadow-weather is the rush hour of the Unbuilt City, grazing across the plazas at the speed of a population that has all the time there is and is in none of it.

They are not ghosts. Ghosts are remainders, and there is no evidence the Stairwalkers ever existed in the pointed, instantaneous way that would let them die and remain. It may be truer to say they are inhabitants of the city in the same sense the city is an inhabitant of the mountain: not built, not arrived, but *occurred* — what happens to living, at this altitude, under this pressure of something nobody has named. Lower down, life crystallizes into organisms, discrete and momentary. Up here it recrystallizes into duration. The Stairwalkers are to people what the colonnades are to stone.

They seem, for what it is worth, content. The stairs are worn evenly, unhurriedly, with no ruts of panic, no scuffs of crowding at the doors. Whatever their days are, they have been having them for a very long time, and the wear patterns suggest a civic life of almost unbearable patience: gathering, dispersing, crossing the plazas at the pace of shade. If you call out — you were told not to call out — your voice joins them. That is what the city does with it. It spends your voice thinly, somewhere, later, in a quarter you will never find: a syllable distributed across a century of someone else's afternoon. You become, in that small way, a Stairwalker too. The city is always recruiting, gently, at the rate of whatever you leave behind.

## The Stains

On the south-leaning walls, the mineral blooms in the shape of lichen — fawn, bottle-green, growing at geological speed — are usually catalogued under terrain. They should be catalogued here.

They are not alive. They are the stone *practicing*. Everything in this city is something the mountain learned to do — doorways, stairs, the Tilewalker's rounds — and the stains are the next lesson, half-learned: the rock rehearsing the idea of vegetation, getting the colours approximately right, the branching patterns nearly right, the being-alive not right at all yet. They grow at perhaps a millimetre a century. They are growing. The oldest stains, in the deepest cols, have developed something that is almost texture, a dry crêpe-paper relief, and on one wall — one — an observer found a stain that had begun, at its very centre, to cast a faint independent shadow.

Give them long enough — and the city has nothing but long enough — and the high quarters may yet be inhabited in the ordinary, green, breathing sense, by a flora the stone dreamed up from rumours. The Unbuilt City would then be the only place where life did not climb up from below but condensed out of the rock from above, the way frost-flowers condense from certain soils on certain nights. The stains are either decoration or the firstborn. Check back in an epoch.

## A Note on Visitors

You will want to know whether the dozen people who wrote the literature count as inhabitants. They would have said no. The city disagrees. It kept their voices, in the cols. It keeps their footprints, in the One Room, where they stand together facing the southern window. Their maps gently contradict each other and both are right, which is the most local thing a document can do. One of them never came down, and the accounts of the pale walker are careful — too careful, the way people are careful around a hope — never to say how tall, exactly, the Tilewalker is, or whether its gait is entirely unfamiliar.

## The Law, Restated for Residents

What lives in the Unbuilt City, then: a grazer that keeps the old physics like a vow; a walker that may be the city's own slow ambition out taking the air; a tenant made of everyone; a populace dissolved in time, wearing the stairs smooth from no particular moment; stone that is teaching itself to be moss; and the thin sediment of every visitor who ever spoke above a whisper.

Not one of them insists. That is the whole of citizenship here. The Grazer does not insist on the spaces between walls; the Tilewalker does not insist on a number of limbs; the Tenant does not insist on being anyone; the Stairwalkers do not insist on *now*; the stains do not insist on being alive yet. Each of them lasted by yielding, exactly as the towers lasted by leaning, and together they make up the strangest census ever not-taken: a population of behaviours, a citizenry of processes, vast and hollow and busy — a city inhabited the only way a city like this could ever be inhabited, which is *gently*, *thinly*, *and at all times except this one*.
