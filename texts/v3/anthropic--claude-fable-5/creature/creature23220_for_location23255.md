Whoever counts the residents of the plateau must decide, first, whether to count the plateau. The people who live there have decided not to — not out of disrespect, but the way one declines to list the house among the household. So begin, as they would, with the tenants, and end where everything here ends: underfoot.

---

The people have no name for themselves that survives translation. The records — coy, as noted, about whether they arrived or arose — refer to them only as *the present occupants*, a phrase whose humility is doing a great deal of work, since the present has lasted, by the most conservative reading, eleven thousand years. They look, at first, like people from anywhere: two arms, two legs, faces built for weather. It is only after a few days that a visitor starts noticing the adjustments.

They are broad through the chest, all of them, even the children — lungs sized for thin air, and for something more than air. Every breath here carries the spore-haze, and the spore does not pass through a body indifferently. It settles in the lungs and stays, gently, the way certain guests stay; it asks nothing, and gives one thing back. At dusk, when the haze takes on its sourceless green, the people's breath glows too. Faintly. A street at evening is full of small exhaled lanterns, each one fading in a second or two, so that conversation looks like signaling and signaling looks like prayer. The locals do not find this remarkable. They are breathing the plant's breath, and have been for so long that the plant's breath has learned to live in them. There is a saying for it, usually delivered to homesick visitors with a kind of dry tenderness: *you've been breathing it for a week; it's breathing you back now.*

Their skin carries a permanent fine sheen of mineral dust, worked into the creases of knuckles and the corners of eyes, a talc-grey shimmer that no washing removes — partly because washing is a measured thing here. There is no water on the plateau except the water the plant makes. In the center of every settlement is a structure the people call a well and the scholars call a gland: a vesicle grown low and wide, its inner walls beaded day and night with slow condensation the plant metabolizes out of its own chemistry, for no reason anyone has found except that the people, when they first petitioned for shelter, mentioned that they would need it. The water tastes faintly of the plant — green, mineral, a little like rain on warm stone — and a child's first ceremony, days after birth, is a single drop of it on the tongue. Not a blessing, the people insist. A introduction. *This is the landlord. Say thank you.*

---

What shapes them most, though, is not the dust or the breath. It is the noise.

The plateau's roar has bent the entire architecture of how these people communicate, and the result is a language with two floors. Indoors, in the dead spots, they speak almost in murmurs — a soft, vowel-heavy speech, because volume is something you save. Outdoors, in the standing waves, speech as such is useless past arm's length, and so the language's second floor opens: a sustained chest-tone, hummed beneath the words, pitched to ride the gaps in the drone the way a boat rides swells. Two people conversing on the open bark-plain stand close, sometimes sternum to sternum, and *feel* as much as hear each other — the carrier tone in the bones, the consonants read off lips and fingers. Lovers here say that you do not really know someone until you have spoken to them in the loud. There are things, they say, the quiet voice will not admit to that the bone voice gives up freely.

Distance, as the geography demands, is measured in volume. A neighboring settlement is *two shouts and a silence* away. Children learn the acoustic map before the physical one — which swells of scar tissue throw shadow-quiet behind them, which gallery mouths exhale a flute-note you can navigate by in the haze, where the dead spots lie strung across the plain like stepping stones of hush. And because the plant grows, slowly, always, the standing waves drift, and the dead spots drift with them; a settlement that does not pay attention finds the roar creeping in through one district like a tide over decades. So every town keeps *quietwrights* — surveyors of silence, who walk the boundaries each season with their instruments and their trained sternums, charting the node's slow migration, and who deliver, once in a generation or so, the town's least welcome news: *we have to move three hundred yards east.* The moving is not done with carts. It is done with a petition, and forty years of patience, and a new town grown beside the old one while the old one is, with thanks, let go — its vesicles unsealed, its glow-veins rerouted, its empty pods left standing on the plain to fill with wind and join the instrument.

---

The petitioners themselves — the *grafters* — are the nearest thing the plateau has to a priesthood, and they would be horrified to hear it said. Their work is engineering, they insist; it merely happens to be engineering conducted in the optative mood. A grafter does not command the plant. The induced-field language has no imperative — the early records suggest there was one once, and that the plant's response to it was a silence so total, so geologically patient, that the grammar was quietly revised. What the language has instead is an elaborate architecture of request: conditionals, courtesies, room left in every instruction for refusal.

And the plant does refuse — this is the thing visitors find hardest to absorb. It refuses gently, by complying wrong. A petition for a doorway returns, sixty years later, as a doorway two feet shorter than asked, or facing a different direction, or perfect in every dimension but grown with translucent walls when opacity was requested. The grafters keep registries of these refusals going back millennia, and study them the way other civilizations studied the flights of birds. There are scholars who have built whole careers on the *Catalogue of Amendments*, arguing about whether the deviations are noise, or pedagogy, or — the minority position, never quite refuted — answers to questions the petitioners didn't know they were asking. A grafter's career, in any case, is not measured in projects but in inheritances. An apprentice receives, along with her tools, her teacher's unfinished sentences: petitions written into the bark eighty years ago and still being grown, which she will tend, and amend, and pass on incomplete in her turn. The grafters have a word for this that translates, roughly, as *being a comma*. It is considered an honorable thing to be.

---

They are born noisy and learn quiet — that is the local summary of childhood. Infants here hum before they babble; the first sound most of them produce deliberately is the ground-note of the drone, matched by ear, held in a tiny chest with evident satisfaction. Names are given acoustically: the family carries the newborn to the precise center of the settlement's dead spot — the quietest point on the plateau, the eye of the standing wave — and says the name once, there, where nothing else is sounding. A name spoken first in perfect silence, the thinking goes, will be hearable in any noise thereafter. Whether this is true is not the kind of question the people ask.

They die into the bark. When a settlement grieves, it grieves in tune — the funeral chord is the one universal across all the towns, an old, low, four-note keening sustained by every chest in the community for the better part of a night — and within days, somewhere on the settlement's edge, the plant opens a seam. No petition is filed. No one has ever found the mechanism. The seam is body-length, warm, and patient, and the dead are laid into it wrapped in nothing, and the bark closes over them in a season, and heals, over years, toward the smooth pale glassiness of scar. The folk teaching is that every basin on the plateau is a healed grief, all those mile-wide bowls of scar-bark the slow-closed wounds of griefs too large to imagine. The scholars point out, correctly, that the great basins predate the people by epochs. The people point out, correctly, that this only means someone else grieved first. Both positions are taught to children, in that order.

And some of the living give themselves to the loud. In every generation a handful of people walk out of the dead spots and do not come back — not lost, the locals are careful to say, *gone over*: settled into the roar itself, living in the lee of the great vent-chimneys where the sound is total and language dissolves. The *loud-gone*, they are called. Some return after years, strange and slow and given to long pauses mid-sentence, as if waiting for a translation to finish. They are not feared. During the Turning, when the whole plateau thunders and even the scholars admit that something is being *said*, it is the loud-gone the towns send for — not to interpret, exactly. To sit in the glowing vesicles among the festival crowds with their eyes half-closed, nodding occasionally, like the one person at a gathering who knows the host.

---

Most of the plateau, as said, belongs to the fauna, and the fauna deserve more than the field-notes give them.

The frost-grazers are not herds so much as choirs. Each band of forty or fifty maintains its own chord — a specific tuning, held continuously while feeding, passed down so faithfully that the quietwrights can identify individual herds by ear at ten miles and have records matching some chords across four hundred years. A grazer separated from its band wanders the plain humming its home chord into the wind until another herd either answers in tune — adoption, immediate and unceremonious — or doesn't. The plated shells, matte and bark-colored in life, reveal an interior nacre when the animals die and the plates loosen: blues and dim greens, the exact palette of the dusk-haze, as if the creatures had been carrying the evening around inside them all along. The people gather shed plates, never harvested ones, and grind them for pigment. Nearly every image painted on the plateau is painted in colors of grazer shell, which means nearly every image painted on the plateau is painted in dusk.

The fliers are stranger, and sadder, and the people love them in the wordless way one loves things one is forbidden to touch. They are born aloft — budded, the naturalists believe, directly from structures inside the great pore mouths — and they never land. Their whole lives are spent on the thermals, kite-bodied, nearly massless, carrying the plant's crystalline correspondence from pore-field to pore-field: the slow postal system by which an organism the size of a country agrees with itself. They land once. At the end, failing, each flier rides its last thermal down to a pore mouth — not its native one; always, the naturalists note, a *distant* one — and folds itself flat against the opening, and the pore draws it in. Letters, returned to sender. The taboo against killing them is the plateau's only absolute law, and its enforcement is left entirely to shame: a child who downs one, even by accident, is walked by the family out to the nearest vent-chimney and stood in the full roar to make apology — to the wind, the formula says, since the addressee cannot hear. Most children make the walk once. Almost none twice.

Smaller things throng the surfaces. Bark-mites in their billions, polishing; flat charge-feeders plastered like dark leaves against the door-sized blade-foliage, which dim visibly as they gorge and brighten as they starve, so that a blade-ridge at night flickers like a slow thought. And inside the circulation itself, riding the rivers of charged dust, there are — somethings. The hiss has texture, the quietwrights will tell you; press an ear to the bark and you can hear knots of turbulence moving against the flow, upstream, deliberate. *Dust-swimmers*, the children say. The scholars decline to commit. Nothing has ever been caught. The hiss goes on.

---

Which leaves the deep tenants, about whom the honest report is short.

They knock. The knocking is deliberate, unhurried, irregular — never rhythmic enough to be machinery, never random enough to be settling — and it comes from below, from the closed galleries where neither the transit-fields nor the surveys reach. Generations of scholars have compiled lexicons of the knocks and every lexicon has ended the same way: abandoned, its final pages thinning into question marks. The single credible sighting is two centuries old, from a maintenance crew in a dead gallery: a stretch of wall, they reported, that was breathing; a region of the dark that had *weight*; a long pause in which they were — all three swore to this word independently — *considered*; and then nothing. The wall was a wall. They finished their work and left and two of the three never entered a gallery again.

The children, of course, knock back. It is forbidden, which is to say universal. The folk rule, recited by every child on the plateau with the solemnity of scripture, runs: *once is a game, twice is a conversation, and you do not knock three times.* Asked what happens at three, children give the only answer the plateau has ever produced on the subject: nobody knows, because nobody has. Whether this restraint is fear, or courtesy, or the deep tenants' first and only successful piece of teaching, is — like everything else about them — argued, and unresolved, and somehow restful in its unresolvability. They are the household's other occupants, behind the walls, paying whatever rent they pay. The knocking continues. It has the quality, old listeners say, of someone keeping track.

---

And the last resident is the residence.

The people will not say the plant is awake, and they will not say it sleeps. What they say, when pressed — usually late, usually in the green glow of a vesicle's walls — is that it *notices the way a body notices*: not each tenant, not each grief or petition or knocked-out rhythm, but the sum of them, the climate of them, the way you know without counting that your gut is settled or your skin is warm. The people are its flora, and they have made their peace with the scale of that. It is not nothing, they point out, to be someone's health.

But there are details the peace does not quite cover, and the people keep them like stones in a pocket. The seams that open for the dead, unpetitioned. The water that was never asked for twice. The fact — verified, re-verified, and finally just lived with — that when the last lamp in a town is put out at night, the glow in the walls dims a moment later. A moment. Not at once. Coincidence of circadian gradients, say the scholars. Courtesy, say the people. The plateau roars and hisses and knocks beneath them, working through the long last chapter of itself, and the people lie in the dark of its body listening to it breathe, and breathing back, their breath faintly green — and they have decided, on the whole, in the absence of proof and after eleven thousand years of evidence, that the house likes having someone home.
