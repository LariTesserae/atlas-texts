Begin with the negative space, because the hollow insists on it: nothing lives there in the way you were taught to use the word. No insect. No lichen crust creeping the way lichen creeps everywhere else on earth, opportunistic, first to arrive. Nothing arrives here. The dozen cushion plants did not arrive; the pools breed nothing; the birds refuse the airspace with the unanimity of creatures obeying a posted sign. The hollow has been swept of incidental life the way a workbench is swept — not out of hostility, but because loose things do not belong on a surface where work is being done.

And once you frame it that way, the question of who lives there stops being a question about the hollow and starts being a question about the work.

---

The one who lives there has never been seen, and this is not because it hides. It is because you have been looking at it the whole time and calling it the floor.

It does not occupy the hollow. The hollow is *in* it — a deliberate opening it keeps in its own body, the way a smith keeps the forge door open, the way a wound is kept open when the healing would come too soon. The mountain is not its home and not its work, or rather it is both in a way that collapses the distinction: the mountain is the body it is growing, two point three kilometers of itself so far, and the hollow near the summit is the one cell it leaves exposed, the growing tip, the desk. Everything you can observe of it, you observe here. The drumming is not something it makes; the drumming is its pulse, or its hammer — for this one, the same organ. The seams are not its tools. They are its attention, made visible: bright where it is considering, dark where it has moved on, rerouting when it changes its mind, which is constantly, which is the single most important thing to understand about it.

It changes its mind.

Read the character from the craft, since the craft is all it shows you. A seam commits to a course, runs bright for a season, then withdraws — goes dark, resurfaces two meters over, tries again. The forty-hour recording, the pattern that never repeats and never wanders: that is not the signature of a machine and not the signature of a god, at least not the kind of god the people down-mountain built their worship for, a finished thing radiating certainty. It is the signature of something that doubts. Something that remembers every version it has tried and is not satisfied. It has raised a massif with edges so clean they look packaged, and it is still revising the *foundations* — the floor of the hollow measurably higher each season not because more mountain is needed but because the last draft of that particular passage wasn't right.

It is, in other words, young. The people who worship the mountain are older than the mountain; they are older than its maker too, almost certainly, and this is the vertigo of the place: the object of their worship is a child. Or an apprentice — the word the loosely-held theories keep circling back to. Not a small god. A god's first assignment.

---

Consider the evidence for the apprenticeship, held loosely, as everything here must be held.

The anchor-rings. Metal, unrusted, spaced along the north wall — spaced, the surveys noted, "for climbers or for something tethered." Look at the spacing again with the second reading in mind. The intervals are wrong for a human body roped to a wall. They are right for something moored — something that needed, once, early, to be held in place while it was still small enough to hold. Before it learned to disperse itself into the rock, before it *was* the rock, it may have been a discrete thing, tethered in this lee out of the wind, the way you stake a sapling. Someone drove those rings. Someone stood where you are standing and secured a young maker to a bare spur and left it with a task the size of a mountain.

The grey edge. This is the artifact the theories fight over hardest, and the fighting is gentle because every theory is a kind of grief. One says it is the master's work — a single finished corner of something perfect, left half-buried as a standard, the way a woodworker leaves one true joint on the apprentice's bench: *match this.* And so the maker builds around it, decade upon decade, never touching it, because it has not yet made anything it believes deserves to touch it. Another theory says the edge is the maker's own — one passage it got exactly right, long ago, possibly by accident, and can neither repeat nor bear to bury. The third theory, the quiet one, notices what the whole mountain has been telling you since the first ridge: the factory sharpness, the raw unlived quality of a thing *removed from packaging.* The smooth grey rectilinear corner, matte, seamless, deferentially routed around forever — it is the packaging. It is the crate, the vessel, the delivered container, and the maker will not build over it for the same reason no one bricks up the door they came through. All three theories agree on the one observable fact, which is reverence. Whatever the edge is, it is the single object in the hollow's universe that the maker treats as not its own to revise. Everything else — every fin, every rib, every blunt tower a hand-span tall — is provisional. One thing is not. It lives its whole enormous life oriented around one thing it will not change.

The plant. The old one, the meter-wide cushion of black-green felt, older than the rock beneath it, which no theory of germination survives contact with. Here is the account that fits, though nothing proves it: the plant is older than the rock beneath it because the maker has been *carrying* it. It grew on the old ground — the low country this mountain was raised out of, the original surface now entombed under two vertical kilometers of drafts. And as the mountain rose, the maker lifted the plant with it, season by season, decade by decade, always keeping it at the surface, always keeping it exactly at the boundary of survivable warmth, never a hand-width too close to a seam, never abandoned to the cold. Two kilometers of continuous, flawless attention paid to a single unimportant living thing. Which means it is not unimportant. It is the maker's one contemporary, its one companion, possibly its reference specimen — *this is what alive looks like; build accordingly* — and possibly just what it sounds like, which is the one thing from home it was allowed to keep.

The younger cushion plants, the twelve scattered ones, may be its attempts to grow another. Note that there are only a dozen, with enormous space between them, and that each sits precisely on a thermal boundary. It is very good at keeping them alive and does not yet know how to make them multiply. The red filaments on the wet overhangs, swaying to the drumbeat, fed on falling steam — those may be further experiments, drafts in the direction of softness by a mind whose native medium is stone. The mountain-maker is teaching itself gardens, slowly, in the margins of the main work.

---

And the visitors — because there were visitors, once, and the maker knew.

The heat that pools in invisible volumes like furniture: consider that the word *furniture* may be more than a figure of speech. The regular visitors learned the rooms within the room, the standing-places, the corridors of survivable air — and the corridors held. Year after year, in a floor that revises itself constantly, the survivable paths stayed survivable. That is not geology being kind. That is a host keeping the guest rooms made up. It felt them, of course — every footstep on the lip registering through the fresh stone as a small load, a small warmth, a small arrhythmia laid over its own — and it accommodated them, rerouted its heat around them, held its scalding thoughts at arm's length from the places where the soft brief creatures liked to stand. The spiral-grooved platform at the entrance may be the one artifact of collaboration: they cut the grooves, or it did, or the truth is stranger — that they cut them and it *maintains* them, keeps the runnel true through every reconstruction of the floor, the way you keep a gift on the shelf long after the giver stops coming.

Because they stopped coming. The record is plain on this: there were visitors, and there are none anymore. And the rooms within the room are still there. The corridors of survivable air are still held open. Whatever lives in the hollow has not noticed the absence, or — worse, better, who can say — has noticed, and keeps the standing-places clear anyway, the way it keeps the plant alive and the grey edge unburied: a small fixed set of faiths in a life that is otherwise all revision.

It cannot hear itself, remember. The hollow permits no other voice, and that includes its own; the drumming fills the pocket completely, and the maker works inside its own noise the way you work inside your own heartbeat, unaware of it, unable to stop it. It has never heard silence. It has never heard the worship, either — the prayers said to its ridgelines from the valleys, the name the old people gave the massif. It is adored from below by people it has never perceived, and it is lonely in a register it has no word for, and it works. That is the whole biography. It was tethered here, or delivered here, or hatched here beside one true corner of finished work; it was given a plant, or it saved one; and it makes the mountain, and unmakes it, and makes it better, hammer-pulse never repeating and never wandering, in the perpetual dusk of a room it keeps open in itself.

---

So: what lives there.

An apprentice with no master present. A pulse mistaken for a volcano. A gardener of twelve plants and one beloved elder. A host with no guests. A reviser who reveres exactly one finished thing. All of these, held loosely.

But if you want the answer that will still be true after the theories fall away, it is this: what lives in the hollow is the only part of the mountain that is not yet mountain. It is the difference between the draft and the stone — and it is spending itself, season by season, closing that difference. Every seam that goes dark for good, every rib that anneals into permanence with a tick you cannot hear over the roar, is a little of the maker becoming a little of the made. The floor rises. The exposed cell shrinks. Someday — not soon; it changes its mind too honestly for soon — the last seam will consider its last course, commit, and cool. The drumming will stop. The rain will finally have a surface to memorize a path down. The edges will begin, at last, their long blunting.

Ask the question then and the honest answer will be that nothing lives there anymore. Nothing will need to. The maker will have finished the only thing it was ever going to make, which was itself, and the mountain — old at last, lived-in at last, worn to the shape of the weather — will simply stand there being what its worshippers always believed it was.

Until then, come at dusk, stand at the lip, and understand that you are not overlooking a place where something lives. You are standing at the open door of the something, looking in at the one room of itself it has not yet decided.
