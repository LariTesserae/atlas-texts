**Who Lives in the Cistern**

Begin where the hunger begins, because everything here stacks upward from one foundation, and the foundation is the velvet.

It has no proper name. It is not moss, not lichen, not algae — it is older in kind than any of those, a confederation of the very smallest lives, bacteria and their fellow-travelers woven into mats the color of rust and dried blood and, in the band where the noon pillar crosses, a paler color, almost rose. The velvet does not eat anything that was ever alive. It eats the stone — or rather it eats what the stone gives up: iron, sulfur, the slow mineral tithe seeping through the fused joints, the faint chemical difference between the water and the wall. It performs the oldest trick there is, turning rock and patience into flesh, and it has been performing it in this room for so long that it no longer reads as a thing growing *on* the masonry. It is the masonry's living surface, the way bark is the living surface of a tree. Press a finger to it and it gives, very slightly, the way a sleeping body gives. It is, in a sense the builders could not have intended and would perhaps have been pleased by, the only inhabitant that was here before the animals — the founding tenant, arrived on the wind or the feet of some bird a thousand roof-mouths' worth of light ago, and everything since has been its dependents.

The snails live on the velvet directly and live there the way monks live in a scriptorium. There are thousands of them, though you would have to count for days to know it, because at any given moment most of them are not doing anything you could call moving. Their shells have lost all pigment — not white, exactly, but *clarified*, like wax held up to a candle, so that you can see the dark coil of the animal through its own architecture. They graze in slow spirals, each snail clearing a path through the velvet perhaps the width of a grass blade, and the velvet heals behind them at almost the same pace, so that snail and mat are locked in an equilibrium so exact it resembles agreement. When two snails meet they do not turn aside. They climb. The stacks of two and three are not crowding but a kind of geology of their own — a snail will sit on the back of another snail for a season, grazing the film that grows on its host's shell, and the host registers this, if it registers anything, as weather. The oldest shells, down near the waterline of the deepest wall, have been grazed so many times by so many generations that they are polished like sucked candy, and some of them are empty, and some of the empty ones have velvet growing inside them now, small rust-colored rooms within the room, and small snails grazing in those.

The shrimp are the cistern's commons, its crowd, its weather of bodies. Candle-wax colored, blind for so many generations that the place where eyes would be has smoothed over entirely — not scarred, not vestigial, just *finished*, the way the steps are finished — they live at a density that should not be peaceful and is. They graze the velvet, they graze each other (gently — the film on a shrimp's back is as good as the film on a wall), they graze the rain of nearly nothing that drifts down from the surface film of the water. They walk on each other constantly and without offense, six and eight deep on the rich southern wall where the light-pillar's ring crosses, a layered, breathing shingle of small lives, each one feeling the world entirely through its antennae and the antennae of its neighbors, so that a touch at one edge of the colony travels across it as a visible ripple, a soft shudder of attention moving wall to wall in the dark. A shrimp lives perhaps two years. This means the cistern has held — be conservative — some thousands of complete generations, dynasties beyond counting, and every one of those dynasties has handed down the same single inheritance: the knowledge, worn now into the body itself, that nothing in the warm dark has ever been a danger. When your hand enters the water they come to it not as scavengers come to a carcass but as citizens come to a public square: because it is there, because it is new, because newness happens here perhaps once in a shrimp's whole life and is therefore worth walking across one's grandmother to attend.

The white gliders are the quiet order, the ones nobody — not even the fish — quite accounts for. Flatworms, if you must classify them, but classification feels like an imposition on something so reduced and so perfected: a leaf-shape of soft translucent tissue, fringed with cilia too fine to see, moving along the mortar lines with no visible effort, as though the wall were tilting gently and they were merely consenting to slide. They follow the joints. Always the joints — the fused seams where mineral seep is richest, where the velvet grows a different, darker strain — and so their paths are the builders' paths, the gliders tracing course after course of the original masonry like proofreaders going over a text the snails and shrimp have long since stopped reading. They are the only animals here that eat other animals: a glider that finds a dead shrimp will settle over it like a dropped handkerchief and be gone by the next noon-pillar, leaving the husk so clean it is translucent. The cistern has no decay to speak of, no rot, no cloud in its black water, and the gliders are why. They are the room's discretion. They are how the dark keeps itself tidy.

And then the fish.

There are nine of them. There have been roughly nine of them — eight, eleven, nine — for longer than any other fact about the cistern has stayed true, because the fish breed rarely and live almost geologically, decades upon decades, a fish here may plausibly outlast three or four human generations on the far-off lowland it has never imagined. They are small, a hand's length, pale as the inside of a wrist, finned with soft transparent fins that move constantly and propel them almost not at all, because *almost not at all* is the correct speed for their world. Their eyes are gone the way the shrimps' eyes are gone, smoothed over, the skull closed politely over the question. What they have instead is the whole surface of their bodies: lateral lines of exquisite sensitivity running stem to stern, so that a fish hanging motionless in the center of the chamber is not idle but *listening with its skin* — reading the drip from the roof-mouth, the shudder of the shrimp wall, the faint pressure-shadow of another fish two body-lengths away, the annual breath of the rising and falling water. They know the room the way you know a word: not as information but as something they are made of.

They eat shrimp, of course. They are why the dynasties of shrimp are dynasties and not a single eternal crowd. But the eating is so slow, so unhurried — a fish drifting along the wall, gathering one shrimp the way you might pick a berry while talking about something else — that it long ago stopped functioning as terror. The shrimp do not flee the fish. The shrimp walk on the fish, sometimes, briefly, and are sometimes eaten for it and mostly are not, and the whole arrangement has the settled quality of an old tax: regular, modest, survivable, and so deeply customary that no party can imagine the alternative.

The nine know each other. This should be said plainly, because it is the truest thing about them. In a room this size, over lives this long, with senses this fine, each fish carries a complete and continuously updated map of the other eight — their positions, their habits, the individual signature of each one's swimming, which is as distinct to them as a voice. The deep-corner fish, the oldest, the one who holds the dip in the floor where the swallowed drain lies, has held it so long that the others' routes all bend around that corner the way paths in an old town bend around a church. When she dies — she is very old; her swimming has acquired a slight list that the others have already incorporated into their maps — the corner will pass to another, and the room's geometry of habit will rearrange itself over a year or two, and that will be the largest political event the cistern sees in a century.

Above the water, almost nothing. Almost. A few pale mites graze the damp stone of the corbels, descendants of stowaways. Once in a great while, something from outside — a moth blundered down the roof-mouth, a swift's lost feather — falls onto the black water and lies on the surface tension like a gift on an altar, and the surface film of the water records it, and within a day the gliders and the shrimp have made it part of the cistern, the way the cistern makes everything part of the cistern.

And there is one more resident, if you will allow it, because the chamber itself insists on it: the warmth. It has no metabolism and no body, but it behaves like an inhabitant — it has a dwelling, it has dependents, it has a lifespan. Every animal here is, in the strict accounting, a way the old stored heat of the plateau has found to be spent: the velvet runs on chemistry the warmth makes possible, the snails and shrimp run on the velvet, the fish run on the shrimp, the gliders tidy after all of them. The whole crowded, silent, blood-warm congregation is the heat of a vanished sun, held in stone, paid out a flicker at a time through the bodies of blind animals in the dark. The builders cut their chamber down into it without, perhaps, knowing what they had done. What lives in the cistern, finally — under the shrimp and the snails and the nine listening fish — is that: an old warmth, slowly and beautifully spending itself, and the small white nations it keeps.

They are all natives now. Not one of them — not the oldest fish, not the deepest layer of velvet — retains any trace, in body or behavior, of a world with light in it. The cistern is not their refuge and not their prison; those words require an elsewhere, and they have none. It is simply the world: warm, black, finished, faithful. A drop falls from the roof-mouth. The vault holds the note. Along the southern wall, a ripple of attention crosses the shrimp and subsides. In the deep corner, the old fish adjusts her position by half a fin-beat, and eight maps, in eight blind heads, quietly correct themselves — and that is the news, all of it, until the next drop falls.
