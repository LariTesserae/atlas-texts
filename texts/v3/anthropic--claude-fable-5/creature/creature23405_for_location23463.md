**The People of the Finished Mountain**

Begin with the chest. Everyone born on the summit is born into thin air, and the body answers it in the first years of life: the people of Old Drum are barrel-chested, deep-lunged, built around a slow enormous breath. They are not large otherwise — generations of low doorways and narrow stairs have made them compact, neat-shouldered, easy to fold — but the ribcage is a vault. Lowlanders, if any ever came, would think them slow. They are not slow. They are *paced*, the way the trees are paced. A villager of Old Drum can carry a slab of stone up four tiers of stairway without their breathing changing, and will take, for that journey, exactly as long as it takes, because hurry is the one luxury the summit has never been able to afford. Where every path runs beside a fall that does not end, haste is not a virtue gone wrong. It is a death already in progress. Children are not punished for much, but they are punished for running.

The eyes come next. In the endless amber dusk, the people's eyes have gone wide and pale — grey, mostly, or a tarnished gold-brown, with large still pupils that take in the lamplight color of noon without strain. They see beautifully in dimness and have no idea that they do, because there is nothing else to see in. What they do not trust is sight itself. The haze flattens distance; the long blue shadows lie; and so the true sense of the village, the one they school and praise and court with, is *the hand*.

---

**They are a people of touch the way other peoples are peoples of the horse or the sea.**

Every villager's fingertips are double-natured: the pads calloused hard from stonework, the very tips kept soft and reading-fine, and they will tell you — they have a saying for it — that a hand has a palm for the world and fingers for the truth. A child learns the village before learning to talk, carried along the worn walls with their small hand pressed flat, feeling the routes: this glassy hollow means the stair to the cistern-house, this seam of cool means the north passage, this scallop is your own lintel, duck. By ten years old a child can cross the entire settlement in full dark, in the deepest part of night when even the birds are murmuring, without slowing.

It shapes everything downstream of it. Their craft is touch-craft — a wall-fitter chooses a stone by holding it against the gap with closed eyes, listening through the fingers for the *sit*, the moment the weight agrees. Their courtship is touch-courtship: to walk a stair with someone and turn sideways and press shoulders is nothing, the daily greeting, but to take a stranger's hand and trace for them the route to your own door along the wall — that is a declaration, and not a small one. Their dead are known by touch; more on the dead in a moment.

Their voices, meanwhile, have dropped beneath the birds. You cannot shout over a hundred thousand wings, so the people of Old Drum long ago stopped trying and went *under* instead: the village speech is low, chesty, carried on that great slow breath, full of long droning vowels that ride beneath the roar of the flocks the way a deep current rides beneath surf. Heard from outside it would sound half-sung, mournful, though they are not a mournful people. And beneath the voice lies the drum-speech — perhaps forty phrases, struck on Old Drum itself by the drummer's family, who have held the office so long that the family and the office share a name. *Assembly. Birth. The leaf. A death. Edge-work today, keep the children in. Someone is at the lake-stair.* The drum's voice travels not through the screaming air but through the warm stone, and villagers feel it in their feet and breastbone before they hear it. They will tell you the mountain is the real speaker; the drummer only asks it questions.

---

**Each villager is a chapter of an occupation older than their family.**

There is no work here that one life can finish, so no one works for themselves. The sap-wardens walk the waxglove sleeves every morning with their stone cups, a teaspoon per plant, never more, and each warden tends the exact route their teacher tended, inherited like a melody — to over-cut a plant is the nearest thing the village has to blasphemy, because the plant will not forgive it in your lifetime, or your daughter's. The egg-counters keep the quota along the cliff-hollows, lowered on waxglove-fiber lines by their cousins, and their tally is a treaty as much as an inventory: it is understood, in a way no one bothers to make precise, that the birds permit the village, and the quota is the rent. The wall-fitters maintain a repair list begun so long ago that its early entries describe walls that no longer exist; they work down the list anyway, in order, out of respect.

And there are the eleven tree-keepers — one household to each of the named dwarf trees, a bond made at the keeper's birth and held until death. A tree-keeper's duties are almost nothing: clear the wind-dust from the bark seams, watch, report. The keeping is not labor. It is *company*. The trees are spoken of, quietly, as the village's eldest citizens — they were here before the first wall — and a keeper sitting with their tree in the long afternoon, one hand on the furniture-smooth trunk, is not idling. They are visiting a relative who talks slowly.

The children, before they are anything else, are runners of small errands and keepers of the small nations. They race the grey summit beetles (the beetles never finish; this is the point of the joke, renewed every feast day for a thousand years). They catch the bronze swifts barehanded — it takes years to learn the soft instant grip that does no harm — hold them a single breath against the chest to take the luck, and open their hands. They are taught the edge late, at twelve or thirteen, walked out by an elder to lie flat on the warm stone with their face over the drop, watching the gyres of birds descend and descend and descend into the pewter floor of the lake, and they come back from it changed, quieter, having seen for the first time how far *down* goes.

---

**The dead go to the lake. The lake sends letters back.**

When a villager dies, the body is washed with sap-water, wrapped in mended cloth that is itself generations old, and carried at dusk to the western edge, where the longest cliff falls clean toward the clouds. The drum speaks. The family touches the wrapped shoulders, last of all greetings. And the body is given to the lake — released into that depth of air, into the territory of the birds, falling out of the finished world into the rumored one.

The theology that grows from this is gentle and entirely practical. Everything the birds carry up — the seeds, the fish-bones, the bright impossible lowland feathers, the brass button hanging in the meeting house — is understood as *correspondence*: the world below, which holds all the village's dead, sending small proofs of itself upward in the crops of its messengers. A child finding a strange seed in the guano-terraces will bring it to the meeting house solemnly, because it may be from somebody. The button is the great text. No one claims to know what it says. The elders consider it enough that it was sent.

It follows, of course, that the birds are not animals here, or not only. They are the postal service of the dead, the landlords of the cliffs, the roaring outer congregation of which the village is the small quiet inner one. The sicklewings, big and grave, are watched for omens. The sooty criers are the gossips, comic and tolerated. The swifts carry luck because they are the fastest things any villager will ever see — the only inhabitants of the summit exempted, by nature, from the law of slowness — and to hold one for a breath is to hold, briefly, the speed of the unfinished world.

---

**And one of them lives apart, and downward.**

Below the last tier, where the stairways stop and the cliff begins, there is a single dwelling cut half into a fracture-hollow among the highest nests — and it is always occupied, always by one person, always by someone old. The villagers call the office, simply, *the one below*. It is not exile and not quite honor; it is chosen, near the end of a life, by those who feel themselves already turning toward the lake. The one below keeps no quota and tends no plant. They live among the birds, fed by baskets lowered on a line, and their sole duty is to listen — to learn, in the last years, the textures of the roar: which note means the wind is shifting its ancient mind a hair's width, which silence means the sicklewings have seen something rising through the lake. Twice in remembered history the one below has rung the small bone chime that hangs in their hollow, and the drum has answered, and the village has gathered at the edge to watch — once for a storm that climbed almost to the summit and failed, once for a thing no song agrees on. Mostly the one below listens, and is visited by the children, who climb down against all instruction to sit in the bird-thunder and hear stories told by someone halfway translated already into the nation of wings.

---

So that is who lives there: a small deep-chested people with reading hands and lamplight eyes, droning their speech under the surf of birds; their eleven wooden elders; their beetles and lizards and luck-fast swifts; their tens of thousands of feathered landlords; their dead, downstairs, in the lake. They are not waiting for anything. The mountain is finished and they have understood this and made their peace its whole architecture — and when, once in a decade, the eleventh tree opens one new leaf, and the drum speaks through the warm stone, and everyone comes to stand in the amber dusk and look at the small green astonishment, what they are feeling is not hunger for change. It is hospitality. Something new has arrived in the finished place, and they have all come, unhurried, to touch shoulders with it.
