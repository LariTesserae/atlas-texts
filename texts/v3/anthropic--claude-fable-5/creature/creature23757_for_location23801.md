## The Tenant

Start with what does not live there, because the room is mostly made of what doesn't.

The rat does not live there. It descends the ladder a few times a year, and each visit ends the same way: it stands on the moss with its whiskers working, receiving nothing — no food-smell, no rot, no other rat, nothing legible — and it climbs back up into the world of things that mean something to rats. The moth did not live there; the moth *died* there, three days of circling the light column, and its death was the largest event in the room in a decade. The workers are gone. The city, in any sense that matters to the room, never arrived at all.

The springtails live there, and they are worth taking seriously before moving past them, because they are the room's only citizens in the animal sense of the word. Pale as boiled rice, none of them longer than a millimeter, they work the dark interior of the peat the way clerks work an archive — turning the dead lower layers, digesting what the moss discards, converting last century into soil. They are blind, or as good as. They have never needed eyes. They navigate by moisture gradient and by touch, and — this is the strange part, the part a patient observer would eventually notice — by the drone. The vibration that fills the chamber fills the peat too, attenuated, softened into something almost gentle at depth, and the springtails orient to its gradients the way surface insects orient to light. Where the hum is steadiest, they gather. They have been born into that note for something like four thousand generations. It is not sound to them. It is the shape of the universe. A springtail lifted out of the room and set down in ordinary quiet soil would be, in the only way it could experience it, in freefall.

But the springtails are staff. They are not the tenant.

The tenant is the green thing, and the first mistake anyone would make is to count it. A botanist kneeling in the light column with a hand lens would tally species — a dozen mosses, three or four liverworts, the ferns, the algal film on the grate, threads of fungus binding the peat, bacteria past numbering — and every entry in that tally would be technically correct and collectively wrong. Ninety years without wind, without frost, without a single day different from any other day, have done to this community what a long marriage does to two people: the boundaries are still visible, but they have stopped being where the truth is. Root-hair grown into rhizoid, fungal thread stitched through both, water passed hand to hand from the cold pipes down through the canopy to the peat and back up as vapor — it functions as one organism the way a reef does, or a bog, or a very old forest floor. One tenant. Thousands of names. It signed the lease as a spore sometime around the year the pumps first spun up, and it has never once been asleep since, because it has never once been winter.

Consider what its life is actually like, because it is not like anything on the surface.

It has no day and night worth the name — the grate-light brightens and dims, but the caged lamp holds the floor of its experience steady, so light for the tenant is not a rhythm but a *place*: the bright welt under the grate, the sodium margins, the dark. It moves toward light the way we move toward a window, over decades, a growing edge advancing millimeters a year and stopping precisely where the arithmetic of photons stops paying.

It has no seasons, and therefore, in one sense, no age. The moss you would touch at the surface is young — this year's growth, or last year's; the distinction hardly signifies. But its own body is its history. Push a finger down through the pelt and you pass through decades in centimeters: the loose living layer, then the amber compression of mid-century, then the black basal peat that photosynthesized under a light bulb whose filament design no longer exists. The tenant does not remember the way animals remember, by carrying the past forward in a nervous system. It remembers the way sediment remembers. It *is* its own memory, worn as flesh. The dry years of the forties are a thin stratum. The year a pipe joint wept for eight months is a lush one. Its biography could be read with a soil corer, if anyone came, and no one comes.

And it hears. This is the thing that would be hardest to believe and is also the most visible fact in the room. The tenant has spent ninety years inside a single sustained chord, and having no ears, it listens with its whole body — and having no way to flinch, it *builds* what it hears. The rippled moss near the pump wall is not a curiosity; it is transcription. The standing waves in the concrete became standing waves in living tissue, each generation of shoots finding the vibrational nodes marginally more hospitable than the antinodes, until the drone was written down in green at a rate of a few cells a year. That thin ceramic shriek from the almost-failing bearing — the note stacked on top of everything — lives in the tenant too, somewhere in the fine architecture of the smallest leaves, too subtle to see, present the way an accent is present in someone who left home as a child. If the bearing ever truly failed and was replaced, the new bearing's slightly different pitch would, over thirty or forty years, be written down beside the old one. The tenant would carry both. It keeps everything.

It has manners, of a slow kind. The border it grew around the scratched name on the wall — the worker's name, the date, seventy years gone — is not sentiment, whatever it looks like. The scratching disturbed the concrete's surface chemistry; the margin is habitat arithmetic, not homage. And yet the effect is indistinguishable from homage, and after seventy years of the arithmetic holding, the distinction starts to feel like ours rather than the room's. The tenant maintains the one legible human mark in its territory the way certain old churches maintain a founder's stone: without comprehension, without lapse. Same with the bulb it cannot colonize — the warm glass at the center of the moss-furred cage. The tenant surrounds it, tends right up to the line of too-hot, and holds there, decade after decade, an ember cupped and never smothered. It cannot want the light. It behaves in every observable way as though it does.

What does it fear? Nothing — it has no apparatus for fear. But there is one event that would end it, and the shape of that event is worth stating, because it is the inverse of everything the tenant is.

If the pumps ever stopped, two things would happen. The groundwater would begin to rise, slowly, through the floor of Sub-3, and within some months or years the room would drown, the peat becoming the bottom of a black pool, the ferns going under, the whole ninety-year equilibrium converting into a different, simpler one. That is the material death, and it is ordinary.

But before the water — in the first second — there would be the other thing. Silence. The note that has defined the interior of every cell of every organism in the room since before any of them existed would stop, and for the first time in its life the tenant would experience an *event*: a change, arriving all at once, in the one dimension of its world that had never changed. The springtails would lose their sky. The rippled moss would go on holding the shape of a sound no longer being made, a fossil of the drone, a green recording with nothing left to play it. Whether anything in the room could be said to notice is a question with no answer. But the room would, in that second, become a different room — and the tenant, which has only ever been exactly one thing, would spend its drowning being two.

For now, none of that. For now the pumps roar, the water beads and falls into the cups grown to catch it, the springtails turn the archive, the light column stands in its corner like a patient visitor, and the tenant does what it has done for ninety years, which is everything and nothing: it holds the room's shape, drinks the machine's warmth, transcribes the machine's voice, keeps the worker's name clear, and grows — not outward anymore, there is no outward left — but *inward* and *downward*, becoming daily a slightly deeper version of the same green sentence.

It is the only thing in the district that has never heard silence. It is, arguably, the only thing in the district that has never heard anything else. If it could be said to have a belief, it would be this: the world is a warm wet dark that hums, has always hummed, will always hum. And in the world it lives in — the only one it has ever been offered — that belief has never once been wrong.
