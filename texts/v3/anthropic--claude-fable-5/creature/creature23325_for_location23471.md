Begin with the smallest residents, because the room did.

**The film.**
The first thing that ever lived here arrived as dust on a thread of warm air — bacteria and a few hard-shelled algae, sifting down through the fissure in the room's first wet winter, when there was nothing inside but heat, condensation, and glass. They are still here, and in lineage they are the oldest inhabitants by decades. The pale mineral bloom the snails graze is theirs: colonies that eat the wall, in effect, pulling iron and manganese and copper out of the slag glass and binding them into crust. The metallic sheen on the pool is not pollution. It is alive — a floating mat one cell thick, breathing metal, healing itself within hours of any disturbance because it is being continuously rebuilt from underneath. Everything else in the room stands on this invisible economy. The bacteria taste the rock; everyone above them eats the tasting.

**The snails.**
The founding population was probably a handful — door snails washed down the fissure in a storm, or carried in pressed to a bat's fur. Thirty years is perhaps forty snail generations, and isolation has worked on them the way it works on islands. They are larger than their surface kin and slower, because nothing hunts an adult here and there is no winter to outrun. Their shells are the strange thing: built from water saturated with the wall's leached metals, they come out banded bronze and verdigris-green, dense, faintly conductive, ringed like tree cores — and they *are* cores. Each band records the room's chemistry the year it was laid down. The drift of empty shells against the cool wall, thousands deep, is the room's archive, thirty years of water chemistry stacked in spirals, and nobody is reading it but the dust.

The living snails are the room's surveyors. When a new hairline fracture opens — and they open every year now, as the bubble contracts — fresh mineral weeps from it, and the snails find it within days, converging from across the room by some gradient of taste in the condensation film. They graze the wound clean and move on. They have mapped every flaw in the glass with their bodies. If you wanted to know where the room is failing, you would not need instruments. You would follow the snails.

**The crickets.**
The cricket band is the room's middle class, its biomass, its weather. Thousands of pale cave crickets in a belt around the walls, packed close enough that the rock's texture seems to be made of them. They are also the room's most altered residents, because the room punished them hardest. A glass chamber is a resonator; in the early years, any cricket that sang advertised its position to two lizards and sixty bats with perfect acoustic fidelity, and the singers were eaten out of the gene pool inside a decade. What remains is a silent species in everything but touch. They drum — a tremor vocabulary tapped into the wall and read through the legs, faint enough that you feel it in your palm before you hear it. There is a pattern for the morning rain beginning. There is a pattern, fast and spreading, for a lizard on the move, and the band flows away from it like iron filings from a magnet. And there is a pattern nobody taught them, a deep frozen stillness that ripples outward seconds before one of the structural *ticks* — the crickets feel the slag's stress building through their feet before the rock lets go. They have been observed evacuating a ceiling dimple minutes before it spalled. They are the room's nervous system, distributed across its inner skin, and the room twitches through them.

**The midges.**
Each mineral straw at each drip-point holds a thread of water, and each thread of water holds larvae. The midges are the residents with the smallest lives: hatched inside a growing stalactite, fed on the bacterial film washing down through it, emerging in grey pulses when the morning rain starts, mating in the light blade, dead by evening, eggs laid back into the drip. A midge's whole world is one straw and one shaft of silver light. They have run perhaps a thousand generations in this room. They have never required the sky to exist, and as far as their biology is concerned, it doesn't.

**The lizards.**
There are always two. The room's energy budget supports exactly one adult pair of wall lizards, and there has been exactly one adult pair since the colonizing pair came down the fissure sometime in the nineties — which means every lizard in the room's history is descended from those two, an unbroken thirty-year dynasty of cousins. They are glossier than any surface lizard, dark with iridescent flecking, near-invisible against the peacock glass, and they are the most precise animals in the room: they do not follow the light blade across the floor so much as anticipate it, arriving at the next basking point before the sun does, because they have inherited the room's clock along with its territory. The surplus young face the room's one hard law. Most are eaten — by the bats, by the adults, occasionally by each other. A few climb the fissure into the black field above, where almost nothing survives. And once in a while one stays low, hunting midges at the pool's edge, lean and patient, waiting for an adult to die. Succession in this room is a held position by the water, sometimes held for years.

**The bats.**
Sixty bodies in the warm cupola, packed into gas-dimples shaped, by molten accident, exactly to receive them. The bats are the only residents who commute — the only ones who know with their own senses that there is an outside — and they are the room's entire import trade. Every dusk the colony pours up the fissure; every dawn it returns carrying the world internally: moths from the river willows, beetles from the field margins, pollen and spore and grit, all of it digested into guano that becomes the floor, that feeds the bacteria and the springtails, that feeds the crickets, that feed the lizards and the bats themselves. The room's whole food economy balances on this nightly arbitrage. The bats subsidize everything, and everything has organized itself downstream of their ceiling. They could roost anywhere. They have a county of culverts and barns. They come back to the warm glass dimples every dawn, sixty votes recast daily for this room over the entire world.

**The fern, and the unseen majority.**
The hart's-tongue fern is a population of one — possibly the room's oldest individual organism, thirty years rooted in the spot where the light blade rests longest, its leaves glassy and oversized from a lifetime of silver half-light. Its spores ripen, drift, settle on warm dark glass, and die, every year, without exception. It is the loneliest thing in a crowded room and shows no sign of caring.

Beneath everyone, in the floor itself, live the recyclers — springtails, mites, pale threadlike worms in the guano and spall-dust — the residents nobody sees and nothing could do without. The floor is not ground. The floor is everyone who has ever lived here, ground fine: shell-grit and chitin and bat-bone and glass, three decades of the room's dead made into the room's soil. To live here is eventually to become the place.

**The one who visits.**
There is, finally, a resident the room counts that the census wouldn't. One person comes down — a few times a year, headlamp, folding rule, a notebook gone soft with damp. They helped pour, or their father did; they found the fissure young and told nobody, and they have measured this room longer than most of its species have existed: the length of the straws, the width of the crack, the slow closing of the walls. Whether they *live* here is a question of definitions, but the room has settled it. The lizards no longer break their circuit when the lamp comes down. The cricket band has a drum-pattern for these particular footsteps — distinct from the spall-warning, closer to the pattern for rain: a known disturbance, scheduled, harmless. The bats do not flush. The room has filed this person under weather, and weather belongs.

**What lives there.**
So: bacteria for chemistry, snails for memory, crickets for nerves, midges for the smallest turns of the clock, lizards for the hand of it, bats for breath and blood-exchange with the outside, a fern for stubbornness, a human for witness. Listed separately they are tenants. But spend a full day inside — the morning rain, the swinging blade, the dusk exhalation of wings — and the separateness stops being the true description. What lives in the bubble is the bubble: a single young organism wearing all of them, assembled in one human generation, running its metabolism on old foundry heat and a daily blade of sun, packed to its glass skin.

And it is mortal, and almost nothing inside it knows. The walls close a millimeter a year. The crickets feel each tick and forget it by the next drum. The snails graze the wounds. Only the archive of shells against the cool wall, and the soft notebook, are keeping count — the room's whole brief, bright, accelerating life recorded in spiral bands and pencil, inside a black field that everyone above is certain holds nothing at all.
