**Who Lives in the Resonant Hollow**

Start with what can be counted, because the hollow itself never could.

---

**The frogs first, since they keep the ledger of time.**

Several hundred pale frogs ring the black pool, and they are not one population but two, distinguishable by ear if you stand long enough at the margin. The hollow-born majority — three, four, five generations deep now — call in the rests of the piston-knock with the unconsidered precision of native speakers. They have never heard a silence that lasted. They were eggs in trembling water, larvae flinching in time, and the rhythm is not something they learned but something they are made of, the way a person raised beside a river does not hear the river.

But up on the highest shelf of the frog margin, where the spray is thinnest, sit the elders: a dwindling handful of the first-fallen, frogs that were already alive when the ground opened. They came down as froglets in the year of the collapse — washed down, perhaps, or drawn by the new wet dark — and they remember, in whatever way a frog remembers, the flat light of the heath, the wind, the old shallow pools that froze to the bottom. You can hear them. Their calls land a hair behind the beat, always, the timing of adult learners, of immigrants singing in a second language with a faint persistent accent. The hollow-born do not seem to notice. The elders, season by season, are being replaced by frogs who have nothing in them but rhythm, and when the last of them dies the hollow will hold no living memory of the silence that came before — only the inherited shape of the song.

The eldest of all is a heavy, scar-flanked female who has survived nine winters and four stoats. She has stopped calling. She sits where the rock is warmest — a hand's breadth from a weeping seam that pulses with the throb — and what she does now is wait, with the enormous economy of an old amphibian, for the precise quality of tremor that means a midge column has drifted within range. She hunts by the rock's voice. She has, in her decade, become a small instrument of the works that does not know she exists.

---

**The swallows, who think the rock is a parent.**

Perhaps forty pairs nest in the upper fractures, and the colony's memory is shorter than the frogs' — a swallow lives a few years, and so for every bird now flying the throb in the walls is simply a condition of the universe, like gravity, like the gray crack of sky. The nestlings hatch into it. Their first begging cries are already in the key of the harmonic shiver, and there is a sober possibility, watching them, that they cannot fully distinguish their parents' voices from the bedrock's — that they hatch believing the whole hollow is feeding them, which, in a sense, it is.

The adults navigate the dark fractures partly by feel: they have learned the standing wave the way the moss has, and they thread the dead air of the nodes at speed, banking where the vibration thickens, using the rock's sound as a kind of touch extended into open space. When the works falls into one of its twice-daily silences, the colony goes briefly, eerily clumsy — birds checking their speed, fluttering, recalibrating — forty pairs of wings discovering all at once that they had been leaning on something.

---

**The pool, and its first people.**

The blind white amphipods are the oldest residents by an unanswerable margin. They did not arrive; they were *revealed*. They lived in the dark water under the heath for ten thousand years before the roof thinned, drifting through flooded seams that had never held light, and for them the collapse was not the birth of the hollow but a catastrophe of sky — a sudden gray wound opening overhead, an invasion of swallows and frogs and falling water and, worst of all, the dim green glow, which they avoid with the whole-body aversion of creatures to whom light is a foreign element. They are the indigenous nation of this place. Everything else is a settler. They keep to the pool's deepest fold and go on doing what they did before the world broke open: combing the silt, breeding in darkness, paying no attention whatsoever to the rhythm, because they predate it — they are the only living things here that may have felt the works *begin*.

The newts are something stranger: they came down the broken slope as ordinary brown heath newts, in ones and twos over the first years, and the pool has been bleaching them ever since. A newt can live twenty years; these are the same individuals that walked in, blanching slowly in residence, their pigment thinning season by season like a held photograph. They drift more than swim, hanging in the clear black water at odd angles, and each carries its history on its skin — the palest are the earliest arrivals, the faintly mottled ones came later, and a newt the color of weak tea slid down the slope only last spring and has not yet understood what the pool intends to make of it.

---

**The short-lived, for whom the rhythm is everything.**

The midge columns rise in the acoustic dead spots — they have found the nodes of the standing wave just as the moss has, and they map the hollow's silence in air the way the moss maps it on stone: standing gray pillars marking, for anyone who could read them, exactly where the rock does not sing. A midge lives days. It hatches into the throb, dances in a pillar of stillness, breeds, and dies, and in its whole existence the works never once pauses long enough for it to learn that the sound is not the same thing as being alive.

The larvae in the pool flinch in unison with each tremor of the surface — a thousand bodies practicing, before they have legs or voices, the entrainment that will organize their adult lives. The chorus is being rehearsed in the water years before it is sung.

---

**The stoat, who has learned the machine's schedule.**

She is not a resident, quite — she dens somewhere in the heath above — but she has become a fixture, and she is the cleverest thing in the hollow by a long way, because she alone has understood the silences. The frogs' early-warning system is acoustic: the chorus drops out section by section ahead of a predator, darkness running along a street of song. But twice a day the distant works rests, the rhythm collapses, and the chorus stutters into ragged panicked noise — and in that noise the warning cannot propagate. The stoat has learned this. She comes down the broken slope in the minutes before the silence, sleek with condensation, and waits flattened against the white fracture-faces; and when the throb steps out from under the hollow and the frogs lose their timing, she hunts through the confusion like a thief who has memorized the watchman's rounds. She is entrained to industry at one remove — a predator whose calendar is set by an engine she has never seen, hunting prey whose song is set by the same engine. The works, in its mindless distant labor, feeds her.

---

**The slow ones.**

The moss, the liverworts, the sideways rowan — these are the recorders, the residents who do not respond to the rhythm so much as transcribe it. The moss bands are a written document: a horizontal score of the standing wave, thickening through the nodes, starving through the antinodes, every year's growth a fresh line of the same long sentence. The rowan is twelve years old and the height of a child and will probably never set fruit, and it does not care; it has the skylight, it has the drip, it has time. The hart's-tongue ferns uncurl at a pace below the threshold of the hollow's busy life, and if the ferns notice the throb at all it is only as a faint perpetual stirring in their cells, a tremor they will have transcribed, in a thousand years, into the very grain of the soil their dead fronds become.

---

**And then the largest resident, which has no body.**

Because once the census is taken — frogs, swallows, newts, amphipods, midges, larvae, stoat, moss, rowan — something is still unaccounted for, and it is the thing every other inhabitant lives *inside*. The sound is the hollow's senior occupant. It was in the rock before the roof fell; it will be in the rock after; it never sleeps, only changes register; and every body in the hollow has been shaped by it the way reef creatures are shaped by the sea. The frogs sing in its rests, the swallows speak in its key, the moss grows in its image, the stoat hunts by its calendar, the old female frog feels her dinner through its tremor. The community has organized itself like an orchestra around a conductor none of them will ever see, and the conductor does not know the orchestra exists.

What most truly lives in the hollow, then, is the *interval* — the four-second rest, the gap in the knock, that little recurring room of silence inside the sound. Everything alive here has crowded into it. The chorus fills it. The colony's calls thread it. It is the hollow's real habitat, invisible, rhythmic, reliable as tide.

---

**Last: who will live here when the works stops.**

Someday — a decade, a century — the distant labor will end for good, and the hollow will fall into a silence it has never known. And for one strange generation, the silence will be inhabited. The hollow-born frogs will keep calling on the four-second cycle, filling rests that no longer arrive, holding the ghost of the rhythm in their bodies the way the elders once held the ghost of the sky. The swallows' calls will drift slowly off the dead harmonic. The midge pillars will wander, the moss bands will blur upward into uniform green over the slow centuries, and the amphipods in the dark fold of the pool will register, dimly, the departure of the long vibration that arrived sometime after the glaciers and is leaving now, and will go on combing the silt exactly as before.

The last resident of the Resonant Hollow will be the memory of the sound, kept warm in living tissue after the rock itself has forgotten — a borrowed thunder, returned at last, one frog generation at a time.
