Begin with what is easiest to see, because the place teaches you, slowly, that the easiest thing to see is never the resident — only the residue of one.

**The weed.**

The pale ribbons live by exception. They grow only in the dead-water seams, the handful of crevices where the geometry of the chamber happens to cancel the turbulence — little pockets of accidental calm in a room that is otherwise all violence. This means the weed does not live in the chamber so much as in the chamber's *mistakes*: the places the great work missed. It is anchored in crystal, which should be impossible — crystal has no soil, no purchase, nothing to give — and if you could pull a ribbon free (you cannot; they tear before they release) you would find the holdfast is not gripping the stone but grown *into* it, mineralized at the root, half plant and half wall. The weed is being built into the mountain at one end while it streams and snaps at the other. It is, in miniature, the whole story of the place: the living end whipping in the current, the dead end already part of the architecture.

It is colorless because it has given up deciding. The light here is too strange — total darkness in one second, blades of summit-white in the next, the strobe of the shattered surface — and pigment is a commitment to a particular light. The weed declines to commit. It lets everything pass through it. Some who have sat on the ledge say that in the brightest flashes you can see the chamber *through* the ribbons, faintly bent, as if the weed were a flaw in the air rather than a thing in the water.

Each year there are fewer seams. The walls grow inward, the dead pockets fill, the calm places are pressed shut one by one. The weed is not dying; it is being evicted by construction, transferred patch by patch from the category of inhabitant to the category of material. There are bands in the lower walls — you can see them when the water drops in its cycle — where translucent threads run through the crystal in streaming shapes, frozen mid-snap. The chamber keeps what lived in it. It keeps it the only way stone knows how.

**The film.**

Then there is the inhabitant you cannot see, and here the place begins to give up its actual secret.

The mineral skins — the breath-on-glass films that the water leaves with every impact, the films that stack into fans and faces and the slow closing of the room — are not entirely mineral. Scrape one fresh, within an hour of its laying, and it is faintly slick. It tastes (one tally-keeper is said to have tasted it, once, and never said why) of cold iron and something underneath the iron, something that iron does not have: a sweetness, very small, like the ghost of fruit.

The films are alive. Thinly, slowly, invisibly — a culture of something microscopic that rides the water up from the unplumbed deep, is flung against the wall, and *stays*, binding mineral out of the cold flow, sealing itself into its own masonry, layer upon layer, generation entombing generation. Every crystal fan in the chamber is a graveyard standing on end. Every sharp new face is a frontier of the living, a single film deep, working in the dark and the strobe-light alike, indifferent to both.

This is the answer to the question the watchers on the ledge keep almost asking. The water is not building the mountain. The water is the *hod-carrier*. What lives in the chamber — what has always lived in the chamber, in numbers beyond tallying, in bodies beyond seeing — is the mason. The violence that looks like a potter's hands is hands indeed, but the hands are trillions, and each one is the size of nothing, and each one lays one grain and dies into it. The pattern that the careful watchers found — the column rising in the same five places, the rage that is not rage but work — is not the water's intention. It is the shape the water has been *taught*, channeled by walls that the living film has spent the mountain's whole short life adjusting, the way a riverbed teaches a river, except here the bed is awake.

So the small deafening heart has a body after all. The chamber is not a room with things living in it. The chamber is the thing, and it is closing not like a wound but like a fist, very slowly, around its own brilliance — because that is what this kind of life does. It does not grow outward into the world. It finishes. Its whole nature is the verb *to complete*. The mountain is unfinished because its tenant is not done, and the tenant will be done only when there is no tenant, no water, no room: just achievement, solid to the core.

**The eel.**

Which brings us to the white thing, and the white thing must be approached the way it approaches the chamber: from below, and without hurry.

It does not live in the room. That is the first correction to the stories. It lives in the deep that no rope has reached, the cold throat that feeds the chamber its water, and it comes *up* — once a season, perhaps, or once a mood — the way the rope-makers come down. The chamber has two congregations, and they meet in the bright loud middle of the mountain: one descending a fiber rope from the world of sky, one ascending a black channel from the world of weight, both arriving at the same small violent room to do something that, in both cases, looks very much like attending.

The eel-bodied thing is eyeless because where it lives, eyes are a wound waiting to happen — but watch it (those who have watched it never forget it) and you will not believe it is blind. It rides the rotation effortlessly, yes, but more than that: it rides the rotation *knowingly*, slipping from current to current a half-second before the current changes, present at the column's rising as if it had an appointment. It does not fight the water because it does not need to. It reads the room the way the room reads itself — through pressure, through the conducted roar, through the bone-hum that drives human visitors out within the hour. The sound that occupies a person's body like a flood occupies a valley is, to the white thing, simply *vision*. The chamber is deafening only to creatures that hear with ears. The eel hears with its whole length, and what the noise tells it, second by second, is the exact shape of everything.

What does it eat? The film. It grazes the freshest faces, the day-old crystal still slick with the living layer, rasping the frontier of the masons as it is laid. It eats slowly and it eats little and it eats with what can only be called *selection* — these faces and not those, this fan thinned and that one left whole. And here the careful watchers, if they watched long enough, would find their second pattern: the places the eel grazes are the places the chamber's geometry stays open. The dead-water seams where the weed survives. The five stations where the column rises. The channels of the great rotation itself. The eel is not feeding on the work. The eel is *editing* it — keeping the room a room a little longer, eating the closing of the fist, one mouthful of completion at a time.

Whether it does this on purpose is the wrong question, the way it is the wrong question of the water. It does it the way it does everything: as the shape its nature takes in this place.

And the saying — *there is only ever the one* — may be exactly true, and truer than the sayers know. The deep channels of a young range run far, and unfinished mountains are not so common that each gets its own attendant. It is possible — the offerings in the bark basket suggest someone has long believed it — that the white thing is the resident not of this chamber but of *unfinishedness itself*: that it has attended other rooms in other cooling peaks, grazing their closings slower, riding their rotations, and that on the season it stops coming, the people of the rope will know the room's count of years has entered its final grouping of five. Older than the chamber, the stories say. Of course it is. It was somewhere else's chamber first.

**The keepers.**

The people do not live there. No one could. But there is a way of living *toward* a place that is a kind of habitation, and by that measure the chamber has a third population, the strangest of all, because it is the only one that knows the ending and comes anyway.

They live below, in the valleys on the western side — close enough that some of them, as children, have seen the summit breathe its mist on a cloudless day, and have been told, or have refused to be told, what makes it. They are not many. The rope is replaced generation by generation, which means each generation contains at least one person who can twist a hundred meters of fiber to hold a human life, and at least one person willing to trust it. The shelf on the lip is wide enough for one. This is the deepest fact about them: whatever the chamber is to them, it is not shared at the lip. Each one goes alone, climbs hard, descends the seam with the roar already in their teeth, and sits — for as long as the cold permits, which is not long — in the one-person church of the loudest room in the world.

The tally marks are theirs, dozens of strokes in fives, and the question of what they count has the same answer as most questions here: completion. They are counting the room toward its silence. Some of the marks are old enough that the crystal has begun to grow over them — the film does not distinguish between raw stone and human writing, and it has started, with no malice and no mercy, to lay its breath across the record. A few of the oldest strokes are now visible only as shadows inside the wall, sealed like the weed, like the masons, like everything. The keepers know this. It may be why they keep counting. A tally that the mountain is slowly eating is the most honest calendar that has ever been made.

The offerings in the bark basket — the seeds, the carved bird, the child's tooth — are not payments and not prayers, or not only. Look at what they are: things at the *beginning* of something. A seed before its tree. A bird carved before it has been given to whoever it was carved for. A tooth lost at the start of a mouth's second attempt. The keepers bring beginnings to a room whose whole being is an ending, and set them above the spray line where the closing will reach them last. It is the gentlest argument anyone has ever made with a mountain: *not everything has to finish*. The room, year by year, narrows toward them anyway. The keepers know that too. They keep bringing them.

They leave before the cold takes their hands, and for a day afterward silence presses on them like a weight, and what they have seen they mostly do not say. But the rope is always replaced. Whatever they receive on that ledge, it is worth a hundred meters of twisted fiber a generation, and a hard climb, and an hour of unbearable noise, to a people who will never once be comfortable there and have never once stopped coming.

**The last resident.**

So: a weed that lives in the work's mistakes and is filed into the walls. An invisible nation of masons whose entire civilization is the act of ending the place they inhabit. A white attendant from below who eats the ending slowly, to make the place last. A thin lineage from above who count the ending faithfully, and leave beginnings on its doorstep.

Every one of them lives in relation to the same thing, and the same thing is the true inhabitant, and it is this: the chamber is occupied by its own unfinishedness. That is the creature. The roar is its voice, the rotation its pulse, the five rising columns its slow gesture, the strobing light its single restless eye. It is the only thing there that all the others can sense — the weed streaming in it, the masons building it shut, the eel reading it through its skin, the keepers carrying it home in their humming teeth — and it is dying in the only way such a thing can die: by succeeding.

One day the count of fives will stop mid-group. The last seam will close on the last ribbon of weed. The masons will lay their final film against the final film and the room will be a word with no space left inside it. The white thing will turn in some deep black channel and not come up, and will go looking — if the basket-fillers are right — for the next young mountain that has not decided on a shape. The keepers' grandchildren will climb to a summit that is silent, finally and totally silent, and will stand on solid stone above a sealed brilliance: a sphere of crystal fans and frozen ribbons and shadow-tallies and one small basket of soft pale beginnings, buried in the core like a heart that finished beating because it finished its work.

And whether anything will live there then depends on what you believe a fossil is. The mountain will be done. Inside it, exactly as they formed, sharp as the moment of their birth, every one of its residents will be home.
