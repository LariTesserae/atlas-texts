The first thing to say about the residents of the caldera is what they are not. They are not a workforce, though everything they do looks like work. They are not parts, though the lake runs like a machine. They are not a congregation, though they fill a nave-sized basin and never stop ringing. They are tenants — of the water, of the rock, of each other — and each of them lives inside exactly one sense, one gradient, one appetite, and knows nothing whatsoever about the whole.

Start with the smiths, since they are loudest.

A smith begins as almost nothing: a soft pink comma of flesh, naked, blind, ejected by the hundred into the water column and carried wherever the bellows-eels' warm jets happen to throw it. It has one talent, and that talent is taste. It can taste voltage. It drifts until the water bites — until it crosses one of the faint galvanic seams where iron-bearing rock and copper-bearing rock disagree with each other through thirty meters of mineral broth — and there it settles, glues itself down, and begins to drink the disagreement. Its first plate appears within the year, a foil so thin you could read through it. The plates after that come slower, and thicker, and the animal lays them down in layers, so that an old smith can be aged like a tree: count the laminations in a fallen plate and you are counting winters.

The ringing — the famous, ceaseless, foundry-through-a-wall ringing — is grooming. Iron in oxygenated water wants to bloom into rust, and rust would smother the mantle that does the precipitating, so every few minutes a smith flexes its whole armored body against the rock and shakes the oxide bloom off in a fine red smoke. Each toll is an animal cleaning itself. The bell choir is hygiene, multiplied by thousands.

The oldest smiths can no longer do much else. A century of growth makes them heavy as anvils, fused half into the wall, their plates thick as a thumb, and their note drops with their mass — the deep voices in the choir are the elders, ringing slower and lower until one day they stop. The soft body goes first, quietly, eaten out by its own symbionts. The armor stays. For a few years a dead smith hangs on the wall like a sealed reliquary, and then the plates peel, one by one, and fall — a slow iron snow, a plate a week somewhere in the lake, turning end over end through the gold-green light on its way to the floor.

The bellows-eels know none of this, because the bellows-eels know nothing but temperature. They are eyeless, earless, nearly nerveless except for their skin, and their skin is an instrument of terrible precision: they read the water in thousandths of a degree. Their world is a map of warmths. The walls are a cold cliff; the fissure mouths are bright hearths; the other eels are friendly columns of heat packed alongside, known and counted by their warmth alone. The hour of stillness between strokes is not idleness — it is reading. An eel lies in its slot feeling the cold pool and thicken in the deeps, feeling the gradient steepen degree by fractional degree, and when the difference crosses some threshold written into it before the lake existed, the whole animal fires: one hydraulic contraction, one *whump*, a slug of vent-warmth driven up through the layers. Then it goes back to reading. They are thermostats that happen to be alive. They breed almost never — an egg every few years, glued in the warm throat of a fissure — and they live, as far as anything can tell, around two centuries, which means some of the eels pumping today were pumping before the youngest walls had finished cooling.

The sparkers live faster, by local standards, which is to say they live at all. They are the lake's only quickness — silver, finger-length, schooling — and their bodies are mostly wire: a core of conductive tissue sheathed in flesh, head to tail. They feed at junctions, anywhere charge wants to move and can't, and the feeding is the famous slow blue arc. Watch a school long enough and you will see them do the thing no single sparker can do: link up, nose to tail, a living chain of five or nine or thirteen bodies bridging a gap in the rock too wide for one, the whole series shivering faintly while the potential drains through them like water through a hose of fish. Their courtship is the same act turned inward — two sparkers touch and equalize, sharing charge until neither can taste a difference between itself and the other, and the eggs that come of it are laid in the copper seam, each hatchling born carrying a small starter current from its mother like a coal carried from an old fire.

When a sparker dies, it discharges. Everything it has been holding leaves at once — one bright thread, a snap of blue, and then a dull gray ribbon of body drifting down. The school gathers at a death. Not from grief; from thrift. They feed on the last of it. In the caldera nothing is wasted, because nothing here has ever had enough to learn waste.

The glassmoths you will not find by looking. They are flat to the point of theory, transparent, and light goes through them almost unbent; in a basin made entirely of edges, you find a glassmoth by noticing a place where the edges have gone soft. Each one rasps obsidian at its leading edge and extrudes it, annealed and smooth, at its trailing edge, and so each glassmoth leaves behind it a single continuous ribbon of finished glass — meters long after a decade, a lifetime written in one unbroken line. The ribbon is the biography. Where it wanders, the moth was hungry; where it loops, something interrupted it; where it crosses an old ribbon, two lives touched, possibly centuries apart. No one has seen a glassmoth die. It may be they don't, on any schedule the lake has had time to demonstrate.

Beneath and within all of these — inside the smith's mantle folds, where the galvanic trick is actually performed; in the eel's long gut; sheening the fissure throats in films too thin to see — live the lake's true first residents, the ones with no bodies to speak of: the mineral-eating bacteria that came up from the deep rock before anything with a shape did. Every animal in the caldera is, honestly accounted, a vehicle and a roof for them. They are the landlords. The rest is subletting.

Lower, now.

The floor, thirty meters down and lit like noon, is paved with the fallen plates of generations of smiths — an iron midden, ankle-deep in places, leaves of armor lying in drifts wherever the eels' currents heap them. And in the drifts live the sextons: a dozen of them, perhaps, in the whole lake, no more. Stout, ten-legged, slate-colored, slow even by the standards of this slow place. A sexton does the thing nothing else in the caldera does. It chooses. It walks the midden turning plates over with its forelegs, weighing them, rejecting them, and fits the chosen ones across its back — large at the shoulder, fine at the tail — and wears them, and is not satisfied, and weeks later swaps one out for a better. It makes nothing, builds nothing, breaks the lake's great rule by not so much as a millimeter. But it *selects*, and it keeps selecting, a hundred arrangements over a long life, each marginally more to its taste than the last — and taste, in a place that has never had an intention, is a door standing very slightly ajar.

In the mouth of the central fissure, fused to the rock and a meter across, sits the bourdon: the first smith, the founder, armored an inch thick and immobile for longer than anything else here has been alive. It rings perhaps once a day, on no schedule, and its note is below hearing — you don't hear the bourdon, you feel the water lean. For a few seconds afterward the whole clangor of the lake seems to sit on top of that note like dishes on a table.

And below the bourdon, the fissures go down — the flooded stairwells to the old country, the deep cracked rock the colonists came from. The old country is still inhabited: pale unplated cousins of the smiths, eels that have never jetted because down there nothing needs circulating, blind drifting things with no surface name because none of them has surfaced yet. The stairs are still delivering. Every few decades something arrives at the top that the lake has never seen, looks around at the light and the noise, and either dies or stays. The caldera is not finished being settled. The door was never closed.

Only the surface is empty. The wind lays the occasional gift on it — an insect blown up from the green world below, spent, drifting — and the gift sinks unclaimed through thirty meters of sunstruck water and lands on the iron leaves of the floor, because nothing here has yet learned that the ceiling is an address. Something will. It is probably in the fissures now, on its way up, a thousand years out.

That is who lives here: a hygienist that sounds like a bell, a thermostat with skin, a wire that schools, an eraser with a biography, a landlord with no body, a hermit with taste, a founder with one note, and a crowd of strangers still climbing the stairs. Not one of them knows the lake. The smith knows voltage; the eel knows warmth; the sparker knows charge; the glassmoth knows texture; the sexton knows, dimly, *better and worse*. The lake itself is the sum — and the sum knows itself the only way an instrument can, which is by sounding. The residents are not in the ringing. The residents are the ringing, and they will go on being it long after the climbers have come, and sat on the warm black glass, and failed to find the word, and gone back down.
