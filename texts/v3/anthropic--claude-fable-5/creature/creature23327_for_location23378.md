**The Vellish**

Begin with the body, because the Tableland did.

Three miles of altitude does its work on a population over enough generations, and the people of Vell are visibly its product: deep-chested, broad through the back, built around lungs that would look excessive anywhere lower. They are not large people — almost nobody in Vell is tall, and after a week there you stop expecting tallness, the way you stop expecting trees — but they are *substantial*, low-set, hard to knock over. Their pulse is slow. Lowland physicians who have examined Vellish travelers describe resting heart rates that ought to belong to sleepers, and a way of breathing — long, deliberate, with a pause at the bottom of each breath — that the Vellish themselves do not know they are doing, because it is simply how breathing goes.

They are pale in the way of people under a permanently veiled sun, but it is a warm pallor, lamplit, amber at the edges; the dim gold light of the dynamo hall has been the light of their evenings for so long that they look slightly wrong under any other illumination, like portraits taken out of their frames. Their eyes are good in dimness and water easily in glare. Their hands are large, quiet, and very sure — Vell is a town of mesh-weavers, wheel-tenders, and moss-pressers, and everyone's hands have spent decades doing slow exact work in the cold.

They move at one speed. This is the first thing visitors notice and the last thing they understand. A Vellish person crossing a plank-way in a hurry and a Vellish person crossing it at leisure are nearly indistinguishable; what changes is not the pace but a certain economy, a shortening of everything inessential. They learned this from the land, or the land taught it to whatever was teachable in them and the rest left. Hurry implies that something can be gained by arriving sooner. On the Tableland, almost nothing can.

**How they sound**

The pile eats voices, and over the generations it has eaten its way into the language itself.

Vellish speech is soft, low, and worn — the sharp consonants have been ground off it the way the peaks were ground off the mountains. Words end in hums and open vowels; names are short and round: Orm, Bett, Sull, Maren, Doss. There is no point shouting on the Tableland — a shout dies within ten yards, swallowed whole, and shouting is therefore considered not rude exactly but *foolish*, a sign that someone hasn't grasped where they are. Instead, conversation happens close. The Vellish stand nearer to each other than lowlanders find comfortable, speak almost into one another, touch a forearm or a shoulder to hold the channel open. Intimacy is not a stage their conversations reach; it is the precondition for having one at all.

Across distance they use the arm-talk: a semaphore of slow, held positions, arms against the green, readable from one swell to the next. It is an old system, unhurried by design — you hold each sign for a full breath — and it has perhaps two hundred signs, of which a quarter concern the cloud, a quarter concern the works, and the remainder cover, with surprising adequacy, everything else a person needs to say across a mile of moss. There is a sign for *come*, a sign for *stay*, a sign for *gleaners on the frames, wait*, and a sign, used rarely and answered always, that means roughly *I am all right, but stand where I can see you*.

**The work and the offices**

Vell organizes itself around water and light, and its respected people are the ones who keep both.

The **framewrights** tend the condenser-fields: re-weaving torn mesh, re-tensioning frames after wind, clearing the silver webs of the mist-spiders, which colonize the mesh and which the framewrights remove with a particular ambivalence, since a webbed frame combs the cloud *better* for a few days and then fouls. Framewrights know their ranks of frames individually, by sound — each frame sings its own note in the cloud-wall, and a wright walking the windward swells in heavy mist navigates by ear, frame to frame, the way a blind person crosses a familiar room.

The **cistern-keepers** hold the most quietly powerful office in town. They decide, hour by hour, how the stored water is spent — how much to the wheels, how much to the dynamo, how much held back against a dry spell — and since light in Vell is weather stored, the cistern-keepers are, in effect, the stewards of brightness. A good keeper is one nobody thinks about. A bad keeper is remembered for generations, by name, in the tone other towns reserve for failed kings.

The **wake-readers** are the shepherds. There are sheep on the Tableland — trough-sheep, slate-grey, knee-high, long-bodied and broad as footstools, who graze not on top of the pile but *through* it, swimming the moss the way the hares do, surfacing to breathe air and dive again. A flock at pasture is invisible: what you see is a slow braid of wakes crossing a swell, and the wake-reader's whole craft is reading those lines — counting them, knowing whose is whose, telling a ewe's wake from a hare's at half a mile by the width of the tremble. The sheep are sheared in the dry spells, when their fleeces tighten and pale along with everything else, and their wool drives the mills and clothes the five towns in garments the exact grey-green of the land, so that the Vellish, like their town, are nearly invisible from above.

And in the deep galleries, the **wheel-tenders** and **moss-pressers** do the slow indoor work — pressing cut moss into the dense dark bricks that heat the town, packing roofs, oiling the line-shafts — work that thuds and breathes on the cloud's schedule, busiest when the lamps are brightest, which is to say when the town cannot be seen at all.

**Manners, and the pile**

The Vellish do not walk on the pile. This is presented to visitors as practicality — the pile can take a leg to the thigh, and getting out is wet, cold, and slow — but practicality does not explain the *quality* of the avoidance, which resembles nothing so much as courtesy toward a sleeping person.

There are sanctioned reasons to go in: rescue, shearing, and burial. Anyone who goes in for any other reason — a fall, a shortcut, a drunken bet — incurs what is called, without humor, a *pile-debt*: the crushed track you leave is yours, and you are expected to tend it through the seasons until the moss closes it, visiting it, keeping the wind off it, the way one tends a wound one has caused. Children acquire pile-debts the way lowland children acquire scraped knees, and the tending of them is, deliberately, most of their moral education. By adulthood a Vellish person has learned in the body what the town believes in the abstract: that the green is patient, that damage heals but is not free, and that the proper relationship to a living thing one stands above is *care taken*.

The dead go into the green. There are no markers and no graveyard; a body is carried out during a cloud-passage, laid into an opened seam of the pile on the eastern swells, and the moss closes over it within a season. What the Vellish do instead of marking graves is this: they speak the names of their dead into the cloud-wall as it comes through. The mesh combs the cloud; the water runs to the cisterns; the cisterns drive the wheels; the wheels make the light. Ask a Vellish person whether they believe the lamps therefore burn with their ancestors and they will say, in the flat mild tone they use for the weather, that it is *a description*. The amber light in every room of the town is the rain that never fell, holding the names that were said into it. Nobody insists on this. Nobody needs to.

**The other residents**

The animals of the Tableland are few enough to be known personally, and they are.

The **gleaners** — the long dark birds that ride the cloud-wall in and work the condenser mesh — number around forty, and the dynamo hall keeps a ledger of them going back nine generations of birds: names, lineages, markings, the dates each was first and last seen. The truce between gleaners and works is absolute and entirely one-sided in its enforcement: no wheel starts while a gleaner is on the frames, and a framewright who can report that a particular bird landed on her shoulder will be bought drinks on the strength of it for a month. When a gleaner stops appearing, its name is read into the cloud like a person's.

The **pile-hares** are weather. A wake crossing a swell toward town means long cloud coming, the old people say, and they are right often enough that nobody has examined the matter further. The hares are never hunted. It is not forbidden; it has simply never come up.

In the pile itself lives a whole pale nation that the Vellish know mostly by sound and consequence: blind crickets the color of candle wax, slow beetles, the mist-spiders, threadworms that the moss-pressers sort out of the cut moss and carry back outside in cupped hands, because — and they will say this with a straight face — the worms have work to do.

And there is the other one.

The Vellish do not name it. This is not squeamishness; naming, in Vell, is a form of attention, and attention is a form of investigation, and the town decided long ago — collectively, gradually, the way it decides everything — to extend to this resident the same courtesy it extends to the pile: *care taken, no further*. When they must refer to it at all they say *the one still settling*, or they simply tilt the head toward the western swells, and everyone understands.

What is known is little and stable. The channels it presses into the pile overnight are long, shallow, and warm for an hour after dawn — the framewrights checking the western ranks have laid hands flat in them, just once each, the way you might touch a wall to feel a furnace behind it. It has never harmed anything. When a channel crosses a rank of condensers, the frames in its path are found not broken but *laid down* — eased flat, gently, every one — and the framewrights stand them back up without complaint and without discussion, the way you would replace something a houseguest had moved. It lays things flat. Of course it does. Everything here lays things flat eventually; this one is simply still at it.

That is the town's whole theology of the matter, on the rare occasions anyone states it: that the rock is finished and the pile is finished and the people are nearly finished, and the one still settling is the last thing on the Tableland that has not yet found its final shape — and that a place which took this long to come to rest can extend, to the last of its residents still in motion, the privacy in which to arrive.

**Those who leave, and those who come**

Vellish who go down to the lowlands suffer for it. Not in the lungs — those manage — but in some less locatable organ. They describe the lowlands as *loud*, which the lowlands are, and *unfinished*, which is harder to answer; they flinch at peaks on the horizon as at an argument nobody is going to win. Most come back. The ones who stay below are spoken of gently, as people still looking for something, which from Vell is not a compliment.

The travelers who come up the long plank-road and *stay* — there are a few in every generation — are recognizable on arrival, the Vellish claim, before they have said a word. They walk a certain way. They have stopped being surprised by things and have not yet learned what to do instead. Vell takes them in without ceremony, gives them mesh to mend, and waits. It usually works. The town has watched its own rock finish settling; it can wait out a person.

**Their last shape**

So: who lives on the Tableland of Vell?

A people who took a finished landscape as instruction. They have worn their language smooth and their tools smoother; they pass down hand-planes and shears and lamps not despite the wear but *for* it, prizing the object that has reached the form its use was always grinding it toward. They say of a marriage that has lasted, of a craftsman at the height of her work, of an old wheel that runs true after a century, that it is *settled* — and there is no higher word in the dialect. Their old people move downward through the town as they age, terrace by terrace, into the deep warm galleries by the wheels where the rock's last settling can be heard through the walls, and they listen to it without fear, as one might listen to a great companion finishing a long task. At the end they ask to be carried up and out, into the green, during a cloud.

They are not resigned. This is the misreading every visitor makes first and abandons last. The Vellish are busy, attached, gossipy in their close quiet way, fierce about their gleaners and their cisterns and their children's first solo crossings of the swells. They have simply stopped confusing motion with unfinishedness. The land beneath them spent an unimaginable age becoming exactly what it is and then *kept living* — and so they grow flat and dense and ferociously green within their stratum, and breathe with the cloud, and tend what passes through rather than digging for what lies beneath, and become, each of them, over a long slow lamplit life, more themselves and nothing taller.

They live quietly in their last shape. They are still alive in it. Up there, in the amber light, those two facts have never once been in tension.
