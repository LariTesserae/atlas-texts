**The Keepers of the Cistern Town**

Begin with what you would expect, because the town itself invites the expectation: small people. Descendants of the builders. The waist-high doorways, the child-width stairs, the thumb-prints pressed into wet clay a thousand years ago and never smoothed away — everything says *someone lives here who comes up to your hip, someone with clever hands, someone hiding*. And this is true, as far as it goes. But it goes further than that, and the further it goes the less it resembles hiding.

They are small, yes. Standing straight, the tallest of them would look up at your belt. Their proportions are not a child's, though; a child is unfinished and they are the opposite of unfinished. They are compact the way a river-stone is compact — everything unnecessary already worn off. Their skin is the grey-brown of the town's own clay, and in the dusk-colored light it has the same faint dampness, so that when one of them stands still against a wall (and they can stand very still, longer than you can watch) the eye slides past. This is not camouflage exactly. It is closer to citizenship. They are made of what the town is made of, and it is an honest question, which you will come to, whether the town was built from the mountain or they were.

Their eyes are large, dark, and lidless-looking — the lids are there but so thin and slow you rarely catch them closing — adapted over unknowable generations to a light that never rises above amber and never falls quite to black. They do not need the blade of noon-light that crosses the terraces; when it touches them they turn slightly away from it, politely, the way you might turn from a lamp held too close to your face. Their hands are the loudest thing about them, if a body can be said to have volume: broad-palmed, long-thumbed, the fingertips flattened and smooth from a lifetime of pressing clay. When you see the palm-print patches in the town's walls, you are looking at signatures — but also, one begins to suspect, at portraits. Each patch is the exact hand of the one who made it, and the walls are therefore a record of everyone who has ever lived here, layered, overlapping, holding the town up.

They are almost silent, and this is the first discipline of their lives. Not muteness — discipline. Sound, in a chamber twenty paces across where a single falling drop is an event, takes up room, and the first law of the cistern town governs more than architecture. A footstep takes up room. A cough takes up room. Being *seen* takes up room — this is the heart of it, the thing that explains the smothered fires and the doorways that are always just empty. To be witnessed is to occupy space in another creature's attention, and they have extended the law against occupation all the way down into themselves. They are not afraid of you. They watched you descend the softened handholds with something like professional interest, noting whether your boots scuffed the moss (which grows only in the seams, because they cut it back from everywhere else, one fingernail-width at a time). They simply decline to be a thing you carry away. When you climbed toward the glowing doorway in the high tier, the one inside did not flee. She put the fire out with a wet palm and sat down in the dark an arm's length from where you eventually stood, breathing so slowly that the smoke settled around her like a shawl, and when you left she relit the ember from the coal she keeps in a clay cup, and went back to her work.

The fire, singular: there is one. It has been one fire for longer than the copper hooks have been green. It is never allowed to grow larger than a cupped handful and it is never allowed to die, and it travels. This is the office of the fire-carrier, who moves it from hearth to hearth through the tiers on a circuit so long and so slow that by the time the flame returns to a given house, the child who watched it leave has pressed her own palm-patch into a wall somewhere. Every hearth in the town is warm in its turn. No hearth is warm always. The glow you saw was the fire mid-journey, and the fire-carrier is the closest thing they have to a priest, though what she serves is not the flame. It is the turn-taking.

They speak. Not often to each other in the open air — but the town is veined with a hundred water-channels, and the channels pass through the hearts of the houses, and the carved spouts shaped like open mouths are exactly what they appear to be. A keeper wishing to speak kneels at the channel where it enters her floor and shapes the water with her fingers — dams it, releases it, splits it, lets it fall from different heights — and three tiers down and across the chamber, another keeper sits with her ear to a spout and listens to the trickle change. It is a slow language. A sentence can take the better part of an evening. It is very difficult to argue in, which they consider one of its virtues, and it has the property that everything said in it is eventually said by the pool at the bottom, where all the channels arrive, so that the pool is full of everything anyone has ever said, greatly diluted. They believe the white salamander hanging motionless in the warm water, fingers spread, is listening to all of it at once. They do not disturb the salamander. They regard it, without ceremony, as the town's memory doing its work, and when a keeper wants to be sure something is never forgotten, she goes down to the lowest tier at evening and says it directly into the pool.

How many of them there are cannot be answered, and not only because they refuse to be counted. They refuse to count *themselves*. A census, they hold, is a way of taking up room in advance — a claim staked on the future — and so no one knows, and the not-knowing is maintained with care. A visitor's honest guess would run anywhere from nine to nine hundred. There are always more tiers than there seem to be; there may always be more keepers than there seem to be; it is possible that most of the town stands empty and has for centuries, kept in working order by a remnant, and it is equally possible that every waist-high doorway you passed had someone seated just inside it, hands folded, waiting with the specific patience of people whose ancestors watched a mountain wear flat.

They are born rarely, and the manner of it is the one thing about them that resists even careful watching, so take this as the town tells it in trickles: a new keeper is *made*, the way the town was made. Clay from the walls — always a little from many walls, never much from one — worked wet with water from the warm pool, shaped small, by many hands, over a long time. And then the shaped thing is set at the base of the pale tree in the plaza, among the offering cups, and left there through some number of turnings of the light, and at some point it is no longer clay. Whether the tree does this, or the water, or the accumulated patience of the chamber, they do not say and may not know. What is certain is that the cups of water at the tree's roots are not worship. They are wages, or gratitude, or simply the return of a loan — every keeper is mostly water, and the water was the pool's before it was theirs, and the tree's roots go down into the pool, and so the cups are how they hand themselves back a sip at a time. The tree, whose bark is translucent like their fingernails and like the snails they will not step on, is either their first ancestor or their first child. They decline to resolve this, on the grounds that a settled answer would take up room.

And they die the way the town's day ends. A keeper who is finished — and they know when they are finished, the way the light knows when to leave the terraces — goes down at evening to the warm pool and walks in, and the pool takes back the water, and the clay settles, and by morning there is a little more mist rising to the ceiling than there was. The mist thickens against the cool stone; the stone sweats; the sweat falls. Every drop that strikes the pool in that irregular, unhurried rhythm — each one distinct, you noticed — is understood by the keepers to be someone, coming home again, keeping the time. This is why they clear the gutters before all other work. The channels are not plumbing. They are how the dead get where they are going.

They want nothing from you. They wanted nothing from anyone who came before you, and their needs are so nearly closed a circle — warmth from below, water from everywhere, clay from the walls, the one traveling fire — that the outside world reaches them only as the daily dark bird, which drinks at the plaza and leaves and does not sing, and which they read the way you might read a newspaper: by the dust on its feet. If it ever failed to come they would know something had happened up in the bright thin air, and they would note it, and go on.

But they are not indifferent to you. Understand that. Somewhere in your climb out — you will not be able to say where — the cup of water you did not know you needed was set on a ledge exactly at the height of your hand, full, cold going warm, in a vessel the size of your two cupped fingers. That was not an oversight in their discipline. That was the discipline. To be seen takes up room; to give does not. You drank it or you didn't. Either way, far below you, someone small pressed a wet palm flat against a wall, adding herself to the record, and the drop fell, and another, and the town kept its time.
