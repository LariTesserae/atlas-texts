The plateau's people call themselves nothing special — *pithfolk* if pressed, *uplanders* to lowland traders, mostly just the names of their districts — but you can identify them anywhere in the world by a single tell: they pause. Mid-sentence, mid-gesture, mid-pour, a stillness of a second or two that they themselves do not notice, falling at regular intervals, roughly forty seconds apart. They are waiting for the beat. They have stopped hearing the pulse the way you stop hearing your own blood, but their bodies have not stopped *keeping* it, and a generation born and raised on the plateau speaks in its meter — sentences shaped to fit between surges, arguments that build across three or four beats and land their point on the boom. Lowlanders find conversation with them maddening, all those small unexplained silences, like talking to people who are forever listening to something through a wall. They are. Down on the coast, pithfolk are said to be slow. They are not slow. They are *entrained*. Take one off the plateau and they sleep badly for months, jolting awake every forty seconds into the unbearable absence of the beat, and the homesickness they describe is not for a place but for a tempo.

They are pale in the way of people who live under permanent cloud, and there is something faintly green-grey in the pallor — not sickness but diet. They drink the well-water; everyone does; it is sweet and there is nothing else. The luminosity accumulates. In full dark, an old tapper's veins show at the wrist like the seams in the ground show after rain, a dim branching glow, and at funerals — where it is customary to drink deep from the well nearest the dead — the whole gathered family will shine softly for a night, standing on the flats in the dark, lit from inside by the same blood that lights the streets. They consider this beautiful and do not understand why it unsettles visitors. *Everything here runs on the same lamp oil*, an old distiller will tell you, tapping her own forearm, then the ground, then the bulb breathing above her door. *We're just the smallest fixtures.*

Their trades are the plateau's trades, and most are inherited, because the skills take longer than an apprenticeship — they take a *childhood*, a body trained early. Pistonwrights work the works-houses, and their craft is essentially obstetric: you do not repair a pulse-engine so much as keep it from distressing the vessel it rides, and a good wright can lay a palm on the housing and feel, in the quality of the surge, whether the plateau is tolerating the machine or beginning to heal around it, in which case the works-house has perhaps a decade before it is enclosed like a splinter. Well-priests are less clergy than diplomats; their theology is procedural. They perform the *petitions* — the slow formal introductions by which a new doorway, a new tap, a new grave is proposed to the ground. A priest will sit beside a fresh cut for days, sometimes weeks, doing nothing a stranger can see, and what they are doing, they say, is keeping the wound company until the plateau decides the wound was its own idea. Cuts made without a priest heal wrong — too fast, or into shapes nobody asked for, or, in the worst telling, into doors that open somewhere else.

Then there are the walkers. Every district keeps a few: the people who can cross the misbehaving flats in fog. Their secret is no secret; they will explain it to anyone. Distance lies out there, and daylight lies, and your own footprints lie, but the pulse does not — the heartbeat is the same beat everywhere on the plateau, simultaneous across a hundred kilometers, the one honest yardstick in the country. A walker crosses the flats counting beats the way a sailor counts soundings, and when the count and the landscape disagree, the walker believes the count. They are strange people, as you'd expect of anyone whose profession is disbelieving their own eyes for a living: flat-voiced, hard to surprise, prone to checking a clock after telling you good morning. The oldest of them have count-errors banked in their bodies — an afternoon missing here, a doubled hour there — and they speak of their own pasts with a careful, auditing tone, as people who know their memories were assembled from parts.

The bell-wardens are nearly a priesthood themselves. Their whole charge is the offset — keeping the living towns' bells out of phase with the bells of Old Tapping, so that no one walking home in fog follows the wrong shift-change into the dreamed city. They keep the ledgers of which bells are real, which is a stranger job than it sounds, because Old Tapping *adjusts*. Shift its bells eleven minutes and within a season the false quarter has drifted to match, patiently, the way a sleeper rolls toward warmth. The wardens speak of it without rancor. It wants company, they say. It was grown to be full of people and it is doing its best.

The cats deserve their own census. Brought up from the lowlands generations back to do a job that did not exist — there have never been mice — they stayed, and the plateau has had time to work on them. They are large now, deep-chested, moss-grey or milk-jade-eyed, and very slow, and they sleep by preference on the warm ground directly over the great vessels, where careful observers have confirmed what everyone already knew: their breathing runs forty seconds to the cycle. They are the plateau's only success at acquiring fauna by ordinary means, and the towns treat them as instruments. A works-house cat will not look at certain things — will groom, elaborately, facing precisely away from a particular doorway, a particular stretch of flat, a particular guest — and the locals read these refusals the way sailors read barometers. Districts keep maps of cat-blindness, updated seasonally. Where the blind spots cluster and thicken, a warden is sent. The cats are paid in fish hauled up the switchback roads at genuine expense, and are considered, in a quiet way, civic employees.

What else lives here is harder to certify. The pale, long-bodied thing that drinks at the pools at dusk has never been caught, tracked, or seen to leave footprints, and the prevailing opinion — held loosely, like all opinion up here — is that it is not a resident but a *memory* of one: the plateau dreaming its last animal, some pollinator or symbiont dead a hundred thousand years, rehearsed nightly at a different well like a name the sleeper keeps almost recalling. The alternative theory is gentler and stranger: that it is the plateau itself, putting a mouth to its own water — the body, in its long sleep, checking that it can still drink. The two theories are not considered to conflict. Children leave it offerings anyway, small twists of mill-fiber by the pools, and the offerings are never taken, and the children keep leaving them, on the principle that politeness costs nothing and you are, after all, a guest in someone's dream.

And there are the settled dead, who must be counted among the living arrangements if not the living. The plateau does not give bodies back. Graves are cut with petition, the dead laid in, and the cut heals — in a generation the grave is a smooth low swell of milky jade, and through it, faintly, on bright days, you can see them: the grandmother in her good coat, suspended in green translucence like a fly in amber, looking very much as if she has merely paused mid-sentence to wait for the beat. Cemeteries here are not visited so much as *consulted*. People walk out to the family swells and talk, and report, without drama, that it helps, and a few of them report more than that — a word arriving in fog, an afternoon at Old Tapping where a familiar figure stood at a window of a building that has no inside. The wardens log these claims alongside the cat-maps. Some of the lost-in-fog turn up in the false quarter years later, visible in the depth of a wall, unaged, furnished around with chairs and lamps the plateau imagined for them, and their families make the two-hour walk between districts to sit outside the wall and ring a small handbell, offset, so the lost one knows which city is calling.

Which brings the census to its largest entry, the one the pithfolk put first when they bother to think of it at all: the plateau. It is the principal resident; everything else is staff. It outlived its world, ate its mountain, lost its animals, finished growing, and then — alone, enormous, horizontal — was found by people. The well-priests will tell you, in their flat practical way, that this is the whole shape of the arrangement: a thing that spent eternity dreaming of fauna woke one morning, insofar as it wakes, to find fauna had arrived on its own — small, warm, busy in its bark, tapping its pulse, drinking its blood, ringing bells, raising children who breathe in its meter and glow faintly with its light. The towns think they live *on* the plateau. The priests suspect the preposition is wrong. They live *in* it, the way thoughts live in a head — its adopted ecology, its late wildlife, possibly its dream, and possibly the reason the dreaming has grown so populous lately, so full of lit windows and shift-bells and quarters built for nobody. The plateau is old, and it was lonely for longer than there have been people, and it is not lonely now. The heartbeat is steady. Forty seconds to the beat, the lamps breathe, the cats breathe, the sleepers breathe, the settled dead wait in their green glass — all of it one household, pulsing together, at the top of the world.
