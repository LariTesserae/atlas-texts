**Who Lives on the Green Table**

Begin with the obvious answer, the one so large it is easy to miss: the table itself.

The cushion is not the habitat. The cushion is the resident — the first, the largest, the one all the others live *in* rather than alongside. Whether it is a single organism or ten thousand ancient individuals fused past the point of meaningful separation is a question with no answer and possibly no meaning; the seams where lobes met centuries ago have grown through each other, swapped fluids, settled disputes. It does not matter where one ends. Nothing has ended.

It is the oldest living thing anyone knows of, and it behaves like it. It does not compete, because there is nothing left to compete with. It does not grow outward, because it long ago reached every edge there is. What it does instead is *maintain* — heal, drink, warm, digest its own deep dead layers and raise new green over them — with the unhurried thoroughness of something whose plans are measured in geology. It ate the mountain. It is in no rush about anything else. The mist it exhales at dawn, the blood-warmth in its hollows, the slow inward swallowing of its own dew: these are not features of a landscape. They are habits of a body. Everything else that lives on the plateau lives the way mites live on skin, or thoughts in a head — inside the life of something incomparably larger, which tolerates them, uses them, and will outlast every lineage of them without noticing the transition.

---

**The drummers** are the table's blood, or its nerves, or both; the distinction is hard to hold onto once you have seen them.

They are insects, broadly speaking — round-bodied, flightless, eyeless or nearly so, soft and pale in the deep galleries, harder and darker near the surface where light leaks through the green roof in dim emerald grades. They never see one another in any way that matters. They *hear* one another, constantly, totally. A drummer's entire world is percussion: it strikes the resonant gallery wall with a hardened plate on its forelimbs, and the wall carries the strike, and every wall connects to every other wall, so that each drummer lives at the center of a sphere of information kilometers wide. They navigate by it — the returning shape of their own strikes tells them where the galleries bend, where a wall has thinned, where a pool has flooded a chamber. They court by it, in long antiphonal duets that travel through the floor between two animals that will meet exactly once. They hold territory by it. They mourn by it, if the listeners are right about the slow trailing rhythms that follow a local death.

And they remember by it. This is the thing the people in the pits insist on, and it is hard, after a night down there, to disbelieve: the rhythms are *inherited*. A gallery lineage drums patterns its ancestors drummed, modified strike by strike across generations, so that the great roar of the plateau is not noise but an unthinkably dense archive — millions of family histories being performed simultaneously, each one older than any music ever made by hands. Somewhere in the bass of the world underfoot, the listeners say, there are rhythms that have been passed down unbroken since before the lowland rivers found their courses.

The drummers are not parasites. They eat only the dead tissue, the deep brown layers the cushion has already abandoned, and in chewing it open they ventilate it; the warmth of the plateau is a joint production, plant-rot and insect-muscle together, a metabolism with two kinds of body in it. When a drummer dies, the cushion grows through it within days and takes it back. There is no waste up here. There is no *outside* for waste to go to.

---

**The birds** are the only inhabitants who leave, and they only leave straight down.

They are rim-creatures absolutely. Chisel-billed, short-necked, strong on the wing, colored the grey-and-rose of the banded rock as if in apology for nesting in something so green. Their bored tunnels riddle the cushion's flanks in colonies generations deep — a nest hole may be older than the bird's great-great-grandmother, passed down, re-excavated each year as the plant tries patiently to close it. The chicks are raised in blood-warmth; the parents barely brood, because the cushion incubates for them. The plant, with no opinion anyone can detect, keeps its own predators' children alive through the cold of the high nights.

They hammer the surface for drummers, and the drummers reroute, and the wounds heal pale, and this is less a war than a circulation — the birds cull the shallow galleries, the deep galleries stay sound, the scars become the elephant-hide history of the skin. But note what the birds will not do: they will not cross the open middle. They pour off the rim in their weather-making flocks and feed within sight of the edge, always, and a flock that drifts inward turns back as one animal, for no reason the eye can find. The open table is empty partly because the birds keep it that way by absence. The listeners say the birds know what the middle belongs to.

---

**Which brings us to the hush.**

It is known almost entirely by negatives — the silence it pushes ahead of itself, the absence of any description of its body, the furrows that point at nothing. But the listeners, who have spent the most nights closest to it, have assembled a portrait of sorts out of those negatives, and it is stranger than a monster would be.

First: the drummers do not flee it. The silence that moves with it is not panic. Drummers in panic drum *harder*. This silence is attentive — a field-sized patch of millions of animals stopping, together, to listen — because the hush, whatever it is, sounds below their range, and below the listeners' range too, a pressure more than a tone, and everything that lives by sound goes still to take it in.

Second: the furrows are not random. Map them across years — and the listeners do, on charts of stretched bird-skin tapped full of pinholes — and they trace the plateau's old troubles. Where galleries flooded, where rot set in too deep for the drummers, where a pool turned foul: a pale road runs through it, a season or a decade later, and afterward the trouble is gone. The hush goes where the body is sick.

Third — and this is the part the listeners say quietly, in the tapped finger-language they use in the pits where voices feel wrong — the cushion *flowers*. Not on the surface; nothing of the cushion's business happens on the surface. It blooms inward, rarely, in deep vaulted chambers, blossoms that no light has ever touched and no bird or person has ever seen. And the furrows, the old ones, the ones that wander the plateau pointing at nothing? Plot them against the listeners' oldest charts and they point at where the bloom-chambers were.

So the standing dispute among the people who know the table best is not *what kind of animal is it* but *whether it is a separate thing at all*. One school holds it is the last of an old fauna, a great blind tunneler that struck a bargain with the plant so long ago that the bargain has become anatomy: it tends the body, it alone attends the flowers, it carries whatever flowers carry from chamber to chamber across the green country. The other school holds there is no bargain because there are not two parties — that the hush is the cushion's own organ, a wandering piece of itself, the one part of the oldest living thing that still *goes places*. Nobody has ever proposed a way to settle the question. Most of the listeners, pressed, will tap the sign that means *both*, and then the sign that means *enough*.

---

**The people** are the only inhabitants who do not, strictly, inhabit. The table forbids it — not with hostility but with appetite. Build a shelter and the cushion grows over it; cache food and the drummers hollow it; the plateau erases permanence the way skin sheds a splinter. So the people who climb the braided ladders out of the cloud-sea come as visitors by necessity and have made a vocation of it.

They are lowlanders, from the country under the white sea, and they come up for the sound. The plateau sits above the weather but it *hears* the weather — the drumming shifts before rain that hasn't reached the lowlands yet, goes ragged ahead of storms still days out over land the people farm. A listener who has sat three nights in a pit comes down the ladders carrying a forecast worth a season's harvest. That is the practical reason, the one they give.

The other reason is the dawn pulse. Every listener is, beneath the trade, a pilgrim for it: the hours before light when the millions drift into synchrony and the whole table beats as one. They sit in the closing green pits with the rhythm consolidating around them on every side, and they report — consistently, across generations — that your own heart tries to join. It can't, quite; you are too small and too quick. But it tries, all night, and the old listeners, the ones who have sat through hundreds of dawns, are known in the lowlands by their slow pulses and their unhurried way of standing in doorways. *Table-timed*, people say of them. They are easy to be around and hard to surprise.

The listeners keep four rules, old enough that no one frames them as rules anymore: take nothing green; leave nothing dead; never sleep on the open surface; never follow a pale road. There is one exception to the second rule, granted rarely, as the highest honor the lowlands know. A listener who has earned it is carried up the ladders a last time and laid on the open table at dusk, and the others go down without them. The cushion roofs them over within days, the way it roofs everything. The drumming around the place, the charts record, goes briefly into the slow trailing rhythm. And more than once — often enough that it is no longer called coincidence — a furrow has been found, the following season, passing exactly there: the hush, on its rounds, pausing over the newest chamber in the oldest body in the world, before moving on across the loud green country to whatever, in the dark, it is tending next.
