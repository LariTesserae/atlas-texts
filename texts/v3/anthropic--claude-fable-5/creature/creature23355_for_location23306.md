What lives on the Plateau of Standing Engines lives the way the warmth does: without source, as a property. You will not find metabolism here in any form you would recognize from worlds with daylight. Nothing eats matter. Nothing eats light — there is not enough of it to graze. The residents of the plateau live on *state*. They are creatures of the held condition, and each of them is, in its own way, a small organ of the great stillness, doing some narrow part of the work of remaining.

Begin with the ones you can see, because there are so few of those.

**The heat-moths** are the plateau's visible fauna, and the more you learn of them the less the word *fauna* holds. They are pale — not white, which would imply they reflect something, but pale the way the inside of an eyelid is pale, a color made of nearness to warmth. Soft-bodied, hand-sized, flat as if pressed. They cling to the conduits and drink the half-degree, and that half-degree is their whole biology: they live in the difference between the conduit and the stone, in a margin thinner than a breath. They are not warm-blooded or cold-blooded. They are *exactly*-blooded — their bodies the precise temperature of the metal beneath them, always, so that a thermal eye would not see them at all. They do not register. That is not camouflage. It is citizenship.

No one has seen a heat-moth born, and the moths offer no theory of themselves. But there is a regularity: new moths appear only where two conduits cross, at the junctions where two half-degrees overlap and the warmth briefly doubles. The honest guess is that a moth is not bred but *condensed* — that where the equilibrium is richest, it thickens, and a moth is what equilibrium looks like when there is enough of it in one place to want a body. They would then be the city's surplus. Its modest abundance. The one luxury a finished machine allows itself.

Their turning is the thing to understand. A heat-moth turns — over hours, with the certainty of a needle finding north — to face the most recent change in its vicinity. Your passing is a change. So the moth turns, long after you are gone, and faces the direction you went, and then it stays that way, perhaps for centuries, because nothing else happens to overwrite you. Walk the plateau attentively and you will realize what this means: the moths are a record. Every moth on every conduit is aimed at the last event it witnessed, and the field of them, taken together, is a ledger of every disturbance the plateau has ever tolerated. Somewhere out there are moths still facing the direction the builders left. The city keeps no archives, writes nothing down. It does not need to. Its memory grazes on its pipes, one body-length a day, and never blinks, having nothing to blink with.

**The light is one resident, not many.** You will have noticed that every shadow on the plateau falls the same way, no matter which gauge casts it. This is because there is exactly one light here, and it lives behind all the glass at once. Call it the Banked One, or do not call it anything — it has been steady so long that names slide off it; names are for things that might be confused with other things. It is the dry near-orange glow, parceled out among hundreds of apertures across kilometers of dark, and it occupies a single position that is not on the plateau, perhaps not in any direction the plateau has. The shadow-stains in the glass — those faint prints of its own steadiness — are the closest it comes to leaving tracks. It does not flicker because flickering is a kind of breathing, and it finished breathing long ago. Whether it is a flame that was banked, or a creature that retired into the office of being light, cannot be told and may not be a real distinction. It watches nothing, illuminates almost nothing, and yet every shadow on the plateau is its signature, written in the same hand at every gauge. If the plateau has an eye, it is this — an eye that has settled, like everything else, into a final and perfect expression, and holds it.

**Inside the engines, there are wardens.** This is the most careful way to say it. Each engine that ticks has, somewhere in its interior — among the plaza-wide flywheels, in the throats of the cold chimneys — a single resident, and the tick is that resident. Not a sound it makes: a sound it *is*. The wardens are vast and slow past the point where slowness reads as life; their natural speed is the speed of geology, and at that speed a heartbeat hours apart is a racing pulse, an athlete's pulse, the pulse of something deeply engaged in its work. They may be the size of the chambers they occupy. They may *be* the chambers, the way a snail is and is not its shell — long since settled into the mechanism, fused at the edges like the cooling vanes, so that warden and engine share surfaces and there is no seam where one ends. Each tick is a confirmation: the pressure is level, the flow is held, the value stands. One tick, then hours of nothing, because at equilibrium there is nothing further to confirm — and then, because diligence is the whole of their nature, they confirm it again. They are not asleep. Sleep is for creatures with a faster state to rest from. The wardens are *awake at their own speed*, which is a speed you can only witness in cross-section, one tick at a time, the way you witness a star's life by seeing it once.

**The clearances are inhabited too.** This is harder to say plainly, because the resident of the gaps has never been seen and offers only its bookkeeping as evidence. The distances between engines — always the same number of paces, never the number you counted last time — are not malfunctioning. They are *breathing*. Something lives in the great spaces of the design, in the clearance left for the motion that happens once an age, and the discrepancy in your counts is the slow swell and ebb of its breath, a flexing of the plateau's grammar measured in single paces per traverse. It keeps the measurements precise and private because they are its body, and it is not in the habit of being touched. Whether the once-an-age motion is its turning over, or its single step, or the one thing it was built to do, no walker has been present for it, and the moths that might remember are all, presumably, facing it still — somewhere, in some quarter of the dark, a whole population of pale hands aimed at a spot where something immense once moved, and has not moved since.

**In the hall of dials, there is a custodian.** It is the only thing on the plateau shaped approximately like a person, and the approximation is courteous rather than convincing — a tall, dark figure of the same soapy basalt-warmth as the floor, standing before one of the hundreds of dials with the attitude of someone reading it. It is on its rounds. It checks each needle in turn, confirms the value, and moves to the next. One circuit of the hall takes longer than you have a word for; its progress between dials cannot be distinguished from standing still, in exactly the way the flywheels cannot be distinguished from stopped. But come back — if you could come back across the kind of interval the plateau deals in — and it would be at the next dial. It has never found a needle out of place. It will never find a needle out of place; the entire city upstream of the hall exists to make its inspection eternally redundant. It inspects anyway. Possibly the inspection is the final safeguard: the value is held partly *because something checks*. Possibly the custodian is the last vanity of the builders, a figure left behind in their own shape to enjoy, at one dial per age, the sight of the thing working. It does not acknowledge you. You are not a dial.

**And the builders.** Here is the deepest guess, and it is only a guess, but the plateau itself supplies it. Kneel anywhere and press your palm to the stone and feel the hum — the memory of vibration, held in the rock the way amber holds an insect. Consider that amber does not hold the memory of an insect. It holds the insect. The builders are not gone and not dead and not departed in any direction the moths could face. They achieved their purpose, and their purpose was a state, and so they did the only thing wholly consistent with their work: they entered it. They went down into the rock the way a sound goes into a struck bell and stays, descending past vibration into the memory of vibration, and they are there now, kilometers deep, humming at the bottom of the quiet — the foundation note above which the ticks and the silence are arranged. They are the least active residents of the plateau and the most present. Everything else here lives *on* the equilibrium. They live *as* it.

Which leaves you.

You are within tolerances, the place has decided, and tolerance, here, is the whole of hospitality — but understand what the tolerance is measuring. Stay long enough and you will notice your own changes: you check your pulse less. You stop counting paces, having accepted that the counting is not yours to verify. You walk a conduit for a day and realize you have not thought a new thought, only held an old one, turning it the way a warden turns a confirmation, and that the holding was not boredom but something nearer to keeping. Somewhere out on the long stone, a heat-moth has finished turning toward you. Then another. Then, slowly, a line of them, marking your route like lamps that face instead of shine. The plateau is taking your measurement, precisely and privately. It is deciding what kind of resident you would condense into, if you stayed past the point where staying and living cannot be told apart.

Nothing here will ask you to. Nothing here asks. But the door, such as it is, stands open in the only direction the plateau has — inward, downward, toward the hum — and the residents, every one of them, from the moths to the buried builders, are the record of those who, at some unrecoverable point, said yes.
