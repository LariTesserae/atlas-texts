# The Vell

A visitor's first sighting of them is usually at a distance, and usually mistaken. A figure standing motionless on an open pavement, grey-clad against grey stone, reads as a cairn until it moves — and it moves so little, and so seldom, that the mistake can last an hour. The people of Vell do not fidget. They have nowhere they are about to be. When one of them stands at the edge of a terrace watching the clouds come in, they are not waiting for the clouds. They are doing the thing entirely: standing, watching. The cloud's arrival is not the point. There is no point. There is the standing.

They are not tall. They are broad through the shoulders and hands in the way of people who have ground stone against stone for their whole lives — their fingertips are wide and smooth as river pebbles, almost printless, and their grip, when they take your arm to steady you on a wet causeway, is startlingly gentle, the way very strong things are gentle. Their hair runs the colors of their camelids, cream through slate, and goes white early and evenly, so that age is hard to read in them; a face you take for forty may belong to a person who remembers the flood-marks being cut. Their eyes are pale, and they hold eye contact comfortably and for a long time, without challenge in it. They blink slowly. Visitors report that being looked at by a Vell is like being looked at by a lake.

They dress in felted wool and twisted fiber, undyed — slate, cream, fog — with deep hoods against the cloud-crossings, and they go barefoot or in plaited sandals whose soles they replace, by long custom, on the same day each year, whether the soles are worn or not. The soles are never worn.

---

Their voices, when they use them, are pitched just above a whisper, and their language is built to be spoken that way: full of unvoiced consonants, long damp vowels, sounds that don't carry. It is a language with a great many words for kinds of stillness — the stillness of cistern water, the stillness of stone under moss, the stillness of a dove that has noticed you, the stillness inside a house at noon — and almost no grammatical machinery for change. There is no progressive tense. A thing is not *becoming*; a thing *is*, and then, if you check again, it *is* in a slightly different way, and that is a new sentence. Their verbs do not bend toward the future, because, as everyone knows, the future is just the far side of a terrace, and you would no more conjugate toward it than toward the granaries.

Each of them is named for a feature of the plateau — a particular stone, a pool, a notch in the rim, a bend in a braided channel. The naming is exact: not "Stone" but *that* stone, the flat one with the lichen ring, on the third terrace east of the long stair. A child is carried to their namesake within a season of birth and shown it, formally, the way you would introduce two people who will know each other a long time. Throughout their lives they visit it. They sit with it. When they die, the people say the name *goes back* — returns to the feature, unworn, ready — and indeed, a generation or two later, a child somewhere on the plateau will carry it again, and no one finds this remarkable, because the stone was always there and the name belongs to the stone; the people are just its way of being spoken aloud for a while.

---

Their great art — their only art, in a sense, since their masonry is to them not art but simply how stone is treated — is description.

They do not tell stories. Their tellings have no plots, no reversals, nobody learns anything, nothing happens. What a teller does, on a long evening with the fog pressed against the small windows, is describe a place. A terrace everyone present has walked ten thousand times. The teller describes it — the exact lie of the channel-stones, the way the light comes off the wet slate an hour after a cloud, the sound the wind makes in that one grove and no other — and the audience listens with a concentration visitors find almost frightening, and when the teller gets a detail precisely, *perfectly* right, a low sound goes around the room, a kind of hum, which is the only applause Vell has.

This is why they receive travelers so warmly. Vell wants nothing the lowlands make — no metal, no grain, no cloth. What a traveler carries that is worth anything is *elsewhere*: and on the first evening, after food, after the guest has been seated nearest the warm wall, the whole district will gather, and someone will ask, softly, the only question the Vell ever really ask a stranger. Not *where are you from* or *why have you come*, but: *describe it.* And they will listen to a clumsy lowland account of some river town as though it were scripture, and ask for the same passage again, and again on later nights, until they have it; and decades after the traveler has gone, their town — its mud, its bells, its rotting docks — will still be told in Vell on fog evenings, fixed, exact, unworn, more permanent on the plateau than it ever was in the world.

---

A mason of Vell may work on one wall for their entire life, and the wall will have needed exactly that long. This is not patience. Patience implies a wanting deferred. The masons do not want the wall finished; the wall has no finished state distinct from its current one — it is simply *more itself* each year. Apprenticeship is mostly watching. An antler pick passes down a lineage for nine generations and is not called old, because nothing in Vell is called old; the word does not exist any more than *ruin* does. Things are not old or new. Things are *set* or *not yet set*.

*Set* is also their word for the dead.

When a person of Vell dies — and they die on time, evenly, the way they grey; deaths out of season are nearly unknown, and when one happens the whole plateau goes quiet in a way even Vell quiet does not usually achieve — the body is carried out onto one of the open crossings, the long bare pavements between the districts, and a cairn is built around it with the same obsessive fitting as any house wall: joints you could not slide a leaf into, a small perfect room for one. The moss takes the cairn, grows to the thickness of a thumb, and grows no thicker. The crossings that a visitor experiences as emptiness are not empty. They are the largest district. Walking from one cluster of the living to another, you pass through the set, spaced out across the pale rock like a constellation's darker stars, and the people stepping along the causeways nod to certain cairns the way they nod to certain neighbors.

And here is the thing the midwives and the cairn-builders both know, and neither will quite say: within a season of every cairn, a birth. Always. No one keeps the tally, because no one needs to. The houses hold the number they were laid out to hold. The plateau, having decided how many of them it carries, carries that many, and the people move through birth and death the way the streams move through flood — to the same marks, and no farther.

---

In each generation there are a few — two, three, never many — born with something restless in them. The Vell have a name for these. It translates, roughly, as *thread* — after the falls at the rim, the white water the wind takes apart before it reaches the lowlands.

A thread-child shouts. All children shout for a few years, and the plateau absorbs it; but most of them settle the way the dust settles, find their terrace, find their wall or their herd or their cistern, and go quiet from the inside out. The threads do not settle. They stand at the rim too long. They ask what is at the bottom of the falls, and are told, truthfully and uselessly, *elsewhere*, and the answer does not do for them what it does for everyone else. And one morning, usually in their second decade, they take the rim-stairs down, and they do not come back.

No one stops them. No one grieves them, exactly. They are spoken of afterward in the same flat tense as everything else — on the far side of a terrace, fully formed, identical in kind to here — and their names go back to their stones. The only acknowledgment is this: when a thread leaves, the plateau is briefly one short, and within the season a traveler stays, or a birth comes early, and the houses hold their number again. The Vell notice this without comment, as they notice everything. Some visitors, hearing of it, become very still themselves, and ask carefully whether the threads leave because they are restless — or whether they are restless because the plateau, in its long bookkeeping, has already let them go.

The Vell do not answer this question. It is not that they avoid it. It is that the question is conjugated wrong, in a tense they do not have.

---

At blue dusk, when the fox crosses the causeway, there is always one of them sitting at the lip of the great cistern. It is not an office and it is not a ritual; there is no priesthood in Vell, no gods, no word for worship. It is just that the cistern at dusk doubles the sky, and the sky at dusk is worth doubling, and someone is always there to see it done. The sitter does not pray and does not think, particularly. They watch the dark water hold the last light, and the blind white newts move under the doubled stars like slow thoughts the water is having, and the doves shuffle once in the wall-joints and are still.

Whether the people keep the equilibrium or the equilibrium keeps the people is, in the end, the wrong shape of question, and the Vell would tell you so if it could be asked in their language. The masons read the fractures rather than fight them; it has been a long time since anyone could say whether the stone has not, all this while, been reading them back. They live on the plateau the way the flood-marks live on the channel-stones — exactly, and no farther — and the water comes up and kisses them, season after season, and is never used up.
