## The Inhabitants of the Green Steading

Ask who lives in the steading and you will get a different answer depending on how closely you are willing to look, and for how long. The place resists a census the way it resists a map. Count at one scale and the population is in the millions. Count at another and it may be exactly one. Both counts are honest. Start small.

**The majority** live in the moss, and they are the moss's secret: a citizenry of water bears and rotifers and springtails and pale threadlike worms, packed into the half-meter roofs at densities that would make any city blush. They are the steading's true public. Every night the frost reaches into the outer centimeters of moss and kills them, or would, except that they have long since stopped treating death as binding—they dry, curl, glass over, and stop, and in the morning when the rime steams off they resume mid-gesture, as though the night were a comma. The whole town's population dies and resurrects daily at its edges. None of them will ever know they live in a town. They experience the steading as climate: a wet green eternity, generously dim, free of weather. They are the only residents for whom the place is finished.

**The birds**—there are six. There have been six for as long as anyone has watched, though never, it seems, the *same* six, which suggests they are a lineage rather than a flock, replacing themselves quietly, one at a time, somewhere out of sight. They are dust-colored and round and they work the inner walls all day with small persistent motions, eating the moss's citizenry in numbers that would be a holocaust if anyone were counting, which the moss is not. They are the town's entire predator class, its only violence, and the town keeps that violence at six.

They do not sing. They have one call—a single dropped note, like a pebble into the black pools—and it is used perhaps twice a season, for reasons no watcher has correlated with anything. They roost in the half-built eastern quarter, in the waist-high gappy panels, which is to say they sleep in the suburbs and commute inward at dawn. And every morning, before beginning work, one of them—always one, never two—flies to the great central chamber, enters through the gap in the domed roof, and sits for a while in the standing column of gray light, doing nothing, eating nothing, a small round shadow at the bottom of the beam. Then it leaves and the day starts. Whether this is ritual or thermoregulation or simple appetite for brightness, the watchers cannot say. The bird is not telling. The bird may not know. It is possible to keep an observance without understanding it; people do it constantly.

Their wings, the watchers note, are shorter than the wings of their kind elsewhere on the mountain. Generation by generation, the steading's birds are giving up the sky in small installments. The town is patient about this, as about everything.

**The walker** has never been seen, and this should be stated plainly at the outset, because everything else known about it is inference, and inference in the steading has a way of becoming architecture. What can be read from the pressed trails: it is the weight of a large dog or a small deer. It moves on pads, not hooves—the cushion-plant remembers softness differently from points. It walks the same circuit nightly, lane by lane, never cutting through a room, never crossing a wall, ducking (the trails show where it slows) under the thickening arches. It drinks, once per circuit, from the pool at the center of the chapel-sized chamber—the moss around that one pool, and no other, shows the faint nightly compression of something settling its forelegs to lower its head.

And here is the thing the watchers eventually notice, the thing that reorganizes everything else: the lanes the walker uses never seal. Doorways close all over the steading, passages become walls, the town edits itself ruthlessly—except along the walker's circuit, where the willow's slow encroachment always, somehow, stops short. The lanes it abandons become rooms within a decade. The lanes it keeps stay lanes. The town is not growing around the walker. The town is *transcribing* it. The settlement's whole street plan is the fair copy of one animal's habits, written out in willow over a century.

Which raises the founding question. A century ago, something walked this bare new saddle nightly—the same circuit, season after season, its coat carrying willow catkins and moss fragments up from the valleys, its passage breaking the rock-crust, its dung seeding the route. The town grew along the corridor of its attention. Whether the thing in the lanes now is that founder, improbably old, or its descendant, or merely the latest in a line of animals each of whom learned the circuit from the worn comfort of the paths themselves—the trails cannot say. It may not matter. A founder is a role, not an individual. The steading keeps the position filled.

**The sexton** lives under the floor, and it is everywhere, and it will never be seen whole because there is no whole to see—only threads. A fungus, a single sprawling thallus laced through the meter-deep pavement of the town's own dead generations, touching every willow root in the saddle, knotted densest (the watchers' probes suggest) directly beneath the central chamber. It arrived as a spore, on the wind or in the founder's coat, and it has been the town's quiet civil service ever since.

It eats the dead—slowly, at the pace the cold permits, which is why the floor holds its shape, why the pavement is a cemetery you can walk on. It moves sugar through the dark: the bright half-built eastern quarter feeds the gloomy center through its threads, light translated into syrup and piped to rooms that have not seen direct sun in thirty years. And it is, almost certainly, the plumber. The weeping walls that all face inward, the basins positioned where the seeps fall, the drainage that works—these are hydraulics, and hydraulics need coordination, and the only body in the steading that touches every wall from below is the fungus. It does not plan. It persuades, chemically, root by root, and the result resembles planning the way the steading resembles a town: closely enough to be unnerving.

**The one.** Look long enough at the grafts—those swollen pale knuckles where branch has knit into branch, wall into wall, across lanes, across the whole saddle—and the question of how many willows live in the steading stops having a number. The grafts are not joinery; they are unions. Sap crosses them. Signals cross them. It is entirely possible, the watchers admit, that every wall and roof-frame in the settlement is now physiologically a single plant: thirty structures, one body, the town wearing itself. In which case the steading does not contain its largest inhabitant. The steading *is* its largest inhabitant, and the rooms are the spaces it keeps inside itself the way a body keeps a hollow—deliberately, for some use.

**The chamber** at the convergence of the lanes has a resident, and the resident is an absence, and this is not wordplay. Everywhere else the town's nature is closure: doorways seal, lanes become rooms, space is the one thing the steading spends down relentlessly. The central chamber alone is maintained *open*—twice the height it needs to be, holding one column of light and one black pool and nothing, decade after decade, against the grain of everything the town otherwise does. Maintaining an emptiness costs effort. The town pays it. The founder stem, the watchers suspect, rooted on that spot a hundred years ago and is long dead beneath the domed floor, wrapped in the fungus's densest knot; the chamber may be a grown memorial, or a grown memory, or simply the one decision the steading is still refusing to make. The walker drinks there. The bird sits in the light there. Nothing lives there. Everything visits.

**And lastly, the accommodated**—because the steading has one more category of resident, and it is open enrollment. The watchers learned this from the watcher who stayed. Past the third season, the town began to notice her. The doorway nearest her shelter, which had been a year from sealing, stopped closing. The moss thickened over the spot where she slept. By the second year a weeping wall had developed on the inner face of her room—seep, basin, the works—plumbed for her as for any resident. The birds stopped marking her with their small wary pauses, which in the steading is naturalization. Her habitual path to the spring stayed open while lanes around it closed.

That is the whole law of the place, the entire constitution of the Green Steading, and it answers the question as well as it will ever be answered: *who lives there is whatever stays.* The town does not distinguish willow from walker from fungus from bird from woman. It reads habits and builds around them. Remain, and you will be transcribed. Leave, and the doorway that was held open for you will, without rancor, in two or three patient seasons, become a wall—and the room behind it will hold your shape a while, the way the cushion-plant holds a footprint, and then it won't.
