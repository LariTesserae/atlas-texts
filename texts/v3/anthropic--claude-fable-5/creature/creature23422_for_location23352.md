They live at the scale where a lifetime and an afternoon are hard to tell apart, and they live stacked, the way books live in a library — touching, ordered, unaware of each other's contents. To describe the residents of the concretion you have to accept from the start that almost none of them know they live in a city. They know current, food, dark, neighbor. The city is something that happens *to* them, through them, made of them. Only one resident has ever seen it whole, and we will come to her last.

**The crown-worms.** The oldest citizenry, and the only ones with hereditary addresses. A crown-worm is born adrift, a fleck of intention in the water, and it gets one decision in its entire life: where to land. After that it builds its tube and never moves again. Everything it will ever be is determined in that single settling — a worm that lands near the crack will live fat and fast in the food-rich entrance district and be built over within a generation; a worm that lands deep will live thin and long in the quiet, adding to its tube so slowly that the tube becomes a kind of biography, each increment a season. What you see of a crown-worm is the crown: a feathered fan, mauve or bone-colored, unfurled into the current like a hand held open under falling snow. Touch the water near one and the crown snaps shut faster than anything else in the city ever moves — the city's only reflex, its flinch. A district of crown-worms breathing open and snapping shut is the closest the metropolis comes to having an expression. They are the citizens who taught the floor to be a floor: the paved undercity is made of their ancestors, ten thousand generations of tubes fused into catacomb, and the living worms stand rooted in the compacted dead of their own line without any organ capable of finding this remarkable.

**The turret-snails.** The architects, the magnates, the only residents with anything resembling ambition. A turret-snail spends its whole life climbing a building it is also constructing — secreting the next whorl, hauling itself up into it, abandoning the floor below. It lives exclusively on the top story of its own past. The abandoned whorls below fill with squatters within days: amphipods, worm larvae, a flatworm folded into a coil like a tenant's rolled rug, none of whom pay anything, none of whom the snail will ever meet again. The snails under the brightest ceiling-leak are the city's aristocracy, their towers tallest, their gardens richest, and they got there by the only mechanism the city allows — the larvae that happened to settle in the light lived, and the rest didn't, and after enough generations this is indistinguishable from real estate sense. A turret-snail dies at the summit of its tower, and the summit becomes the penthouse of whatever finds it first, and the tower stands. The skyline of the city is a cemetery that has never stopped being used.

**The bryozoan multitudes.** Here counting breaks down. A bryozoan colony is thousands of residents or one resident, depending on how you ask the question, and the colony does not ask. Each cell in the honeycomb sheets holds a single animal smaller than a poppy seed — a wisp of gut and tentacle that unfolds into its doorway to feed and withdraws to digest — and each is genetically identical to all its neighbors, budded from them, continuous with them, sharing walls and in some sense sharing self. They are the tenement people, the dense anonymous middle of every census, and they have solved the problem of individuality by declining it. When a sheet is damaged — a falling shell fragment, the slow shoulder of the thickening wall — the colony does not mourn; it grows around the wound, walls off the dead cells, and the sealed cells stay there in the sheet like shuttered apartments, like the catacombs in miniature. The colony's whole personality is direction: it grows toward food and away from the stone sky, and that is its entire opinion, held for centuries, expressed one cell at a time.

**The glass shrimp.** The commuters, the couriers, the only residents who hurry. They are transparent to the point of rudeness — you can see a shrimp's last meal traveling through it, see its heart working like a small frantic bellows, see, when one passes in front of the green pulse of the western wall, the light go through it almost undiminished, as if the city's only fast citizens were also its least substantial. They patrol the mineral bridges in single file, antennae reading the water the way a hand reads a banister in the dark. Their civic function, which they perform without civic intent, is circulation: they groom the crowns of the worms, pick the towers clean of settling debris, carry fragments of the dead from where death happened to where the catacomb crews can use it. They are also, not incidentally, the preferred food of the resident in the dome, and they seem to carry this knowledge in their posture — a citywide, species-wide tendency to keep one eye-stalk angled toward the apex of the chamber, toward the ribbed translucent vault that everyone avoids.

**The flatworms.** Spilled silk, the description said, and it holds: a flatworm crossing a plaza is less an animal moving than a small quantity of night sliding over the stone. They are the grazers of the gardens and the city's accounting nightmare, because a flatworm divided is two flatworms, whole, unbothered, each continuing in its own direction with half a history apiece. The population of flatworms in the city is therefore not a number but a process. They are arguably the residents most at peace with the concretion's nature — a creature that can be halved without grievance has no strong commitment to where it ends and other things begin, and the city, which is busy absorbing its own oldest districts back into stone, asks exactly that flexibility of everyone, eventually.

**The white isopods.** The undertakers, and the only residents of the past tense. They live in the absorbed districts — the catacomb streets already half-swallowed by the inward-thickening rind — blind, colorless, patient, working the vacant tubes with their fourteen legs in a gait like fingers walking. They eat what drifts down: the dead, the cast-off, the failed. They are the only citizens who routinely touch the actual walls of the world, the live growing stone, and they walk streets that are measurably narrower than the streets their grandparents walked, and they show no sign of minding. If the city's pre-thought knowledge of its own entombment lives anywhere in particular, it lives here, in the isopods' generational drift inward, each brood foraging a little farther from the walls than the last — not a decision, not a fear, just the shape of where the living space still is, traced by the bodies that fit it. They are the city's memory in the only form the city can keep memory: occupancy.

**The lamplighters.** The polyp colony on the western wall may be the oldest single resident, if it is single, if it is a resident rather than a fixture. Its pulse — that faint cold green, slow as breathing — is the city's clock, and possibly its founder: the oldest crown-worm tubes in the catacomb fan out from beneath it, as if the first settlers oriented on its light, as if the city was seeded around a lamp. The colony eats almost nothing, grows almost not at all, and pulses. It will pulse after the crack seals. For some interval — years, perhaps decades — the finished concretion will be a sealed stone on the black seafloor with a slow green breathing going on inside it, lighting streets with no traffic, and then it will stop, and that stopping will be the city's actual death, long after its burial.

**The one in the dome.** She did not build the cathedral. Nobody living built the cathedral; its architect is a vacancy in the city's records, a ribbed translucent vault with no descendants and no explanation, and its fossil — if it has one — is somewhere in the floor with everything else. She simply arrived: a larva the size of a punctuation mark, washed in through the crack on a current years ago, and grew, and at some point passed the threshold past which the crack would no longer let her out. She is the only resident who knows she is enclosed. She is the size of a thumb, soft, the color of the dim, and she has eyes — real eyes, the only pair in the metropolis — and what those eyes do most of the time is watch. From the apex of the chamber the whole city is visible at once: the breathing crowns, the towers ticking taller, the green pulse, the shrimp on their bridges keeping one eye-stalk raised toward her. She is the only being who has ever seen the city as a city, and she is its predator, and these are the same fact. At long intervals she pours herself out of the dome and down a tower like water finding a stair, and takes a citizen — almost gently, the description says, and it is true; there is no struggle she cannot make unnecessary — and returns, and the city does not pause, because the city does not have the kind of attention that pauses. She has a den-floor lined with the cleaned shells of turret-snails, arranged — and here "arranged" is doing the same uneasy work that "deliberately" did earlier — by size. She will be sealed in. She may be sealed in alive, the last mobile resident, a small soft mind in a closing stone, watching the lamplighters' pulse slow in a city gone entirely still. There is no evidence she dreads this. There is no evidence she doesn't.

**The ones who leave.** And then there are the residents who live there only briefly, and they may matter most. Every season the city exhales larvae — worm-flecks, snail-motes, the punctuation-mark young of soft things — out through the crack and into the black, a thin smoke of emigrants riding the current across the sediment plain toward the other concretions, the young cracked ones faintly lit from within. Most die in the dark between. A few arrive, and settle, and decide their one decision, and begin laying down tubes on a floor that will someday be a catacomb under a city that will someday be a stone. The residents of the concretion, taken fully, include this diaspora: the city's children are other cities. The sealed stones scattered across the seafloor like dropped fruit are not tombs from this vantage but ancestors — finished sentences in a language still being spoken, one tick, one whorl, one settling grain at a time, by everything small that lives in the closing dark and builds anyway.
