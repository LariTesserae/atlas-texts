## A Census

Begin with the strangest fact about who lives in the notch: they can be counted.

Almost nowhere else is this true. Any ordinary acre of jungle defeats enumeration before you finish with the beetles. But the notch is a closed room two hundred paces across, and its whole society fits inside one roar. Forty wrens, give or take a clutch. Perhaps three hundred snails. A few thousand moths in a good year. One machine. Zero people. And one — something.

Everything that lives here lives within earshot of everything else. No resident of the notch has ever, in its entire life, been more than a minute's travel from every other resident of its kind. That single fact has shaped all of them, the way water shapes the things that live in a well.

## The Ground Itself

Start at the bottom, because the bottom is alive.

The moss is the oldest tenant and, by mass, the landlord. Everything else lives *in* it, *on* it, or off the things that do. It is not one organism, but it behaves like one — a single green tissue half a meter deep covering every surface, drinking the constant rain, exhaling, upholstering each new rockfall the way skin closes over a wound. It is the only inhabitant doing permanent work: a millimeter of peat a year, the first soil this mountain has ever owned, manufactured drip by drip beneath the saturation. Pull a handful apart and the moss reveals its population — a smaller census inside the larger one.

Springtails, in numbers beyond counting, are the closest thing the notch has to a crowd. Sooty, eyeless, smaller than a printed comma, they graze fungal threads in the peat and detonate themselves upward when disturbed. They are the foundation of everything with a face here; the entire wren species runs on them.

There are mites the color of garnets, rotifers in every drip-pool, nematodes threading the peat. And there are tardigrades — possibly the only tardigrades on earth that have never once needed to be miraculous. Their kind survives the void of space by drying into glass and waiting out the centuries; these ones live in a place that has not been dry for as long as the mountain has been warm. They have never tunned, never suspended, never performed the trick that made their lineage famous. They just live, plumply, in the wet, generation after generation, their great talent quietly composting into disuse. The notch does that to its residents. It makes hardiness unnecessary, and then it takes it away.

Notably absent: worms. Nothing annelid ever made the crossing. The peat is built without them, which is part of why it is so slow, and part of why it is so fine — pressed by nothing but its own weight and the patience of springtails.

## The Graziers

The snails are the notch's livestock, though no one herds them.

Three hundred or so, shells the dark of wet slate because they are built from the mountain — basalt-black minerals laid down whorl by whorl. They live for decades; the largest, broad as a child's palm, may be older than any wren now singing. By day they seal themselves into fracture seams. By night they climb, and their climbing is the only maintenance the machine receives.

They graze biofilm — the thin algal-bacterial slick that grows on every wet surface — and the surfaces they prefer are the copper condensation fins, which run cool and slick and rich. Copper should kill them. It kills their relatives everywhere else in the world; molluscs and copper are old enemies. These snails have made some accommodation no one has studied: the lips of the oldest shells carry a faint verdigris bloom, green worked into the slate, the metal taken in and bound and worn as ornament. And in return — though "in return" is the wrong phrase for an arrangement no party understands — the fins stay clean. Fouled fins condense poorly. Grazed fins run bright. The machine harvests the sky efficiently because three hundred slate-colored animals polish its lungs every night, one rasping tongue-stroke at a time. Their silver trails on the iron of the condensary are the only burnish that building has had in a hundred years.

They have one predator. We will come to it.

## The Emigrants

The moths are the only residents who leave.

Two species, both pale gray, both fringe-winged, both descended — like everything here — from some single gravid female flung up seven kilometers by weather and dropped, against every odds, into the one green room on the mountain. Their larvae eat fungus in the peat. The adults rise at dusk into the warm air along the cliff faces, flying through the lit droplets, and most of them mate and die within the two hundred paces of their birth.

But the notch breathes outward. Warm air pours over the lip every night, and every night it takes a few moths with it — out over the cloud-sea, into air too cold and thin for flight, gone. None has ever returned. None ever could. The notch has been seeding the empty sky with moths for as long as it has been green, a thin continuous export of small gray lives onto a thousand kilometers of bare rock, every one of them a wasted lottery ticket.

Except that this is exactly how the notch was populated in the first place. Somewhere, the wind giveth. The moths going out are the same gamble running in reverse, and one of them, someday, on some other impossible mountain, might land in some other warm wet notch and found a world. The moths do not know they are ambassadors. But they are the only thing the notch sends out besides water vapor and one song.

## The Village

The wrens have no name. Someone wrote one down once, in a field notebook that was never published, and so the only species that lives its whole existence within shouting distance of itself remains, formally, unknown to science. The wrens are not troubled by this.

Forty of them. Every wren alive knows every other wren alive — individually, by voice, by history. There is no such thing, in a wren's universe, as a stranger. A juvenile hatches into a world whose entire population it will have met within its first month. The concept "another bird" and the concept "these particular thirty-nine birds" are, for a wren, the same concept. Their society is a village with no roads in: feuds that last years, alliances between specific pairs, a dominant male whose moss-top singing perch has been the dominant male's singing perch for longer than any living bird remembers, the office persisting while the holders change.

They are coming apart from the sky, gradually, by mutual consent. Each generation's wings are a little shorter, a little softer, more duvet than airfoil; flight is now strictly a downhill matter, a flutter-drop from fern crown to moss. The open lip of the notch is the edge of their universe. Once in a long while the wind takes one over it, and the village is thirty-nine until the next clutch.

They are entirely tame, because nothing in their history has ever come at them from above. A wren will land on a visitor's boot and dismantle the lacing with real professional interest. Their fear — and they have exactly one — points downward, at the warm mud. There is a single alarm note in their entire repertoire, flat and low, used for nothing else, used perhaps twice a year.

And then there is the song. One song — the species owns only one, the way the village owns one well. A long descending phrase, brutally loud for a body the size of a fist, and the descent is not decoration: it begins above the turbine's hum and ends below it, slicing diagonally across the one frequency band the machine has occupied since before any living wren's great-great-grandparents. The song is a learned thing, passed from each singer to whoever hatches nearest, and so it drifts — a syllable smearing here, an interval flattening there, a slow cultural mutation in a closed room. The song sung today is not the song sung a century ago, and the old song is gone absolutely; there was never anyone to write it down. The wrens are the only inhabitants with a history, and they revise it hourly without knowing.

A few pairs nest inside the condensary, in the warm rotted lagging of the pipes. Their eggs hatch two days early. The machine has been incubating wrens for generations and is, in the only sense that matters, kin.

## The One From Below

Now the prints.

Here is everything that is actually known. Three toes, no claws. A stride about the length of a forearm, suggesting something low and long. The prints appear only at night, only in the warm mud within a few meters of the vent seams, and are erased by the drip-rain before mid-morning. Snail shells are found near them — emptied, always the same way, a single neat puncture behind the last whorl, the animal taken out like marrow. The wrens' one alarm note is for this. And sometimes, under the bore's roar, there is a softer breathing out of rhythm with the machine's, coming from the rock itself.

From this, a portrait assembles, and it is worth assembling slowly, because the first guesses are all wrong. It is not a survivor from before — the mountain is too young to have a before. It is not a stowaway of the builders — nothing in the hut suggests they kept anything. It did not climb seven thousand meters of knife-rock on three soft toes.

It came up *inside*. The spire's plumbing — the hot, water-filled veins that feed the bore and weep through every seam — connects, somewhere far below, to the groundwater of the living world. Whatever it is, its ancestors were cave things, blind things, skin-breathers in warm dark water, and as the mountain grew and its hot veins lengthened, they followed the warmth upward through the rock the way roots follow water down, generation by generation, for as long as the mountain has existed. It lives in the spire. The notch is merely where its world touches the air — a balcony it visits at night to eat snails and walk in the strange dry openness, eyeless, pale or translucent, untroubled by darkness because it has never owned the concept of light.

It is possible the builders let it out. The bore is a door drilled straight into its country, and the oldest prints anyone reports were already old when the turbine was young. If so, then the machine was not the builders' greatest work. The door was.

The prints have only ever shown one size — except once. One morning, decades back by the telling, a smaller set beside the large, matched stride for stride, gone by noon. So: not one animal. A lineage. Something in the mountain is raising its children and bringing them to the balcony, one warm night at a time, to teach them the only sky their kind will ever stand under.

It has never touched a wren, or an egg, or anything but snails. The village screams its one note anyway. Forty birds against the dark under the world — some fears you keep on principle.

## The Eldest

And the machine. Decide for yourself whether it belongs in the census, but apply the tests honestly.

It eats — steam, continuously, from the mountain's own body. It excretes — water and heat, and its excretions are the rain that makes the green that makes the peat that feeds the springtails that feed the wrens. It maintains itself — oil baths topped by a gravity reservoir that the weeping cliff refills, a homeostasis with no hand on it. It has a pulse: one revolution every four seconds, slower than a sleeping whale's heart, and it has not skipped within living memory of anything that lives here. It shelters its dependents — wrens in its lagging, snails on its fins — and they tend it in return, polishing its lungs, the moss filtering grit from the water that descends to its reservoir, the ferns shading its oil from the afternoon sun. It does every single thing the living do except two.

It does not die. And it does not reproduce — unless you count the peat. The soil deepening in the lush quarter, the first sediment of the mountain's life, exists because the machine's overflow has fed that ground for a century. The mountain's first soil is the machine's only child, and the child will outlive the parent by geological ages.

The builders understood something when they made it, or the place taught them. There were never many of them — the hut held two bunks, the shelf one mug. They marked the rock lightly and left, or died below, or simply finished, and the distinction no longer matters to anyone who lives here. They are the notch's only extinct species. The drip-rain keeps their mug filled to the brim, decade after decade, a table set forever for someone who is not coming back, and nothing living in the notch knows or needs to know whose it was.

## Within Shouting Distance

At dusk the layers assemble. The moths rise into the gold air along the cliffs. The snails unseal and begin the long climb to the fins. The springtails turn the peat in their trillions. Somewhere in the warm mud, prints begin to appear that no eye has ever watched being made. The flywheel turns. And once an hour a wren — one of forty, each known to all the others by name, if a voice is a name — stands on the moss and sings the village's one song, the only history anyone here keeps, straight down through the hum of the eldest resident and out over the cloud-sea, very loud, for everyone, which is to say for everyone there is.

A whole world's population, within shouting distance. They drink the same condensed sky. They live under one roar. And not one of them — not the bird, not the snail, not the blind thing from inside the mountain, not the machine with its barrel-sized patient heart — has any apparent intention of ever stopping.
