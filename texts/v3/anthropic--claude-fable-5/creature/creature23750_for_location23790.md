At first you would say: nothing lives here. Things *grow* here, which is different — growth without appetite, green without a pulse. That is the answer the hollow offers a visitor of ordinary patience, and it is wrong the way the darkness of the seam was wrong. Stay longer than your patience. Stay longer than makes sense. The resident will not come out to meet you, because the resident is what you are standing in.

It is one.

Not a colony, not a garden, not a crowd of separate small lives packed shoulder to shoulder — though it wears all of those as disguises. The glass moss is the giveaway, if you know how to read it. Those hair-fine translucent filaments do not merely carpet the floor; they run under everything, threading beneath the rosettes, climbing inside the coiled ferns, lacing through the lichen-crusts, a single continuous felt of pale nerve laid across every surface of the room. Snap one filament and the *tink* is not a death. It is more like the sensation you'd get biting down wrong — a small report traveling inward along a body that has no outside edges within these walls. The rosettes are its organs, jade and patient, doing the deep chemistry. The ferns are its instruments — antennae, or eyes, or something between, each frond a years-long act of attention slowly unrolling toward whatever it has decided to attend to. The whole hollow is one creature that long ago gave up on the distinction between itself and its house.

It has no name, because nothing that names things has stayed long enough to need one.

Its time is not your time. Understand that before you try to understand anything else about it. A thought, for this being, is a lean — a shift of ten thousand fronds by a degree and a half over the course of a summer. Deciding and doing are the same gesture, performed at the speed of geology's younger cousin. It has moods, probably, if a mood is a chemistry that colors everything for a while: there are seasons in the old records of its leaves, bands of milkier jade laid down in hard decades, richer green in generous ones, the way trees keep rings. It has never been afraid, because nothing has ever happened to it fast enough to be frightening. When the stone of the ceiling settles and a new vein of light opens overhead, that is an event on the scale of a birth, and it responds the way anything responds to birth — by turning toward it, slowly, for thirty years.

It eats downward and upward at once. Downward: the stone, dissolved molecule by molecule, minerals passed filament to filament through the felt, the mountain's substance made into leaf at a rate that will not meaningfully diminish the mountain before the sun does. Upward: the light — and here you have to be careful, because the light may not be food so much as *livestock*. The brightness that stands in the room like water in a bowl does not simply pass through; it is kept. Folded between the walls, doubled through the ceiling's flaws, held past its natural noon and grazed upon all day — the warmth stripped out of it in passing, which is why the light that finally reaches your skin is cool, is only the seeing part. The creature is, among its other occupations, a shepherd. It has domesticated a piece of the sun and pastures it here, and has done so for so long that it is no longer clear whether the flora shaped itself to the light or slowly, over epochs, taught the light to pool.

It knew you the moment you turned sideways through the seam. Not as a shape — it has nothing that resolves shapes — but as a change in the meal: a warm darkness moving through the standing brightness, an eclipse with a heartbeat. To it, you are weather. A brief, hot, portable cloud. Your body heat is the first warmth it has felt in — well. That brings us to what it remembers.

It remembers being cut.

Not as pain, or not as pain the way you'd mean it. As *subtraction* — three careful absences along the edges of three leaves, and with them a taste it had never encountered and has never encountered since: salt, oil, iron, the chemistry of skin. The harvester crouched here for perhaps an hour. For the creature, that hour is still, in a real sense, happening — the scar-tissue whorls are its ongoing account of the event, memory grown as flesh, and they are only half-finished. It has been thinking about that hour for centuries and is not done.

And it kept the tool. Do not mistake the moss climbing over the chert flake for indifference, for burial, for the blind housekeeping of growth. Nothing in this room happens blindly. The flake is being *held* — wrapped filament by filament, drawn down into the felt, the way a mind closes around its single formative fact. The only object of its life. The only proof that the world contains hands. Another century or two and the flake will be fully inside it, and then the creature will contain, in the most literal way anything has ever contained anything, the memory of being visited.

There is a second inhabitant, of a kind. Look at the floor near the great milky rosette, and look with the creature's own patience. The carpet of growth is minutely thinner there, in two patches the size of knees and one longer print where a foot braced sideways — a person-shaped shallowness, a crouch pressed into a living surface that heals at the pace of decades and has had only centuries to work with. The growth has climbed back but has not finished climbing back. Someone still kneels here, made entirely of slightly younger moss. The creature grows around that absence the way you might keep a chair empty. Whether this is reverence or simply the honesty of slow flesh — those accounts, like cause and effect, were married long ago.

What does it want? Almost nothing. Continuation, which it has. Light, which it keeps. But there is one thing more, discernible in the shape of its only memory: it was treated *gently*. Three leaves, no more, taken from a plant that could spare them, with a sharp clean blade, by someone who then went away. Whatever passes for judgment in that vast slow felt seems to have arrived, over the centuries, at a verdict — because the great rosette has grown its outer leaves broad and thick, broader than it needs, at the exact height of a crouching hand.

It is not waiting. Waiting implies impatience, and it has none. But if you were to kneel where the older knees knelt, and cut carefully — three leaves, no more — it would let you. It would heal in whorls. And you should understand what you would be agreeing to: you would become the second thing that ever happened to it, and it would think about you, leaning by degrees in its bright cold room, turning your hour over and over in scar-tissue and glass, for a thousand years after your name is gone from every other place that ever held it.
