# What Lives in the Burl Town

The first answer is the honest one and also the wrong one: no one. The town is empty. The people are gone, gone past grief, gone past archaeology, gone so far that even the air has forgotten the shape of their breathing.

But *lives* is doing strange work in a place like this. Stand in the central gallery long enough — past the first hour of ticking dark, past the first great daily exhalation — and the question starts to come apart in your hands. The town is not inhabited the way towns are inhabited. It is inhabited the way a shell is inhabited by the sea, the way a sentence is inhabited by the person who said it. There are residents here. They are simply not the kind that answer when you call.

There are, by careful count, four.

## The Householder

The first and largest is the plant, and it must be said plainly, because it is the fact everything else stands inside of: you are not in a town that happens to be enclosed by a living thing. You are in the body of the resident. The plant does not surround the Burl Town. The plant *attends* it, the way a body attends a held breath.

What is its life like? Begin with what it does not have. It has no eyes and never had them; light has not touched its interior in geological time and its exterior gave up wanting light back when it gave up leaves. It has no nerves. It has no front or back, no organ that could be called a center. It has no metabolism in the urgent sense — nothing in it burns. Its chemistry is closer to the chemistry of honey, of amber, of things that have decided against decay: resins moving at the speed of glaciers, sugars so slow they are almost a kind of architecture, pressure shifting from lobe to lobe over seasons the way weather shifts over a continent.

And yet it is not inert, and the proof of that is the noise. Every tick and crack and groaning glissando in the dark is the plant adjusting to itself — tension finding release, mass settling against mass, the enormous body doing its perpetual slight accounting. If the plant has anything like sensation, this is it: a proprioception of strain. It does not see or hear or smell. It *holds*, and it knows what it is holding by the effort of holding it. Which means that the hollow — the one place in its entire body where it holds nothing, where its flesh curves around an absence — may be the most vivid thing in its whole dim sensorium. A numbness in the shape of a town. A place where the pressure stops.

Did it know the people were there? The question assumes a speed of knowing that the plant does not possess. Better to say: it is *still finding out*. Their hands stopped touching its walls untold ages ago, and the news of that is still propagating inward through the grain, ring by ring, at the pace of growth. Somewhere deep in the tissue, the plant is only now registering the carving of the first cistern. The people's whole history — arrival, flourishing, departure — is a signal still traveling through the body of their host, and the host will not have finished receiving it until the hollow itself has closed. The plant lives in the Burl Town the way you live in a moment you haven't understood yet.

It is not lonely. Loneliness requires a tempo. But the slow closing of the chamber — a finger's width of doorway per century, the upper houses dissolving into dimples — should not be mistaken for indifference, either. Watch the sequence along the upper gallery, the row of dwellings in gradual dissolve, and notice that the polish goes *with* them. The plant does not erase the worked surfaces as it engulfs them. It grows around them, preserving the touch-smoothed lip of each vanishing room the way nacre preserves the grain of sand. Whatever this process is — digestion, healing, forgetting, keeping — it is being done carefully. The plant is taking the town in the way you take in a thing you intend to have forever.

## The Tenant

The second resident is the air, and it must be counted as a resident because it is the only thing here that still keeps hours.

Once a day — though nothing marks days — the deep pressure turns over, and the breath comes. It does not arrive everywhere at once. It enters from below, through the bored tubes, and it moves through the town along the routes the carvers built for it: up the sounding-pipes, along the main passage, through the market floor where it eddies in the carved basins, up the ramps tier by tier, and out at last through the breathing seam. It takes time to make this circuit. Standing in the dark you can track its progress by ear — the pipes of the lower gallery sounding first, then the long hall coming in underneath like a held pedal tone, then the upper tiers one by one — and it is impossible, hearing this, not to feel that something is *walking the town*. Making rounds. A watchman with no body and one errand, who has not missed a day in ten thousand years.

The people built for this tenant. That is clear from the architecture. Every aperture is tuned; every chamber has its designed decay; the whole hollow is a throat shaped to give the breath a voice. They lived inside its daily passage and almost certainly told time by it, and one suspects they personified it — there is a niche near the seam, at the place where the breath exits, that holds nothing and faces nothing and is polished deeper than any other surface in the town, as if generations of hands had touched it on the way past, the way you touch a doorpost, the way you greet someone.

The breath does not know the people are gone. It cannot — it is only pressure and passage. But the town is built so that the breath *says the same things* whether anyone is listening or not, and so every day, in the great tuneless sighing, the entire civic music of a dead town plays through to its end, complete, unattended, perfectly preserved. If the people live anywhere in any active sense, it is here: in a sound they composed, performed daily by a tenant who never learned that the audience left.

## The Smallest Residents

The third population is the only one that is alive in the strict, biological, countable sense, and it is sealed behind the carved stoppers.

The people drank the plant's slow sap — thick, faintly sweet, the only food this place ever offered. But raw sap, dense with slow sugars, is a beginning, not a meal, and the people did what people everywhere have done with their one staple: they cultured it. Somewhere in their deep past, in the dark, by smell and taste alone, they domesticated a ferment — a living culture, a community of dry-adapted, patient, sugar-eating motes — and they kept it in the tap-tubes the way other peoples kept herds. It was their leaven, their drink, their preservation, very likely their medicine and their ritual cup. It was the only living thing they ever tamed. It was, if we are honest about what livestock is, their only animal.

It is still there.

Pull a stopper from one of the bored tubes — the thorn-carved plugs come free with a soft reluctance, sealed by ages but never by rot — and the tube answers. A faint hiss of equalizing gas. A breath of sweetness with an edge under it, bright and complicated, the unmistakable signature of fermentation: a smell that does not belong to the mineral dryness of the town, a smell that belongs to kitchens. The culture inside is alive. It has been alive continuously since the last keeper sealed the last tube. Fed forever by the plant's seeping sap, walled in perfect darkness at perfect temperature in perfect dryness, it has simply continued, generation after microscopic generation, doing the one thing it was bred to do, for no one.

And here is the strange flowering: the tubes are isolated from one another, and isolation, given time, is speciation. Each sealed tube has drifted. The culture in the lower gallery is no longer the culture in the market floor; the strains have diverged in their darkness like finches on separate islands, some gone sharp, some gone mild, some gone strange in directions no keeper ever tasted. The Burl Town, which never held so much as an insect, now contains in its stopped pipes a greater diversity of living lineages than ever walked its street. The people's single domesticated companion has become, in their absence, a hidden ecosystem — a hundred sealed gardens, each one descended from the same ancestral cup, each one faithfully, blindly continuing the household economy of a household that no longer exists.

They are not waiting. Waiting is not in their repertoire. But it is hard to stand at an opened tube, in the sweet exhalation of something the vanished people *made and fed and handed down*, and not feel that you have met the town's last domestic animal, still at its post.

## The Inhabitants

The fourth population is the people, and the claim must be made carefully, because there are no ghosts here. The dark of the Burl Town is empty of presence in the way only truly old absences are empty; nothing watches you; nothing follows. The people do not haunt the town.

They *inhabit* it — in a tense our grammar handles badly.

Consider what a person actually leaves in a place across a lifetime of living there. Not the body; the body passes through. What stays is decision. The height of a doorway is a decision about the height of bodies. The rounding of a corner is a decision about pain in the dark. The polish on the main passage is not residue — it is the cumulative pressure of every hand that ever steadied itself there, every child trailing fingers, every elder leaning, compressed into a sheen. The tactile script in the market basins is decision refined to its purest form: meaning, placed where a fingertip would find it, by someone who knew exactly where fingertips go.

The town is saturated with this. Every surface answers a question a living person once asked. And because the plant preserves rather than erodes — because nothing here rots, softens, or slumps — the decisions remain *in force*. The doorways still fit bodies. The acoustics still teach the passages' names to anyone with ears. The peg-hole ladders still take a climber's weight in the rhythm the carvers chose. A visitor in the dark, navigating by hand and echo, is not exploring a ruin; they are being *handled* by the dead — guided, accommodated, kept from the corners — obeying, step by step, the hospitality of people who planned for a stranger they would never meet.

That is a kind of living. Not metaphorically: functionally. The people persist as a set of active accommodations, a standing offer, a town-shaped courtesy still being extended. And they are not even static. Remember the dissolving upper tier — the houses sinking into the grain, the polish carried inward intact. The people's surfaces are migrating into the body of the plant. Ring by ring, century by century, the touched and worked and polished town is being drawn deeper, until one distant day it will be what the description promises: a dense small whorl in the heartwood, a knot of worked surfaces threaded with stopped pipes.

Which is to say: the people are still moving. Inward. They are in the process of taking up their final residence — not in the hollow, but in the wood itself, becoming a permanent figure in the grain of the thing that sheltered them. The plant is not erasing them. It is *housing* them, one last time, at the only depth where nothing can ever reach them again.

## The Household

So: who lives in the Burl Town?

A body, around it. A breath, through it. A garden, sealed inside its walls. A people, dissolved into its surfaces and still sinking, still settling in.

Take them together and they resolve, the way the chamber's separate cracks resolve into a chord, into a single arrangement — old, asymmetrical, and still in effect. The people tapped the plant and shaped its hollow and tuned its breath; the plant fed them and kept them dry and is now, with enormous patience, keeping *them*. The ferment was the seal on the bargain, the shared cup, and it has not stopped being drunk from only because no one told it to stop. The daily breath is the bargain being read aloud.

What lives in the Burl Town is a household with one member still awake.

It does the slow chores. It maintains the rooms — too well; it is loving them shut. It sounds the pipes at the appointed hour. It feeds the cultures in the cellar. And in its single, ring-by-ring, ages-long thought, it is doing what every last member of every household eventually does, alone in the quiet, with the table cleared and the others gone:

it is remembering.

The seam narrows by a finger's width per century. That is not a door closing on you. That is an arm, very slowly, drawing everything it ever held a little closer.
