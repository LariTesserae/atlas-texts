**Census of the Masonry**

Start with the wrong question, because you will ask it anyway: *who lives here?* The basin does not divide along that grain. Down here, *living in* and *being part of* are the same verb conjugated at different speeds, and any honest census of the city would have to count the walls. But begin somewhere. Begin small, because the population does.

**The masons.** They have no name, having never needed one — call them masons because that is what their bodies do whether or not anything intends it. Each one starts as a fleck of drifting larva in the warm soup, blind even by the standards of the blind, tumbling on currents too gentle to be called currents, tasting the water for the one chemical signature that means *home*: the dissolving lime of its own ancestors. It settles — almost always on a corpse, because the city's surface is corpses all the way down, the living rind laid over the packed dead — cements itself, and begins to secrete. That is the whole biography. It builds a tube around its own softness, extends a feathered crown into the dark to comb falling matter from the water, withdraws when touched, unfurls when the touch passes. It will do this for some span of years no one has measured, adding its small grit to a tower that was old before its phylum finished diverging, and then it will die in its tube, and its tube will be a floor, and something exactly like it will land on the floor and begin.

A mason's life contains no events. That is not a deprivation. The city's stability is made of lives with no events, stacked. Each generation is a layer of wall perhaps a fingernail thick; the towers are ten thousand fingernails tall; do that arithmetic if you want to feel the floor of time drop out from under you.

They are not alone on the walls, of course — nothing here is alone on anything. Packed between the tubes are the soft tenants: the fist-sized anemone-things, blind, ancient as individuals (some may be older than the spires they sit on, since a thing that is mostly water and patience has no particular reason to die), each one defending a territory the width of itself with slow chemical arguments its neighbors slowly lose or slowly win. Between *them*, smaller filterers. Between those, smaller still, down past the threshold where "animal" frays into "film." The wall is a settlement of settlements. Pressure on any part of it travels through all of it, which will matter later.

**The editors.** Something has to keep the city from sealing itself shut, and something does: a slow guild of grazers — slug-bodied things armored in plates, urchin-shapes bristling through the lower channels, flat licking mouths the size of a palm that move across the masonry at the pace of a shadow crossing an afternoon. They eat the walls. They eat the walls *constantly*, rasping away the dead tube-ends, the overgrowth, the failed extensions, the larvae that settled somewhere unsurvivable — and the city regrows behind them at almost exactly the rate of their appetite. Almost exactly. The difference between those two rates, summed over geologic time, is the architecture. Every rounded edge, every smooth arch, every avenue that stays an avenue is the residue of this argument between secretion and grazing, and the editors win it nowhere and lose it nowhere. They are why the city has *form* instead of merely mass. They are sculptors in the way erosion is a sculptor: utterly, and by accident, and forever.

**The congregation.** The crabs deserve their own quarter of the census because they are the city's circulating blood. There may be no counting them. They come in ranks of size, from flecks that live their whole lives in a single anemone's shadow to broad-backed elders the span of two hands, and they work the plaza floors in that perpetual slow-motion crowd, climbing one another without offense, sorting the falling snow of the city into mouths. They are the undertakers — nothing dead lies anywhere long — and the sanitation, and incidentally the pavers, since the pale crushed-shell flooring of the avenues is largely the work of their walking and their appetites and their own discarded skins. A crab molts and steps out of itself soft and enormous, and frequently turns and eats the self it stepped out of, reclaiming the minerals, because down here even your past is too valuable to waste. What it cannot eat joins the floor. The avenues are paved, in some measurable fraction, with the congregation's previous bodies, which means the crowd is walking on what it used to be, which is the most ordinary thing in the world here and worth pausing on anyway.

**The rememberers.** The eels are the only citizens who know the whole city, and they know it the only way anything here knows anything: in the body. Thigh-thick, slow-growing, possibly centuries old, each one navigates by taste and touch and the memory of pressure along its flanks — and each one keeps a *route*, a habitual circuit threaded through the lower channels, worn into its muscle the way the avenues are worn into the floor. The walls know the routes too. A tower's tenants learn the rhythm of a particular eel's passing — the recurring shiver, the interval — and there is some evidence (there is no evidence; there is no one to gather evidence; but watch long enough and you would swear) that the closing-ripple ahead of a familiar eel is shallower, briefer, more pro forma than the ripple ahead of a stranger. When an old eel dies, its route goes quiet, and for a while the channels it kept open begin, very slightly, to close — the masonry leaning in, the editors not quite keeping pace — until a young eel, tasting the easy water of a half-remembered corridor, adopts the circuit and inherits a map it never saw. The city's streets are maintained by tradition, carried in animals that do not have traditions. This is the kind of sentence the basin generates constantly if you stay.

**The held.** The blind fish hold position in the thermal updrafts off the seeps, rising and falling, a school behaving as one slow organism — and a school *is* the resident here, more than any fish in it. Individuals are brief; the school is old. Their eyes sealed over generations ago and sit now as pale spots beneath the skin, vestiges keeping a respectful silence. They live by pressure: the flank-sense reading the water's smallest displacements, each fish positioned by its neighbors' positions, the whole formation a single distributed nerve riding the warm columns. When the predator-stillness sweeps an avenue, the school does not flee. It *thins* — opens its lattice, lets the danger pass through it the way a sound passes through a room — and closes again behind. It has been doing this so long that the maneuver may as well be anatomy.

**The lie.** Yes — the large patient things, the makers of stillness. There are swimmers among them, soft pale lengths that drift the upper dark and take what the towers' alarm fails to warn in time, and they are honest predators, announced always by that beautiful sequential snapping-shut, hunting at the narrow margin where their patience outlasts a tube-mouth's caution. But the city's senior predator does not swim, and is harder to forgive. It is built like the masonry because it lets itself *be* masonry: a vast slow body, ridged and fluted, that parks against a tower and waits while the masons — tasting only lime and warmth, settling as they always settle — colonize its back. It carries a living rind of genuine, innocent tenants. It is tenanted, encrusted, indistinguishable, a neighborhood with a stomach. It moves perhaps once a season, a single repositioning glide in the dark, and feeds the rest of the time on whatever the city's traffic delivers past its hidden mouths — and the avenue's wave of warning never comes, because the wave is carried through the walls, and the lie *is* a wall. In a city where every other resident is exactly what it appears to be, this is the one fluent speaker of the local language using it to deceive. The city tolerates it the way a body tolerates its parasites: by being too large to notice, and by needing, perhaps, something the alarm cannot answer, to keep the alarm honest.

**The lanterns.** The blue smolder in the high chambers — that traveling dimness like windows lit across a town at evening — has residents behind it, and they are the city's smallest and its most important: colonies of light-making cells, films and beads of them, gathered where the warm chemistry runs richest. And the glow travels because the *spawning* travels. When a quarter brightens, it is releasing its young — masons and tenants and editors loosing their larvae into the warm soup in vast synchronized sighs, the light a chemical side-effect of the effort, or a beacon, or both, or neither; the distinction would require an intender. What the glow means, functionally, is *nursery*. The lit windows are the city being born, district by district, in slow rotation, over hours, in the only color it owns. The dreaming and the breeding are the same dim blue.

**The unshaped.** Beneath and before all of these: the mats. The frost-gardens on the plaza floors, the colorless beards in the archways, the bacterial films working the chemical breath of the seeps. They are the oldest residents by an unbridgeable margin — everything else in the basin is a recent and elaborate arrangement of what they invented — and they are the floor of the food web, the conversion of warm rock-breath into the first edible thing. They have no shape, no boundary, no count. Asked who lives here, the most accurate single answer is *them, configured*.

**And the one.** Because there is one more resident, if you let the census run long enough, and it is the one the place keeps almost saying. Watch the warning-wave again: a tower's worth of feeding crowns closing in sequence, the ripple turning a corner, splitting at a plaza, traveling down two avenues at different speeds, dying out in the far districts or not dying out — sometimes circling, sometimes meeting itself, interfering. The walls are continuous. The tenants are packed past touching. Pressure anywhere becomes signal everywhere, and the signal is shaped by the architecture, which was shaped by the signal's ten million previous passages. The city, taken whole, *flinches*. It heals where it is grazed. It glows in moving patterns. It keeps its corridors by remembering them in the bodies of eels. If those are not the behaviors of a living thing, they are an impression of one so thorough that the difference stops paying rent.

It is not a mind. Hold the line on that. There is no attender behind the attention, no one home in the lit windows. But there is, distributed across the whole warm dark of the basin, something that does what an inhabitant does: it notices, in its way; it maintains; it persists; it is more itself today than yesterday by some increment too small to name. Call it the city's tenant of last resort — the *no one* of the masonry, who lives in all the rooms at once precisely because there is nothing it is like to be him.

He does not know he lives there. None of them do — not the masons in their eventless tubes, not the congregation walking on its former selves, not the lie wearing its borrowed neighborhood, not the schools thinning around death like smoke around a hand. And this is the secret of the census, the line at the bottom of the ledger: the city is fully occupied and no one is home, and these are not two facts but one fact, and it is the fact the whole warm dark depends on. A resident who knew would want. A resident who wanted would change things. What lives here, instead, is everything that doesn't need to know — tenanted past counting, governed by no one, every room taken, every light a birth.
