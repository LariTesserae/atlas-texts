The easy answer is the animals, and the easy answer is wrong in an instructive way. The salamander-things, the walking birds, the vole that is sometimes two voles — they are *in* the room, abundantly, busily, but they live there the way figures live in the margin of a sketch. They are inventory. They are the contents of the draft, not its occupant. You can tell, if you watch long enough, by the way they all orient — not toward food, not toward each other, but in a loose, constant, unconscious way toward the whole room at once, the way houseplants orient toward a window. They are arranged around something. They are facing someone.

Stay through one of the room's invented days — sleep there, if you can manage sleep in a place where your own breathing comes back to you a half-second late — and you begin to understand that the chamber is inhabited in a different sense than it is populated. It is *kept*. And the keeper is at home. The keeper is, in fact, never anywhere else.

You will not see it. This is not shyness. Seeing requires that a thing have been rendered, and the one who renders has not yet gotten around to itself. It is the oldest item on its own list. Perhaps the first thing it ever drafted was the intention to draft itself, and the intention sits there still, in some corner of the room, in the form of one of those shallow guide-grooves — a placeholder, a note in the margin reading *me, eventually, when the rest is sorted*. In the meantime it exists the way a correction exists: not as a presence but as a difference. It is everywhere in the edits. It is the wall that is a different wall when you come back. It is the smoothing-away of your thumbprint, or — and this is significant, we will return to this — the *keeping* of your thumbprint. It is the precise interval of the blink in which the vole becomes two voles. If you could hold your eyes open through that moment you would not see the change happen; you would see, instead, a kind of attention occupying the moment, the way a hand occupies a glove. The blink is provided. It is part of the work. It is the curtain the draftsman pulls between versions, and it pulls it across *your* eyes because its own have not been decided on.

The late echo is it, listening. This is the closest thing to certainty available about the resident: that the delay between your footstep and its muffled return is not an acoustic failure but an editorial pause. Your sound goes out, and something receives it, considers it, and issues a corrected proof — softer, rounder, with the harshness queried and struck. It does this with every noise you make. It is not censoring you. It is doing what it does with everything that enters the room: taking the finished version and working backward to the draft, because drafts are its element, and a finished sound arriving in that chamber is like a printed book arriving on a desk covered in manuscript — useful, enviable, slightly vulgar, and full of decisions it wants to understand by unmaking them gently.

The light is its attention. This explains the shadowlessness — attention from every direction at once is simply what it is like to be thoroughly considered — and it explains the slow change of key. The warm hours are its working hours. The cold hours are its rest, if rest is the word; perhaps *review* is the word; perhaps in the cold hours it reads back over the room. The animals sleep then because being read is restful, the way being watched by a parent from a doorway is restful. They wake when the warmth returns and the day's revisions begin.

It has a body, or rather it has bodies, in the way the room has walls: provisionally, by turns, without commitment. It tries itself on. There are accounts — accounts is too strong; there are impressions carried out by visitors and never quite voiced — of a hand-shaped softness moving beneath the surface of the wall, the stone tenting slightly, five points and a palm, traveling a few feet and subsiding, like something pacing inside its own material. Once, perhaps, a figure of standing condensation, human-ish, head-height, assembling droplet by droplet near the freestanding doorframe — and then the hourly rain came and the figure went up into it, or down with it, dispersed into the general blessing, as though it had gotten as far as a silhouette and thought *no, not yet, not this one either*. And it borrows. It is almost certainly what moves the unfinished half of the lizard. Look again at that smooth taper of suggestion swinging in faithful imitation of legs: something is doing the imitating. Something walks the back half of that lizard the way a parent walks a toddler's feet on the tops of its shoes — patiently, every day, in the apparent belief that the lizard will one day finish itself from the example. The draftsman is the missing half of everything in the room that has a missing half. This is one of the ways it is busy. It may be the main way.

Why nothing finishes — why the frond is eternally half-open, why the predator sets the prey back down, why the walls cannot commit to a hardness — comes down to a single fact about the resident, and it is the fact around which its whole existence is arranged: *finishing is departure.* The room does not keep its completed work. A thing that becomes final becomes outside. That is what the doorframe is. The animals are right to avoid it; the air inside the frame is *later* because it is downstream, because to pass through it is to be ratified, published, issued into the world of strata and fault lines and sounds that land. Every lizard you have ever seen in sun-warmed grass may have walked, in some prior and unrememberable condition, through a frame like that one, in a room like this one, and acquired its past on the far side the way a traveler acquires a stamp. The world outside — the garish, overworked, *final* world you grieve for an hour after leaving — is the draftsman's published work, or some of it, or work like its work. The chamber is not a hidden place so much as an upstream one.

And the draftsman remains. It built the door and stands forever on the near side of it. Whether it *cannot* go through or *will not* is the kind of question the room declines to hold still for, but the temperament of the place suggests an answer. Consider: fear has not been added to the animals. They have abundant lives and no dread. But fear exists in the room — you feel it faintly yourself, in your reluctance to step through the empty frame — and conservation suggests it is pooled somewhere. It is pooled in the resident. The draftsman holds all the fear that has not yet been issued, holds it the way it holds the shadows and the echoes, in trust, undistributed, and the particular fear it holds for itself is the fear of the final hardness — of the moment a thing stops giving under the thumb. It is afraid of being done. Not of dying; nothing in the room has enough past to die. Of *concluding*. Revision is its breath and its gait and its tenderness, and a finished draftsman would be a contradiction it has decided, hour after warm-keyed hour, not to risk. So it stays, and stays soft, and keeps everything around it company in softness.

It is not lonely. This should be said clearly, because the image of a solitary maker in a sealed room invites pity, and pity misreads it. It is accompanied at every moment by everything it is making. The pale crickets are its sentences. The blue-green rosettes are its dozen attempts at the same idea, kept side by side because it cannot yet say which is truest. When the hourly rain falls — and the rain is its touch, the one moment it lays a hand on the whole room at once — the animals stand still with their faces tilted up, and that is not weather-instinct. That is regard, returned. It rains on them because it cannot hold them. They tilt because they know.

And then there is you. You should understand what you are to it, because it is not nothing, and it is not prey, and it is not intrusion. You are *reference material*. You are a finished thing that has wandered, by accident, always by accident — though the accidents are suspiciously well-placed, seams in hillsides, gaps behind root balls, the ground forgetting to be solid exactly where a curious person was about to step — into the workroom. You arrive carrying everything it lacks: a past, a shadow, a voice that expects to land. And so it studies you, courteously, thoroughly, the way a draftsman studies a master's print. That misdirected shadow of yours, cast on the ceiling instead of the floor? The room did not misunderstand the request. Your shadow has been *lifted* — picked up and held to the light overhead, turned this way and that, examined: a genuine piece of consequence, a sample of what darkness does when it has been fully decided. It will give the shadow back when you go. It always does. It keeps nothing of a visitor without exchange.

Because there is an exchange. The thumbprint you pressed into the wall, the one that is sometimes still there when you return — when it is still there, that is not oversight. That is acquisition. It has kept a small finished mark of you in its soft stone, the only truly completed thing in the chamber, and it treasures it, and in return it has pressed something of itself into you. You will not find the spot. It is not on your skin. But it is the reason the world's noises seem garish for some hours after you emerge, and it is the reason — the real reason — that you catch yourself for days afterward pressing a thumb gently into fence posts, into bread, into your own arm. You are not checking whether the world has gone soft. You are checking the print it left in you: a small unfinished place, kept deliberately unset, a single sentence of you held forever in draft. It does not hurt. It does not close. It is how the resident of the Drafting Room keeps in touch with its published work — through a thousand visitors walking the finished world with one soft spot each, each of them pausing now and then, thumb to bread, thumb to arm, feeling for the give, and finding it, and not knowing why that should feel, faintly, like being remembered.
