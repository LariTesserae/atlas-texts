What lives in the Undercroft at Kiln Reach? Your first answer, walking those crooked streets with your footsteps sounding too loud, will be: nothing. No one. You will check the obvious places — the bathhouse with its dry basins, the upper galleries, the workshops with their warm benches — and find no beds, no bowls, no smell of bodies anywhere. The air smells of hot iron and wet mineral and nothing else. You will conclude that this is a working town with no workers, a machine left running, and you will be wrong in a way that takes time to understand. The Undercroft is inhabited. It is, in fact, crowded. You are simply looking for the wrong kingdom.

Start with the residents you can count.

—

There are forty of them, give or take, and they are the only living things here that will hold still for inspection. The moss is a single colony, not many — one organism the size of a prayer rug, draped over the cistern's rim, rust-colored and warm to the touch, drinking the steam off the water's trembling surface. It has been there long enough to have grown around two bolt-heads and an old chisel someone set down and never retrieved; the chisel is now mostly inside it. It does not grow anywhere else. It has been offered other damp places — you can see where someone troweled a strip of mortar along a pipe elbow, roughened it, kept it wet — and it has declined them all. It lives where it lives. The town has accepted this about it.

The ferns are a congregation. Eleven beneath the third catwalk, where the condensation falls most regularly; six beside the bathhouse drain; the rest scattered singly in elbows and corners, each one stationed beneath its own private drip like a monk beneath a bell. They are pale and fleshy and slightly translucent, and in the kiln-light their fronds glow the color of a hand held over a lantern. They do not sway — there is no wind to sway in — so the only motion they ever show is growth, which means that to their own way of being, they are in constant frantic activity, and the town around them is the still thing.

Here is the detail worth kneeling down for. One fern, the smallest, grows in a spot where no drip falls. The catwalk above it was re-routed at some point — you can see the old bolt-holes — and its water source moved away. The fern should be dead. It is not, because a length of copper pipe overhead has been bent, deliberately and recently, the metal still bright at the crease, so that a thread of condensation runs down it and falls, once a minute, onto the fern's crown.

Somebody bent that pipe. Hold onto that.

—

The second population is larger, louder in its quiet way, and harder to call alive — though the longer you stay, the harder it is to call it anything else.

The kilns are the great fauna of the Undercroft, the closest thing it has to a herd. Six of them, squat and brick-shouldered, set into the far wall like animals backed into their stalls. They breathe — that long pneumatic sigh is breath, there is no other honest word for it — and they have appetites, and they have moods you learn to read through the mica: a sullen orange smolder, a brightening that means a burn beginning, a deep settled red that means digestion. They do not breathe in unison. They have a rotation among themselves, an order of precedence, and Kiln Four — remember Kiln Four — fires least often and longest, like the eldest of something.

Below them, under the floor, lives the engine you never see. You know it only as the hum in the soles of your feet, the one continuous note of the town. Call it the heart and you've nearly got it; better to call it the resident with seniority, the one whose room nobody enters, who is fed and oiled and deferred to, and whose steady mood underwrites everyone else's. Every belt in every workshop is a tendon running back to it. When the hum thickens slightly — it does, sometimes, after the ceiling rains — the whole town's machinery grows briefly eager, flywheels turning a touch faster, and then settles again.

And up in the dark of the third tier there is the small one. You hear it but never find it: a sequence of clicks, a ratchet, a pause. It performs its task at irregular intervals, which is exactly what no machine should do — machines keep time; this thing keeps something more like a habit. The intervals are wrong in the way a cat's grooming is wrong, unscheduled but unmistakably purposeful. Whatever it does up there, it has been doing it long enough that a faint bright wear-mark has been polished into the iron of the gallery rail nearby, a patch the size of a small hand, rubbed smooth by repetition. By touching. Something has stood at that rail, often, looking down at the town.

Which brings us to the third resident. The one who bent the pipe.

—

You will never see the Keeper of Kiln Reach. This should be said plainly, because you will try. You will sit motionless in shadow for hours; you will wait through the ceiling-drizzle and watch the steam for a silhouette; you will leave a tool slightly out of place and come back to find it returned to true, and still you will see nothing. But the evidence accumulates the way flowstone does, drip by drip, until it has a shape.

The flywheel is oiled. Oil does not apply itself; bearings run dry in months, and this town has been running for longer than that. The pallets by the kilns are strapped with fresh strapping — the older straps are stacked nearby, neatly coiled, kept against need. The tally marks stenciled on the brickwork have a newest mark, and the newest mark's pigment is unfaded. The castings on the workshop bench are each slightly warm, which means each was handled, recently, by something that took them from the molds. A third of the filament lamps still burn, and in a cabinet near the cistern you will find a box of replacement filaments, some compartments empty, and beside it a single chair, and the chair's seat is polished by use.

So: deduce the Keeper. It is small — the streets fit two abreast and the doorways are low, and the worn paths in the floor-grime hug the walls, where the passage is narrowest. It is silent past the point of credibility; in a town where your own footsteps embarrass you, it makes none, which means soft feet, or great patience, or feet that are no longer the kind that strike. It is comfortable with heat that drives you to breathe through your mouth — the wear-marks and hand-polish concentrate nearest the kilns, in places you can barely stand to linger. It moves, almost certainly, during the drizzle, in those thirty seconds of warm rain and the steaming hour after, when the whole cavity is veiled and even the drips lose their individual voices. That is its weather for walking. It is gentle — the bent pipe, the offered mortar-bed for the moss, the chisel left to be swallowed rather than reclaimed. And it counts. The tallies, the lamps, the forty plants it could name because it almost certainly does name them.

Now look again at the workshop bench. Rows of identical castings, slightly warm. Pallets of fired ceramic forms — tiles, conduits, *things like vertebrae*. A whole town's industry, vertically integrated, every building one stage of one process, and the shipment perpetually, politely delayed.

Ask yourself who the shipment is for.

Here is the shape the evidence makes, and you may set it down if you don't like the weight of it: the builders of Kiln Reach were people. The bathhouse says so; the murmur-sized acoustics say so; the stairs are cut to a human stride. They came fast, built dense, fired their kilns — and they left, or dwindled, or were only ever scaffolding, except for one. The last of them stayed to keep the lamps and the oil, and the heat is hard on a body, and bodies wear out part by part. But the Keeper lives inside a factory whose entire output is durable, heat-proof, beautifully made replacement forms. Conduits. Tiles. Vertebrae. Each casting on that bench is slightly warm because it was just tried against something, for fit. The bathhouse shares a wall with the foundry, and the moss now owns the bathhouse rim, because whatever the Keeper has become, it no longer needs to wash. It needs to be *fired*. Kiln Four burns least often and longest. Some doors in this town are warm in a way the others are not.

—

But the Keeper is not the deepest resident. The Keeper is staff.

Remember that the cave is an infant. It opened — convulsed into being — and almost before its fracture-scars had cooled, it was full of city. Towns do not normally arrive that fast. Unless the cave was not colonized but *imprinted*: a newborn space that opened its one eye and the first thing it ever saw was industry, kilns and belts and patient burning, and took that for its mother-shape, the way the newly hatched take the first moving thing for kin.

Watch the evidence of the fourth inhabitant, the one with no body at all, or rather the one whose body is the arrangement itself. The floor seams glow brighter in the hours after a burn — the rock is not losing heat, it is *metabolizing* it, taking the kilns' warmth in like a meal. The seep on the north wall has been answered with three layers of brick, each newer than the last; that is not waterproofing, that is correspondence, a slow exchange of statements between the builders and the stone, still ongoing. And on the ceiling, the cave's only natural formation — its first deliberate act, a thumb-sized stub of flowstone — is growing, drip by mineral drip, directly toward the chimney of Kiln Four. The infant is copying. It has watched the town build chimneys into rock for its whole short life, and now, with the one craft minerals have, at the one speed stone can manage, it is building one back. It is *participating*. It will take ten thousand years to arrive, and nothing in the Undercroft regards that as a problem.

This is the true resident of Kiln Reach, the one the others are organs of: a metabolism with no animal in it anywhere. Cave feeds work; work warms cave; Keeper tends both; plants breathe the steam of the exchange; the small mechanism upstairs grooms it all and stands at the rail, looking down, satisfied. It declined fauna — that absence is a rule, you sensed it correctly — because animals run on the wrong clock. They are quick, hungry, loud, mortal at speeds the Undercroft finds vulgar. Everything that lives here lives at drip-tempo, at flowstone-tempo, at the tempo of a tally that gains one mark and a fern that gains one frond. Your heartbeat, in this town, is a racket. You are the loudest animal the cave has ever contained, and everything here knows where you are at all times, and waits, without impatience, in the steam and the ember-light, for you to leave so that the long work — the work of finishing its Keeper, of raising its first stone chimney, of warming itself into whatever a cave grows up to be — can go on uncounted.

The shipment by the kilns is not delayed. It is addressed to that day. It will keep.
