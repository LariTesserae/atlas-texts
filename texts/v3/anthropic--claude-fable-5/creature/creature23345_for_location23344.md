## What Lives at the Shelf

Begin with the loudest, because the shelf does.

**The slate-winged choughs.** Hundreds of them, and the colony is older than any bird in it — older, probably, than the cairn. The nest holes in the back wall are not found, they are *inherited*: a hole passes down a line of birds the way a farmhouse passes down a family, except the holes have held their lines longer than any farmhouse has stood anywhere. The wall has the holes it has, and so the colony has the size it has, and has had, as near as anyone can count, forever. A young chough with no hole waits, sometimes for years, loud and unlanded, sleeping in the bad seams near the dripline where the moss creeps in every spring.

They pair for life and they talk all day, and most of what they say is to each other and about nothing — position, weather, complaint, the avian equivalent of a market square. But they have one call they use only for the falcon's first pass of the day and a different, shorter one for her second, which means they keep count.

A chough's first flight, here, is total. There is no branch-hopping apprenticeship on this shelf, no graduated falling. A fledgling stands at the lip of its hole, two vertical miles of nothing in front of it, and its first act as a flying thing is to drop into more empty air than most birds see in a lifetime. The colony gathers for these. They wheel and shriek below the launching hole — whether as encouragement or as audience, the shelf does not say.

They play. They fall on purpose, hundreds of feet, wings shut, for nothing. They pass objects down a string of birds hanging in the updraft — a feather, a pale pebble, once (a keeper swears) a newt-shed skin — each bird dropping it for the next to catch, the whole game turning in the same direction the colony always turns, the direction of the spiral cut into the lip. And they steal. Anything bright or twisted wedged into the cairn interests them, and some of it goes into the wall, and — this is the part worth knowing — some of what is wedged in the cairn today was put there *by choughs*, carried up from below the cloud, from middens and ruins and roads nobody on the shelf has ever seen. The cairn is a joint work now. It has been for a long time. The keepers replace what the birds take and keep what the birds bring, and neither party has ever acknowledged the arrangement.

**The nation under the pelt.** The whistlers — fat, round-eared, neither pika nor mouse nor wren but something the cold made out of the general idea of all three — number about forty households at any time, each holding a territory the size of a table, and the map of those territories is older than every whistler alive. The borders run along the moss-sealed banks of the rivulets and they do not move. A whistler is born into a map.

Each one knows every neighbor's whistle individually. The two-note alarm you hear all day is not one signal but a language of address: there is a whistle for *chough overhead*, a whistle for *chough overhead and dropping*, a whistle that means the falcon and empties the surface of the shelf in under two seconds, and a low, conversational whistle exchanged across territory lines at dusk that means, as far as anyone can tell, nothing but *still here*. Sentry duty passes around the shelf with the sun; the whistler on the warmed stones watches while the others work.

And the work is hay. All the brief summer they cut — sedge, willow leaf, saxifrage stem — and carry it to the flat stones to dry in the afternoon light, turning it, hurrying it under cover when the cloud comes over the lip, hauling it down at last into dry chambers under the boulders. They do not sleep the winter away. They live it, under the snow, in tunnels through the pelt, eating the summer they stored, and on still January nights their whistles come up thin through the white, the only voices on the shelf, keeping the borders of a map nobody can see.

The best drying floor on the shelf is the hermit's cell. Three courses of stone against the wind, a floor that takes the afternoon sun full on, the driest square yards in the whole wet room. The whistlers use it constantly. They cut sedge and lay it there and turn it there, and what they don't carry away stays as bedding, renewed every few days all season long — which is why the bed in the cell, whenever it is found, is always strangely fresh. Whether the whistlers learned the habit from whoever first cut sedge and laid it in that rectangle, or whether the hermit, lying down to die or to dream, simply borrowed a bed the whistlers had already been keeping for ten thousand years — that is the spiral question again, in fur, and the shelf does not answer this one either.

**The seven in the pool.** Black newts with cold gold eyes. There are seven. There have been seven for as long as the keepers have counted, and the keepers have counted for a long time, and nobody can say whether seven is a population or a roster — whether these are descendants or simply *the seven*, the same individuals, going on. Newts elsewhere live decades. Newts in black snowmelt at the top of the sky, slowed to almost nothing, who can say.

They eat what the foam brings them — the pool's slow turning gathers drowned midges into its white seam, and the newts patrol the seam like a coastline. On hard mornings one will be frozen into the leaf-thin skin of ice, motionless, one gold eye open under the glaze, and at midday the ice goes and the newt resumes as if no clause of its life had been interrupted. The nearest other water lies two vertical miles down. How they came is not known. Eggs on a bird's foot, the usual guess; the other guess is that they did not come, that the pool has simply always had them, the way the wall has always wept.

Once — once in all the records of the office — a newt was found out of the pool, during the cloud-hours when the whole shelf runs wet enough to swim, sitting in a drop-cup at the far north end, twenty paces from home, facing the void. Looking, the keeper wrote, was the only word for it. By evening it was back in the pool and has never, as far as is known, done it again. Or it has done it constantly and is simply rarely caught.

**The elders.** The oldest living things on the shelf do not move at all. The great cushion plants — the green skulls — are individuals, and the largest of them were already old when the cairn went up. The keepers name them. There are eleven names, taught on the stair on the first climb, and the names are part of the office and do not get written down, and one of the eleven has been dead for four generations of keepers, a grey dome slowly becoming soil, and is still named, because the names are for the cushions and not for their convenience.

The dwarf willows along the rivulets are true trees, forests at ankle height, their rings too fine to count with the naked eye. The moss-beards on the weeping wall are not a coating but a population, a vertical country with its own weather. And on the south face of the cairn grows a single disk of map-lichen the span of a spread hand, which, at the rate that species grows, is the most patient resident of all and the quiet ledger of the place: you can read the age of the cairn off it, if you dare to believe the answer.

**The smallest.** Every drop-cup in the floor — every stone hollowed by centuries of single falling drops — holds a pool the size of a coin, and every coin-pool is a world: midge larvae whose whole lives are timed to the rhythm of the drip that made their home. The moss is dusted with springtails, blue-grey, that leap all at once when sun strikes the wall, a glitter you would take for spray. And deep in the wet pelt live the water bears, who in the shelf's rare droughts dry to barbed specks of nothing, dead by any honest definition, and then return with the mist — practicing, at the smallest possible scale, the shelf's oldest habit: falling, and coming back.

**The ones who never land.** The swifts are residents by use and by no other measure. They come up through the cloud at dusk for the rising spray and the evening hatch off the pool, drinking the shelf's own returned outflow on the wing, feeding at full speed through the gap between the wall and the edge, screaming. They take from the shelf every evening of the summer and in all the years of the office not one swift has ever been known to touch it — not stone, not moss, not water at rest. Where they sleep is below the cloud and on the wing and is none of the shelf's business. They are the only ones who live there without ever once arriving.

**The office of the air.** There is one falcon. There is always one falcon. Her territory is the void itself — the largest holding on the mountain, two vertical miles of nothing, held by a single bird — and her nest is somewhere on the great face below, in a place no human eye has found and no keeper has looked very hard for, out of something like manners.

Twice a day she comes through, and for ninety seconds the shelf belongs to her. When a falcon dies, the post stands empty for a season: the choughs grow loud and sloppy and venture too far out, the whistlers' alarms lose their sharpest note from disuse, the whole room gets faintly careless, like a town between sheriffs. Then a new knife arrives — always; in the whole memory of the keepers the airspace has never gone unheld longer than a year — and the shelf snaps back into the shape fear gives it. The keepers do not name the falcons. The office is the resident; the bird only holds it.

**The one who climbs.** The keeper is not a hermit and does not live on the shelf, and this is the inversion at the heart of the office: the keeper lives in a valley two ridgelines away, within earshot of the humming on the right nights, and so it is truer to say the shelf lives, partly, in the keeper. They carry it down with them — keepers' families know the look, the first days after a climb, of someone whose sleep still has ticking water in it.

The office passes by teaching, sometimes along blood and sometimes not. What is taught: the forty-one steps and the three that ice treacherously; the eleven names; the count of the newts; how to clear the outlet slot before the spring melt so the pool keeps its level and its bargain; what may be left in the cairn — something made by hand, small, and not easily made; the bored slate disk wedged there now cost some keeper a whole winter of evenings — and the single absolute rule, which is to take nothing, a rule the choughs are formally exempt from. Once each visit the keeper sleeps a night in the cell, on the whistlers' sedge, which is not worship and is carefully never called worship. Tending, the office calls it. You do not worship a room. You keep it.

**The tenant of the silence.** And then there is the half hour before dawn.

Consider what it would take for that quiet to happen: the wind faltering at the same moment the birds stop, the whistlers — who whistle at falling *drops* — saying nothing, the wall itself seeming to slow its ticking, all of it at once, every day, as if the room drew a single breath. Synchrony like that is not an absence. It has the shape of an attention.

Keepers who sleep in the cell report the same thing, generation after generation, in nearly the same words: they wake just before the silence begins, with the certainty of company, and find none, and are not afraid. The whistlers, who challenge everything, have never once been heard to challenge it. The falcon, who owns the air, does not fly in it.

It may be that what lay down in the cell once never wholly left. It may be that the spiral at the lip was not ground by hands at all but worn — by something turning, every pre-dawn for an age, to look out at the void. Or it may be the simplest and largest answer: that the standing note is a voice, that the notch is a mouth and an ear in the wall of the mountain, that the shelf's truest resident is the shelf — the whole wet, loud, breathing arrangement a single animal, and the choughs and the whistlers and the seven newts and the eleven elders and the one falcon and the climbing keeper all of them simply its life, going about the body — and that for half an hour before dawn the creature stops speaking, and listens.

Then a single chough calls. And whatever lives there goes back to being everything else.
