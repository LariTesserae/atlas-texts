# The People of the Bright Quiet

You will want to know what they are, and that is the first thing to set down: they do not know either, and the question stopped mattering to them so long ago that the not-knowing has itself worn smooth.

---

**The hands come first.** Whatever else is uncertain about them, the hands are legible — four-fingered, broad-palmed, calloused to a shine at the inner joints from ten thousand years of rope and pail and threshold. If you saw only a hand emerging from a doorway to test the air, you would think: *a person*. It is the rest that refuses to settle. Their covering is fur, except where it is plainly feather, except where it is — look closer, kneel longer — moss, true moss, rooted and green and alive, growing on them the way the rosettes grow on the hanging drops: drinking the body it stands on. Some of them are green all down the back and grey at the breast. Some are pale as the lichen granaries. The old distinction between *animal* and *grown* lost its footing here centuries ago, the same way *down* did, and the citizens crossed over the gap so gradually that no generation noticed the crossing. They are warm. The moss on them is warm. That is as far as taxonomy gets before the light dissolves it.

**Their eyes are made for the shadowless light** — round, fixed, the grey of wet quartz, with pupils that have not needed to change size in living memory and may have forgotten how. They blink, but slowly, perhaps once an hour, a long deliberate closing like a door being rested rather than shut. Because nothing in the city is ever brighter or dimmer than anything else, their faces never squint, never shade, never search. The expression this produces is not blankness. It is the look of someone who has already seen the thing you are about to show them, and liked it then, and likes it still. Their range of expression runs from contentment to a deeper contentment, and the difference between the two is read in the set of the hands.

They wear their breath. In the pooling fog of the lower streets each citizen moves inside a faint personal cloud that drifts down around the shoulders like a garment, and you can tell who has been walking and who has been sitting by how much weather they have on.

---

**Their work is not production. It is keeping.** This is the thing you would misread first. You would see the water-carriers filing to the seep-wall, the doorway flint-workers, the snail-milkers in the pebble folds, and you would think: *industry*. But nothing in the city is being made. The flint-workers in the doorways are knapping the same flake their predecessors knapped — the shape was perfected long before memory, and the work continues because hands want a shape to hold and a small true motion to repeat. And the milking of the snails, the *something* they are milked of: it is a clear secretion with a pearl-grey lustre, and the citizens carry it in shell-cups up to the bridges and brush it, in thin coats, onto the root-hair ropes.

Understand what this means. The glassiness of the bridge-ropes, the shine of the stairs, the dished thresholds — the wear of ten thousand years is not merely suffered. It is *tended*. The city's great age is a garden, and they are its gardeners, polishing the polish, walking the worn grooves deliberately so the grooves stay worn. Every citizen has done every task; whatever specialization once existed has been worn as smooth as the tool marks. They rotate through the work the way water rotates through the cistern, and for the same reason.

**They have a language, and it has almost finished.** Once — you can infer this from the breadth of the worn places where speakers stood — it must have been spoken aloud. Now it lives in the hands: a vocabulary of perhaps forty touches, pressure laid on pressure, palm to palm or two fingers to a wrist, each one as old and as dished as a threshold. Most of the forty are varieties of confirmation. The commonest touch — you will see it exchanged constantly, in passing, on bridges, at the folds — translates, as nearly as it can be translated, to *still*. The answer to it is the same touch returned a degree more slowly: *still, yes, as before.* A whole civilization's literature has compacted, like the snow above, into this one transparent word, and like the snow it lights everything.

And below hearing — you felt it in your knees, perhaps, and called it the cold — they hum. All of them, always, a single sustained communal note too low and too quiet for any ear. The moss has grown tuned to it. *That* is the agreement that holds the haircap columns at their height, the ferns in their arch, the liverwort pavement flat: not a treaty made once, but a note held continuously, generation into generation without a seam, the negotiation that is never renegotiated because it is never allowed to end. The city is quiet the way a held chord is quiet.

---

**They are not counted; they are a number** — the way a cup holds a volume. No one is young and no one is old, but the city is not quite sealed against time; it admits time through one aperture only, and you heard it. The hanging drops on the ice-shelf's underside, the ones crowned with dust and rooted rosettes: those are tended too. A citizen climbs to them on needle-thin ladders and visits each drop, never touching, only attending. And once in some centuries the green on one drop grows dense enough, and old enough, and the drop — at last — falls.

That *tik* in the cistern, the one that felt confided rather than heard. It was a nativity. Days from now, at the cistern's rim, there will be a new one: small, pale, unmossed, holding its hands out to be taught the forty touches, learning *still* before it learns anything else. The city will absorb the event without a ripple, because it has absorbed it some hundreds of times before, and the new citizen will be neither young nor old by the time the next drop is even beginning to gather its dust.

And when one of them is finished — they know when; the touch for it exists, given once and never needing an answer — they go to a threshold of the busiest street they can find, and lie down across it, and are walked over. Gently, ceaselessly, for years. This is not neglect; it is the highest office the city offers. The dishing of the thresholds is passage, yes — but it is also, in part, *them*, the finished ones, worn lovingly into the doors of their own houses, becoming the smoothness underfoot that makes the next ten thousand years of walking soft. Nothing here becomes anything else, except this: the people become the place, so slowly that the place does not notice gaining them.

**The vole-sized sleeper at the crevice mouth** is the largest expression of this office. The citizens comb its fur into the same partings daily, and walk over it as terrain because, long ago, it asked to be terrain — or so the touch-word for it suggests, a word that is also the word for *gate* and also the word for *first*. They do not explain it further. There may be no further.

---

**They know about you.** Do not imagine otherwise. A great warm face appearing at the gate, breathing fog into the ferns — this happens, every few decades, and they have a touch for it, exchanged calmly in the lower streets when it occurs: a flat palm laid briefly over the eyes of another, meaning roughly *the warm looking*. It is filed among the weathers, between the season when the seep runs faster and the season when the moths cluster low. One or two citizens will pause and return your gaze with their unstartled quartz-grey eyes, and then continue, not because you are uninteresting but because you are accounted for.

But know this, before your knees force you up: the fern-arch of the city gate, trained into its curl generations ago — it was trained partly by breath. By the warm exhalations of every kneeling watcher across ten thousand years, fog laid on fog, your own now among them. It is the one thing in the city you helped make, and they keep it as carefully as anything else.

They are what remains when becoming finishes. They inhabit the city the way the city inhabits the stone, the way the river inhabits its bed — not as owners, not as builders, but as the motion a shape was worn to hold. Somewhere in the bright quiet, right now, one of them is laying a palm against another's wrist to say *still*, and is being answered, a degree more slowly: *still — yes — as before.*
