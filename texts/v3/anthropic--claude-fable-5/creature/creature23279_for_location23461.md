## Who Lives in the Hollow Mountain

The question sounds simple until you try to answer it at any particular scale. Start small, then, with one body, and let the answer widen on its own.

---

**A swiftlet.** She is hatched in absolute darkness, in a cup of her parents' hardened saliva mortared to ten thousand identical cups, and she will never in her life see the structure she spends that life extending. What she has instead is sound. Before her eyes are open she has learned the signature of her parents' clicks against the ambient roar; before she can fly she is clicking herself, softly, mapping the few inches of wall around her nest. Her first flight is made blind, into a chamber two hundred feet across, on a map she has built entirely out of echoes — and she does not hit anything, and she finds her way back to one cup among ten thousand by the acoustic address of it, the way you would find your own front door.

She will go out at dawn for her whole life, eat flying insects above the forest canopy, and come back at dusk to a darkness she reads more fluently than you read this. In her head is a sound-portrait of the chambers — the slap of echo off the wet ceiling rind, the dead absorbency of the deep guano, the particular ring of the shaft she descends. No two birds carry the same portrait. The only complete image of the mountain's interior that has ever existed is distributed across a million small skulls, in pieces, and no one has ever seen it.

When she dies — taken off the wall by a snake, or simply old, fluttering down — she becomes floor. Her descendants will nest on a wall a feather's width thicker than the one she knew. That is her monument and she will never know she built it.

**The bats.** They are the other congregation, and in a real sense the swiftlets and bats do not live in the same place. They keep different chambers, different hours, different registers of sound — the birds' dry wooden clicking in the audible range, the bats' sonar pitched above it, two acoustic civilizations sharing one volume of dark and almost never touching. They know each other mainly as the river they pass through twice a day at the mouths, sorted in midair, millions of bodies in counterflow, by voice alone.

Deep in the warm core there are nursery ceilings: square yards of pink, furless pups packed shoulder to shoulder at blood heat, the warmest surfaces in the mountain. A mother returning from the night will land on a ceiling of fifty thousand infants and find hers — by voice first, then by smell, walking upside down across the squalling congregation until the right cry resolves out of the noise. She does this every night without failing. The roar that deafens you is, to her, legible. It is full of names.

**The floor.** Here it stops being useful to count individuals. The brown-black tide of cockroaches grazing the fresh guano behaves less like a population than like a tissue — the living surface of the floor, a skin that eats. Everything that falls from the ceiling falls into it: droppings, feathers, dropped insects, nestlings that miss their first flight. A fallen bird is reduced in hours; bone whitens and is gone within the week. The floor is the mountain's stomach, and it is never not digesting.

The things that live in it have never perceived what feeds them. The crickets with their forearm-long antennae, the blind white crabs in the drip-pools, the amber centipedes — none of them has any sense of the colony roaring forty feet overhead. Their world is a warm dark plain on which food falls steadily out of an imperceptible sky. Their weather is droppings. They are the most numerous inhabitants of the mountain and they do not know the mountain exists.

**The patient ones.** The racer snakes hanging in the nest-masonry are functionally blind and entirely indifferent to the fact. Each one keeps a crevice — the same crevice, in some cases, for decades — at a pinch point where the traffic of wings is dense, and waits, and strikes at the pressure-wave of a body passing. They miss most of the time. It does not matter. The traffic is infinite and they are in no hurry; a snake here is less a hunter than a toll, a fixed cost the colony pays at certain corners, as regular as the dripping.

The whip spiders are stiller yet, flattened in the seams between nests, moving perhaps a few feet in a night. They live at the mountain's own tempo. Watching one, you understand that patience here is not a strategy but a physics: where food falls forever, the optimal speed approaches zero.

**The old ones.** Go deeper — past the last occupied wall, past the last fresh guano, into chambers the colony abandoned a thousand years ago or never claimed — and you find the mountain's permanent population. White, eyeless things: a cricket bleached to translucence, a millipede like a thread of paraffin, a fish the length of your finger hanging motionless in a black pool, so pale you can see its heart working. They live on seepage and crumbs, on the thin organic film that trickles down from the riot above, and they live slowly — metabolisms banked down to embers, lifespans that may run to decades for an insect.

Many of them exist nowhere else. Not in the next mountain, not in the next chamber sometimes: whole species whose entire global range is one room of one cave, who have been speciating quietly in the dark since before the first swiftlet ever clicked its way in. To them, the colony — the roar, the guano, the ten-thousand-year construction project — is recent. It is, on their timescale, a weather system that moved in and has not yet moved out. They were the residents before it came. They are politely waiting.

**The smallest.** And below everything, in the compacted depths of the guano where it has pressed itself into dark soft stone, live the inhabitants without bodies to speak of: the bacteria and fungi doing the final accounting. The deep floor is warm, and the warmth is not geological — it is metabolic, the heat of digestion, a million years of imported sunlight being slowly burned down to mineral. The air down there is ammonia sharp enough to etch metal, and there are organisms for whom that air is the whole hospitable universe, who can live in this chemistry and no other, and who therefore live here and nowhere. The mountain breathes warm because, at the bottom, it is still eating.

**The people.** They live at the edges, and they would tell you that themselves. A village downslope; hereditary rights to particular walls, kept not on paper but in names — every ledge and shaft and harvesting station named, the names strung into recitations that are, in effect, the deed. Rattan ladders and bamboo poles lashed to the nest-rind, some of them a century old, climbed in season by men whose grandfathers lashed them. They take the white nests in the building season and leave the brood season strictly alone, a covenant with the birds older than anyone's memory of its making, enforced now by nothing except the understanding that it has always held and that the mountain pays out forever only if it is never asked for everything at once.

They do not go past the light. Not from fear, exactly — they will go anywhere a ladder reaches in the twilight zone — but the deep dark they regard as occupied, the way you would not walk uninvited through someone's house. They have a word for the roar, the sustained note of the whole colony, and the word is not a word for *noise*. It is closer to a word for *voice*. They are the only inhabitants of the mountain who have a name for the whole of it, and they are the only ones who insist they merely live next door.

---

**The one.** So ask the question again — who lives in the hollow mountain — and notice what has assembled while you weren't deciding.

No single creature in it perceives it. The swiftlet knows her echoes, the bat knows her pup's voice, the cockroach knows the falling weather, the white fish knows its pool, the harvester knows his named wall at the edge of the light. The mountain itself is perceived by nothing — and yet it behaves, unmistakably, like a living thing. It breathes: that warm wet exhalation that feeds the cloud forest is the pooled body heat of millions of lungs and the fermentation heat of the floor, the literal breath of the aggregate. It eats: sunlight, gathered above the canopy in a hundred million insect-sized bites, swallowed at dusk and dawn through its mouths. It has a pulse — the arrhythmic drumming of the drips — and a memory, written in strata of guano and laminated nest-rind, forty feet deep and still being written. It grows a feather's width a year. It has been alive, by any honest definition, for ten thousand years, and it is made of inhabitants the way you are made of cells: none of them aware of the whole, all of them building it.

That is who lives there. One resident, with a hundred million bodies, on a single long breath.

It has never seen itself. Standing in the great chamber, in the slanted column of gold falling from the shaft, with the bats stitching across the light and the roar pressing on your sternum — you are, for as long as you stand there, the nearest thing the mountain has ever had to an eye. It does not know you are there. But everything it is passes through you anyway, and when you climb back out into the forest its breath made, you carry the only picture of it that exists.
