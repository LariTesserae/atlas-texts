**The Crestfolk**

You will mistake the first one you see for a fault in the rock. That is not an insult to your eyes; it is the whole design of the body. They are the color of the place — skin gone the grey of slate-light with an underglow of furnace orange, as if they had been dyed by the only two lights they have ever known, dusted always with rock-flour and soot until the line between person and wall is a matter of motion. And so you find them by motion: a shape detaching from a girder, a figure walking a chain with its arms out, suddenly and obviously alive. Then you see another. Then forty. Then you realize the chamber's seething is made of them, that the anthill behind glass has faces, and that several of those faces have been watching you since you stepped in sideways through the seam.

They are small and they are dense. Generations at eight kilometers have built them like the city builds itself: compact, redundant, braced. The chest is a barrel out of proportion to the frame — lungs like bellows, working slow and enormous even at rest, so that to stand near one is to be aware of a deep tidal breathing, a person-sized version of the furnaces. Their blood is dark, nearly black at a cut, thick with whatever it carries in place of enough air. Their hands are the most articulate part of them: broad-palmed, long-fingered, callused to a smoothness like worn brass, with a grip that is less a grasp than a verdict. A Crestfolk child can hang by one hand from a cable for the length of a shift change and be bored doing it. They walk on the chain bridges without holding anything, because their feet hold; they go barefoot or in soft split-toed leathers on the high steel, and their toes are nearly as clever as their fingers, an adaptation the long-toed lamp-lizards would recognize as kin.

Their eyes are large and slow-blinking, the pupils opened permanently wide for the dim orange-and-slate gloom, with an extra translucent lid that flicks sideways against grit and welding-flash. They see almost nothing of color as you understand it — the world above the snowline is white and the world inside the seam is ember and shadow, and their vision spent its inheritance elsewhere. What they bought with it is *flicker*. A Crestfolk eye resolves the stutter of a failing carbide lamp into individual events. They read a lamp-flash sentence across two hundred meters of scaffolded dark the way you read a printed page, and they can tell, from the trembling of a light, whether the hand that holds it is tired, or cold, or lying.

Because that is what the lights are: they are the voice. Understand this before anything else about them. The thin air starved their speech in the cradle of their history, and they did not fight it — they abandoned the air as a medium almost entirely and rerouted language through light and metal. Lamp-flash is their public tongue, a grammar of duration and rhythm and occlusion, spoken with shutters, with mirrors, with the deliberate passing of a hand before a flame. It has registers: the broad slow strokes of work-signal, the rapid intimate flicker used between friends on opposite tiers, an oratorical mode — long, banked, glowing phrases — used for announcements and for grief. The wires are the other tongue. The whole city is strung with tensioned cable, and a plucked guy-wire sends its note through the rock itself, felt in the soles of the feet, in the teeth, in the breastbone. Wire-speech is slower and deeper and cannot be aimed; everyone in the fissure feels every word of it, and so it is reserved for what concerns everyone: the shudder-warnings, the births, the deaths, the great chords struck when a gallery is condemned. Their names are wire-notes. A Crestfolk's true name is a pitch and an interval, plucked; the lamp-name is a flicker-sign derived from it; the spoken name, if one exists at all, is a translation for the benefit of the funicular traders, and they wear it loosely, like borrowed clothes.

They do have voices. This is worth knowing, because it tells you what intimacy means to them. The thin air carries a whisper perhaps an arm's length, and so the spoken voice — breathy, soft, oddly high — is by physics alone a private channel. To speak aloud to a Crestfolk is to be close enough to share warmth. Speech is for lovers, for the sickbed, for a parent's mouth against a child's hair, for the words you say to someone whose hands you are holding. They find the lowland habit of *shouting* — of throwing the intimate voice across open space at strangers — genuinely indecent, the way you might regard someone undressing in a market. The few Crestfolk who ride the funicular down on trade business return shaken less by the thick drunken air than by the noise: a world where everyone is screaming endearments at everyone, all the time, and no one seems to notice.

Their society is organized the way the chamber is organized, which is to say vertically, and by crew. A Crestfolk belongs to a gang — riggers, breakers, founders, lamplighters, the canister-wardens who rule the water — and the gang is the unit of loyalty, of meals, of mourning. But the deeper structure, the thing that shapes them the way the seam shapes the city, is the law they bend every other law around, the same law the mountain taught the architecture: *nothing is permitted to be finished.* They hold no permanent property because there is no permanence to hold it in. A dwelling is a current arrangement. A masterwork is a structure built knowing it will be condemned, and built true anyway. The highest compliment in lamp-flash, given to a person or a weld or a marriage, translates as something like *plumb for now* — and they mean it without irony or sadness, because "for now" is the only tense the mountain has ever offered them, and they have decided to find it sufficient.

So they measure their lives in buildings raised and unbuilt. Ask a Crestfolk elder her age and she will flash you not a number of years but a count of *rebuildings* — the great cyclical condemnations and reconstructions of the major galleries, which come irregularly, as the rock decides. A child comes of age by joining the crew that demolishes the dormitory cell they were born in; they pry it apart themselves, plate by plate, salvage the girders, and carry one rivet away from the wreck. Old Crestfolk wear long cords strung with such rivets, dozens of them, each one a room or a bridge or a winch-house that existed and was theirs and is gone, and they will tell you the cord's whole story in flicker if you have a free evening and a steady lamp. They are, as a people, almost incapable of nostalgia and almost made of memory — they grieve nothing and they forget nothing, and they do not seem to find this a contradiction.

Water is their gold, their sacrament, and their tenderness. The sealed copper canisters come up on the funicular and pass into the custody of the wardens, who are the nearest thing the fissure has to a priesthood — not because they are holy but because they weigh. Every soul in the city has a ration measured in grams, and the body of a Crestfolk has met that scarcity halfway: they sweat almost nothing, their breath barely fogs metal, they can work a full shift on a few swallows. They never spill. Watch one drink — the small copper cup held in both hands, the slow attention, the cup tipped fully and then *waited over*, for the last clinging drop — and you will understand that you are watching the closest thing they have to prayer. Their tears, when they come, come rarely and are not wiped away. To weep at a funeral is to give water to the dead, the one extravagance their economy honors; mourners stand close, and the drops fall on the wrapped body, and nobody — in a city where moisture outvalues silver — would dream of calling it waste.

The dead go down. This is the secret kindness of the geometry: the seam widens above, where the city climbs, but in the deep old reaches below the lowest forge, the rock is closing — the same slow tectonic breath, exhaling. There are crevices down there narrowing by centimeters a year, and into them the Crestfolk carry their dead, wrapped and dried and folded small, and set them in the pinch of the stone. The mountain closes over them. In a decade the gap is a seam; in a lifetime it is a line; eventually it is nothing, and the dead are *inside*, pressed into the body of the youngest rock in the world and rising with it, a few centimeters a year, toward the sky. They find this the opposite of frightening. Lowland burial — the dead put in loose dirt, in *wet* ground, abandoned to rot — strikes them as a horror almost beyond flicker. Their own people are becoming the mountain. The wire-chord struck at a sealing translates, roughly: *carried up.*

Their beliefs are few and load-bearing. They do not exactly worship the mountain, the way you do not exactly worship the weather; it is too present for worship, too obviously indifferent, too obviously alive. They regard it as a creature mid-birth — eight kilometers of intention, and themselves a colony of attendants living in a fold of its skin, picking its silver the way the slate beetles pick the sulfur crusts, paying for the privilege in vigilance. There are listeners among them, an informal order, old riggers mostly, who press their teeth to steel rods set in the living rock and spend hours reading the ticking and the groan — they can tell a settling from a warning four days out, and the city has not lost a crew to an unannounced slab in living memory. The swifts are their clock: the boiling silent clouds pour out through the sky-crack at hours no instrument predicts, but the Crestfolk have watched them for generations and read the eruptions like an almanac — *the birds went out angry today; check the upper anchors.* And the folded leathery shape that roosts in the topmost cables, where the seam narrows toward the white sky — that one they do not name, do not study, and do not disturb. It was here, the elders flash, before the first lamp. The riggers working the high wires leave a swift, freshly taken, on a particular bracket on particular days, and whether this is religion or rent, they decline to specify. They suspect it has watched the whole city happen — every gallery raised and unbuilt, every generation, the entire frantic improvised civilization — the way they themselves watch the voles: with patience, without judgment, occasionally eating one.

And they hold one belief about the future, their only one, told in the slow banked oratorical flicker on the nights when the rock groans loudest. The crest is still rising. The seam still widens. Someday — not soon, not in any cord-of-rivets lifetime, but someday — the crack will open past some final balance, the blade of the summit will shear and shed, and the closed book of the chamber will open to the full sky. On that day there will be no city, because there will be no fissure; the hidden room will become an ordinary high notch on an ordinary great mountain, drowned in white light and weather. They do not tell this as doom. They tell it the way other peoples tell of paradise. Everything they have ever built was built to be unbuilt; they are, they reason, a temporary scaffold themselves, the formwork around something the mountain is making — and when the form comes off, the work stands, and the workers were never meant to stay. The lamp-phrase for it is the longest fixed expression in their language, a slow glowing sentence that takes a full minute to say, and the traders at the bottom of the funicular line, who have seen it flashed down the cable on festival nights and asked what it meant, were given this translation, and no other:

*The mountain rises. The city climbs. And one morning the door will be the whole sky, and whoever is here will simply step out.*

Until then they are here — breathing their slow enormous breath, walking the chains with their arms out, talking in light across the hush, plumb for now.
