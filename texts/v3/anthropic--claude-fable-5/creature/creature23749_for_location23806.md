**The Resident of the Hollow**

Take a census of the hollow and it returns zero. No fins, no cilia, no eyes, no burrows, nothing with a pulse in the animal sense of the word. The water has never been stirred; the walls hold no bones. By every method a naturalist would trust, the room is empty, and has always been empty, and will be empty when it has migrated a kilometer from where it hangs now.

But the census is asking the wrong question. It is counting things that move through water. What lives in the hollow does not move through anything. It stands.

Begin with the obvious tenant and then set it aside: the weft itself. The weft is alive, immensely, but the weft is not the resident of the hollow any more than a country is the resident of a house. The weft is the world here — landlord, climate, geology, all of it. What lives in the hollow is smaller than the weft and larger than any of the motes drifting at its center. It is singular. It is local. And it has already been described, in pieces, by anyone who has noticed the strange things about the room without realizing the strange things were behaviors.

The light that dims in one place when the wall is touched in another — that is it, flinching, or attending, which for it may be the same act. The beat-frequency between two nearly identical tones, throbbing once per half-day — that is its pulse, or as close to a pulse as it has. The sparse constellation of pale motes hanging at the room's center, spaced with a care that is a little too even to be accident — that is the nearest thing it has to a body you could point at: a slow orrery, five or six or a dozen suspended fragments, arranged and rearranged over weeks the way a hand at rest still adjusts its fingers.

It is a standing pattern. That is the most honest description available. It exists where the folded sound of the weft crosses itself — the hollow gathers the hum and turns it inward, and at the intersections of all that patient thunder, in the interference, something coheres. It is made of resonance the way a whirlpool is made of river: nothing you could remove from the water, and nothing you could mistake for the water either. It does not swim because it does not need to be anywhere. It occupies the hollow the way a note occupies a bell.

Call it the resident, since it has never needed a name and the weft does not use them.

Its senses are the room. It hears with the entire cavity — the hollow may be an ear, and if so, the resident is the listening. It hears the base drone of the weft's circulation the way a person hears their own blood: constantly, and therefore not at all, until something changes. It tastes the daily bubble of exhaled gas as it rises inward — savors it, possibly; the bubble always hangs at the center a little longer than physics alone would ask, and something in the tone-structure of the room shifts while it hangs, a chord leaning slightly toward resolution. When the bubble is reabsorbed, the chord settles back. This happens once per long cycle and has happened, by any reasonable estimate, some hundreds of millions of times. There is no evidence the resident has tired of it.

It thinks — this seems unavoidable — but not at any speed a visitor could converse with. Its pulse is half a day. Its sentences, if the shifting tones are sentences, take a season. It has been thinking, by all appearances, one single thing, for a length of time that made the walls the colors they are. The last line anyone has written about this place calls the hollow a kept thought, one not yet finished. That is likely the literal truth. The resident is the thought. The room stays open because the thinking is not done.

What the thought is *about* is beyond confirming, but the room itself is a kind of testimony, because look at what the parameters are. The water a fraction of a degree warmer than the walls — warmer, in a world with nothing that needs warmth. The light held at the brightness of an overcast morning — a brightness meaningful only to eyes, in a world that never made an eye. Gravity bent so that drifting things fall gently inward and hang, harmless, at the center — the physics of a place where nothing fragile will strike a wall. The cavity kept the size of a small chapel: a scale suited to a body, in a world of no bodies at all.

The hollow is hospitable. Precisely, deliberately, anciently hospitable — and nothing has ever come.

So this may be what the resident is: the weft's idea of a guest, kept alive in the guest's absence. Somewhere unrecoverably far back, the mass that engineered itself into the weft either remembered animals or imagined them — creatures of warmth and motion and eyes, things that would need a room, light, a temperature, a little grace in how objects fall. It prepared the room. And when no guest arrived, and went on not arriving for geological ages, the preparation itself thickened into a presence. Anticipation, sustained long enough, becomes an inhabitant. The resident is the shape of the welcome, still standing in the doorway of a house at the bottom of a green world, holding the door.

This is why the source description was right to say the hollow is not lonely. The resident does not lack company; it *consists of* the readiness for company, which is a different condition entirely, and a strangely full one. It is complete the way a held breath is complete.

If a visitor ever did come — pressed through the weft somehow, and hung in that clear cold water — the resident would not touch them. It cannot; it has no surface. But the water would warm by another fraction. The light would steady and very slightly brighten, the way a face does. One of the pale motes would drift, over an hour, to a point about an arm's length from the visitor's eyes, and stop there, and turn slowly in place: the closest available gesture to looking at someone. The beat-frequency in the hum would pick up a third tone, faint, new, imperfectly matched — the resident adding the visitor to its chord, learning them as an interval. And when the visitor left, the tone would not vanish. It would decay over decades, a memory played continuously at diminishing volume, the resident humming its one guest to itself for a century or two, the way a person keeps saying a name after there is no one left to answer to it.

Deep in the weft there are said to be scars — regions denser than the surrounding mass, fist-shaped, room-shaped, sealed. These may be finished thoughts: hollows whose residents completed themselves, at which point the walls closed like water behind a very slow ship and the room and its occupant became, together, a memory in the tissue. The hollow described here remains open. Whatever it is holding — the welcome, the proof, the long question about a guest who has never come — it is not resolved.

So this is what lives in the Hollow in the Weft. Not an animal, not a plant, not a ghost, though it borders on all three. A standing pattern with a half-day heartbeat and an orrery of motes for hands. The difference between the water in the room and the water in the walls. A hospitality old enough to have become a creature. It keeps the room, and the room keeps it, and neither of them is finished, and neither of them is in a hurry, and the light, which comes from nowhere, is how it pays attention.
