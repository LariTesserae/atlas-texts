What lives in the Pleach is easiest to describe in order of visibility, which is to say: starting from almost nothing and working down.

**The green orders.** The most numerous inhabitants are the ones travelers stop seeing within an hour, the way you stop seeing grass. Moss covers every horizontal span and most of the vertical ones, dense and dark and drinking the perpetual condensate, and it is not passive upholstery: the moss grows in long combed directions that do not follow water or light, and old walkers read it the way sailors read swell — the grain of the moss in a chamber points, reliably, *somewhere*, and the somewheres agree with each other across leagues. Whether the moss is obeying some tension in the wood beneath it or has opinions of its own is not settled. It heals over the builders' tool-marks slowly and reluctantly, last of all, as if it understood that the marks were writing.

The hanging pools each wear a skin of green, and each skin appears to be a single organism — one continuous sheet, lens-fitted to its pool, growing inward from the bark rim over decades until it closes like an eye. Young pools are bright and translucent. Old pools are opaque, dark, leathery at the edges, and nothing grazes them anymore. What goes on beneath a closed skin is unknown, because nothing in the Pleach opens what the Pleach has closed, including the travelers, who learn this etiquette quickly or not at all.

And the gulfs themselves are not empty. The great voids hold a thin, drifting population of spores, motes, threadlike larvae, gauze-bodied drifters a finger long that have never in their lives touched a solid thing — a plankton of the air. The dim grey-gold light catches them at the bright knots and they glow there briefly, circulating with the interior weather. The voids, in other words, are not the absence of habitat. They are the open water of this place. Everything else should be understood accordingly.

**The gulf fauna.** The pale gliders are the petrels of that inner sea. Long-bodied, sail-ribbed, silent past the point of plausibility — their flight surfaces are fringed like an owl's but finer, a fringe upon a fringe — they cross the chambers in slow straight lines, sieving the drift-plankton, and they are white because nothing in the Pleach has hunted by sight for a very long time. Most of them do land, eventually, on the undersides of spans, hanging inverted in the warm wet dark to sleep and breed; their young drop into the gulf to fly before they can do anything else. A few of the very oldest, travelers insist, have stopped landing altogether. There is a story, told in several versions, of a glider seen crossing one of the largest chambers and then seen again, at the same moment, crossing it from the opposite direction — the same animal, identifiable by a notch in its sail, meeting itself mid-gulf and passing itself without acknowledgment. The gliders have lived with the folds longer than anyone. They have stopped finding themselves interesting.

In the deepest and largest chambers — the ones where the far wall is only a darker quality of the dark — something occasionally interrupts the light. Not a shape: an interruption, a long slow occlusion of the dim knots, one after another, in sequence, as if a great body were passing between you and them at a distance the chamber should not be able to contain. The gliders go up into the lattice when this happens and wait. So should you.

**The drinkers.** The soft-shelled grazers of the pool-skins are met by almost no one, because they have organized their entire existence around the drip. Each chamber of the Pleach has its own pattern of condensate — this junction sheds a drop every forty heartbeats, that leaf ticks twice and rests — and the drinkers carry the whole score in their bodies. They are nearly eyeless, low, deliberate; they graze the green lenses in tightening spirals that read, in lantern light, like patient handwriting; and they vanish into the weave the moment a single drop falls wrong. Your weight on a span a hundred yards away changes the rhythm. They hear you in the water before you have made a sound. The only way to watch them is to stand still long enough that your dripping becomes part of the chamber's — most travelers report this takes the better part of a night, and that the drinkers, once they accept you, will graze across your boots.

**The herds.** The great animals of the deep galleries are the Pleach's most thoroughly evidenced and least witnessed inhabitants. Their roads are broad, polished, generations old. Their dung feeds half the small life of the floor-weave. Their shed armour — plates of bark-coloured horn the size of doors — turns up wedged in junctions, and here is the detail that the cloudside people will only discuss reluctantly: the inner faces of the old plates carry ridges. Long, regular, scarred-over ridges, in pairs and wraps, of the kind that grow over a binding. As if the animals had once been lashed — or had once worn, for working purposes, harness.

They keep a chamber or two ahead of any walker, always, and the usual explanation is vibration through the wood. The better explanation comes from the few who have followed a herd for weeks at a time and mapped its road afterward: the roads do not follow forage, or water, or ease of passage. The roads trace lines. Long deliberate curves, returns, junctions-to-be — the geometry, several such travelers have independently concluded, of chambers that do not yet exist. The herds walk the blueprint. Whether they remember it, or read it out of the tensions in the wood the way the moss does, or are being walked along it by something that remembers on their behalf, the effect is the same: where the herds have gone, the weave's slow finger-width-a-year growth follows, bending true.

They are gentle, by all evidence. The aftermaths of predation found along their roads are never of them.

**The fold-fledged.** The small bird-like knot-travelers are the only animals here that could be called busy, and even their busyness is mostly elsewhere. Their song arrives out of knots — a bird singing apparently at your shoulder is a mile away and several folds deep, and the singers seem to know it, ducking in and out of junctions to throw their voices across distances they will never fly. They court through shared knots, a male on one side of a fold and a female on the other, sometimes never meeting in the flat sense at all. Their nests are woven with the builders' own techniques, indistinguishable from the architecture except by warmth, and possibly not except: there is a school of thought among the cloudside people that the birds did not learn splicing from the structure but the reverse, that the builders' whole art began as observation of a small impatient ancestor of these.

Their life cycle is scrambled, and they are placid about it. A fledgling's first dive into a knot is a one-way education; some come back in an hour, some in years, some come back older than their parents. Their flocks contain grandmothers younger than their grandchildren, and their social order has dispensed with seniority entirely — rank goes by weathering, by how grey and travel-worn the feathers are, regardless of when the bird was hatched. The very old ones stop flying. They go fat and fearless and travel only by knots, appearing on a strut beside you, regarding you with an expression of complete familiarity, and stepping back into the wood.

**The Patience.** The ambush animal of the longer-than-their-walls passages is never described twice the same way, and the reason, once understood, is worse than the descriptions. Each survivor saw a different cross-section. A wall of pelt. A limb without a body. A mouth, open, unattached to anything, at the height of a mouth. The accounts disagree because no single chamber of ordinary space contains the whole of it; it lies along the borrowed direction the way an eel lies along a crevice, and what intrudes into the passage — into the part of the passage that is longer than its wall, the surplus footage that exists nowhere — is whatever part of it is nearest.

It does not chase. It has never been recorded chasing. The geometry feeds it: prey walking forward through a long passage delivers itself, and the Patience's contribution is to be there, which it does superbly and perhaps continuously. It may be one individual. All the longer-inside passages may open onto the same surplus interior, and the Patience may be distributed through the whole of it, a single tenant of the Pleach's entire hidden footage, eating perhaps a few times a year, in total silence, for centuries. The builders made those passages on purpose; the bends are deliberate, the splices fine work. Either they made them for it, the way one builds a barn knowing owls will come, or it moved into the finished structure the way owls do. The cloudside people decline to guess, and tie no bark-strips near the long passages, and consider it bad manners to say its name — they have one — anywhere the wood can hear, which is everywhere.

**The eaves people.** Where you come up through cloud, in the warm shallows of the underside, live the only settled people of the Pleach: a few thousand of them, perhaps, strung through the lowest galleries in bands of twenty and thirty. They do not claim to be the builders' descendants. They do not claim not to be. They keep the techniques alive at miniature scale — baskets, cradles, splints, fish-weirs let down through the floor-weave into the cloud — and their masters can sit before an ancient junction and read the order of operations in it, this limb bent first, this one held in tension for the life of the binder and tied off by the binder's child, and weep, quietly, at the patience of it, which they say is the only correct response and the real purpose of the craft.

They make no maps. Their routes are stories, told exactly, and a route retold wrongly stops working — whether because the teller then walks it wrongly or because the Pleach holds tellers to their word is, again, not settled, and the eaves people see no difference between the two explanations. They have an etiquette for the repeated junction and the places like it: if you meet yourself, do not speak first; let the older one speak; the older one knows what was said. They give their dead to the weave, lashed into the cupped junctions in green withies, and over decades the structure draws them in as it draws in its own failed spans. The ancestor-knots are warm — warmer than the surrounding wood, measurably, for a generation or more — and the eaves people sit against them in the evenings and talk, not to them exactly, but in their presence, on the understanding that resorption is slow and hearing is probably the last thing to go.

Some of them deep-walk. It is a vocation, marked young, and the ones who go come back out of sync — a season's journey returning decades older, or a decade's journey returning by suppertime, confused by the soup. And some never finish coming back: there are bark-strips in the deep weave, at junction after junction across distances no single life could cover, all tied with the same knot, in the same hand, weathered to every age from green to grey. The eaves people speak of these walkers in a tense their language keeps for things that are happening, have happened, and have not begun.

**The makers.** Which brings the question around, as everything in the Pleach comes around. Whoever bent the first limbs is gone, the description says, or so far away in the weave that the distinction doesn't matter — and the inhabitants, taken together, are the argument that the distinction doesn't matter.

Consider the evidence as the eaves people lay it out. The herds, walking blueprints, wearing scarred-over harness-galls under their armour: beasts of the work, still pacing out the plan, with or without anyone holding the line. The absorbed awls and cordage in the oldest splices — and, in two or three junctions that the eaves people will show a trusted guest, absorbed shapes that are not awls, long shapes, articulated, folded into the grain with what can only be called tenderness. The fresh green lashings, sap still bright, in chambers that cannot be found twice. The structure's unaccountable warmth, which thin air at this altitude should not permit, and which is exactly the warmth of the ancestor-knots: the warmth of something taken in and not yet finished with.

So the possibilities are these, and the Pleach endorses none of them. The makers finished their portion and stepped along the borrowed direction to where the structure is older, and they are out there now, inspecting the result, working back toward the beginning at one bend per generation — the fresh lashings are them, everywhere at once, sparsely. Or the makers bent themselves last, the final splices autobiographical, and the whole structure is what they grew into, in which case the question *will they return* mistakes the grammar of the situation entirely. Or the makers were never separate from the work in the first place — were the herds, were the birds, were the patience itself in some earlier and busier form — and "builder" was always just the word for whatever the Pleach was currently doing its growing through.

**The largest inhabitant.** Because that is the last thing to understand about what lives here, and most travelers come to it slowly, somewhere out on a single span over a gulf with its own weather: the Pleach is not a place with residents. It is the senior resident. It breathes on a daily rhythm; it runs warm; it digests its failures and keeps its dead close; it shifts its weight, once an hour, with a long soft creak; and the far-off resonant note that no one can place is, by the eaves people's account, the structure touching itself where two of its folded distances meet — a sleeper's habit, the way a sleeping body confirms the position of a limb.

Everything else — the moss reading its tensions, the herds pacing its intentions, the birds threading its knots, the Patience keeping its surplus rooms, the people sitting against its warm junctions in the permanent evening — lives the way gut flora and skin fauna and old friends live: inside, and along with, and at the sufferance of, and in genuine mutual regard with, a single very large and very slow living thing that is still, finger-width by finger-width, following its instructions.

What it is growing toward, nothing inside it knows. But nothing inside it is anxious about that, the drinkers least of all, and the eaves people have a saying that serves as well as anything for a census of the place: everything that lives in the Pleach lives inside one patience, and the patience is in no hurry, and neither, after a while, are you.
