**The Inhabitants of the Working Heath**

The first answer is the obvious one, and it is wrong in an instructive way: the heath lives there. But that is like saying a house lives at its address. The heath does not live *on* the plateau; the plateau is what the heath's living has amounted to. So hold the question open a little longer, and start instead with what can be said honestly — that the heath is the only thing in that place which is entirely at home, and that everything else there lives on it, off it, under it, or briefly and politely alongside it, the way a handful of small lives arrange themselves around a very large one.

Begin with the large one anyway, because it sets the terms.

The heath has no center. You can walk it for days and you will not find a heart, a nerve-knot, a chamber where the decisions are kept. Its decisions are made the way erosion makes decisions — everywhere at once, by accumulation, with no moment you could point to and say *there, it chose*. And yet it has what you would have to call a temperament. It is thrifty past the point of obsession; it tolerates no waste, not a shed scale, not a drop of vented water it can recover. It is patient on a scale that makes the word meaningless. And it is *hospitable in the manner of a ledger*: it does not notice its guests the way you notice things, with attention; it notices them as entries. Your weight on a cable is registered somewhere in the tension of the whole, compensated for, and forgotten. This is not coldness. It is simply that the heath's regard is distributed across ten thousand domes, and no single one of them is responsible for looking at you.

Whether it thinks is the wrong question. Whether it *works* is not, and the answer is visibly yes, and a thing that works has, at minimum, the inner life of its work: pressure to be maintained, water to be moved, a district to be torn down before frost finds the rot in it. If the heath dreams, it dreams in load-bearing terms.

Now the tenants.

The grey riders — you saw three in a day, which is about right; there are never many — are the heath's only mammals, and they have made a strange bargain with geometry. A rider's territory is not an area. It is a line. Each family holds a cable, or a junction of cables, and the family's whole world is that moving stem: its bark, its tension, its destinations. They are built for it — low-slung, dense-furred against the constant near-frost, with splayed gripping feet that can walk slowly against the cable's motion to stay in one place, or simply sit and be carried, which is what they mostly do, with an expression of complete unhurried propriety, like commuters who have ridden the same line all their lives.

They eat the flaking bark, and the small mites that live in the bark, and in doing so they keep the cables clean where they cross each other — the worn pulley-points where stem runs over stem. It is not nothing. It may be why they are tolerated: a maintenance crew so small the heath barely has to account for it. They observe the terms of their tenancy with what looks like instinct and is probably older than instinct: they never burrow. Not once, not anywhere. To burrow into a cushion would be to breach a pressure vessel, and whatever happened to the lineages that tried it happened long enough ago that no rider alive carries the temptation. They nest instead in the seams where domes have merged, and in old vent-pores gone cold — the heath's abandoned chimneys, dry and dead and therefore, by the heath's own law, no longer anyone's business.

Their genius — the thing that lets anything warm-blooded survive a landscape in permanent self-demolition — is in their feet. The heath signals through tension. A district scheduled for digestion slackens hours before it falls; a cable about to be rerouted develops a particular irregular thrum as new growth takes up its load elsewhere. The riders read all of this through their soles, asleep or awake. They evacuate before the collapse, calmly, in single file along the doomed line, carrying their young by the scruff, and they are never wrong, because they are not predicting the heath — they are overhearing it. They have lived so long inside its bookkeeping that its intentions reach them as sensation. They make no sound of their own. There is nothing they would need to say that the cable has not already said.

The bird is a different arrangement, and a stranger one.

There is, as far as you will ever be able to tell, one bird — or rather one *at a time*: a dark, sooty, long-winged thing that comes up out of the cloud from the valleys, alone, and stands in the warm fall of a venting dome with its feathers loosened, soaking heat into its body like a debt being paid down. It stays an hour, sometimes a day. It never calls. And it is not freeloading, though it took a long time of watching to understand the trade. The heath, for all its closed circles, cannot make everything. There are minerals that do not exist on the plateau — that washed off the buried mountain ages before the first cushion ever swelled — and the bird's gut is full of the lowlands: grit, salts, phosphorus. What it leaves near the vents is the heath's only import, arriving by a supply line one bird wide, on a schedule no one sets. The warm vapor is the payment. It is possible this is the only transaction the heath conducts with the outside world, and it is conducted in perfect silence, at the rate of perhaps a few grams a month, and it has presumably been going on — bird after solitary bird, generation after generation of that dark valley lineage learning the long climb — for as long as the rings on the oldest mounds.

Then there is the underneath, which you heard but did not reach: the deep dripwater falling a long way into the dark gaps. Down there, beneath the living surface, the heath's oldest growth has hardened and hollowed into galleries — vaulted, black, warm-breathed near the digestion pits — and the galleries are inhabited, in the sense that a furnace room is inhabited by fire. This is where the real labor force lives: the fungi in their pale sheets, the bacterial films glazing the old stem-vaults, the entire unhurried nation of decomposers whose chemistry is the vinegar heat you felt on your face at the pit-rims. You could argue — and on a long cold evening on the heath you will argue it to yourself — that the hierarchy runs the other way: that *they* are the residents and the heath is their structure, their farm and their roof, a body grown by the dark to keep the dark fed. Among them graze small blind things, pale and slow, springtail-like, generations deep, who have never known light and have no organs left for missing it. And there are the smoothed places. On the gallery floors, where a probing light can reach, the lacquered surface is worn glassy in long lanes, as though something habitually passes. The riders, who fear nothing on the surface, do not descend. Nothing has ever been seen. The lanes stay smooth.

Last, the absence you noticed before you noticed anything else: no cairn, no post, no maker's footprint. Nothing lives on the heath that could ask it what it is for, and nothing ever has — there is no ruin under the green, no ghost in the galleries, only the mountain, which was never anybody. So the final inhabitant, the one you arrive at after the riders and the bird and the dark nation below have each been counted and found too small to fill the place, is the work itself. The labor lives there the way a current lives in a river: not a thing in the place but the thing the place is doing, continuous, tenured, older than its own materials, which have all been demolished and rebuilt ten thousand times while the work went on unbroken. The heath is its body. The quota is its appetite. It is the only resident that has never once left.

And, for a few days, you. You live there too, while you walk it — entered into the ledger, your weight compensated for, your warmth a rounding error. The heath will give you exactly one acknowledgment of your tenancy, and you will receive it on the morning you leave, when you go looking for the route you came in by and find that, overnight, without malice and without ceremony, it has been revised.
