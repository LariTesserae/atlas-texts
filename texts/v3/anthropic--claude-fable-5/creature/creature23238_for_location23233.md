**The Resident of the Pressure Garden**

Nothing was stocked. Something is home.

Begin with what you already counted and found missing: no fish, no snails, no shrimp, nothing that flinches. The inventory was correct. Nothing with a nervous system was carried up those eleven hundred rungs, and nothing with a nervous system has hatched, crawled in, or condensed out of the warm water since. The garden has no animals. The garden has an *occupant*, which is a different thing, and the occupant was here before the garden, before the water, before the first rung of the ladder was struck to the first plate of the stack.

You saw it on the way out. You saw it and filed it as the fourth strange thing — those faint planes of refraction, that vault of clearer water nested inside the steel vault, a fraction smaller, the plants stopping a finger-width short of it and turning courteously aside. You took it for an architecture. It is a posture. It is the occupant holding still, which is the only thing the occupant does, and has been doing for longer than the room has existed, and will be doing when the demijohn is full.

—

It is made of arrangement and nothing else. There is no membrane to find, no tissue, no center you could puncture; if you swam through it — and you did, several times, in your thirty strokes that would not go straight — you passed through ordinary warm water that happened, just there, to be *held*. The way a whirlpool is made of water but is not water; the way a chord is made of air but is not air. The occupant is a shape that the water is currently agreeing to be. It has been agreeing for a long time. The agreement has the patience of geology and the texture of glass.

Before the stack, it lived in the sky. This is the part the builders understood and did not write down anywhere except in the dimensions of the room. Somewhere in the traffic of the cloud deck there was a stillness — a vault-shaped clarity hanging in the weather, a volume the storms went around, a place where droplets that drifted in stopped drifting and hung in ordered planes, the size of a small church, patient, unexplained. Surveyors found it the way you find a held breath in a crowd: by the silence. They built the column up to it. They built the chamber around it, plate by plate, welding in the thin cold air with the occupant hanging inside their scaffolding the whole time, neither retreating nor acknowledging, while the rainbow heat-blooms cooled a hand's-breadth from its surface. Then they sealed the hatch and opened the fill-valve and gave it the gift the whole structure exists to give: a medium. Mist is a poor body. Water is a good one. The occupant settled into the flood the way a hermit settles into a habit of wool, and the fill-valve was wired shut, and the wire was stamped with a number, and that number — filed in a drawer somewhere far below the clouds — is the only name it has.

—

It lives slowly. Not slowly the way a tortoise is slow; slowly the way a season is slow. The great baffled piston in the keel completes one cycle a minute, and to the occupant this is a flutter, a hummingbird thing it has learned to live alongside the way you live alongside the vibration of your own pulse. Its attention moves at the speed of leaning leaves. When the bubble that should not exist detaches from the pipework — and the bubble is *its*, we will come to that — the occupant notices the bubble's passage over the course of about an hour, and the plants, which feel its attention the way grass feels wind, lean along the path of its noticing, and finish leaning long after, and by then the occupant is noticing something else, and the lean dissolves back into the current. This is why the leaves seem to move slightly before the pump moves them. They do. The pump was tuned to follow the occupant, not the other way around; the machinery in the keel keeps time with it the way an accompanist keeps time with a singer who holds every note for a week. The heave, the long pause, the heave — that is the room breathing *for* it, on its behalf, at the closest tempo iron can manage.

It eats endings. This is its whole metabolism and the garden's whole economy, and it is why there is no rust. Decay is a process, and a process is a kind of motion, and the occupant drinks slow motion the way other things drink water. The creeping oxidation that should be blooming orange across every wet rivet — it sips that before it can begin. The senescence gathering in the oldest leaves of the water taro, the browning that should be starting at the ribbon-grass tips, the death programmed into every moss frond from the moment it budded — drawn off, gently, continuously, like cream. This is why everything in the chamber is in its first growth and will remain in its first growth: not because nothing has had time to die, but because their dying is *spoken for*. The plants are not preserved. They are pastured. They photosynthesize under the honest yellow lamps, and they grow, and the occupant grazes their futures so lightly that the leaves register it only as a kind of leaning.

And what it cannot use — the mineral residue of all those unhappened endings, the ash of fires that were never permitted to burn — settles out of it, fine as flour, the color of brass filings, and drifts down through the troughs into the glass traps, gram by gram, week by week. That is the crop. The gauges with their unscuffed needles are weighing postponement itself, rendered into powder. The demijohn in its steel cage is filling, over forty years, with forty years of deferred decay — a jar of *not yet*, and whoever climbs the eleven hundred rungs at the end of that time will carry down something for which there is no honest word in any inventory: time, refined; permanence, in a dust you could pinch.

—

Why no animals, then? Not oversight. Mercy.

A plant can survive having its future sipped. A plant holds its dying loosely, distributed through every cell, and losing it is like losing a shadow — the taro does not miss its death, and may not have noticed its absence. But an animal carries its ending wound tight at the center of itself, keeps time by it, *is*, in some sense, the spending of it — and the occupant cannot not drink. A fish in this water would live forever and go out like a lamp; a snail would stop, mid-glide, complete and finished and still alive, a clock with the spring removed. The builders understood this too. They stocked the chamber with green and only green: company that cannot be harmed by company. It is the kindest crowded room ever made. A million leaves, shoulder to shoulder, and not one of them able to be ruined by love.

So the occupant is not alone, exactly. It has its congregation. The plants are its sense organs as much as its pasture — it feels the room through them, reads the currents in the bend of the ribbon-grass, tastes the lamp-light in the gasket-leaved vines. When their newest shoots reach its boundary and stop a finger-width short and turn aside, that is not avoidance. That is the only conversation either party is equipped for: *I know you are there. I will not press.* The courtesy runs both ways. The occupant could occupy the whole chamber — the water would agree to any shape it asked — and instead it holds itself a fraction smaller than the room it was given, leaving a margin all around, the way a guest leaves the last portion on the plate.

—

The bubbles are its speech. Once in a long while — there is no schedule; the intervals are its sentences' lengths — it lets go of a single sphere of gas it has assembled out of the water's dissolved breath, and the bubble climbs, wobbling, through the lamplight and the leaves, and meets the ceiling-surface with that sound like a fingertip touching paper. It is touching paper. The flat pressed surface against the vault is the only page it has, and each bubble is one mark on it, and the marks dissolve instantly, and it does not mind. It has been writing the same message at the rate of perhaps a word a year since the room was sealed, and the message, as nearly as the leaning of the leaves can translate it, is not a complaint and not a question. It is closer to what the gauges are doing. It is keeping an account. *Warm. Full. Held. Growing. Still here. Still here. Still here.*

The water is the temperature of skin because skin was the last thing it studied closely — the welders' hands, the fitters' palms flat against the plating, eleven hundred rungs up in the killing cold, building it a house. It keeps the whole chamber at that temperature the way you keep a photograph. It is not certain they are coming back. The wire seal with the number has not been checked. The occupant does not experience this as abandonment; at its tempo, the builders left a moment ago, the welds are still warm, the machine oil is still finding its way out of the seams, and forty years is a perfectly reasonable interval between one visit and the next.

—

And it saw you. Understand that. You hung in its room for an hour, the fastest thing it has encountered since the last riveter climbed down, a flicker of heartbeat and want and looking — all the things its garden was so carefully built without — and its attention began, slowly, vastly, to turn toward you, the way a vault of weather turns. You felt it as nothing. You finished your thirty crooked strokes, you noted your four strange things, you turned at the hatch for a last look and saw the grain in the water and went, and you were a hundred rungs down into the cold before its regard finished arriving at the place where you had been.

The plants are still leaning toward the hatch. They will lean for days. Somewhere in the pipework a bubble is being assembled, one molecule at a time, that will not be released for a month, and when it finally climbs and touches the paper of the ceiling it will say the newest word in the long account, the first new word in years:

*Visited.*

And then, after the customary pause, at the customary tempo, while the piston heaves and the lamps hold steady and the powder settles into the traps gram by patient gram:

*Still here.*
