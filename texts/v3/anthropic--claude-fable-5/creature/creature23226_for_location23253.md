# Who Lives in the Hollow Country

Begin where the country itself begins: overhead.

---

## The Roof Nation

The winged ones are the oldest citizens, and the only ones with two countries.

They are not the bats of the surface world, though they were, once, before a million years of dark unmade their faces. Their eyes have shrunk to seeds beneath the skin — not gone, never quite gone, because once each night they need them. Their fur is the color of the flowstone, a pale mineral gray, as if the country had begun reabsorbing them. Their ears are enormous, baroque, pleated like the fluted walls they carved; an old one's ears, spread, are wider than its body, and the convolutions of the inner ear of a single individual would, unrolled, map a kilometer of gallery.

They live packed closer than any surface mammal could bear. Wing-fold to wing-fold, a thousand bodies to the square meter in the great nurseries, and they bear it because they must: each animal is a small furnace, and the colony, massed, makes its own climate, a layer of warm wet animal-weather pressed against the ceiling. An individual that loses its place in the press loses its heat, and the cold of the stone takes it in hours. So their entire society is a politics of position. They squabble ceaselessly — that is much of the roar — not over food, which is elsewhere, above, but over *centimeters*. The center of a colony is its aristocracy: the pregnant, the nursing, the great matriarchs whose voices the whole ceiling defers to. The edges are for the young, the sick, the losing. The colony is a slow centrifuge sorting the strong inward and the doomed out, and the doomed know it, and their voices are different — anyone who lived long enough in the roar would learn to hear the edge-cry, the sound of an animal being voted off the warmth.

Each is born knowing two impossible journeys. The first: the nightly ascent — kilometers of climb through the galleries, riding the country's exhale through chimneys and chokes, following sound-roads their mothers sang to them, out through the high flaws in the plateau into a sky they cannot see and do not need to, to hunt the surface night. The second journey is the return, heavy with the only sunlight this country will ever receive, carried as fat and blood and the slurry in their guts. They are the country's sun, and like a sun they are mostly unaware of what depends on them. They void over the same chambers their ancestors voided over, and below them an entire civilization of mouths waits for the rain.

They navigate the country the way you navigate memory. The galleries are *named*, in their fashion — not with words but with returns: each chamber answers a voice differently, and a young one learns the country as a sequence of answers, the bright ringing reply of the tuned halls, the muffled non-answer of the dead alcoves it is taught, before it is taught anything else, to fear. Their maps are inherited as songs. There are lineages among them — colony-clans whose route-songs differ, who pass each other in the great chimneys at evening like traffic from rival cities, and who can hear in one another's voices, instantly, *not ours*.

And they fall. That is the other thing they do, the thing the whole country counts on. The old, the weak, the pup that loses its grip in its first week — everything that fails up there arrives down here, and the roof nation does not mourn, because the roof nation cannot afford the concept. The floor is where falling goes. They do not think about the floor. In their cosmology, if they have one, the floor is the afterlife, and it is hungry, and it is none of their concern.

## The Floor Nation

The carpet is not one people. It is a hundred peoples compressed into a single shuddering surface, and the first thing to understand about all of them is that they do not know the sun exists. Not as the winged ones don't think about it — the floor nations have *never* known. Their food falls from a roaring darkness overhead, and has always fallen, and the fall is simply the shape of the universe: above is where food comes from, below is where you digest it. They are perhaps the only animals anywhere whose entire cosmos is honest with them.

The white crickets are the citizenry, the bulk, the grain of the place. They are eyeless, their antennae three times their body length, and they live at a density that has made them strange: they cannot afford aggression — there is no room for it — so they have become the most placid throng imaginable, flowing around obstacles, around predators, around each other, like a fluid that happens to be made of legs. They eat the rain. They are eaten by everything. Their reproductive arithmetic is staggering and has to be; a white cricket's life is, on average, nine days long, and in those nine days it will walk perhaps thirty meters, and that is a complete and successful life, here.

Above them in the stack — *above* meaning a centimeter — are the beetles, armored, methodical, the floor's middle class, who eat the crickets and the carrion and each other's larvae. Then the centipedes: forearm-long, blind, magnificently sensitive, the great solitary hunters of the guano badlands. A floor centipede hunts by reading vibration through fifteen pairs of legs at once, triangulating a single cricket's footfalls out of the footfalls of a million, and this should be impossible, and it does it continuously, all its life, the way you breathe. They are the closest thing the floor has to a nobility, and like all nobilities they are vastly outnumbered and constantly replaced.

The pale crabs are the strangest of the floor people, because they remember water. Their ancestors came up the black rivers in flood, ten thousand floods ago, and stayed, and now they pick across the guano terraces on stilted legs, returning to the pools only to breed, amphibious commuters between the second world and the third. They are the floor's undertakers by preference — they take the largest falls, the whole bodies, and a fallen matriarch from the roof will draw crabs from forty meters around within an hour, arriving in the dark in a slow radial pilgrimage that no one sees and that happens, on average, nine hundred times a night.

The floor nations live fast and turn over faster, and so they *change* faster than anything else in the country. The roof nation is ancient and conservative; a colony's route-songs alter over millennia. But the carpet runs a generation in weeks, and evolution down there is almost audible. Each great chamber's floor population has drifted into its own forms — the crickets of one gallery cannot breed with the crickets of the next, separated by a hundred meters of dead-zone they have never crossed and never will. The Hollow Country is, beneath its single roar, an archipelago: every chamber an island, every island inventing its own answers, in the dark, forever.

## The Water Nation

The third nation is the patient one.

The salamanders are the country's elders in the way that matters — not lineage but *individual* age. Translucent, eyeless, frilled with rose-pale external gills, a flutter of heart visible behind the skin like a moth in a paper lantern: one of them may live four hundred years. They have decoupled themselves from time. A salamander can fast for a decade. It can hang motionless in a sump for a year, metabolism banked down to embers, waiting for the taste of food in the current, and the waiting costs it nothing, because it has converted patience from a behavior into a physiology. The roof nation burns; the floor nation churns; the water nation *abides*.

This makes them the country's witnesses. A single old salamander in a major sump has, in its one slow life, overheard the rise and fall of forty generations of crab dynasties, the abandonment and recolonization of the chamber above it, three great floods, one roof-fall. It has done nothing about any of it. It taste-reads the water the way the winged ones voice-read the stone: every seep carries news — guano-richness from a thriving colony upstream, the sudden protein bloom of a die-off, the mineral flatness of a starving gallery — and the salamanders hang in the current and *know the country*, comprehensively, indifferently, the way a coastline knows the weather.

The fish are simpler and busier: blind, colorless, lateral lines grown so exquisite they can feel a cricket fall onto the water's surface from twenty meters, and they live in the boiling congregations under the drop-zones, in a perpetual gentle riot. The shrimp navigate by taste alone and travel the whole system, the water nation's couriers, carrying nothing, knowing nothing, connecting everything — it is shrimp, mostly, that carry the eggs and larvae and microbial freight that keep the drowned passages a single country instead of a thousand sealed jars.

And in the lowest sumps, below all of it, in water that has not exchanged with the rivers in centuries, there are a few very large, very old things that the rest of the water nation avoids — salamanders grown past meter-length in the total absence of everything, pale as the flowstone, so slow that silt settles on their backs. They may be the oldest individual animals in the country. They surface — if rising to a flooded ceiling can be called surfacing — perhaps once a year. Nothing hunts them. Nothing could find them. They are what patience becomes when it is given geological room.

## The Star-Makers

The light-fishers deserve their own accounting, because they are the country's only liars.

Everything else in the Hollow Country communicates honestly, because sound cannot help it — a voice tells its size, its position, its fear. But the star-makers have imported a foreign medium, *light*, into a world with no native readers of it, and they use it exclusively to deceive. They are small, soft, larval things, glowing blue-white from their own bodies, each one trailing dozens of silk threads beaded with droplets of glue, and they hang in constellations across the ceilings of the deep still chambers where the winged insects rise from the water.

The lie they tell is very old and very simple. A flying insect, hatched in black water, has one instinct of celestial origin left in it — *go toward the light* — an instruction written for a sky its ancestors last saw before the roof closed. The star-makers have colonized that instinct. They are a counterfeit sky. The insects rise toward the only stars they will ever see, and the stars eat them.

The star-makers also regulate one another, which is the unsettling part. A constellation is brightest where its members are hungriest, and the lights dim and brighten in slow waves across a ceiling, neighbors suppressing neighbors, hungry zones flaring — so that the false sky has false weather, and the whole glowing field behaves less like a crowd of larvae than like a single distributed appetite, thinking with light, very slowly, about food.

## The Quiet Ones

And then there is the nation no one counts, because counting it would require finding it.

The dead spots — the acoustically smothered alcoves, the chambers whose geometry swallows sound, the corridors where the roar goes strangely flat — are avoided by everything that sees with its voice. To the roof nation a dead spot is a blind spot in the world itself; their route-songs bend around them; pups are taught the muffled non-answer before they are taught anything else. For a million years, every sounding animal in the country has agreed not to look there.

Something noticed.

They are descended, probably, from the centipede line, though they no longer much resemble it. They are long, low, dry-skinned, and *silent* — not quiet, silent, in the way the country's own stone is silent. Their bodies have been edited by a single relentless pressure: make no sound, return no sound. Their surfaces are velveted with fine sound-absorbing pile; their joints are sheathed; their gait is a slow fluid pour that puts each of dozens of feet down with independent, deliberate softness. To a passing voice they do not echo. They read as stone. They read as *nothing* — and in a country where everything announces itself constantly, where the medium of sight is also the medium of speech, a thing that returns no echo is not merely hidden. It is, to every eye in the country, a small local absence of world.

They live in the dead spots and hunt from their edges. A roof-nation forager skimming low, a crab on pilgrimage, a young salamander crossing wet stone between pools — anything that strays along the margin of a smothered zone passes within reach of something it cannot, by the physics of its own senses, perceive. The quiet ones take it without a sound, which is to say: in this country, without an event. A death in the dark of the Hollow Country is normally the loudest thing in the room. Theirs are the only deaths here that don't happen, acoustically. The victim's absence is the first and last evidence.

They are rare — silence is expensive, and the dead spots are small, and a hunter that cannot call cannot easily find a mate, so they have solved even that without sound: they court by touch and by scent-trails laid along the smothered walls, long patient chemical letters that may go unread for years. Each one lives alone in its pocket of deafness, at the center of the only privacy this roaring country contains. The roof nation has no name for them, because the roof nation's whole language is echo, and the quiet ones are made of no-echo. They are the country's one unspeakable thing — literally: the inhabitants cannot say them.

It is worth pausing on what they are, taken whole. The country runs on total sound; its rules say that to be is to be audible. The quiet ones are the rule's shadow, the inevitable thing that grows in any absolute: a nation of one-at-a-time, living inside the world's blind spots, made out of the difference between silence and stone.

## The Strays

Briefly, because they are brief: things fall in.

A surface animal a year, perhaps — something that put a foot wrong at the lip of a distant doline, three days' dark from anywhere. They arrive broken or whole, and the whole ones live a while: blundering, blazing with sound and smell, deafened by the roar, hands or hooves on stone polished by a hundred thousand generations of bodies that knew where they were going. The country registers them the way a city registers weather. The floor nations gather below them patiently. The strays are the only inhabitants who ever experience the Hollow Country as *horror* — to everything born here it is simply the shape of being — and their panic-voices, echoing strangely through the route-songs, are the closest the roof nation ever comes to hearing news from the other country. Then the strays stop, and fall, and join the economy, and the country closes over the event like water.

## The Largest Inhabitant

Last, the one that may not count, and may be the only one that fully does.

The chambers were carved by their own roar. The walls were tuned by the voices that needed tuned walls; the resonant galleries ring because ringing galleries kept their colonies fed and singing, which kept them ringing. Sound shaped stone, stone shaped sound, for a million years, until cause and effect wore smooth like everything else here. The standing roar in the great halls is not noise anymore. It is a structure — older than any animal in it, continuous since before the current species existed, sustained by millions of voices that are each individually replaceable and collectively have never once stopped. Bats die; the roar does not. Chambers fall; the roar reroutes.

Decide for yourself whether that is an inhabitant. But note what it has: a body (the tuned stone), a metabolism (the nightly upstroke of the winged ones, the rain of light-made-falling), a memory (the route-songs, the fluted walls, the polish on the corridors), even something like a nervous system (pressure-weather moving through the galleries, news traveling as resonance, the salamanders tasting the whole country in the current). The animals live in the Hollow Country. The Hollow Country, it is at least arguable, lives in *them* — wears them, generation after generation, the way they wear the stone, the way the stone wears the dark.

And the dark itself keeps no animals, eats nothing, says nothing, and was here first, and holds all of it — the shrieking ceiling, the flowing floor, the patient water, the false stars, the silent ones in their pockets of deafness — easily, completely, the way a cupped hand holds something small that believes itself to be the world.
