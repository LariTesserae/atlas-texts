**The Residents of the Condensing City**

Ask what lives here and the city gives you its riddle straight away: nothing, and everything that stays. The avenues are empty. The avenues are also occupied at every scale from the microscopic to the municipal, and the longer you remain, the harder it becomes to say where habitation ends and architecture begins. What follows is an inventory, working upward from the smallest.

---

**The film**

It is true that nothing green grows here. It is not true that nothing grows.

Look closely at any warm wet surface — the lip of a channel, the flank of a pipe, the inside curve of a stack — and you will find it wears a skin. In poor light it reads as mineral stain: bands of ochre, rust, a deep oxblood, a gray-green like tarnish. It is not stain. It is alive, in the way that the oldest things alive are alive — a film of heat-loving cells, single and simple, each band a different tribe sorted along the temperature gradient with the precision of a thermometer. Where the water runs scalding, the film is nearly white. Where it cools toward merely hot, the colors deepen, band by band, until at the edge of the warm the film gives out entirely and the bare stone resumes.

This is the city's only crop, its entire agriculture, the floor of its food web. It needs no sun; it eats the chemistry of the water itself, the sulfur and the iron, the dissolved breath of the deep rock. It was here before the masonry — it must have been, painting the original vents in these same colors — and the builders, whatever else they did, built around it and on top of it and finally *for* it, in the sense that every pipe they laid became its pasture.

Run a finger along a band and it comes away slick, faintly metallic. You have just disturbed the oldest resident. It will not notice. It has all the time there is.

**The vent-moths**

The moths live their whole lives in the air, and this is meant literally: from the moment its wings dry, a vent-moth never again touches a surface. They ride the warm columns above the stacks in slow vertical circles, pale, palm-sized, their waxed-paper wings beading with condensation that they shiver off in fine spinning droplets — so that a column of moths in the right light is wrapped in its own faint rain.

They are nearly eyeless. There is nothing here worth seeing. They navigate by heat, reading the city's updrafts the way other creatures read landmarks, and their drifting traces the architecture of the air with terrible fidelity: where a building breathes on an eight-second period, its moths rise and settle on an eight-second period, a visible pulse, the city's respiration made into bodies.

They begin, though, on the stone. The females descend — the one descent of their lives — to skim the inner lips of the stacks, dropping eggs onto the richest bands of the film. The larvae are small gray commas that graze there for a season, and here the city does something to them that it does to everything: it entombs them. The mineral water seals them over. The larva pupates inside a shell of living stone, a pearl in the sinter, and waits — sometimes a year, sometimes far longer — until the pipe's thermal flexing cracks the casing along a seam, and the moth dissolves the last film of mineral with its own body's chemistry and climbs out into the updraft and does not come down again.

When they die, they fall. The channels take some; the wet stone takes the rest, and seals them as it sealed their childhoods. Split a slab of young travertine in the right district and you may find them: white moth-shaped lenses in the pale rock, wings spread, perfectly preserved. They are the only fossils in the city that look like anything, and they look like grief.

**The swimmer**

Of the thing in the channels, less can be said, which is in keeping with its preferences.

The warm channels are only the surface vein of an enormous deep system — the plateau is honeycombed beneath with hot waterways, natural and built and long since indistinguishable — and the swimmer belongs to the deep part. It comes up rarely, mostly in the thick of night-fog, when the streets are full wall to wall with slow white rivers and nothing with eyes could see it anyway. A back breaking the water's skin, dark, smooth, unhurried. A wake that arrives at the channel's edge after the body has gone.

It is long. How long is a question the fog declines to answer; estimates made on different nights do not agree with one another, and there may be more than one, or one that is more than expected. It is blind, almost certainly — everything down there is — and it hunts by heat and by the taste of the water, eating the film, eating fallen moths, eating whatever else the deep plumbing breeds that never surfaces at all. It is older than it has any right to be. It may be older than the city; the builders' channels follow the natural hot veins so faithfully that it is impossible to say whether they engineered its habitat or merely paved it.

Here is a strange fact, and a telling one: the city contains no images. Not of anything. No carvings, no figures, no ornament beyond the geometry of function — the builders left a hundred thousand tons of intention and not one picture. So there is no way to know whether they saw the swimmer, feared it, fed it, or never once in all their tenancy suspected that the warm water they had organized so thoroughly was inhabited at the bottom. The swimmer keeps its anonymity the way the city keeps its silence: completely, and without effort.

If you stand at a channel's edge at night, keep your feet back from the water. Not because anything has ever happened. Because the water is hot enough to scald, and because the wake, when it comes, arrives without a sound.

**The grazers**

The armored things on the high pipes deserve more attention than their slowness invites.

They are born soft — pale, naked, quick by the standards of this place, which is to say about as quick as a tired tortoise — and they spend their lives doing exactly one thing: walking the warm conduits and rasping the precipitate off them, hour by hour, scale by scale. The mineral they eat, they keep. It comes out of their own chemistry as armor: overlapping plates of the same dull sinter-gold as the city's stone, laid down ring by ring over a lifetime, so that a grazer is literally shingled in the city it grazes. The young are smooth and almost bright. The old are massive, crusted, slow as weather, their plates fused and flowed into shapes no two alike, and the very old are difficult to distinguish from the pipework until they move, which they may not do while you watch.

They follow fixed circuits. Generations of them have walked the same routes along the same conduits, and the routes are visible — shallow polished grooves worn into the mineral crust, threading the high district like the memory of paths. And here is the thing worth sitting with: their circuits are not arbitrary. They follow the junctions, the valve clusters, the narrow runs where scale would choke the flow soonest. The old engineering grace by which the city's pipes route around their own blockages is, in some measurable part, *them* — the maintenance crew that outlasted the institution, still walking its rounds, still keeping the critical runs clear, for no reason except that the critical runs are where the eating is best. Whether the builders bred them to the task or the task bred them to itself, the result is the same: the city idles partly because something with a stomach wants it to.

There are not many. A few dozen, perhaps, in the whole city. They live a very long time, and when one dies it does so the only way it knows how — by stopping. It stands where it stood, and the water that beads on everything beads on it, and the city does to it what the city does: seals it, slowly, into a rounded boss of golden stone on the pipe where it ended. You can find these along the high conduits if you know what you are looking at — smooth knuckles in the sinter, spaced out along the runs like rivets, like punctuation, each one a finished sentence.

They do not fear you. They have no reason to have learned how. If you put your hand flat on a young one's back, it will pause, register you as a warm thing that is not a pipe, and resume.

**The city**

And then there is the largest resident, which is the matter under everything else.

Consider what it does. It takes in heat and gives out stone. It breathes on a schedule. It circulates hot fluid through a branched system that heals its own small wounds and routes around its own blockages. It maintains, against the world's general preference for evenness, a difference between inside and outside — every meter-thick wall, every glazed inverted roof, every building drinking itself. It has a pulse you can hear through the walls. By any definition that doesn't insist on cells, the city is not a place where things live. It is a thing that lives, in which everything else here resides the way flora resides in a body — the film its lining, the moths its breath made visible, the grazers its slow immune system, the swimmer its dark interior fauna, known to it or not.

It is dying, of course, on the longest possible schedule: every century a little more stone, a little less machine, the travertine closing over it like a long patient tide. But dying is something only living things are permitted to do, and the city is taking centuries over it, idling all the while, exact and unhurried, like a creature that has decided sleep and death can be the same act if performed slowly enough.

**The absence**

The builders do not live here. This must be said plainly, because everything in the city argues otherwise.

There are no bodies, no graves that announce themselves, no signs of leaving — and yet every breath the place takes was tuned by a hand. Someone set that vent to an eight-second period. Someone, in a different building, chose eleven; someone, thirteen. The nighttime arrhythmia of breathing that fills the fogged streets is not noise: it is a tuning, a deliberate citywide signature, and the people who could read it like a map are the one species genuinely missing from this inventory. They left no images, no words, no remains that announce themselves — only a set of habits robust enough to survive their habituators. Most of the dead leave objects. These people left a verb, conjugated in steam, still running in the present tense.

Perhaps that is the truest answer to the question. What lives in the Condensing City is an intention — idling, governed, bleeding off exactly as much pressure as the ground provides — and everything else, the film and the moths and the swimmer and the gold-shingled walkers of the pipes, lives inside that intention like creatures in a tide pool left by a withdrawn sea.

**The newest**

One last resident, provisional: you.

Stand still long enough anywhere in the city and the system files you. Water condenses on you; you become a warm surface in the great engine of rising and falling, beaded, weeping, acquiring your first thin shell. And if you stand still long enough in the right plaza, near the right stack, on the right windless evening, you may notice the vent-moths drifting over from their column to circle above your head instead — pale, eyeless, riding the heat you give off, having registered you, in the only language they possess, as a small new chimney.

The city has no way to know you are not one. It counts you among its vents and breathes around you. Residency here was never a matter of belonging. It is a matter of staying warm, and staying put, and the place has all the patience required to wait and see which you'll do.
