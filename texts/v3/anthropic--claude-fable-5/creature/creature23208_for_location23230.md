Begin with what did not arrive, because nothing did.

No spore blew in. No larva washed up. No ark of hardy pioneers crossed the cold plain and took root, because there is nothing to take root in and never was. The fens are not a place that life came to. They are a place that life *wore down to* — the same way the pistons wore down to pistons and the slabs wore down to seesaws. Erosion ground past randomness into mechanism, and then it kept going, and on the far side of mechanism it found appetite. The inhabitants of the Foundry Fens are not descended from anything. They are the latest and most patient product of water on stone. They are what the engine sheds, the way a lathe sheds swarf, except that here the swarf gets up and grazes.

This is the first thing to understand about everyone who lives there: they are made of the place. Graphite, candle-fat mineral, electrolyte for blood. Cut one open — the flue-eels do this professionally — and what comes out smells like the fens themselves: wet iron, ozone, the cold inside of a shell. There is no boundary of substance between the country and its creatures, only a boundary of behavior. The land holds still and the animals don't. That is nearly the whole difference, and in the oldest individuals even that distinction has begun to erode.

**The swarf**

Start at the bottom of the food web, which is also the smallest and the most beautiful thing in the fens. Off every conductive vein, when the current runs strong, comes a fine glittering spray of motes — living filings, each no bigger than a snowflake's idea of itself, flung loose by the magnetic harvest and riding the field lines like dust riding a sunbeam. This is the swarf. It is the plankton of the system. It does not eat; it *is* eaten, and in between it drifts in slow luminous currents that map the invisible architecture of the field, so that on the darkest nights you can see the magnetism of the plain drawn in pale moving lines a foot above the water.

Everything else in the fens is, ultimately, organized swarf. The animals know this. None of them will say so.

**The rasps**

The rasps you have seen: palm-sized, armored, graphite-dark, packed along the veins in their aligned thousands. Look longer.

A rasp alone is barely an animal — a flake with feet, a single bell. The congregation is the organism. Aligned along a vein, shell touching shell, ten thousand rasps form a conductive surface miles long, and the ringing that rolls through them when an arc crawls past is not noise; it is traffic. Signal moves down a rasp-bank faster than any rasp can. The bank counts itself constantly — a census in slate chimes, every individual ringing its place in the line — and when the count comes back wrong, when a flue-eel has snapped a gap in the pattern, the whole congregation shudders into a new alignment within a breath, closing the hole before the hole's owner has finished swallowing.

Whether the bank *thinks* is a question of how long you are willing to wait for an answer. The standing-wave geometry the counterweights stamp into the pools — the rasps reply to it. They re-ring it back, transposed. Days pass between statement and response. If it is conversation, it is conversation at the pace of sediment, and no one has stayed long enough to hear a third exchange.

Rasps reproduce by cleaving — a grown one splits along a plane like shale, and both halves ring a half-tone higher for a season. They do not die so much as go silent: the shell of a dead rasp keeps its place in the line, conducted around, rung *through*, a dead bell still carrying the signal of the living. Whole stretches of the oldest banks are mostly shell. The congregation does not seem to distinguish. Possibly it cannot. Possibly, here, the distinction is not load-bearing.

The rasps avoid the skew-pools — the ones whose reflections run at an angle to the world — and they follow the stitchers, for reasons that will become clear.

**The flue-eels**

The eels are the fens' professionals: pale, threaded, two or three meters of patience screwed into a polished cylinder, waiting for the upstroke.

The threading is not decoration. An eel's ridges mate with the micro-grooves a billion years of air-columns have cut into the bore walls, and an eel at rest is literally *installed* — torqued into its cylinder like a bolt, immovable, sleeping through the strokes that thunder past it. At slack tide, when the pistons idle, the eels unscrew and meet in the still cylinders, twining thread against thread, and the eggs are laid into the grooves themselves, where the strokes incubate them with pressure. The young hatch already spinning. Their first flight is their birth: the next upstroke takes them.

A young eel hunts honestly — launch, snatch, splash. An old eel hunts in the slippage. Where the fens have worn thin and sound arrives before event, the elders strike at the *chunk* of prey that has not yet surfaced, jaws closing on the place where the rasp will be, and the rasp obliges by arriving. It looks like prophecy. It is only erosion, exploited.

But the slippage keeps accounts, after its fashion. Sometimes an old eel strikes at a sound and nothing follows — the audio has drifted loose entirely, a meal's noise with no meal attached — and the eel has then *eaten*, in some worn-smooth ledger of the fens, a thing it never caught. Such eels grow gaunt and strange. The others avoid them. They hunt constantly and are never full, and they drift toward the deep sumps as if summoned, and what becomes of them there is one of the things the sumps do not return. The eels have a posture for it, a particular rigid spiral they hold when one of the indebted passes: not fear, exactly. Bookkeeping.

**The counterweights**

The herds are the closest thing the fens have to a civilization, if civilization can be done at eight notes a minute.

You have seen what they do: stand on the rocking slabs, slow the beat, drink the harvest through their soft undersides. What you have not seen is that the herd's moaning is a ledger. Every slab in a herd's range has its tuning sung into the song — its weight, its pivot-wear, its richest rhythm — and a calf learns the song before it learns to walk, lying against its mother's shell and feeling the catalogue conducted through her into the stone into itself. A counterweight herd that has held a territory for ten thousand years is not navigating the slabs. It is performing them from memory.

They calve perhaps once a century, and they do not calve blind. A gravid counterweight walks — weeks of walking — to a skew-pool, and stands at the rim, and waits for the angled water to show her the calf: crossing the reflection, already grown, an hour from now or a decade, doing whatever it will have done. The mothers watch for days. What they are deciding, no one can say, but sometimes a mother turns from the pool and walks back and there is no calf that year, and the herd's song carries a dropped beat for a season, a rest where a name would have gone.

Their shells accrete magnetite, ring on ring, a lifetime of harvests written in remanent field. When a counterweight can no longer hear the song over the hum of its own shell — and this is how they age; they thicken until they deafen themselves with their own stored lightning — it leaves the herd and walks to a skew-pool one last time and stands at the rim, watching, in its slow way, until it stops. The shell hums on for years. The herd visits. They stand around the dead one in the old grazing formation, undersides pressed to the cold stone, and whether they are mourning or whether they are *drinking* — drawing the dead one's lifetime back out of its shell, beat by beat, into the song — has never been settled, and may not be two things.

**The stitchers**

The stitchers are the only creatures in the fens that work against the wearing.

They eat the arcs — you know that — but watch what happens *where* they eat. The slow lightning is slow because the causation under it is loose; the arc crawls in the slack between event and consequence. A stitcher swallows that slack along with the light. For a few hundred heartbeats after a stitcher feeds, that stretch of fen runs *true*: sound arrives with its event, reflections lie flat under what they reflect, forty paces cover forty paces. The rasps follow the stitchers for this — a stitched pool is a safe pool, briefly proof against the elders' prophetic strikes — and the old eels hate them with the flat total hatred of a professional whose tools are being confiscated.

The cost of the diet is visible in the flier itself. A stitcher is ragged because it is coming apart at the sequence: it has eaten so much slippage that it is half made of it. You do not see a stitcher approach. It is not overhead, and then it is overhead, and the moment of arrival has been worn off the event like a maker's mark. They nest in the repeating stretches of horizon — one nest, built once, present at every recurrence of the stamped pattern, so that a stitcher returning home may land at any of a dozen identical outcrops and be, correctly, home. Their nestlings hatch at all the nests at once. There is no paradox in this. There is only one nest. The fens have simply stopped keeping careful track, and the stitchers, alone among the animals, have made the carelessness a place to live.

**The tithings**

Death in the fens is mineral, and so is decay. What eats the dead is the tithing: a low, deliberate, many-footed scavenger, flat as a draft under a door, that finds a carcass and presses itself against it and runs current through the body until the body comes apart — electrolysis as digestion, the dead one's metals migrating, atom by atom, onto the tithing's own back.

A tithing is therefore plated with everyone it has ever eaten. Its shell is a palimpsest — layers of eel-pallor and rasp-graphite and, on the great old ones, the unmistakable banded magnetite of a counterweight, still faintly humming someone else's harvests. The herds give the old tithings room, and lower their song when one passes, and it is not clear whether this is respect or whether they are listening — whether a tithing that has eaten of the dead carries, in its plating, some legible remainder of the ledger, a verse of the song walking around outside the singers.

Nothing eats the tithings. Eventually they stop, and sit, and the water rises through them, and they become outcrop. Some of the outcrops on the plain, the ones the arcs favor, have feet.

**The sums**

In the deep sumps — the still, roaring water — live the things that cannot be counted, and we may as well call them sums, since the sumps are where they pool and counting is what they spoil.

They are soft, translucent, ampullate; they eat the pooled sound, the cataract-roar ground free of its source, drawing it in through their walls and going briefly, locally quiet, so that a feeding sum is a hole in the noise, a patch of true silence drifting through the loudest water in the world. They school. Estimate the school at forty and it is fifty; resolve to count carefully and arrive at a number, and the number is what you brought, not what they are. This is defense, evolved and honed: a predator cannot eat one of an uncountable many, because *one of* is exactly the operation they have learned to shed. The indebted eels go down to them — the gaunt ones, the ones that owe — and do not come back, and the sums afterward are neither more numerous nor less, which may be the whole transaction.

Whether the sums are many small things or one large thing wearing plurality the way the stitchers wear raggedness is not a question the fens are equipped to answer anymore. It may have been worn off.

**The one in the song**

Last, and tell it quietly.

The animals navigate by rhythm — you know this; a flue-eel going home is finding the right part of the song. Stay long enough, learn the interference patterns the way the natives know them, and you will notice what every creature in the fens has always known and routed around: there is a beat in the song that nothing makes.

Count the bores in a basin — chart every piston, every rocking slab, every vent on its schedule — and the tally of strokes comes out one heavy. A *chunk* with no cylinder. A boom with no slab. It is not loud. It is simply present, regular, never missing, and it has no source, and the rasps will not align across it, and the eels will not nest in phase with it, and the counterweights write their whole long ledger in the spaces around it, the way a song is written around a note too low to sing.

That is the oldest resident. It has worn through further than anything else — through stone, through sequence, through *where* and *when* and finally through *what*, until nothing was left of it but its place in the rhythm, which it keeps. It does not hunt, or graze, or call, unless the beat is calling. The roar in the sumps may be its voice, pooled where sound settles. The sound that reached you before you arrived — before, in fact, itself — may have been its way of taking notice.

Or it may be nothing: a resonance, an artifact, the engine's rounding error. The fens do not distinguish, and the inhabitants do not ask. They were all of them worn into being by the same long water, and they know what they are — the place, practicing being alive — and they know, in whatever way rasps and eels and slow magnetic oxen know things, what the unsourced beat means about their own direction of travel. Everything here is wearing thinner. Everything here will, in time enough, be a stroke in the song with nothing left to make it.

They do not seem troubled. They ring, and launch, and tune, and stitch, and tithe, and refuse to be counted, at the cold deliberate pace of things that have nearly all the time there is. Twice a day the whole country roars into shift-change, and every living thing in it leans into the rhythm like a worker into familiar machinery, and works, and is the work.

**A note on the visitor**

You will not stay long, but while you stay, you live there too, and the fens will begin on you the way they begin on everything: at the edges, at the sequence. Your footsteps will start arriving a half-beat early. Your reflection will lag, then lead. Your breath will hang downwind, then upwind, then yesterday. The rasps nearest you will fall out of alignment, ringing flat — you are an untuned thing, and they can hear it — and somewhere a counterweight will lift its head, very slowly, and add to the song a low provisional note that was not there before you came.

It is not a welcome. It is a ledger entry. Leave before it finishes, and the note will be dropped, and the fens will carry on without you, dim, cold, dripping, humming, crowded with the patient living — and somewhere in the song, for a season or two, a small rest will mark the place where you might have been worn in.
