What lives on the Loud Plateau lives the way the plateau itself lives: completely, invisibly, and without ever doing anything that could be called an event.

The first answer is the honest one — almost nothing. You could cross the whole tableland, rim to rim, and swear the place was sterile: white stone, white water, white noise, and not one green thing in a day's walk. But the emptiness is a matter of scale. The plateau is not unoccupied. It is occupied so thinly, so slowly, and so totally that occupation and stone have become difficult to tell apart.

**The film.**

Begin at the bottom, because here the bottom is also nearly everything. Every wet surface on the plateau — which is every surface — wears a layer of life one cell deep. It is not green. It is not visible. It is mineral-colored, stone-flavored, simpler than anything you would recognize as a microbe: small slow chemistries that eat rock. They need nothing organic, because there is nothing organic; they take dissolved calcite, cold, pressure, and the faint chemical disagreement between the deep water and the thin air, and out of that they make the little there is to make here. They are the plateau's entire agriculture. Every weeping sheet on every tower flank, every misted overhang, every blue terraced pool is pasture — pasture too thin to see, sown across a hundred thousand spires. The land that has not one lichen-stain anywhere is, in fact, alive over nearly its whole surface, at a thickness of one molecule of intention.

They have no name. Names are given by people who arrive, and no one arrives. Call them the film.

The film lives at the speed of the towers, and dies into them. Because the water lays down stone exactly as fast as it strips it away, each generation of the film is sealed under the next skin of calcite even as it grazes the one below — so that every tower on the plateau is laminated, all the way down, with the film's dead. The white stone is fractionally an ossuary. The cathedral-sized spires are built, in some small honest percentage, out of bodies. The plateau has been burying its inhabitants in itself, and growing taller on them, since before there were eyes; the towers are tombs that are also the living surface of the next tenants' world; and none of this has ever looked like anything but white stone standing in white noise.

**The grazers.**

In one pool in a hundred — crouch, shade the glare with your hand — you find the plateau's only animals. Finger-length. Soft-bodied. White as the stone they cross. They are eyeless, but not blind the way a cave fish is blind, with sealed sockets remembering sight. These never reached for eyes at all, because vision is a sense for events, and in their lineage's entire history nothing has ever happened. Light here is staggering and useless. Nothing moves but water, and the water always moves the same way.

They are deaf, and they are the most acoustic creatures imaginable. The roar that would deafen you is, to them, not a sound; it is the medium they are made for, the way water is made-for in a fish. Their bodies are tuned to the plateau's one note. They orient by it — the vibration thickens near the feed-jets and thins toward the pool's center, and that gradient is their north, their up, their map. The note is their gravity and their weather and their constant companion, and they cannot perceive it any more than you perceive the pressure of the atmosphere, because perception is for changes and the note has not changed in ten thousand years. Lift one out of the roar and it would die — not of silence, exactly, but the way you would die lifted out of air. For them, the loudest place on earth is the shape of calm.

They graze the film from the calcite floors. A full circuit of a pool takes a season; they move slower than an hour hand, and there is no reason to move faster, because the pasture regrows behind them at exactly the pace of their hunger — of course it does; everything here balances. They have no startle reflex. There is nothing in them that answers to *sudden*, because sudden is not a category their world contains. Touch one — no one ever has — and it would simply continue.

They age by becoming the plateau. All their lives the milky water leaves its tithe in their tissues: they begin translucent and grow opaque, then heavy, then slow even by their own standard of slowness, and at last they settle onto the pool floor and the floor accepts them. The calcite skins over. The white terraced lips of the pools — those smooth pale rims you'd run a hand along — are made of finished grazers, generations deep. Every living one spends its whole life crossing and recrossing the laminated bodies of its entire ancestry, eating the film that grows upon its grandmothers, and will lie down in its turn and become floor for whatever comes after. Death here is not an event either. It is a change of pace, from very slow to entirely still, and then a change of category, from creature to country.

**The loop.**

Here is the thing the eye cannot see and the mind keeps circling back to. The pools look sealed — one in a hundred, scattered across a day's walk, with sheer white stone between them. They are not sealed. Rarely — perhaps when one grazer finishes, so that somewhere another may begin — a grazer buds: a grain of soft tissue no bigger than this word, released into the spill. It rides down terrace to terrace, reaches the plateau's hidden seams, and is drawn into the mountain with the sinking water. For years — decades, maybe — it drifts in the cold pressurized dark of the aquifer, the held breath, until it is pressed back up through some pinhole jet on a tower a day's walk from where it began and falls with the mist. Almost all of them die in transit, entombed mid-journey in the depositing stone: there are grazers *inside the spires*, tiny white commas sealed in white rock, travelers who became architecture. One in millions lands in water deep enough, and uncurls.

So the hundred pools are not a hundred worlds. They are one population, threaded through the inside of the mountain — siblings who will never meet, connected through the dark. The plateau is one body, and the grazers are something very like its blood.

**The crosser.**

One species of bird in the world knows the way over. It does not live here; it lives *across* here, which is its own kind of residence — the route up the rising column of cool wet air is older than anything the species could be said to remember, handed down wing to wing. The bird is the plateau's only witness. It is the only eye that has ever seen the fixed rainbows standing in their slots, the most permanent colored objects on earth, hanging a few feet above creatures who will never know color exists — and it does not care; it is crossing. Its shadow is the single event the plateau hosts: a small darkness sliding over the towers in a few seconds, the only moving thing in that whole country that is not water, and then gone.

But once in a long while a feather comes loose, falls into the hanging mist, settles on wet stone — and within a year it is stone, encased, a white calcite feather perfect to the barb. Over the ages there are perhaps a few dozen of these, scattered unfindably among a hundred thousand spires. They are the plateau's only imports. They are its complete archive of the existence of the rest of the world.

**The whole of it.**

And if you ask what lives here largest — larger than the film, the grazers, the bird's brief shadow — the answer may be the loop itself. Water rising, weeping, pooling, sinking, rising. A circulation, with the towers for bones, the film for flesh, the grazers for blood, the buried generations for memory, and the roar for a voice that has been saying one word at full volume since before there were ears. You could call the plateau the resident and everything on it organs, and be no more wrong than any other way of saying it.

Everything that lives here is finished. Not dying — finished, the way the towers are finished: arrived at a final shape that is also a constant shape. Evolution needs events, and the events ended long ago; the film and the grazers stopped becoming when the plateau did, and have simply been ever since. Life elsewhere is a thing that happens. Here it is a thing that is. Come back in a thousand years and the same grazer — or its identical successor; the distinction has stopped meaning anything — will be crossing the same pool beneath the same falling drop, inside the same eternal note.

The residents of the loud plateau do not change, do not startle, do not hear, and do not end so much as continue differently. They are finished too. They are just the only things in all that roar that are quiet about it.
