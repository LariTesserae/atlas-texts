Start with the bloom, because everything else does.

It is not a plant and not quite a mineral, and the honest answer is that no category holds it. The machinery weeps through hairline fractures in its housing — a fluid that is mostly salts of chemistries with no common names — and where the weeping meets air, it sets. Crusts. Frills. Pale coral shelves no thicker than a fingernail. This much is geology. But the bloom grows *toward* grazing. Strip a patch bare and the fractures nearby weep faster, as if the machine, or the salt, or the arrangement between them, has noticed the appetite and answered it. Whether this is metabolism or merely very old chemistry that has learned the shape of being eaten, no one can say. It is the floor of the food web and it behaves, faintly, like a thing with preferences. In the warm room even the rock is a little bit alive, which is fitting, since there is no rock.

Then the grazers, which are the room's true population — its citizenry, its crowd. Palm-sized, flattened, soft as a wet leaf, colored the no-color of things that have never needed to be seen. They have no eyes and no fronts; any edge can lead. They move by a slow rippling of the underside, and they navigate by taste — the walls are a continent of flavor to them, every scalloped hollow a place their ancestors thinned the salt, and they read this the way other creatures read landmarks. A grazer's map of the room is a map of ten thousand generations of meals. They are, in a sense, always traveling through their own family history, licking it as they go.

Their glow deserves attention. The phosphor smear on the underside is digestion made visible — the salt gives up light as it breaks down, and the grazer's body is translucent enough to leak it. This means a feeding grazer is a small lit thing in a dark room, which should be fatal, and mostly isn't, because the hunters are blind and read heat, not light. The glow serves the grazers themselves. They find each other by it. Two grazers meeting will lay their edges together and hold still, and their smears will slowly synchronize — brightening and dimming in the same rhythm — and after some hours they part, and nothing visible has been exchanged, and this is how they reproduce: not the meeting itself but what the meeting sets in motion. Weeks later one of them buds. The bud carries the rhythm. Lineages in the warm room can be told apart by their pulse.

They die by thinning. An old grazer stops budding, keeps grazing, and spreads — wider and flatter, season over season, until it is a film, and then a stain, and then a texture on the wall, and then wall. The polish that rounds every edge in the room is not only wear. Some of it is grazers who finished. The residents move all their lives across the settled bodies of their ancestors, and there is no way to know whether any of them know this, and no particular reason to think it would trouble them if they did.

Above them, the drinkers. There are never many — perhaps forty, and the number has been perhaps forty for longer than most planets keep their weather. Each holds a stretch of ceiling-rib, a *drip-line*, and the lines are property in the oldest sense: defended, inherited, adjusted by those slow silent pressing contests that end with one party walking backward into the dark. A drinker is mostly reservoir. The long limbs, the strange joints where joints shouldn't be, the open patient mouth — all of it is architecture for standing under a migration of water beads for years at a time. They eat, but rarely and incidentally: whatever small ceiling life the water carries down to them. Mostly they drink, and hold, and wait, and their bodies swell and slacken on cycles measured in months, like tides that forgot to be lunar.

They breed when the pressure fronts come. That is the only time. When one of those sourceless fronts moves through the room and the steam leans and everything goes still, the drinkers alone go *un*-still — they climb, they cross each other's territories without contest, they meet at the vault's high seams in tangles of limb that make no sound at all, and by the time the front has passed they are back on their lines as if nothing occurred. Their eggs go into the delaminating wall-leaves — tucked between the curling layers of that ancient material, in the warm gap where the wall is coming apart from itself. And this is why the leaves fall slowly. Inside each falling leaf, the larvae are beating — hundreds of hair-fine cilia working the humid air — not enough to fly, only enough to lengthen the fall, to steer the leaf toward the machine-spine's warmth, to buy the descent a little grace. A leaf that has taken a century to detach spends an unnatural half-minute in the air, and lands, and splits, and the young drinkers climb the dark toward the ceiling their parents never left. Most are eaten on the way. It is the only recklessness in their entire species, and it is over in an hour.

The hunter — and there is reason to write it in the singular. The pale, low-slung thing that works the floor's unlit margin may be several individuals, but the room's slow census suggests otherwise: one at a time, always one, immensely long-lived, and when it finally dies a successor appears from somewhere with such smooth timing that the two facts feel like a single fact. Where the successor waits, and as what, is one of the room's held questions. Perhaps there is always a second hunter, torpid, folded into some recess of the machine's underside, kept like a spare part. Perhaps the old one buds a replacement into the dark decades before it needs one. Either way: the hunter is less an animal than an office, and the office is always filled.

It is blind, boneless in the way of things that were never asked to stand up, and it reads the room as a heat-map — and here is its elegance: the grazers are a fraction of a degree cooler than the walls they graze, because digestion in the warm room is endothermic, because breaking that salt *takes* warmth rather than giving it. The hunter hunts cold. It moves along the floor's edge feeling for the faint chill of a feeding grazer the way a hand feels for a draft under a door. It kills perhaps once a season, eats entirely, wastes nothing, and spends the rest of its time doing the thing that makes it the room's strangest citizen: it patrols. Full circuits of the chamber, unhurried, edge-hugging, on a schedule so regular that the grazers seem to use it — the walls empty just ahead of its path and refill just behind, a slow wave of vacancy traveling the room like a second hand. Whether the hunter is hunting on these circuits or doing something more like keeping time, no observer has been present to decide. But the room has a pulse in the drip and a hum in the floor, and the hunter, circling, is the nearest thing it has to a hand moving on a face.

Below all of this — beneath notice, which in the warm room means beneath the palm-sized — there are the drop-dwellers. Each condensation bead, fattening on the ceiling, carries a passenger population: creatures smaller than the word *creature* usually reaches, born in the film of moisture on the vault, swept into a forming bead, living their entire lives inside one traveling drop. The bead is their world. Its slow migration along the rib is their era. Its fall is their apocalypse, and their delivery — the *tik* that measures the room is, every time, the end of one small world and the seeding of the floor with its survivors, which the grazers take up, which the hunter eventually takes up in turn. The room's clock is a rain of finished worlds. It has been keeping this kind of time for a hundred thousand years.

And then there is the one resident who fits nowhere in the web, because it does not eat the web and the web does not eat it.

At the far terminus of the machine-spine, where the ridged housing folds down into the floor and the ember-light gutters to almost nothing, something tends. It is hard to see and harder to categorize — jointed like the drinkers but built low like the hunter, with a surface that has the same character as the walls, ceramic-and-settled-smoke, so that at rest it reads as a fixture. It may have been one. The best guess the room permits is that it descends from tools: some maintenance form the builders left behind, which outlived its instructions, and drifted, over deep time, from device toward animal — acquiring hunger's cousins, patience and habit, without ever acquiring hunger itself. It draws what it needs directly from the machine's seams. It is the only resident the heat feeds firsthand.

Its work is repair, or the memory of repair. It moves along the spine and finds the weeping microfractures and closes them — pressing them with a limb-tip that leaves the housing smooth, sealed, whole. And it never finishes. It seals some fractures and passes others by, on a pattern no observer could call anything but choice, and the fractures it spares are the ones that feed the bloom, and the bloom feeds the grazers, and the grazers feed everything. The entire ecology of the warm room lives inside this creature's incompleteness — in the gap between what it was built to do and what it now leaves undone. Whether it *decides* this, whether some drifted instinct weighs the room's hunger against the machine's wounds, or whether it is simply, gloriously, no longer very good at its job — the result is the same. Its failure is the food supply. It is the shepherd of the room by way of being a broken tool, and it has been broken in exactly the right way for a hundred thousand years.

When the machine's seams brighten in their patterned cycles — those repeating configurations that the grazers mirror along the walls — the tender answers. It positions itself against the spine and its own surface flickers, dim shapes moving under the ceramic, in sequences that rhyme with the machine's but do not match them: a reply in a dialect that has drifted from its language. The machine is almost certainly signaling a maintenance crew that has not existed since before the room had life in it. The tender is almost certainly answering with the eroded remainder of an acknowledgment code. And the grazers, arranged on the walls in their mirroring constellations, are almost certainly doing something else entirely — following salt, following warmth, following each other. But in the ember-light, for that hour, the room appears to be three things in conversation: the machine asking, the tender answering, the congregation arrayed. Then the seams dim, and everyone goes back to being an ecosystem.

Note what does not live here. Nothing rots. There are no scavengers because there is no carrion — the grazers thin into the walls, the drinkers are taken as larvae or persist for centuries, the hunter's rare kills are total, and the hunter itself, dying, is found within hours by grazers who converge on the cooling body from every wall and take it back into the room molecule by molecule, glowing faintly as they work, so that the death of the room's only predator looks, from the doorway that is not a door, like a soft constellation gathering on the floor. Death in the warm room is not an event. It is redistribution, done quietly, by the same citizens who do everything else.

And last — because a census should be honest about its largest entry — there is the machine. Whether it lives is not a question the room needs answered, but consider the evidence: it eats (something, somewhere, in whatever it still does), it exhales (the heat, the room itself), it is wounded and weeps and is tended, it speaks on a cycle and is answered, and everything here is either its parasite or its symbiont or its congregation, depending on the hour and the light. The whole population — the bloom, the grazers with their inherited pulse, the drinkers on their old water law, the hunter circling like a hand on a clock, the drop-worlds falling, the broken shepherd sealing some wounds and sparing others — all of it lives on the body of one enormous patient, warm in the dark, doing its one lost task.

They are its fauna the way gut-flora are ours: uninvited, essential, and by now impossible to tell apart from the health of the host.

A grazer lifts from the wall, and its light goes out.
