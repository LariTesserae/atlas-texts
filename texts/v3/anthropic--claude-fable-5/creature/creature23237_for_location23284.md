**The Tenants of the Lower Condensery**

Start with the smallest claim and work upward: the crew does not, strictly speaking, live there. They sleep in the dry barracks two hundred feet up the bore, where the air thins out to something a stranger could breathe without noticing it. But ask any of them where they live and watch the pause before they answer. The Condensery lives in them, which over enough years amounts to the same thing.

**The crew.**

You can identify a Condensery tender out of uniform, in daylight, at a distance. They are pale in the particular way of things that have grown in warmth rather than been bleached by cold — a soft, even, faintly luminous pallor, the pallor of the woodlice and the snails, the pallor the chamber issues to everything that stays. Their hands are a contradiction: the palms callused hard from pipe and rail and ladder rung, the backs soft as a child's, the skin perpetually plumped by twenty years of saturated air. They squint above ground. Not at the light — at the dryness. The first hour of every leave, a tender's sinuses ache and their lips crack and they describe the surface air, without irony, as *thin* and *abrasive*, and they drink constantly, baffled that anyone lives where water has to be looked for instead of simply breathed.

Their hearing is the real adaptation. A new hire — a *drylander*, until they've sweated through their first full month — perceives the Condensery as a single overwhelming roar and shouts to be heard over it, which doesn't work, because shouting sits in exactly the frequency band the engine owns. The veterans don't shout. They speak in a low, clipped, oddly pitched register, almost a croon, threaded into the gaps of the engine's four-beat breath — they talk *between* the compression and the discharge without knowing they do it, the way people walking together fall into step. Their dialect is dense and brief. Whole instructions ride on three words and a gesture. Outsiders find them taciturn; the truth is that they are fluent in a language that has been compressed, like everything in the chamber, to fit the space available.

And they count by the engine. Not consciously. But put a tender in a silent room and ask them to estimate a minute, and they will be uncannily accurate, and if you watch their hands you will see the fingers moving very slightly: *intake, compression, discharge, settle.* Eleven breaths, give or take. The engine is their clock, their metronome, their pulse's pacemaker. Old tenders who retire to the surface report the same complaint, every one of them: the silence keeps them awake. Some of them, the family will admit quietly, sleep with a hand on their own chest.

**The figures.**

Every shift has its roles, and the roles have hardened over generations into something between job titles and minor offices of a faith.

There is always a *breath-reader* — the senior engineer, the one who stands closest to the black mass and listens. The engine's exhale runs long when the seals are tired, short and sharp when the deep water is pushing back; the breath-reader knows perhaps forty distinct exhalations and what each one asks for. The current one is a heavy, slow woman who has worked the chamber for thirty-one years and is said to have once diagnosed a cracked valve seat from the third gallery, in the dark, with her eyes shut, by the grace note at the end of the settle. She denies this. Everyone repeats it anyway.

There is the *cage-keeper*, who rides the freight cage on its slow groaning cycle and has therefore spent a career passing through every layer of the chamber twice an hour — the seethe of the floor, the lamp-haloes, the cricket walls, the bat-fog at the top — and who consequently knows the vertical geography of the place the way a river pilot knows a channel. The cage-keepers are, by long tradition, the gossips and the historians. Everything that happens in the Condensery happens, eventually, in front of the cage.

There is the *Gardener*. Not an official post. The eldest tender on the roster simply inherits it: the duty of looking after the moss colony by the second-tier valve bank, keeping the lamp above it alive past the lamp's natural span, scraping the mineral crust back from its edges with a thumbnail, and accepting, on behalf of the green, the crumbs the crew leave when they eat their bread nearby. The current Gardener is a stooped, wordless old man who was a drylander once, fifty years ago, from somewhere with fields. He has been seen touching the Garden with two fingers at the start of every shift, the way other people touch a doorframe, or a name.

And there is the *channel-walker*, youngest of the offices, usually given to whichever tender has the patience for it — the slow circuit of the condensate channels at the chamber's rim, counting salamanders. Not for love, though love comes with the job; for telemetry. The salamanders climb when the deep table rises. The channel-walker's tally, chalked each shift on the slate by the cage, is the chamber's most trusted instrument, and no engineer has ever proposed replacing it with a gauge, because the salamanders have never once been wrong and gauges have.

**The neighbors.**

Because that is what the animals are, to the crew — not infestation, not even ecosystem, but *neighbors*, in the full, complicated, half-affectionate, half-resigned sense of the word.

The bat colony is spoken of in the singular, as one resident: *the Roof.* The Roof is consulted like weather. The Roof going quiet over the northeast quarter empties that quarter of people within a minute, no order given. The Roof's evening departure — the great spiraling exhalation of bodies up the bore at dusk, a soft thunder that lasts ten minutes and leaves the ceiling stone-colored and strange — marks the shift change more reliably than any bell, and the crew descending in the cage pass through the rising column and feel the wind of it on their faces and consider it, privately, each one of them, the best moment of the day.

The crickets are the walls' nerves and are treated accordingly — never crushed, gently displaced from valve handles with the side of a hand, their slow chirring waves read the way a sailor reads swell. The snails have names, the big ones, the ones whose pace and patience have made them landmarks: there is one the size of a fist, on the seventh pipe run, that has been traversing the same forty feet of lagging for longer than anyone now on the roster has been employed, and three generations of tenders have checked its progress on arriving at shift the way one checks the time. The shrimp drift in their warm shoals and are scooped from intake screens in cupped palms and returned to the channel, every time, without comment, because of the rule.

The rule is the closest thing the Condensery has to scripture. *Nothing is poisoned. Nothing is killed that isn't eating the engine.* It began as sanitation policy after the die-off, but a rule kept absolutely for long enough stops being policy and becomes character, and the character of the crew is now simply this: they are people who keep a truce. New hires learn it before they learn the valves. There are tenders who have been disciplined, in their careers, for fighting, for drink, for sleeping on watch — and one, in living memory, for stamping on beetles in a temper, which the crew speaks of in a lower voice than the other offenses.

**The largest resident.**

The crew calls the engine *her*, mostly, and sometimes *the Old Lady*, though she is younger than the bat colony and younger than the Gardener. She was built by people who are all dead now, assembled rivet by rivet in this hot wet dark by a crew whose names survive only on a brass plate that the flowstone has already half-swallowed on the abandoned lower run — entombing the builders' names in the mountain, which the current crew has decided, after some thought, is fitting rather than sad. She is tended like livestock: fed, listened to, her moods accommodated, her seals changed in long careful surgeries during which the whole chamber goes eerie with her silence and the bats audibly fret. The mountain is adopting her the way it adopts everything, drapery of pale stone creeping up her lowest footings a finger-width a decade, and the crew lets the lowest inch go. It is understood to be rent.

**And the whole of it.**

What truly lives in the Lower Condensery is not any one of these — not the crew, not the Roof, not the Old Lady, not the ten thousand pale grazers on the warm new stone. It is the arrangement itself: the truce, the layered breathing, the slow circulation of heat and water and guano and bread crumbs and attention through every tier of the dark. The chamber is one organism wearing many bodies, some furred, some shelled, some in boots, one made of riveted iron — all of them keeping time to the same four-beat pulse, all of them warming themselves at the same young rock, all of them party to the same far-off prophecy, which the crew calls, when they speak of it at all, *the Leaving*: the cold generations hence, when the infant stone finishes cooling and everything that lives here, animal and engine and tender alike, will have to go up the bore together.

They do not dread it. It is too far away, and besides, it confirms what they already know and the surface never quite believes: that they are all, every one of them down there in the wet amber dark — the breath-reader and the beetles, the Gardener and the green, the salamanders and the snail on pipe seven — the same kind of thing. Tenants of a warmth. Residents of a breath. For now, the breath continues, and so do they.
