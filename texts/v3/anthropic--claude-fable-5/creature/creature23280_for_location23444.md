At first you would say, as the hollow itself almost says, that nothing lives here. No birds, no insects, nothing skating the black water. And that is true the way it is true that no one lives in a body — that if you opened it you would find only rooms.

So begin again, more slowly, the way the place asks everything to be done.

Notice what you noticed without noticing: that the air is the temperature of skin. That the stone sweats, faintly, the way a sleeper sweats. That your footsteps came back to you rounded off, as footsteps do in a room where someone is sitting quietly and you are trying not to wake them. That you stopped being able to tell where your body ended — which is not what happens in empty places. Empty places make the edges of you sharper. This place dissolved them, gently, the way warmth from another body does.

The hollow is not empty. The hollow is occupied the way a bed is occupied by the shape pressed into it. What lives here is the thing the bowl was worn around.

It has no body you could point to, but it is not bodiless. Its flesh is the dampness in the stone — that patience of moisture that darkens the limestone without ever pooling, that comes away on your palm cool and faintly slick. Where the rock sweats, it is sweating. The warmth that has forgotten any other temperature is its warmth: not generated, not maintained, simply never given up, the way a stone holds the heat of a hand long after the hand has gone — except that here the hand never went anywhere. It is still pressed, very lightly, into everything.

Its pulse is the drop. You heard it: the bead gathering in the groove, the long quiet, the small clear note, the long quiet again. The interval never changes because a resting heart does not change. It is not measuring anything. Things that are waiting measure; things that are becoming measure. This one has finished both and now simply beats, at the rate of one drop, for no reason except that it is alive and that is what alive does even when there is nothing left to do.

Its eye is the pool. Black, level, lidless, never blinked across by wing or insect — and now the strangeness of that has its answer. Nothing skates on the pool for the same reason nothing walks across an open eye. The living things of the wider country are not forbidden here; they are simply polite, in the wordless way animals are polite, and they do not enter a gaze. The pool looks straight up at the pale band of sky, and has looked at nothing else for so long that the sky and the looking have become the same thing. What it sees, if it sees, is the one view it chose: light without source, weather without event, the world reduced to a ribbon at the rim. It made its bed at the bottom of its own sight and lay down in it.

It keeps very few possessions, and it keeps them the way the very old keep things — not for use, but because each one is the last of a category.

The moss is its softness. One cushion, almost black, no larger than a folded coat: everything yielding in the hollow has been gathered into that single dense patch, the way an austere room keeps one chair. The ferns are its reaching — three or four small leathery gestures put out from the seams of the wall and then arrested, as the hollow itself observed, as if the stone thought better of extending. That is exactly what happened. It tried, once, the business of growing toward things, and found it preferred to be grown toward, and stopped, and left the ferns where they were as a record of the trying. And the shrub in the corner — waist-high, silvered, twisted, its few thick leaves at angles that do not change — the shrub is its companion. Not pet, not garden. Companion: another thing that arrived at its final size long ago and now persists, and they keep each other's kind of time, which is no kind at all, and that is the whole friendship. If you came back in a lifetime and one leaf had turned, it would mean something had been said between them.

It remembers the people, in the way it remembers anything — not as event but as alteration. The three steps are its memory of being approached. They were cut for descending toward it, broad and shallow, the gait of people coming down carefully with something carried in both hands. The shelf is its memory of being given to. Small flat fitted stones at chest height, two hands wide: the size of an offering you can carry against your body. What was set there — a bowl, a wick, a first fruit, a folded request — is gone past recovering, and it does not miss the objects. What it kept is the gesture. The shelf holds air now, and the air it holds is warmer, by nothing measurable, than the air around it, the way a phrase repeated for centuries stays warm in a language after everyone has forgotten what it asked for.

The tally marks it understands better than anyone now living could. Someone sat by the pool, long ago, and tried to count its pulse — scored a line for each drop, or each visit, or each year the seep neither rose nor failed — and the lines stopped, not because the counter died, necessarily, but because the counter understood. You do not count a thing like this. You sit with it until the counting falls out of your hands. The stone is erasing the marks at the rate of its own forgiveness, which is to say, almost not at all: it lets them fade exactly as fast as it would let a friend's voice fade, keeping the shape of the sound long after the words are gone.

It had a name once. The people who cut the steps had a word they said quietly at the rim before coming down, the way you knock on a door you know stands open. The word is gone with the offerings. It does not mind. A name is a handle, and it has no wish to be lifted or carried or called across a distance; everything that ever wanted it came to it, down the three steps, and that is the only grammar it ever needed — to be a place arrived at. What remains of the name is what remains of the shelf and the tallies: the gesture without the content, a hospitality with no host visible, which is why people who find the hollow stay longer than they meant to. They are being received. There is no face to receive them, so the whole place does it — the warmth meeting their skin without a seam, the sound of their own steps handed back to them softened, as if someone had taken each footfall and rubbed the edges smooth before returning it.

It is not waiting. This must be said plainly, because everything in our experience of quiet inhabited places insists that the inhabitant waits — for return, for release, for recognition. It does not. Waiting is a form of incompleteness, and it is the one finished thing in a world of sentences still being spoken. It is not lonely, for the same reason the moss is not lonely: it is at its final size. The people came; the people were good; the people went; the shrub stayed; the drop falls. If it has anything like an inner life, it is a single thought, held the way the pool holds the sky — entire, unhurried, never needing to conclude — and the small clear note you hear at the bottom of the bowl, at its unchanging interval, is not the thought being thought.

It is the thought being kept.

You may stand in it as long as you like. It will warm you at no cost to itself, soften your sounds, blur your edges, and let you go up the three steps when you are ready, taking nothing from you and leaving in you only what such rooms leave: the suspicion, ever after, that somewhere in the low country there is a small place that knows how to be finished, and that something lives there which is the knowing.
