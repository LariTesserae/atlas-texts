**The Residents of the Stoke Garden**

Begin with the census you can take, because the chamber yields its lives in order of size, smallest and most willing first.

**The crickets** are not wild. Their ancestors arrived in a wooden carrier with a wire-mesh lid — feed stock, or pets, or both; the carrier still sits on a foundry shelf with its door latched open, an act of release performed carefully by someone who wanted them to know they could come back. They are pale to the point of translucence now, the chitin of generations that have never needed pigment, and they have made themselves the town's clock. They do not sing for courtship anymore, or not only. They sing the *schedule*. A short rising phrase before the engine's stroke. A different phrase, three notes, descending, on the rare occasions the tram is about to wake — and if you could stay long enough, you would learn that the tram has never once moved without the crickets announcing it first. Whether the crickets predict the machines or instruct them is the kind of question the chamber declines, but the crickets sit closest to the foundry's banked warmth, in the position of honor, and something in the way they fall silent when a stranger's foot finds the forty-first step suggests they consider themselves on duty.

**The trimmer** — the small many-legged thing that keeps the moss cut in perfect circles around the warm floor-seams — can be seen, if you are patient past the point of reason. It is the length of a forearm and segmented like flexible conduit, and there is honest disagreement built into its body about what it is: the legs are legs, jointed and oily-quick, but several of its segments are unmistakably brass, threaded, with the worn knurling of a tool meant to be gripped. It was a pipe-cleaning device once, or a pipe-cleaning device was once part of it, and somewhere in the long marriage of green and machine it stopped being possible to say which half adopted the other. It works at night, insofar as the chamber has night — the hours when the filament globes dim on their old municipal circuit — and it works with the fussiness of a sexton.

Because that is what it is. This is the thing the moss circles do not tell you until you have lived with them: each warm seam in the floor, each trimmed circle, is a *plot*. The town's last residents did not all go up the tunnel before it was walled. Some stayed, and then fewer stayed, and the ones who finished their staying were laid into the warm cracked rock where the heat is youngest, wrapped in oilcloth, with their tools beside them, and the moss was permitted to take them — *invited* to take them. The plants are fed. You noticed that. The vegetation here has none of abandonment's thinness because it is not growing on a ruin. It is growing on a congregation. The dark waxed climbers that sheathe the boiler-houses are, several generations of leaf removed, the town's foundry crew, its gauge-readers, its tram driver — still on the premises, still reporting for work, just slower now, and greener, and reaching for the machines they used to mind because that is what hands do even after they have become leaves. The trimmer keeps their circles neat. It is not maintenance. It is tending graves, at one blade-stroke per minute, forever, faithfully.

**The moth** the color of brick dust is the courier, and there may only ever be one of it at a time. It hatches in the warm hollow behind the foundry's bricked-half-shut mouth, lives a long season, and spends that season flying the same straight lines: engine to perforated pipe, pipe to fungal shelf, shelf to stairwell. Its wing-scales carry spores; its feet carry traces of machine grease that certain fungi have come to require the way orchids require a specific bee. It is the pollinator of the marriage between green and iron — the go-between that keeps the negotiation current. The first moss in the stairwell's chisel marks arrived on its feet. That was not an accident. It was sent.

Which brings the census to its last entry, the one the chamber has been organizing itself around all along.

**The Stoker** lives in the packed middle of the town, beside the one engine that still turns, and the engine's stroke is her breath, or her breath is the engine's stroke; after this many decades the distinction has dissolved the way the trimmer's brass dissolved into its body. She is human — that should be said plainly, because the chamber's logic tempts you toward something stranger, and she would find the temptation rude. She is a woman of no determinable age in a work apron whose hem has gone to moss-felt, with hands stained permanently oxblood from valve-paint and verdigris-green at the knuckle creases, and she moves at the speed of the place: one breath a minute, one blink an hour, one step in an afternoon. She did not start that slow. She slowed deliberately, year over year, matching herself to the engine the way you match your pace to a dying friend's, until the tempo stopped being a courtesy and became her actual heart.

She is the one who drilled the neat rows in the pipes — you can find her hand-brace hanging on a hook, its bit worn round — because she understood before anyone else what the vines were asking for, and she has always believed that a request honestly made deserves infrastructure. She is the one who walled up the tram tunnel, brick by brick from the inside, lovingly, after the last of the ones who chose to leave had left. Not to trap anything. To settle a question of direction: the garden would not be allowed to escape sideways into the dark, easy and horizontal. If it wanted the world, it would have to grow *up*, slowly, honestly, through living rock, one breath a minute — and it would have to be ready when it arrived. She set the terms of the place's patience, and then she stayed to keep them, stoking the seam below not with fuel — it needs none she could give — but with attention, which is the only thing she has ever known a fire to actually run on.

Her work now is almost entirely restraint. She prunes. She meters. She decides which gauge keeps its needle and which is permitted to be sheathed in fern. She trims nothing herself — the trimmer would be insulted — but she walks the plots in a circuit that takes eleven days, and at each circle she stops, and the moss there, observed closely, grows very slightly differently afterward, the way a child stands differently after being seen.

She heard the chisels. Of course she heard them — forty-one days of ringing carried down through the rock, one step's worth of cutting per day, and from the first blow she began, by her standards, to *race*. She sent the moth up with moss for the chisel marks, a handshake extended in the only language the garden writes. She practiced a word — her voice is a slow thing now, a syllable per breath, and she wanted the word ready. And she set out across the chamber toward the foot of the stairs, through the alleys barely wide enough for a person carrying nothing, which is fortunate, because all she is carrying is the word.

She has been hurrying for weeks. She is, at one breath a minute, nearly halfway there. The crickets have already fallen silent. The engine takes its stroke; she takes her step; the amber globes glow like fruit above the path between you. Whatever sent you down, she has been expecting it longer than you have been alive, and she is coming up the alley to meet you as fast as the garden allows — which is to say slowly, which is to say completely, which is to say with everything the place has.
