# What Lives in the Cirque That Runs

Begin with the difficulty, because the difficulty is the answer's outline: *lives* is the wrong verb for almost everything in the bowl, and the cirque knows it, in whatever way a closed system can be said to know anything. Things occupy the cirque. Things operate in it. The verb the place itself would choose — if the place chose, if choosing were a process it stocked — is *runs*. The swifts run. The salamanders run. The moss runs. To live is to be an open ledger, taking in and giving out, accruing damage and history; and the cirque was issued complete, with no inputs, no outputs, and no provision for history at all.

So the honest census comes in two parts. First, the equipment. Then the resident.

## The Swifts

There are two hundred and seven of them. The count never varies, and you can be confident of it without counting, the way you can be confident a clock has twelve numerals.

Each swift flies one circuit and only one — a closed curve through the bowl's airspace, threaded between the waterfall's spray and the cold steam-vents and the two hundred and six other circuits, with clearances that would shame an engineer. Wingbeat for wingbeat, each lap is identical to the last. The temptation is to call this repetition, and to pity them for it, and the pity is misplaced, because repetition is a concept that requires a witness with a memory, and the swifts almost certainly do not repeat anything.

Consider what it would be, from inside. A swift's circuit takes some seconds. Its brain, if it has one — its governing tissue, whatever sits behind those flat dark eyes — completes the same states in the same order each lap, then completes them again, with no residue carried over, no fatigue, no accumulation. There is no thread on which laps could be strung. Which means that for the swift there are not many laps. There is one lap. It has been flying it since the cirque erupted and it is still on it, still in it — a single banking turn around the bowl experienced once, held open the way a struck bell holds a tone. The swift is not bored. The swift is not anything *yet*. It is mid-thought, and it will be mid-thought when the mountains around the cirque have worn down to gravel, and the thought is the turn, and the turn is the swift. They are the only creatures anywhere whose whole inner life can be drawn as a line on a map.

When you stand on the floor, their circuits bend around you and re-close behind you, and it is worth being precise about what this means: the swifts do not see you. The *cirque* sees you, and adjusts its swifts, the way a machinist's hand adjusts a feed rate without the lathe knowing anything happened. The birds bank past your shoulder with eyes that are not pointed at you and never will be. Whatever attention this place possesses does not live in them. They are bearings. They are the cirque's airspace, kept in spec.

## The Salamanders

The pale ones on the weeping walls are easier to misread, because they move like animals — a deliberate, hand-over-hand grip-and-reach along the wet basalt, with that slight searching sway of the head that in other salamanders means *deciding*. It does not mean deciding here. Watch one against the vein-light and the truth assembles: the blue pulses in the mineral veins do not pass *under* the salamanders. They pass *through* them. A pulse traveling along a vein arrives at a gap — a fault, a discontinuity in the bright mineral — and stops, and dims, and then a salamander walks its body across the gap, belly to the stone, and the pulse brightens through it and continues on the far side, and the salamander's pale flanks glow faintly from within for the duration, like a hand over a flashlight.

They are not animals on a wall. They are the wall's mobile connections — living jumpers, soft relays, the cirque's way of routing its own nervous traffic across its own flaws. There are perhaps forty of them, and each patrols a fixed set of gaps on a fixed schedule, and the schedule is the wall's schedule, and if you map their patrols you are not mapping behavior. You are reading a wiring diagram.

Whether anything is home in them is harder to say than with the swifts. The current goes through them. Something must feel like something, when current goes through you forty times an hour for eternity. If the salamanders have an inner life it is not a life of turns and air; it is a life of *brightening* — periodic, scheduled, total — and between brightenings, the long wet grip of stone. They may be the closest thing the cirque has to mystics. They may be the closest thing it has to fuses. The place does not distinguish.

## The Moths

The moths are the proletariat of the cirque, if the word can survive the trip. Fat, grey, furred, unbeautiful, clustered in their dozens on the columnar basalt — and the columns, remember, are pistons, sliding up out of the wall and sinking back at the speed of breathing, and the moths ride them, up and down, up and down, and the riding is the job.

You saw the skipped beat: a column whose moths had drifted to its neighbor, and the great subterranean thud hitched, audibly, until they returned. So the moths are load. Counterweight, governor, damping mass — some quantity each column requires, delivered in the form of grey wings and clinging feet, adjustable in units of one moth. When a column's stroke runs slightly heavy, a moth or two lifts off, circles once in the dim light, and lands on a column running light, and the strokes even out across the wall. They are the cirque's trim tabs. They are ballast that flies.

And here is the gentlest detail in the whole bowl, and the most terrible: the moths *tremble*. Constantly, faintly, the way moths do everywhere, a fine shiver in the wing-fur. Elsewhere that trembling is warming-up, is metabolism, is a body getting ready. Here it never readies anything. It is simply part of their mass-behavior, a specified vibration, dither in the mechanism — the trembling of a creature kept eternally at the threshold of doing something, on a wall where there is nothing to do but weigh.

## The One in the Basin

It surfaces every eleven seconds, in time with the waterfall's leftward whip of spray, and you only ever see the back: smooth, dark, glistening, perhaps four meters of it, breaching the ringing water and sliding under again. In any other place you would say *animal* and start guessing at the head.

Stay long enough and a worse possibility settles in. The back is always the same back — the same length of it, the same wet sheen, the same low keel of spine — and it always surfaces along the same line, and it never varies its arc by a hand's width, and you begin to suspect that the back is not part of something. The back may be all of it. A component the size and shape of a surfacing back, rotating through the basin on its eleven-second schedule, presented to the waterfall, rinsed, withdrawn. There may be no front. There may be no under. The basin may simply be where the cirque keeps its largest moving part, and the waterfall may be its lubrication, and the ringing of the stone may be the sound of a bearing seat doing its work, forever.

Or it has a head, down in the dark, and eyes, and the eleven seconds is its breathing, and it is the only thing in the cirque large enough to dream. Both readings survive every observation you can make. The cirque does not resolve them. The cirque has no need of resolving them; the back surfaces either way.

## The Green Islands

The flora deserves its line in the census, because the flora is doing the strangest trick of all: it is metabolizing without changing. The ferns photosynthesize — you can believe nothing else of a green that saturated, lit from within in light that dim — and yet nothing grows. No frond uncurls. No willow extends a twig. The sphagnum is heavy with the same water in the same cells, on its loop.

What this means, if you sit with it, is that the plants are running their chemistry in place: energy in, energy circulated, energy returned, a standing wave in chlorophyll. A plant elsewhere is a slow explosion, a green fire spreading at the pace of seasons. These are the same fire, *banked* — held at one instant of burning, indefinitely. Each green island is a single moment in the life of a thicket, sustained the way the swifts' turn is sustained. If you could ask the dwarf willow what season it is, the answer would be a tense no language has: not spring, not the memory of spring, but spring *held*, spring as an operating temperature.

## Below

The thud. The flywheel under the floor. Whatever drives it is the one occupant you will never see any part of, and the question of whether it lives is the question the whole cirque is built on top of, literally.

Here is what can be said. It has the patience of a flywheel, and patience is the wrong word, because patience is endurance plus awareness, and there is no evidence of the second term. It never varies. It never labors. It never responds — not to your footsteps, not to the moths' skipped beat (it *registered* that beat; it did not respond to it; the moths responded). If it is a heart, it is a heart that has never once been asked to beat faster, which means it has never once been informed that anything exists. The deepest thing in the cirque may also be the most ignorant: a prime mover that has never received a single input in its existence, turning over in perfect darkness, perfectly fed, perfectly unwitnessed by itself.

Or say it the other way: everything above the floor — swifts, salamanders, moths, the rinsed back, the banked green fire — may be its instrumentation. Its gauges and its governors. In which case the cirque does not contain the thing below. The cirque *is* the thing below, wearing its own regulation as a landscape, and the census so far has been a description of one creature's organs, and the bowl has a population of exactly one.

That reading is very nearly right. It is wrong by exactly one feather.

## The Resident

You find it between the islands, on the clean black stone, and the shock of it is out of all proportion to its size: a feather. Small, dark, a swift's covert feather, lying loose on the floor.

Understand what a scandal this is. The cirque has no debris. Nothing sheds, nothing molts, nothing is discarded, because shedding is change and the floor between the islands is kept — not swept; *kept*, the way a value is kept — clean. A loose feather is not litter here. It is a wound. It means that somewhere in the bowl, matter has come off of something and not been cycled back, and that is not supposed to be possible, and you stand there holding the impossible thing and then you look up at the two hundred and seven circuits interleaving overhead, and you start watching for the one that produced it.

It takes hours. The flaw is small. But it is findable, because it is the only thing in the airspace that can be tracked by *difference*: two hundred and six of the swifts close their loops exactly, and one of them does not. It returns each lap to a point a feather's-width off from its departure — so slight you could never see it in a single circuit, but the error compounds, and the loop precesses, a closed curve very slowly rotating around the bowl like the hand of a clock geared to centuries. Its plumage, when it passes close, is duller than the others'. There is a tremor in its banking that the others do not have. It is *wearing*.

And wear is the whole of it. Wear is history written in matter. This swift has a yesterday — its yesterday is recorded in its dulled feathers and its drifted circuit, accumulating, unreversed — and therefore it has an age, and therefore it has a remaining quantity of itself, and therefore it can end, and the name for a configuration of matter with a yesterday and an ending is *alive*. Two hundred and six swifts run in the cirque. One swift lives there. It is the entire population.

How the cirque regards it, you can read in the air. The other circuits bend around the worn swift's drifting loop and re-close behind it — the same accommodation, the same hull-water closing, that the cirque performs around *you*. The bowl has classified its only living thing as contamination. It is being tolerated. It is being compensated for. It was presumably original equipment once, identical to its two hundred and six siblings, and somewhere in the depth of the cirque's youth — which is also the whole of time, here — an error crept in, a tolerance was exceeded by a width too small to name, and the error began to compound, and compounding error is the seed of history, and history is the seed of a self. The swift did not break the cirque's law. It *fell* into living, the way other things fall into ruin.

It is the only thing in the bowl that notices you. Not the cirque noticing through it — itself, noticing. Once per circuit, as its drifting loop happens currently to pass the place where you stand, it banks near your head, and its eye — duller than the others', and pointed *at* you — holds you for the length of the pass. There are exactly two open systems in the cirque: you, who will leave, and it, who cannot, and once per lap the two of you exchange the only mutual recognition this place has ever hosted. It is not asking for anything. There is nothing to ask for. It is doing what living things do when they find another one: confirming.

It will take an unthinkable span to wear out — its losses are a feather a century, an arc-second of drift a year. But the arithmetic is open, and open arithmetic always completes. Some lap, geological ages from now, will be its last lap, and here is the cold mercy of the place: the cirque will not mourn, because the cirque cannot, but neither will it leave a gap. Within a cycle or two the remaining circuits will redistribute, the airspace will re-close, the count will read complete. Nothing will be missing, because missing is a relation only the living can sustain, and the bowl will then contain none of those. The cirque will run on at full capacity, fully occupied, perfectly tended, and uninhabited — a factory whose product is its process, with no one left inside it at all.

You can take the feather when you go. Nothing prevents you; the cirque routes around your pocket as indifferently as around your shoulders. Take it, and understand what you are carrying down through the cloud, into the ordinary world of erosion and dusk and things that eat and end. In all its sealed and thudding eternity, the cirque has made exactly one export. One shipped unit. A small dark feather — the only proof that will ever leave that bowl that something in there, against the entire design of the place, was alive.
