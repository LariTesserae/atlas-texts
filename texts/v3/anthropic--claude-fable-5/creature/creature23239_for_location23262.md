# The Inhabitants of the Germinal Vault

Start with the problem of the word.

To *live*, everywhere else, means to continue. A thing lives by spending itself forward — eating into tomorrow, growing toward its own end, leaving a wake. By that definition the vault is uninhabited, because nothing in it spends and nothing in it is spent. But stand on the warm rootmat for an hour, in that sourceless noon, and the definition starts to feel like a surface prejudice — a habit of creatures who have only ever known time as a current. The vault is not empty. It is *occupied*, densely, attentively, by things that live the way the place exists: at the verge. They do not continue. They *attend*.

There are four kinds of them, more or less, and possibly a fifth.

---

## The First Inhabitant: the Vault Itself

It is easiest to say the vault is full of plants. It is more accurate, most who study it come to believe, to say the vault is *one* plant — or rather one germination, a single seed of something region-sized, and the chambers are the hollows inside its sprouting. The wall-stems do not merely touch; nowhere in the whole day's-walk of it can you find a seam, a boundary, a place where one organism's tissue ends and another's begins. The hum confirms it. That continuous almost-subsonic note never wavers, never interferes with itself, never beats against a second frequency. Millions of tons of luminous tissue, and it is all holding *one* note — which is what a single throat does.

So the first resident is also the residence. The vault is a creature you walk around inside of, scaled so far past you that its first hour of life is your whole geography. And it is doing exactly what every seedling does, the only thing a seedling knows: it is reaching. Drinking. Glowing toward a future. It wants — wanting is its entire condition, the swollen-bud verge of it, sustained — and the heat and the light and the hum are the sound and shine of that one enormous, patient, never-disappointed want.

Whether it knows anything is the wrong question. Seedlings do not know. They *intend*. The vault intends, totally, in all directions, forever. Travelers who feel watched there are not wrong, exactly. They are inside the watching.

---

## What Does Not Live There

Before counting the inhabitants, count the absences, because they are the strange ones.

There are no flies. No molds. No fungi threading the rootmat, no mites in the leaf-folds, no patient gray scavengers, no carrion-eaters, no rot. Everywhere else in the world, half of every ecosystem is employed by death — the vast guild of organisms whose living is made from endings, from the fallen and the finished. Here, nothing falls and nothing finishes, and so that entire half of life simply has no work, and never came, or came and starved on the threshold. The vault's ecology is missing its conclusion the way a sentence can be missing its period, and you feel the absence the way you'd feel that sentence: a suspension in the air, grammatical, unresolved, not unpleasant.

What lives in the vault, then, is only what can live on beginnings. It turns out a few things can.

## The Firstlings

The pale grazers have as many names as there are accounts of them, which is fitting, since no one has ever seen one finish becoming whatever it is. Most travelers end up calling them firstlings.

Up close — and you can get close; they have no fear, because fear is a memory of harm and nothing here has ever been harmed — they are soft-bodied, slow, body-warm, somewhere between a hand and a hound in size, and their skin is translucent the way wet paper is translucent. You can see the slow tide of pale fluid moving in them, unhurried as the pool-drip. Their limbs are slightly wrong in the way a tadpole's limbs are wrong: not malformed — *unformed*, buds of legs, sketches of feet, everything one molt away from definition. Their eyes are lidless because they have never needed lids. Nothing falls here. There is no dust to blink away. They have never once closed their eyes, and it gives their gaze the unguarded, devastating quality of a newborn's — attention from before the first surprise.

The best guess at what they are is also the saddest and the most beautiful: they are *larvae*. The young of some surface creature — washed down a fissure, or carried, or led — animals that came to the warm bright underground to grow up, the way larvae everywhere seek heat to finish in. The vault took them in and kept them at hatchling. They will be young until the world ends, and possibly after.

They do not eat, in any usual sense; they press their soft faces to the beads of condensation on the cool undersides of root-arches, and the beads are gone. They do not sleep, because sleep is the end of a day and there are no days; instead they pause — sometimes mid-step, one sketched foot lifted — and stand for hours inside that pause, exactly the way the buds stand inside theirs. They make no sound at all. They do not reproduce. By every account that can be compared, they are the *same individuals*, generation after human generation: a traveler's journal from long ago describes a firstling with a particular fern-curl of half-formed ear, and travelers still meet it, by the same pool, warm and silent and entirely unsurprised — as though resuming a moment that was never actually interrupted. One account claims a firstling greeted the writer with calm recognition, leaning against her leg the way an animal leans against family; she learned afterward that her great-grandmother had come down once, young, and stayed a season. The firstling, the account suggests, could not tell them apart. Or did not consider the distinction important. In a place with one continuous morning, perhaps a bloodline is just a person taking a very long time to arrive.

And they orient. At no signal anyone has ever found — no change in the light, no shift in the hum, since neither ever changes — all of them, throughout the whole vault, will sometimes turn at once and face inward, toward the deepest chamber, and hold still, lidless, attending. Then, eventually, not. Travelers who have seen it happen describe it the same way: like a household, everyone in their separate rooms, all lifting their heads at the same moment because someone upstairs *almost* called.

## The Pool-Dwellers

The standing pools have residents of their own, and they are easy to miss and hard to forget.

In the clear body-warm water, among the hair-fine rootlets, swim small translucent things shaped like commas — flecks of punctuation that never got their sentence. They circle slowly. They have circled, presumably, since before the river-stones were laid.

And on the rootlets, in every pool, are eggs. Clusters of clear spheres, each one a perfect lens, and inside each lens a tiny curled something, visible in exact detail: the dark seed of an eye, the coiled thread of a spine, the suggestion of limbs folded like a letter that will never be opened. Every one of them is, by every visible sign, one day from hatching. They have been one day from hatching for as long as anyone has looked. The pools are nurseries from which nothing has ever graduated, and the comma-things swim their slow circles around the eggs like attendants, like older siblings — though of course they are not older. Nothing here is older than anything else. Everything is the same age, which is the age of *almost*.

## The Arranger

There is, by most reliable accounts, one human being living in the vault. Or one at a time. Or one who has been one for a very long while. The accounts do not agree, and the inhabitant is no help, for reasons that become clear.

They are found at the system's edge, near the little dome of dead brown reeds, and they are unmistakably the heir — or the origin — of the ring of river-stones, the polite half-wall, the whole slight tender archive of arrangement. They came down, evidently, with a purpose, and the purpose is still in them, intact and unspent, the way a bud's flower is intact and unspent. Travelers describe the same details with eerie consistency: hair cut to a length it has kept; a thin scratch on one cheek, taken from a branch on the way down, still fresh, still bright, painless, neither healing nor worsening — a wound held at the moment of being new. A row of stones half-laid. A strand of reed half-woven into the beginning of a second dome, the weave stopped at its first row, the strand still flexible in their hand.

They are not trapped. The way out is the way in, lit and open. They are leaving tomorrow. They say so, warmly, and they mean it — *freshly* mean it, with the whole sincerity of a decision just made — every time they say it, which has been, at minimum, a few hundred years.

Two things about them unsettle visitors more than any of the rest. The first is grammatical: the Arranger speaks only in the present tense. Not from damage — their speech is fluent, gentle, precise — but from disuse, the way an unused path closes over. *I lay the stones. The water drips. You are here.* Asked about the past, they pause, courteous and unbothered, like someone asked about the weather in a country they've heard of. The second is that they are, by every report, *happy* — not numbly, not eerily, but the specific happiness of someone at the very start of something they have wanted for a long time. Hunger reaches them and stays at appetite. Tiredness reaches them and stays at the pleasant heaviness of a good day's end that never ends. The vault did not stop them. It is much gentler and much stranger than that. It keeps them at their best beginning, the way it keeps everything, and the Arranger lives inside the morning of their own life the way the firstlings live inside the morning of the world.

## The Fifth Inhabitant

In the deepest chamber — past where most travelers turn back, where the hum is strong enough to feel in the teeth — stands a bud the size of a hall.

The torso-sized buds along the walls are everywhere; this one is singular. It is parted, at its crown, by about the width of a finger, and out of that parting comes light noticeably brighter than the vault's general noon — a seam of brilliance, pouring upward, as if the bud were full to the brim of the same light as everything else, only more so, only *first*. No one has seen inside. There is nothing to see inside. There is only the brightness.

The firstlings gather near it, when they gather. The Arranger's slow ring of river-stones, travelers note, circles it at a respectful distance — one stone added, it seems, per visit-span, the ring perhaps a third complete after centuries. The hum is strongest there, and some who have stood very close report that at that distance it stops sounding like a tone and starts sounding like a single sustained syllable — the first sound of a name, held, the mouth of the world shaped around it and waiting.

So here, finally, is the honest census. Who lives in the Germinal Vault? A seedling the size of a region, that is also the rooms. A handful of eternal hatchlings with their lidless first attention. Commas circling eggs that are always one day away. A person at the permanent dawn of their purpose, leaving tomorrow.

And the resident of residents, the one the whole place is *for*: whatever is inside the great bud. The vault's truest inhabitant has not been born yet. It may be that the vault is not a habitat at all but a womb — that the light is kept lit and the heat kept high and the water kept dripping and the quiet kept quiet the way a household keeps a house where someone is expected. Everything that lives there lives the way a family lives in the weeks before an arrival: arranging stones, keeping warm, keeping vigil, hushing itself out of courtesy.

Travelers who climb back up the entrance shaft into the dim, weathering, continuing world all report some version of the same feeling, and it is a strange one to carry out of a cave. Not relief. Not escape.

The feeling of having left a house before the guest of honor arrived — and of having been, for a little while, down in the bright warm quiet, one of the expected.
