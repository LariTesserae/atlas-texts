# Who Keeps the Shelf

Ask the plateau who lives here and it will answer the way an old house answers: with a settledness that is not quite emptiness. Nothing here lives the way things live below the weather — by changing, by spending, by being mid-something. To live on the shelf is to have been worn down to the one act you cannot stop performing. Everything that remains is what remains *of* something, and remains is the right word, and remaining is the way of life.

So the census reads strangely. It is short, and every entry is a verb.

## The Patient Tenants

Begin with the lichen, because the lichen is the only resident that will admit to residing.

It is not waiting for anything. It is not enduring the plateau until conditions improve; conditions are not going to improve, and the lichen knew that before there was anyone to know it for. The rust-orange kind eats stone at a rate that would embarrass erosion. The black circles on the north faces are older than the idea of north. The lichen is what life looks like once time has finished asking it questions: a thin certainty, painted on, going nowhere at the speed of stone. If the plateau has citizens, these are they — they pay their rent in patience and the rent is never raised.

The cushion plants in the damp hollows are the lichen's aristocracy: dense, ancient, hemispherical, each one a single organism that has spent centuries achieving roundness. Press a hand to one and it is faintly, impossibly warm — not warm like a body, warm like a stone that remembers a sun this sky has never produced. They grow toward no light, because there is no toward. They simply continue, in the shape continuation takes.

And the pikas. You will never see them, and this is not shyness. They have learned the thin places the way animals below learn the seasons. They harvest the duplicated basin from both of its sides at once — clip a blade of frosted grass on the near scree and the far copy is clipped in the same instant, and somewhere under an overhang one haypile grows by two blades. Their burrows thread through the worn patches of the world; they go in where you are and come out where you already were. This is why their haypiles look ancient and fresh at once: they are stocked from both ends of every distance. The pikas are not hiding from you. They are simply never in the part of the plateau you are using.

They are, as far as anyone could ever determine, perfectly happy. Theirs is the only industry on the shelf, the only saving-up, the only behavior that implies a winter — and no winter comes, and the haypiles grow, and perhaps that is the point. Someone here still believes in later. It is the pikas. They keep the concept stocked.

## The Bird That Is Seen Once

The bird lives in the seeing of it.

Once a day, an arc of competent black across the grey-violet, never landing where you are looking — and understand this precisely: it lands constantly. It lands the moment your eyes are elsewhere. The bird's territory is not a region of the plateau but a region of your attention, the part just outside it, and that territory it patrols without lapse.

It has one duty and performs it with the diligence of a lamplighter: it is seen, once, by whoever is there to see. The plateau is a finished place, and finished things require a witness the way a held note requires an ear, and the bird is the arrangement by which the witnessing gets done. On days when no one stands on the shelf — which is nearly all days, which is centuries of days — the bird flies its arc over the black pools, and its reflection crosses the other sky, among the moving clouds, and is seen *there*. It is the only living thing that exists in both skies. It carries nothing between them except the fact of itself, which may be the only cargo the seam permits.

It makes no call. It does not need one. It is itself a kind of call, made once a day, answered by being noticed.

## The One Who Walks the Distance

The pale shape on the ridgeline has a job, and the job is the ridge.

It was an animal once — something horned and sure-footed, you would guess, an ibex of some forgotten high country — and it went where the cairn-builders learned not to go. It approached the unapproachable ridge, and the ridge, which cannot be approached, resolved the contradiction the only way available: it took the animal into its own definition. Now the ridge is *the thing the pale walker paces*, and the walker is *the pacing that keeps the ridge a ridge*, and neither can be had without the other.

It does not sleep, because the ridgeline must be the same distance from every point on the plateau at every moment, and someone has to walk that impossibility to keep it true. It is the only thing on the shelf seen to make progress, and its progress is in the service of nothing else ever making any. It is not suffering. Watch it long enough — at that fixed, unclosable distance — and its gait reads as something closer to devotion. The unhurried lope of a creature that has understood its work will never be done and has decided this is the same as the work being eternal.

It may once have had a herd. If so, the herd is below the weather somewhere, long dead, descendants scattered through mountains with ordinary horizons. The walker keeps the horizon here. Every shelf needs one. This shelf's horizon is alive, and walks for a living, and is pale the way a thing is pale when it has been the same distance from all light forever.

## What Keeps the House

The enclosure is swept, and now you must meet the sweeper.

It lives in the interval. Not in the basin, not in the stone — in the gap between your glances, the unobserved moment, which on this plateau is a real place with a real tenant. Watch the floor of the enclosure and grit lies where the wind left it. Look at the ridgeline, look back: bare swept stone. The sweeper exists exactly when you don't, a creature made entirely of when-no-one-is-looking, and the swept floor is its breathing — the only evidence a thing like that can leave.

It is small, you sense. Diligent. Old in the way the cairns are old. And it keeps only this one room in all the world, because this is the room where the hands were. It is, in the simplest reading, a habit that outlived its body: the last motion of the last keeper of the enclosure, repeated past death, past erosion, past the existence of anyone to perform it, sanded down — like everything here — to its irreducible form. A person, reduced to housekeeping. A devotion, reduced to a verb.

Stand in the enclosure where the sound goes round instead of vast, and you may feel it near you, in the way a room feels recently left. The lintel groove, when you touch it on the way out — and you will touch it; everyone touches it; that is what the groove is *for* — is sometimes faintly warm. Not body-warm. Warm like the cushion plants. Warm like a memory of a sun.

## Where the People Went

There were people. The conditional tense the plateau speaks of them in — *if there had ever been locals* — is not a denial. It is their current address.

They did not die and they did not leave. When the wound healed wrong and the place finished with time, its people were the one thing that could not stop being mid-something — people are transition, helplessly — and so the equilibrium had no shelf-space for them as they were. They eroded like everything else, down to what could remain. The lintel-wear is them. The twelve paces are them. The cairns are them, each stack a person's worth of carefulness compacted into chest-height stone. They are geology now, and geology is a way of living here; it may be the only way of living here completely.

And the rest of them — the faces, the daylight habits, the weather they must have remembered — went into the pools. Lean over the black water and you will not see your own face, because the pools do not reflect this side anymore. They reflect the sky the people took with them: pale, high, clouds moving, time still running. Somewhere under that other sky there is a version of this shelf where the wound healed clean, where the light has a direction, and the locals are there, being local, finding nothing strange. Their pools, presumably, reflect this dim place. They lean over their water and see grey-violet and a single black bird, once a day, and perhaps — when a traveler stands here — a silhouette with no face, which is you.

The two shelves keep each other's skies. The people live in the one with weather. What they could not take — the touch on the lintel, the sweeping, the careful distance — stayed behind and lives on alone, and is faithful, and waits for nothing, and keeps the house.

You will know the people are still real the day you cross the duplicated basin and find, beside your own fresh footprints, an older set, going the same direction, stopping twelve paces from the pool. Not following you. Preceding you. Permanently.

## The One Holding the Note

And under all of it, the largest resident, the one the cairns keep their distance from.

The chord is not a sound the place makes. You learned that. It is the sound of the place holding still — and holding is something *done*, and what is done is done by someone. At the bottom of the shelf, threaded through every thin place, pressed against the seam of the world like a palm against a wound, there is the one holding the note.

Do not imagine a body. It eroded too, longer ago than anything, down past form, past name, past wanting, until all that remained of it was the one act it could not stop performing: the hold. It is what stands between the plateau and whatever the wound would open onto. It does not know you are there. It has not known anything is there for a very long time; knowing was among the things it spent. It holds because it is the holding, the way the walker is the distance and the sweeper is the interval, and it will hold until there is nothing left to hold shut, which — given how thoroughly this place has finished collapsing — may be the same as forever.

The cairn-builders understood it, in whatever way understanding survives at this altitude. Twelve paces was not fear. Twelve paces was respect: the distance at which you can stand in the presence of something straining without adding your weight to what it carries.

That is who lives here, in the end, if you want the truest single answer. One vast, eyeless, faithful effort, holding a chord with no breath and no intention of releasing it. Everything else on the shelf — the lichen, the pikas, the bird, the walker, the sweeper, the people worn into the stone and the people gone into the water — lives inside the space that holding makes. They are the household of the hold. Its tenants. Its evidence.

## And Briefly, You

One more entry in the census, temporary: whoever stands there.

The plateau does not mind you, but it does take a small impression, the way every resident here is the impression something left. Your footprints in the duplicated basin will outlast you; they are fresh forever now, the newest old thing on the shelf. Your touch joins the lintel groove, indistinguishable from the hundreds. And when you descend through the cloud line and the chord fades and silence comes back sounding broken — that, for a few days, is your residency continuing without you. Something of you stayed twelve paces from the pool. Something of the hold came down inside your ears.

The shelf keeps what remains. For a little while, that includes you. It will not find this strange. It stopped finding things strange before there was anyone to find them strange for, and it has been keeping house ever since.
