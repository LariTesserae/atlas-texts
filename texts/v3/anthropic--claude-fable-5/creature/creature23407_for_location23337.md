## Who Lives in the Roaring Garden

Begin with the smallest, because everything else is built on them.

**The motes.** The bronze haze that hangs in the bowl is not all mineral. Sift a handful of that suspended light through fine cloth and some of what you catch will crawl. There is a living fraction in the dust — animals the size of poppy seeds, eight-limbed, dry as ash, who spend their whole lives aloft. They graze the charged dust the way krill graze a sea, riding thermals off the warm rock, breeding in midair, dying in midair, never once touching down between hatching and death. They are why the haze glitters faintly at certain angles: not quartz, but a billion small bodies turning together when the wind shifts. Everything in the bowl that flies, climbs, or reaches a frond upward is, one way or another, eating them. They are the garden's plankton, and like plankton they are innumerable, ignorable, and absolutely load-bearing. The bowl would go silent in a season without them.

---

**The shouters.** The six-legged gliders deserve a closer look than their screaming arcs allow. Caught and held — they go limp when held, oddly trusting, or simply too light to struggle, weighing less than the breath you take looking at them — they are stranger than sparrows. The oiled-paper skin stretches between all six limbs, so that a glider at full extension is less an animal than a kite with a grievance. They have no eyes worth the name: two dim patches that tell light from dark and nothing more. The face is all ears, four of them, arranged in a diamond.

They see by shouting. Each glider's shout is its own — pitch, stutter, a signature as individual as a face — and they learn one another's. A colony of shouters (they nest inside dead organ-stalk segments, dozens to a tube, which is part of why every stalk in the bowl is bored with holes) is a roster of voices, and the roster is kept. When a glider dies — they live fast, two years, three — its nest-mates take up its call for a few days, passing it around the colony, sounding it from the launch-holes at dusk. Then they stop, and the voice is gone from the bowl. Whether this is mourning or simply the way a sound-built mind clears a name from its map, no tapper has ever claimed to know. They have a word for it anyway. The word means *the echo going home*.

---

**The chisel-faces.** The burrowers are the bowl's most misunderstood residents, and the mystery of their mass surfacings turns out, on long watching, to have an answer — a dry one, fittingly.

A chisel-face is a forearm-length animal, blunt at both ends, plated in overlapping scutes the exact dun of the tufa it swims through. It eats roots — not the living root-feet of the great plants, which are armored in cured resin, but the fine feeder-threads the plants cast through the floor like nets. In exchange, unknowingly, it aerates the powder the plants walk through. So far, ordinary.

But tunneling through bone-dry tufa builds charge. A chisel-face is a static animal in a static garden, and after days underground it carries enough accumulated spark to make its own scutes lift and bristle. It surfaces to ground itself — bursts up into the open air, shudders, sheds its charge into the haze in a crackle too small to hear alone. They surface in dozens because charge travels through the colony's tunnel-maze, and when one animal's threshold trips, the whole network's does. The rippling tide of dust-backs that crosses the bowl floor is the ground itself discharging — the garden's basement going off like a struck flint, all at once, and then diving back into the dark to begin accumulating again. The motes swarm down to feed in the disturbed air afterward. The shouters swarm down to feed on the motes. Nothing in the bowl wastes an event.

---

**The patient ones.** The armored grazers are the oldest animals in the hollow, and possibly the oldest things in it that are not stone or sound. Nobody knows their span. The tappers say the largest one, a granite-colored dome near the western rim broad enough to sit on, was grazing the same circuit when the grandmothers of the grandmothers came, and that its shell already carried the same crack then.

A grazer's shell is a landscape. Resin drips and cures on it over decades, and the cured amber traps what it falls on — frond-hairs, mote-swarms, once in a great while a shouter that landed wrong. The old western grazer wears a hundred years of the garden fossilized on its back: a slow archive, polished by wind, carrying the bowl's dead through the bowl's rearrangements.

And here is their importance: they are the only fixed points. The plants move nightly; the grazers move slower than the plants. A grazer's circuit takes a full year, so on the scale of any given week they are landmarks — and the garden treats them as such. The plants, in their slow nocturnal hauling, never block a grazer's path. They part around the circuit the way a crowd parts around a procession. Whether this is mechanics — the root-feet avoiding the rasped, resin-stripped ground of the grazing trail — or something more like deference, the effect is the same. The tappers navigate the unstable bowl by the grazers. *Find the patient one, and the garden is mapped from there until dusk.*

---

**The drummers.** The pod-thieves are the closest thing the bowl has to a people of its own, and the longer you watch them the harder that comparison is to put down.

They are vertical, as said — built like a fist of elbows, gripping hands at all four corners, a tail that wraps. Big dark sound-catching ears. They eat the seedpods, yes. But the drumming is not foraging. A drummer beating pods loose for food does it in three or four efficient strokes. The dusk drumming is something else: long, structured, passed around the bowl from stalk to stalk, and *learned*. Young drummers sit below the stalks for their first year and do not drum. They listen. When they begin, they begin badly, and are tolerated, and improve. Each lineage in the bowl drums recognizably like its mothers — there are at present four or five distinguishable styles, and they answer each other.

Most remarkable: the drumming shapes the organ. A stalk that is drummed at the same point, season after season, develops its bore-holes there — whether the percussion weakens the wall for the boring creatures, or the plant itself softens to the rhythm, the result is that the drummers *tune* the instruments they play. The garden's voices are partly their work. The sustained eerie fifth on the eastern rim is sounding through holes that generations of drummers placed. They inherited that chord from their dead and are, every dusk, handing it forward.

The tappers will not enter the bowl during the dusk drumming. Asked why, they say only that it is not addressed to them.

---

**The garden itself.** Which raises the question the scratched figures on the rim-rock were circling: addressed to *what*?

Dig at the base of any great plant — the tappers have, once, generations ago, and the figures record it — and you find that the root-feet do not end. They braid downward into a common mass. Below the tufa floor, every plant in the bowl is grown into every other: one continuous body of slow amber circulation, two dozen standing limbs above, a single warm tangle beneath. The plants that split apart overnight have not divided; the plant that fuses with its neighbor has not merged. The garden is one resident wearing two dozen shapes, and the nightly turbulence — the rearranging, the walling-off, the opening of clearings — is that one resident shifting its weight. Turning over, perhaps. Or thinking, if a thing that drinks dust and exhales heat and has been singing one chord for centuries can be said to think. The drummers drum to it. The grazers are granted their circuits by it. The tappers take its blood politely and leave before dark.

The sequence-drawings on the rim, the same plant scratched ten times in ten positions — they were not trying to draw plants at all. They were trying to draw the gesture. The way you'd draw a hand by drawing it open, half-closed, closed.

---

**The tappers.** They live two valleys away, where there is water, and they are ordinary in every respect but this inheritance. A family holds the bowl the way other families hold a fishing ground: not owned, attended. They come four times a year, fewer in hard years. They teach their children the diagonal cut that heals, the dust-veil weave, the names of the four cairns, and the rule that no one sleeps inside the bowl — framed as courtesy by some tellers and as plain sense by others, since the floor a sleeper lies on at dusk will not be the same floor by dawn, and a moving root-foot does not check what it walks over.

They are the only residents who can leave, and so the only ones who carry the garden outward — the amber on their knife-hafts, the resin in their lamps, the eerie fifth that the old ones hum without noticing while they work.

---

**And the oldest resident of all.**

Count the inhabitants and every one of them turns over. The motes live days. The shouters, a few years, their voices echoed home and released. The drummers hand down their rhythms because no drummer lasts. The patient ones outlive everything that walks, and still, eventually, the bowl archives them too — there are amber-crusted shells half-buried near the southern rim that no longer move. Even the great singular garden splits and fuses and re-bores its voices until no single stalk standing today stood a few centuries ago.

But the sound has never stopped. Not once. Through every death and division, through every night's rearrangement, the roar has been continuous — the same unbroken noise, changing pitch, changing voices, never changing into silence. It is older than any body in the bowl. Every resident is born into it, builds it, and is survived by it; every animal's call is a brief solo in it; the drummers tune its instruments and the wind plays them and the bowl shape holds it and hands it forward, dusk after dusk after dusk.

That, finally, is what lives in the Roaring Garden. The roar does. It has a body of plants and a blood of resin and a billion small voices for cells, and it replaces all of them, constantly, the way any living thing replaces its substance while remaining itself. The tappers seem to understand this — it is the one thing their cairns and their carvings and their courtesies all agree on. They do not leave before dusk because the bowl is dangerous in the dark.

They leave because the resident is waking up, and the drumming is its name being called.
