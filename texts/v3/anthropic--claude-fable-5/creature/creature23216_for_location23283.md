# Who Lives on the Caldera Stairs

Begin with the easiest answer, the one a traveler gives after a week: *frogs, gliders, moss, and a few quiet people.* That answer is true the way a map is true. It takes longer — it takes the place's own kind of time — to understand that the massif is inhabited in layers, like its jungle, and that the deepest resident may be the one you arrived standing on.

## The Stayers

There are people on the stairs. Not many — no one has counted them, because counting implies a day on which the count is finished, and nothing here finishes on a day. A few hundred, perhaps, scattered across a dozen summits' worth of terraces, living one and two to a shelter, sometimes a small cluster of households around a particularly musical fall of water.

Every one of them arrived as a traveler. This is the first thing to understand about the stayers: they are not a people, not a tribe, not descendants of founders. The massif does not really have descendants — children are born here, but rarely, and the population is sustained the way the pools are sustained, by condensation. People come up the long stairs to escape something or decide something, as the old observation goes, and the heat and the water and the chorus hold steady around them for as long as it takes. For most, it takes a season. For some, it takes the rest of their life, and they discover this not as a decision but as a fact, the way you discover you've been asleep: one day a traveler realizes they have stopped thinking of the lower world as the place they are *from*, and that is all the ceremony there is. The others begin lending them tools.

They are quiet, the stayers, but not solemn. The massif is so loud — the spillways, the dusk chorus, the sternum-deep harmonic — that human voices have simply ceded the field. Stayers speak softly and close, mouth near ear, and their language has drifted toward the water. Places are named for their sound: there is a terrace called something best transcribed as a falling whistle, and a gorge-mouth whose name is a held breath followed by two clicks, and these names are not labels for the places so much as imitations of them, so that to say where you are going is to briefly *be* the place, in miniature, in your mouth. In heavy steam, when the world goes white and you navigate by spillway alone, stayers identify themselves to each other by humming — each person keeps a private interval, two notes, chosen young or upon arrival, and a hummed greeting in the fog is answered in kind, pitch for pitch, so that two people can pass within arm's reach unseen and each know exactly who the other was.

They age the way the mountain ages, which is to say: visibly, gently, and without urgency. Whether the stayers actually live longer than lowland people is argued by the few scholars who still make the climb. The stayers themselves do not argue it. They will tell you that years here are not shorter or longer, they are simply *wider*, and a person can fit a great deal of life into a wide year without hurrying through any of it.

## What the Stayers Do

The mountain provides almost everything, so the stayers' work is not survival. It is tending, and tending shades into vocation, and vocation, over a wide enough life, shades into identity. A few of the recurring kinds:

**Pool-tenders** keep the terrace lips. The travertine thickens a fingernail's width per decade and needs nothing from anyone; the tenders clear fallen fronds anyway, smooth the mossed edges with their palms, walk the rims at dawn listening for any spillway whose pitch has changed overnight. They will tell you honestly that the pools do not need them. They will also tell you that a person needs something to walk the rim of every morning, and the mountain, courteous as ever, permits the arrangement.

**Measurers** continue the work of the incised slabs. The old script is unreadable; the measurers long ago made peace with this, partly because several of them, comparing slabs across centuries of weathering, came to suspect the script changes — that the carvers of the middle period could not read the carvers of the early period either, and carved on regardless. So the measurers carve in their own notation: water levels, the night temperature of named veins, the date the chorus in a given valley changed a note in its chord (this happens perhaps once a generation, and is treated as significant news). They do not expect to be read. The record is not for a reader. The record is the massif's habit of being noticed, performed by hands, and the measurers consider themselves less authors than organs of it.

**Listeners** are the rarest and the strangest, and there are never more than a handful. They live nearest the gorge-mouths. Their work is the note from below — hearing it, placing it, keeping its history. More on them shortly.

When a stayer dies, the body is not buried and not burned. It is carried to one of the pale basins — the shallow, perfect, ancient depressions of vein-material half-swallowed in the roots — and laid there, and the household withdraws. What happens then is the only circumstance anyone knows of in which the vein-material *approaches* rather than retreats. The basin warms. Over some days the warmth rises to blood heat and holds, the way the mountain holds everything, and the body is unmade with a slowness that observers describe as indistinguishable from care. Afterward the basin is merely warm again, merely stone-that-is-not-stone, and the moss at its rim puts on a thick bright band that year, and the stayers consider the person to have gone *in* — not up, not under, but into the circulation, into whatever moves in the veins. They say it without theology and without grief's usual shapes. The mountain holds, the way a body holds. The dead are held.

## The Neighbors

The stayers do not think of the animals as wildlife. The word implies a wilderness apart from a settlement, and there is no apart here. The animals are neighbors of different speeds.

**The bell-throats** — the great amphibians of the terraces — are the civic voice of every valley. Each valley's dusk chorus is a chord, and the chord is *stable*: the same intervals, generation after generation of frogs, taught (it is assumed) from the water itself, tadpole to adult, the way an accent is taught. Individual bell-throats hatch, swell to the size of a curled child, sing for their decades, and are replaced, and the chord persists over them like a roof over its successive tenants. The stayers regard a valley's chord as the truest name the valley has — truer than the whistled human names — and a measurer recording a changed note in a chord will travel days to verify it, because it means the valley has, in some sense no one can specify, made up its mind about something.

**The gliders** barely belong to the ground at all. They are born in the weep-curtains — their eggs adhere behind the beaded falling water of the overhangs, and the hatchlings' first act is to drop through the curtain into the rising warm air and be caught by it. Many appear never to land again except to breed. They sleep aloft in the slow spirals, riding the thermals that ride the veins, and the stayers read them constantly: a flock circling where no flock circled before means the heat below has shifted its branching, and the pool-tenders will find, within a season, a new warm seep on that slope. The gliders are, in effect, the mountain's pulse made visible, and they seem entirely unburdened by the role.

**The slow folk** — the moss-furred canopy browsers — are treated with something adjacent to reverence and identical to neighborliness. They are old; the moss banding on a big browser's back can be counted like the banding on a shelter floor, and the counts run to spans that would make them the eldest animals anyone has named. A browser may take a year to cross a valley. Stayers route their paths around them, leave the fronds nearest them uncut, and accept as ordinary the occasional event of waking outdoors to find a browser's hand — heavy, warm, deliberate — resting on one's shoulder, placed there at some point in the night for reasons the slow folk have never been in a hurry to explain. It is considered neither omen nor blessing. It is considered the way a very old neighbor says good evening, at the only speed available to them.

**The vein-runners** are small, sleek, nearly boneless-looking things that den along the thermoclasts and travel them like roads. No geologist ever mapped the veins; the vein-runners have, with their bodies, and their worn trails through the moss are the only reliable chart of the mountain's hidden circulation. Stayers follow runner-trails the way lowland people follow rivers. The runners tolerate this. They tolerate everything. Animals that sleep pressed against the warm pale veins, generation upon generation, seem to absorb the mountain's whole disposition: they do not startle, they do not flee, they simply make unhurried room.

## The One Below

And then there is the resident of the unmapped deep — the answerer.

Once or twice a night, from far down in the gorges, a single note rises to meet the chorus. The Listeners, who keep its history, will tell you the two things they know for certain, and they are both the same thing: the note is always *in the chord*. Whatever valley it sounds beneath, whatever that valley's chord happens to be, the note from below fits it — transposed down further than any throat-sac should reach, felt in the breastbone like the mountain's own harmonic, but in tune. Always in tune. In the whole carved and remembered record, the answerer has never once been dissonant.

This is why no one has ever gone down to look, and why "there has never been a reason good enough" is only half the truth. The whole truth, as the Listeners give it, is this: a thing that has spent uncountable years participating correctly in every valley's chord is not a mystery in the sense that demands solving. It is a neighbor in the sense that demands courtesy. You do not climb down into a neighbor's house to find out what they look like. You learn their voice, and you keep its history, and on the rare nights when the note comes three times instead of one, you carve the date.

Some Listeners believe the answerer is the chorus's teacher — that the chords are stable across frog generations because something below keeps the pitch, the way a deep bell keeps a town honest. Others believe the answerer and the sternum-hum and the warmth in the veins are not three things but one thing heard in three registers: the engine, audible. The two beliefs are not in conflict, and the Listeners, characteristically, have never felt any pressure to choose.

## The Resident

Which brings the answer around, at last, to where the traveler is standing.

Something lives in the veins. Everyone on the massif knows it, and almost no one will say it that plainly, because plainness implies you know what kind of thing you mean. Consider what is actually known: it conducts heat with the steadiness of purpose or the steadiness of metabolism — no one can say which. It withdraws from drills the way flesh withdraws from a needle, patiently, and returns. It registers offense and expresses it as a few days' coolness, a turned shoulder, and then forgives, or forgets, or simply continues, which on the massif are all the same act. It accepts the dead with warmth. It was here before the shelters by an unguessable factor, and the oldest constructions on the mountain were not built on it but *as* it — which admits two readings, and the stayers hold both at once without strain. Either something built itself a body, long ago, and the massif is the body, and the jungle and the frogs and the gliders and the people are what the body grows on its skin. Or something *was built* — an engine, a work, a kept promise — and has been tending itself so long, so well, and so gently that the distinction between machine and creature wore away like everything else here, rounded into agreement with the weather.

The stayers do not resolve it. Resolution is a lowland appetite. What they will tell you, mouth close to your ear under the roar of ten thousand spillways, is the conclusion their wide years have settled on: everything that lives on the Caldera Stairs lives *in* something, the way warmth lives in a body — and the resident, whatever it is, appears to have noticed all of them long ago, the frogs and the slow folk and the runners and the people, and to have extended to each the same courtesy it extends to the seasons. It holds steady around them. It is not waiting for anything from them. It is, as far as anyone can hear, simply glad of the company, in the only register available to something that large: one patient note a night, always in the chord.

So: who lives there? Everyone who stopped hurrying, and the thing that never started. The massif keeps them all warm without effort and without end, and they, in their layered, unhurried, well-tended way, keep it loud. It seems to be the whole arrangement. It seems to be enough.
