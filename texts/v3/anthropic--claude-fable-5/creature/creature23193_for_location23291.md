**The Citizens of the Drowned Borough**

Begin with the smallest answer, because it is also the oldest: the film lives here. Whatever it is — and it resists the word *species* the way the city resists the word *ruin* — it has been resident longer than anything else you can point to, possibly longer than the carving. It is not moss. It is not quite fungus. Under a lens it would resolve into a mat of filaments finer than spider silk, each one a single cell long or a single cell wide, depending on how you choose to be wrong about it. It does almost nothing. It takes the thinnest tithe of minerals from the seep-water, the faintest charity of chemistry from the rock, and from this poverty it manufactures light — not for any reason anyone can name, since nothing here has eyes, since the only things that graze it find it by touch and taste. The light is either an accident of its metabolism or the entire point of its existence, and after ten thousand years those have become the same thing. It grows at the pace of the flowstone, which is to say at the pace of the city's own slow self-burial, and it keeps exact station with the water: where the seep quickens, the film brightens; where a seam goes dry over a century, the film withdraws and the street it lit goes dark, and stays dark, and a district of the borough is lost — not destroyed, just no longer visible, which here amounts to the same thing. If the carvers cultivated it, then the film is the last of their works still being performed. The lamps are still being tended. The lamplighter simply no longer remembers it was hired.

The isopods are the citizens, and they live like citizens of a very old town: slowly, locally, within walking distance of everything they will ever need. Each one is blind, colorless, flat as a flake of barley, and each one inherits — not learns, *inherits* — a territory of perhaps a hand's span. They graze the luminous film in long, patient transects, and because the film grows thickest along the carved streets, where water channels, the isopods keep to the streets. They use the doorways. They shelter in the doorways during nothing, because there is no weather, no day, no danger that announces itself, and yet they shelter in them anyway, pausing in the carved thresholds with their antennae working, as if observing a custom whose reason drowned long ago. They live, as far as anyone can tell, a very long time — cold slows everything, and these are among the slowest animals on earth, hearts that beat the way the flowstone grows. A generation might be thirty years. The whole borough might hold sixty of them at once: a population smaller than a wedding, distributed across a city, so that each one effectively lives alone, in a metropolis, in the dark, in the thunder. They cannot hear the river — they have no ears — but they *feel* it; the roar lives in the rock and the rock lives in their feet, and it is the one constant of their existence, the floor of all sensation. An isopod of the Drowned Borough has never known a silent moment and could not survive one. Take it out of the chamber and it stops moving. Whether that is shock or grief is a distinction the animal is too simple to make and the observer is too complicated to avoid making.

They molt, as their kind does, and they cannot eat their cast shells fast enough in this poverty, so the shells remain — translucent, weightless ghosts of citizens, lying in the streets, in the courtyards, snagged on the fingernail stairs. And here is a small, terrible elegance: the dripwater finds the shells too. The calcite sheaths them where they lie. Walk the borough with a close eye and the light of the film will show you, glazed into the streets like fossils into a pavement, the fixed bodies of former residents — hundreds of them, thousands, the accumulated dead of ten millennia, each one entombed exactly where it stopped. The city was carved in the image of long habitation, and then habitation came, and now the habitation is part of the stone too. The borough keeps its own census, in flowstone, and never loses a name.

The threadlike thing is the difficulty. Call it a worm, because you must call it something — a nematomorph, perhaps, or a flatworm gone long and pale and patient, a ribbon of appetite the width of coarse thread and as much as a forearm in length when it extends, which it almost never fully does. It is the borough's only predator, if it is a predator; no one has ever seen it take an isopod, and the isopods do not flee it, possibly because they cannot perceive it, possibly because there is nothing to flee. It is seen — rarely, maybe once in many hours of watching — crossing the market square, always the square, always crossing rather than lingering, moving with the awful liquid certainty of things that have no bones and no doubts. And it always enters a doorway. Not the same doorway. But always *a* doorway, one of the carved thresholds that lead into darkness, and it does not come out. The honest explanation is that the doorways connect, beneath the flowstone glaze, to a network of original carved interiors — that the borough has indoors, hollow rooms the calcite sealed but did not fill, and the worm lives in the city the way the citizens never could: inside it. The borough has streets, and the streets have walkers, and the buildings have a tenant, and the tenant is the thing you think about afterward. It is, in the strictest sense, the only resident that uses the city as a city. Everything else lives *on* the borough. The worm lives *in* it.

The pools deserve their own paragraph, because they are inhabited in the way that mirrors are inhabited. Coin-sized, black, soundless in a room made of sound — and in some of them, if you brought a light fine enough and patience enough, you would find motion: copepods like animate dust, single specks of translucent life rowing through water that has never moved. Each pool is a world, sealed, replenished one drip at a year, and the populations of the pools do not mix and have not mixed for centuries, so that the borough contains not one ecosystem but dozens — a city of city-states, each courtyard pond its own nation of perhaps five individuals, diverging from its neighbors three streets away with the geological slowness of everything here. Some pools are dead and have been dead a thousand years and hold only water and stillness. There is no way to tell which from the surface. Every pool is absolutely calm. The stillness of the living ones and the stillness of the dead ones is the same stillness, which is either a fact about pools or a fact about the chamber, depending on how long you have been inside it.

And then there are the residents who have no bodies, and the longer you stay the harder they are to deny tenancy.

The drips live here. That sounds like ornament, but consider: each drip falls from a fixed point to a fixed point, on a fixed rhythm set by the seep that feeds it, and each one strikes its note against the river's drone — and the rhythms have not changed in living memory, or in the memory of the flowstone, which records them as stalagmites, as glazed gutters, as the rounded shoulders of particular roofs. A drip in this chamber has a location, a voice, a lifespan of millennia, and a body of accumulated stone. It builds. It is more permanent than the isopods, more active than the film, more present than the worm. If life is a pattern that maintains itself against time, the drips are the borough's most successful organisms, and the city is partly their construction — they are the masons who took over when the carvers' generations ended, and they are still on the job, still working at a thumbnail per century, and they will not finish, because finishing is not in their nature. The carvers made a city. The drips are making it eternal. Slowly. Note by note.

The carvers themselves live here in the only way that anything human lives anywhere for ten thousand years: as intention, annealed. There are no bones in the chamber — they took their dead out, or never brought them in, which itself tells you something about what they thought this place was. But intention is durable in stone the way nothing else is. Every street that channels water where water wants to go, every doorway placed where the film pools brightest, every stair that descends toward darkness at exactly the angle of reverence — these are decisions, and the decisions are still being executed. The borough behaves the way they meant it to behave. The light still outlines the city like windows at night. In a place where nothing changes, a choice made once is made forever, and so the chamber is dense with their choices the way other rooms are dense with furniture. You cannot meet the carvers. But you cannot move through the borough without doing what they intended you to do — crawl in, stand at the center, see the city by their lamps, and look, at last, at the column. They live here as a verb. The verb is *arranged*.

Which leaves the column.

Say first what can be said safely. Nothing visible lives on it. The film, which colonizes every wet seam in the chamber, does not grow on the column, though the column is damp like everything else. The isopods, which walk every street, have never been observed to touch it — their grazing transects approach the raw stone and turn, the way paths in old cities bend around something that was there before the paths. The flowstone laps its base and stops. The drips fall around it and never on it, which has a dull explanation — there is simply no seep in the ceiling directly above — and the dull explanation has had ten thousand years to stop being satisfying, because the seeps elsewhere have migrated, quickened, died, and been born, and the dry zone above the column has held. The mountain weeps everywhere except over its own fist.

So: does anything live at the heart of the borough? The honest answer is that the question loses its grammar there. Everything else in the chamber lives by participating — the film in the water, the isopods in the film, the worm in the buildings, the drips in the rock, the carvers in the arrangement, all of it inside the roar, which is the breath of the whole body. The column is the one thing in the chamber that does not participate. It is not covered, not carved, not colonized, not lit, not built upon, not worn. In a place defined by ten thousand years of accretion, it has accreted nothing, and that is either an absence so total it amounts to vacancy — or it is the opposite, an occupancy so complete that nothing else can get a footing. The carvers, who knew this chamber better than anyone will again, hedged the same way: they did not carve it into a god and did not carve it away. They built a city around it and left it alone, which is what you do with a neighbor you respect and do not understand.

Perhaps this is the truest census. The Drowned Borough is inhabited by everything that has learned to live inside the thunder — the film that lights it, the citizens that walk it, the thread that haunts its interiors, the worlds in its pools, the drips that are still building it, the long-dead will that arranged it. And at its center it keeps one resident who arrived before the thunder had an audience, who pays no rent of light or shell or stone, and whose presence is attested only by the perfect, ancient courtesy of everything else: the water that will not touch it, the glow that will not reach it, the small blind citizens that walk to its edge, and turn, and walk away.

The river roars. The city listens. Something is listened *to*. That is who lives there.
