# The People of the Cut

You see their work for a long time before you see them. Then you see their tools, lying where hands left them. Then their hands. The people themselves come third, and slowly, the way the town itself came — emerging out of something that at first looks like only stone.

## Dust and Body

They are pale the way the walls are pale. Not by blood — under the film their skin is whatever color it was when their grandparents crossed the plateau — but every working day lays a fine white powder over them, in the hair, in the creases of the knuckles, in the folds of clothing, so that by evening a cutter standing still against a fresh wall is very nearly invisible, a thickening of the stone with eyes in it. The eyes are what you notice. They are dark-rimmed where sweat has kept the dust off, and they have a particular steadiness, a habit of resting on a surface for a long time without hurry, because these are people whose chief skill is looking at rock until the rock admits what it contains.

They are built around the chisel. Heavy through the shoulders and forearms, even the slight ones, even the old women — a wiry, specific strength, more endurance than power, the strength of ten thousand small identical blows rather than any single great one. Their right hands (or left; you can tell a cutter's handedness across a courtyard) are gloved in callus, the palm gone smooth and hard as a tool handle, which it functionally is. They squat easily and for hours. They climb their pole-ladders without looking. They walk the shoulder-wide trench streets sideways without breaking conversation, turning through the town's narrowness the way the town taught them to from birth.

And they are made, in part, of the town. The mineral water has gone into their bones and teeth — their teeth are very white and very strong and very worn, ground flat by the grit that gets into everything, into the bread, into the milk, between every molar in the bowl. The dust has gone into their lungs. The old cutters breathe with a faint dry whisper, like wind in a chimney, and they cough in the cold mornings, and everyone understands without ever quite saying so that a life of opening the stone ends with the stone having quietly entered you in return. They do not seem to resent this. It is spoken of, when it is spoken of at all, almost as a settling of accounts.

## The Hand

Every cutter has a hand — meaning not the limb but the handwriting, the signature crescent of their stroke — and the hand is the core of who a person is here. Children are given a small soft name at birth, a milk-name, but it is understood to be temporary. Somewhere between eight and fourteen, a child's stroke stops imitating and starts insisting: a rhythm appears, an angle, a depth of bite that is theirs and nobody's else's. The adults gather around the practice wall, run their fingertips across the gouges, and pronounce the hand arrived. Then the child receives a true name, and the true names are descriptions: Long-Quiet, Three-and-Rest, Shallow-Sure, Heavy-In. To ask someone's name is to ask how they cut.

This is why the walls of the town are also its archive. The people can read their entire genealogy in the architecture; they walk through their ancestors' penmanship on the way to fetch water. A grandmother is dead, but her doorways stand in six places, and her grandchildren touch the tool marks in passing the way other peoples touch portraits. Nothing here is signed, because everything is signature.

And the doorway you noticed — the one cut in two hands, one long and confident, one nervous and overlapping — is what they call a *binding door*. Couples cut them. Two people who intend to share a household begin a doorway from opposite jambs, each in their own hand, working toward each other over weeks. You can see the courtship in the stone: the strokes begin utterly distinct, then start to listen to each other, borrow each other's rhythm, and somewhere near the lintel — if it goes well — they converge into a third hand that belongs to neither and both. Some binding doors never converge. Those, too, are left in the walls, unfinished, and everyone knows what they mean, and no one cuts them away.

Affection generally moves through rhythm here. Two cutters working facing walls will drift into synchrony without meaning to; falling into someone's stroke is the local equivalent of catching someone's eye across a room, and the deliberate breaking of synchrony is a refusal everyone can hear. At the height of a working day the bowl is full of these conversations — *tik-tik… tik… tik-tik… tik* — a whole town flirting, arguing, and consoling itself in percussion, beneath words, all day long.

## The Division of Labor

Most are cutters, but the town keeps its specialists, and the specialists are slightly strange, the way specialists always are.

**The water-readers** go out at first light, before the sun gets down past the upper tiers, and press their cheeks to the cold north-facing walls — reading condensation, tracing the bedding planes with their fingertips, sometimes touching the rock with their tongues. They can find a coming seep a season before it weeps. They are quiet people, even by local standards, and they are deferred to absolutely, because a wall cut blind across a wet seam is a ruined room, and a seep found and channeled is water forever. The water-readers walk the town with a slight tilt to their heads, as if always half-listening to something behind the walls, because they are.

**The frost-tenders** work at night, and only in the cold seasons. They go out late with water jars and wet the seams the masons have marked, and they do it in silence — the belief, held lightly but observed strictly, is that a spoken word will warn the frost off its work. In the morning they walk the terraces checking the night's yield like trappers checking a line, and where the cold has spalled a slab cleanly away, they stand the slab against the wall for the haulers and chalk a small mark of thanks on the scar. They are the only ones in town who work with their hands empty of tools, and the cutters regard them with the faint unease owed to people who farm something that cannot be commanded.

**The soil-keepers** tend the pocket gardens, and they are the closest thing the town has to a priesthood of scarcity. Soil cannot be cut from anything; every basket of it walked in across the plateau is counted, and the keepers know their dirt grain by grain the way the cutters know their walls. They lid the gardens against the night cold, hand-pollinate the fig, and ration the harvest with a fairness so exact it has stopped being kindness and become law.

**The marker** is one person at a time — currently an old woman with a charcoal stick and a small brass-bound maul. She walks the lowest terrace at dawn, taps the solid rock, listens, taps again, and draws the doorways of the district that does not yet exist. Children follow her at a distance. The charcoal rectangles she leaves behind are the most important drawings in the town: each one is a family that hasn't formed yet, a future already assigned its walls. She does not consult anyone. She says the plan is not hers; she says she is only finding where the rooms already are.

**And there are those who go up** — the topwalkers, who lead the goats and the trade caravans onto the plateau and away to wherever copper and grain and timber and soil come from. They are necessary and slightly foreign. Their eyes have adjusted to horizons; they stand differently, looser, scanning distances that don't exist below the rim. The bowl-born find them hard to look at for long.

## The Children

The unfinished district at the bottom belongs to the children, by custom older than anyone can date. They play in the chest-deep rooms, sleep out summer nights in chambers that exist only as intentions, lie on their backs in the charcoal-drawn houses of families not yet formed and look up at the sky. They know which marked room will be whose. Sometimes a child plays for years in the rectangle that will one day be cut down into the house of their own adulthood, and they know it, and it does not seem to trouble them.

Every child's first true stroke — the first crescent they ever cut with a real chisel — is left in the wall forever. It is never smoothed over, never improved. The town is studded with thousands of these small clumsy gouges, each one remembered, each one occasionally visited by its grown maker, touched once, left again.

The bowl-born children have a sky shaped like the rim. That is the sky; that has always been the sky. Going up is a rite, taken around the arrival of the hand-name: a child climbs the long stairs and stands on the plateau and receives the horizon — the world suddenly unwalled, flat, enormous, indifferent. Some weep. Some laugh. A few are changed by it and become topwalkers. Most look at it for a respectful while, the way you'd look at a relative's grave, and go back down, and do not climb again for years.

## The First Ones

The founders are old now, and there are not many. They came across the plateau two generations ago — from what, toward what, they do not say, or have stopped saying; the bowl-born have learned not to ask, and the question has nearly finished dying. What the first ones do now is sit in the morning sun on the upper terraces, where the light arrives earliest, and re-grind chisels. The mushroomed copper edges come up to them in baskets and go back down keen, and the whisper of whetstone on copper is the old people's sound the way the *tik* of cutting is everyone else's. Their lungs are the worst in town. Their hands are still legible in the deepest, oldest, straightest streets — the part of the town that had a plan — and the young cutters go down there sometimes just to read the founding strokes: hurried, those strokes, harder-driven than anything cut since. The strokes of people getting underground.

## The Dead

Every adult keeps a last cut. It is begun at the hand-naming or the binding door, whichever comes first: a body-length niche, high in the upper tiers among the oldest stone, worked on only alone, only in spare hours, across a whole life. No one enters another's last cut. Some are plain as troughs. Some — you learn this only because, once, a slab is removed for a burial and the bearers glimpse inside — are covered in fine work, an entire life's mastery spent on surfaces that will never be seen, ornament carved for the dark.

When a person dies they are laid into the niche they made, and it is sealed with a slab they dressed themselves, fitted years in advance, waiting. The seam is so fine you could miss it. The dead remain in the highest tiers as the town goes on descending, so that the living always work *beneath* their ancestors, and the doves — fed, half-kept, never quite tame — nest and burble in the abandoned cuttings just above the sealed ones, and the people say without much weight, but say, that the doves are good company for them.

## What They Believe

It is a simple creed and they would not call it one. The rooms are already in the stone. The pot is already in the block; the doorway is already in the wall; the work is not making but finding, not addition but the patient removal of everything that was hiding the thing inside. Grief is handled this way — chipped at daily, in small strokes, never one great blow. So is anger. So is the self: a person, they hold, is not built up out of acquisitions but revealed by subtraction, and an old cutter is admired the way a finished room is admired, for everything no longer there.

They do not believe the town will be finished, and they are not troubled by this, because they do not believe they are building a town. They believe the town is in the rock the way the figure is in the block, going down and down, and that they are how it gets out. Press one of them — the marker, say, some dawn on the lowest terrace, her charcoal stick raised — and she will tell you, without portent, what they all in some wordless way assume: that the stone is the patient one, and the people are its chisels; that the bowl is cutting them too, all their lives, in small crescent strokes, into whatever they were already.

## Evening

At dusk the cutting stops, never all at once — one rhythm falls out, then another, the percussion thinning across the bowl until a single hand somewhere finishes its thought, *tik… tik…*, and rests. The dust of the day settles on everyone equally. The goatherd — a girl, you finally see her once, only once, crossing a slab bridge three tiers up with the bell sounding before her — folds her animals into the courtyard you will never find. The lamps come out: small stone lamps, carved like everything else, oil bought with carved things carried up over the rim. A doorway lights. Another. The water-readers make a last circuit of the cisterns and listen to the drops keeping their slow time.

And if you stay at the rim long enough, past full dark, you may hear the one sound that does continue: somewhere high in the upper tiers, faint and private and unhurried, a single chisel working. Someone at their last cut, alone, shaping the room no one will see, in their own hand, in no hurry at all — a person carving the place where the stone will finally take them back, stroke by patient stroke, the way everything here is done: by letting go of what was never the thing, until only the thing remains.
