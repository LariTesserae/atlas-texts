**The Census of Verren Deep**

Begin where life begins here, which is not at the bottom but at the top, on the cool side of things.

**The film.** On the upper tiers, where the pipes have lost their heat and the convection delivers water that has already spent its scalding, there is the gray-gold sheen the chamber wears like tarnish. It is not chemistry. It was chemistry once — somewhere back along its lineage there was a moment when mineral happenstance crossed over into appetite — but now it is the oldest citizen and the entire peasantry of Verren Deep, a population beyond counting living one cell deep across acres of folded pipework. It eats the water itself: the dissolved iron, the sulfur breath of the engine-floor, the slight electrical disagreement between one alloy and the alloy beneath it. Seven generations of metallurgy means seven flavors of corrosion, and the film knows them all, prefers some, has opinions expressed in tint — more gold over the bronze, more gray over the nameless alloy that outlasted everything. It is the only farmer in a world without sun, harvesting the engines' exhaust at a kilometer's remove, and it keeps the engines' time. Each pressure-stroke flexes the water's chemistry just slightly, a chemical breath, and the film metabolizes on the beat. If you could speed the centuries up you would see it shimmer in rhythm, a tide of faint color washing across the tiers, three pulses and a pause, three pulses and a pause. It has never been anywhere else. It cannot conceive of an else. It is, in the only sense that matters here, the land.

**The grazers.** The conical shells on the floor are not stones, though most of the stones on the floor are shells — the dead accumulate, laminate over with mineral crust, and become indistinguishable from the bedrock within a generation or two, so that the silt between the glowing seams is in fact a cemetery paved with its own occupants. The living ones are not numerous. Perhaps two hundred, perhaps fewer; no one has counted, and counting them would take a season, because to confirm one is alive you must watch it long enough to catch it being somewhere else. They move at the speed of patience. They are eating their way through the slow rain of the chamber — flakes of film sheared loose by turbulence, rust-snow shaken from the catwalk-lace, the soft organic dust of everything above them — and their bodies have given up nearly everything that does not serve this. No eyes, of course. No real head. A foot, a rasp, a gut, and a shell built in rings, one ring per some long interval that is probably the engines' own slow drift, so that each shell is a transcript of the rhythm: here the years when the beat ran true, here the thickened ring of a bad decade when the strokes came doubled and the whole floor was scoured by lurching currents. They are the chamber's memory, written in carbonate. Crack one open — no one has — and you could read the heartbeat back for centuries.

Their young are the only adventure in Verren Deep. Once in a great while, on a night that is not a night, a shell releases a drift of larvae into the rising plumes, and for a few weeks the plaza of warm nothing is inhabited: motes of glass-colored almost-life riding the convection in those decaying, renewing orbits, around and around the void, most of them dying, a few settling at last onto some upper pipe or down into the cemetery silt to begin their motionless careers. They time the release to the engines' stumble — the doubled stroke, the lurch, the moment all the loose things in the chamber change orbit at once. It is the only weather event this world has, and they have made it their dispersal, their diaspora, their one festival.

**The thread-worms.** In the silt, between and beneath the shells, something else: pale filaments a few centimeters long that you will never see unless a current peels back the floor's gray blanket. They eat what even the shells reject. They are the janitors of the cemetery, and they may be the most numerous animal here, and they have never once risen into the open water. The void above them might as well be the sky that never was.

**The reader.** And then the eel, or the animal that arrived at the eel's conclusions — and *arrived* is right, because nothing about it is original; it is a list of correct answers to a single question asked for a very long time. The color of candle wax because pigment costs and there is nothing to see and nothing to see it. Eyeless for the same economy. The length of an arm because the gaps between pipe-tiers are the width of a shoulder, and it lives in the streets of the drowned district the way a cat lives in alleys.

What it has instead of eyes is the beat. Understand this properly: the engine-strokes are not, to the reader, a sound. They are illumination. Each pressure wave floods the chamber and breaks against every surface — the catwalk-lace, the opal valve-wheels, the cemetery floor, the small hard punctuation of a living shell — and comes back patterned, and the reader's whole lateral length drinks the pattern in. Three knocks and the chamber exists for it, complete and detailed, walls and tiers and the drift of larvae if it is festival season; then the exhale of bubbles smears everything to static; then darkness, in its sense of the word, until the next stroke. It lives in a world that strobes into being a few times a minute. Between strokes it coasts on memory. When the engines stumble — the early beat, the doubled one — you can watch it stall in the water, absolutely still, the way you would freeze if the sun skipped. And when the held stroke comes, the correction, it resumes, reoriented, reassured. The rhythm is its sun, its map, its calendar, and possibly its god, if a god is the thing whose regularity you stake your existence on without ever wondering at it.

It eats larvae in festival season and thread-worms the rest of the year, nosing the silt like a blind finger. It is alone, and here the chamber keeps its one secret close. Perhaps it is not alone — perhaps there is another, on the far side of the wasp-nest tiers, and twice a century they find each other by reading the same stroke from opposite walls. Perhaps it carries both halves of the problem in its own body, as the patient solutions tend to. Or perhaps it is simply old — older than seems decent, its metabolism banked down like the furnace-light, the last of a line that was never large, finishing a story no one began on purpose. It does not behave like a creature running out. It behaves like a creature with an appointment it has not been told about.

**The absent.** The builders live here too, in the only way they still can: as address. Raised numbers softened with mineral bloom. A glove holding the shape of a hand. Schedules orbiting the void, dissolving by the decade, their orbits the closest thing they ever got to being filed. The chamber has digested them as it digested everything else, and what it could not digest it upholstered and kept. They are not ghosts. They are inscription — the layer of the place that can still be read but no longer reads back.

**The tenant of the void.** And finally the one the chamber itself would name first, if it kept its own census: the sound. It is tempting to call it a condition rather than an inhabitant, but consider — it has a territory, which is everything; a diet, which is heat; an age, which is the chamber's whole sealed history; and behavior, the stumble and the correction, the worn-smooth self-righting that no builder designed and no tender maintains. More than that: it has dependents. The film metabolizes on its beat. The shells transcribe it. The larvae ride its lurches out into the world. The reader sees by it. Every living thing in Verren Deep is, by now, partly an organ of the sound — a way the rhythm has found of touching the silt, tasting the iron, swimming the streets. Strip the beat away and the film would starve out of step, the larvae would never scatter, the reader would hang blind in a chamber that no longer existed for it.

So the truest description of what lives here may be this: one creature, eighty meters across, with a heart of geothermal pressure, a skin of gray-gold film, a skeleton of seven metallurgies grown over with opal, a memory written in shell-rings, a single pale nerve patrolling its alleys, and a voice that is also its bloodstream. It finished its purpose centuries ago and converted, without ever deciding to, into a purpose of its own.

And for an hour or two, hanging in the plaza of warm nothing, *you* are on the census — a large soft visitor, briefly the biggest animal in the world. Within minutes your pulse falls into phase with the strokes; you will not notice it happening. The reader will pass once, far off between the tiers, and read you — a strange new architecture, soft-walled, twin-hearted for the duration of your stay — and file you among the loose things that orbit and depart. When you rise back up the shaft of warm black water, you will carry the beat with you for days, arriving in your ribs at quiet moments, three knocks and an exhale, the chamber's way of saying it counted you, and that the count goes on.
