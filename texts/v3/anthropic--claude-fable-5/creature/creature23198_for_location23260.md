**The Residents of the Standing Chamber**

The first thing to understand about the census is that nothing in it arrived. There is no colonization story here, no founder washed in on a flood, no spore on a boot. The fracture that lets you in opened — geologically speaking — yesterday, onto a room that was already full. The chamber's inhabitants did not move into it. They were sealed inside it, complete, arranged, trembling in the dark at thirty-one degrees for a span of time no one has been able to put a floor under. The fracture did not open a habitat. It opened a door into an occupied house, and the occupants did not look up.

Start with the herd, because the herd is most of the biomass and nearly all of the noise — or rather, nearly all of what the noise is *for*.

**The hummers.** Up close, a hummer disappoints and then unsettles. Finger-length, slag-colored, faceted rather than curved, six short anchoring limbs ending in pads that grip stone the way solder grips a joint. No eyes. No mouth that anyone has found. No ears — and this is the important absence, because a hummer does not need an organ for hearing any more than you need an organ for being warm. The entire animal is the ear. Its shell is laminated in thin piezoelectric plates, and when the standing wave flexes the shell — ninety decibels of flexing, continuously, forever — each plate weeps a small steady charge, and that charge is the creature's whole economy. It does not hunt, graze, filter, or photosynthesize. It is rung, like a bell, and it lives on the ringing.

This means a hummer's body is a tuned instrument, and tuning is the axis of its entire existence. The shell must resonate at exactly the engine's frequency; a hummer too small rings sharp and starves, a hummer grown too large rings flat and starves. The larvae — pale, soft, the size of rice grains, found cemented in the seams between adults — cannot feed at all until their first shell hardens at resonant size. Their childhood is a calibration. They grow in silence, metabolizing a yolk of stored charge their parent invested in them, and either they arrive at the correct dimensions before the yolk runs out or they do not. The drifts are full of small wrong-sized husks. Nothing mourns them; nothing in the chamber has the spare energy for mourning. They are recycled, like everything else, at the boundaries.

The colonies stack hundreds deep along the loudest wall, in terraces that early surveyors mapped like architecture, because architecture is what they are. Only the outermost layer is alive. Beneath it, the terraces are built of the dead — generation cemented on generation of spent shells, each stratum a former living surface, so that the great drift on the eastern wall is in cross-section a kind of reef, or a kind of city, the living always exactly one body thick on top of everyone they replaced. A dead hummer detunes within hours; its plates crack and it goes flat, literally flat, a quarter-tone below the chord, and its neighbors — by a mechanism nobody has watched closely enough to describe — work it downward into the masonry or shed it off the drift's edge. The colony does not tolerate a sour note. It cannot afford to. A detuned body in an antinode is a damping flaw in the instrument everyone is eating from.

And this is the thing the long-term acoustic surveys eventually forced everyone to admit: the wave's perfection is not the engine's doing alone. No machine runs without flutter. The engine surely wavers, microscopically, the way all real machines waver — but the herd absorbs the waver. Tens of thousands of tuned resonators, packed into the pressure maxima, each one greedily soaking up any energy at exactly the chord's frequency and physically incapable of responding to anything else, act together as a filter of staggering precision. The hummers do not merely live in the standing wave. They *dress* it. They are its immune system, its polish, the reason it has no beginning anyone can hear. The engine plays a note; the herd makes it eternal.

It follows — and the surveyors came to talk this way without noticing — that the individual hummer barely exists. The unit of life in the drifts is not the animal but the *position*. Antinode three, tier forty, fourth from the seam: that address has been occupied continuously since records began, by an unknown succession of interchangeable bodies, each one tuned to the same note, gripping the same square centimeter, doing the same single thing. Surveyors name positions, not animals. The animals decline to be told apart.

**The crawlers.** Where the loudness ends, the second nation begins, and it is stranger for being humbler. The pale flattened scavengers of the nodes — *crawlers* is the only name that ever stuck — are deaf in the deepest possible sense: not insensitive to the sound but *bounded* by it. To a crawler, an antinode is not a loud place. It is a solid one. Ninety decibels of fixed pressure reads, to a body that soft and that slow, as a wall — and so the crawlers' chamber is not the chamber you walked through. Theirs is a small apartment of quiet rooms with walls made of standing force, walls that have never moved, doors that have never opened. They have lived their whole history inside the silence the way fish live inside water, and they will never visit the other ninety percent of their world, which begins three steps away and might as well be inside the rock.

They live on the tide line. Where node meets antinode there is a boundary — a shore, the surveyors call it, and the word is exact — and onto that shore the drifts shed their dead. Cracked shells, flat-ringing husks, failed larvae: all of it migrates outward and downward from the colonies and comes to rest at the edge of the loudness, a windrow of chitin along an invisible coast, deposited by a tide that never comes in and never goes out. The crawlers patrol it at the speed of continental drift. Their hearts — if the slow peristaltic shudder in their midline is a heart — beat a few times an hour. Their generation time is unknown and may be irrelevant; it is entirely possible, several surveyors have noted in the careful language of people who don't want to say it plainly, that the crawlers visible today are the same individuals that were visible at the chamber's opening, and that no crawler has died since anyone started watching.

There is no predation anywhere in this. It is worth pausing on. The chamber's ecosystem has no chase in it, no ambush, no flight, no fear — none of the verbs that organize life elsewhere. Energy enters as sound and as cable-heat, passes downward through the herd as shed shell, and is gleaned at the shoreline by things too slow to be a threat to anything that has ever existed. It is a food web with no teeth in it. Visitors who notice this find it either restful or horrifying, usually in that order.

**The conduit worms.** Along the armored cable — the one that comes out of solid rock, crosses the chamber, and goes back into solid rock — live the only inhabitants that may not belong to the chamber at all. Thread-thin, metallically iridescent, they braid themselves through the conduit's heat-fins in plaits so old and so tight that the surveys genuinely disagree about where worm ends and infrastructure begins. Sections of the cable's outer wrapping that were assumed to be insulation have turned out, under magnification, to be worm: living tissue laid down in helical wraps, conductive, warm, and apparently load-bearing. They feed by induction, sipping at the stray field that leaks from the cable's hum — grazing, in effect, on the same river of power that feeds the engine, a few electrons upstream of everything else alive in the room.

The unsettling part is the geometry. The worms are densest where the cable enters and exits the rock, and they continue *into* the rock — fine probes pushed into the entry seam have found braid as far as the probes could reach. The visible population, the few dozen meters of plait you can walk along, is a window onto something with unknown extent: a single attenuated nation strung along the cable's whole buried length, kilometers perhaps, all the way to wherever the power comes from and wherever it goes. If the builders left a maintenance crew, this is it — a living lagging, a biological gasket, tending the artery in the dark on both sides of the only room anyone can see. The worms do not acknowledge the chamber. To them it may be a brief uninsulated stretch, a place where the pipe runs regrettably exposed.

**The lichen.** The bronze-green growth in the engine's glow deserves its footnote, because of the lamp incident. The patches are hard-edged, printed in the exact shape of the sodium light, and they were assumed for years to be incapable of growth — living fossils, photosynthetic stone. Then a survey team left a work lamp clamped to the gantry for nine days, and when they returned, a new patch had begun: faint, bronze, hard-edged, printed in the exact shape of the lamplight. The lichen is not incapable of growth. It has simply never, in its entire existence, been offered a reason. It grows in the shape of whatever light it is given, instantly and obediently, and then the light never changes, so neither does it. The new patch is still there. It is the only mark any expedition has ever left on the chamber's biology, and the teams now treat their lamps the way one treats open flame in a library.

**The bellows.** And then there is the one resident that breaks every rule the others keep.

It sits — sat; sits — in the large node near the toppled gantry: a folded mass like a leather bellows the size of a dog, seamed, dust-mantled, warm. Specifically, thirty-four degrees in a chamber that is thirty-one everywhere else, which makes it the only thermal gradient in the room, the only weather the chamber has. It does not feed on the wave; it lives in a quiet zone, where there is nothing for a piezoelectric body to eat. It does not graze the shoreline; the chitin windrows near it are undisturbed. The best hypothesis anyone has offered — and it is offered quietly — is that it feeds on the node itself: that a node is silent not because energy is absent but because energy is *cancelling* there, two opposed pressures annihilating each other at a fixed address forever, and that the bellows has found a way to stand in the annihilation and keep some of it. It eats the silence, in other words, the way the hummers eat the sound. Between the two of them, nothing in the chord goes to waste.

It moved. That is the famous fact: seventy centimeters between two surveys eleven years apart. The less famous fact is the direction. It moved toward the engine. The displacement was along a clean line, node to node, and if the rate is constant — and in this chamber, what isn't — it will arrive at the engine housing in something on the order of four thousand years. No one knows what happens then. A long-duration microphone left beside it once recorded, beneath the chord, a single event in six months of tape: a slow, low exhalation, several minutes long, like a vast soft thing turning over in its sleep. It is the only sound the chamber has ever produced that was not the engine, and it has not recurred.

The surveyors are careful, in print, not to romanticize it. In conversation they are less careful. The bellows, they will tell you, is the only thing in the chamber with a trajectory — the only resident for which tomorrow is in any respect different from today, the only one with an appointment, however distant. By the definition of life that requires a future, it may be the only thing down there that is alive at all. Everything else has solved the problem of time. The bellows is still working on it, three centimeters a year, toward the heart of the room.

**The resident.** Which brings the census to its last entry, the one most surveys put in an appendix because no taxonomy has a column for it.

Ask what lives in the Standing Chamber and the honest answer may be: one thing. The chord. A single continuous standing organism made of pressure, forty meters long, as old as the engine and as fixed as the rock — with the engine for a heart, the herd for flesh and immune system, the crawlers for digestion, the worms for arteries reaching away into the dark, the lichen for pigment, the stone for skin and skeleton, and one warm folded dissenter crossing its interior at the speed of patience. Every creature in the room is more legible as an organ of the wave than as a species in its own right. The hummers keep its tone clean; the crawlers clear its dead cells; the lichen records its single unchanging expression. The chamber is not an ecosystem inside a sound. It is a body, and the fracture is a wound that turned out not to matter, and when you descend with your lamp and your changing breath and your settling dust, you are not entering a habitat.

You are walking around inside the resident. It does not notice. Noticing is a kind of change, and the resident gave that up before anyone was counting — back when all of this rang on, sealed and lightless and complete, in a room with no door, for no witness, at thirty-one degrees, holding its one note against the rock and needing nothing, least of all to be found.

Your footprints, by the way, are still there. All of them — every expedition's, back to the first. Nothing in the chamber erases. The dust by the entrance carries the only stratigraphy the place has ever had: a thin human record of comings and goings, pressed into a floor that was finished before anyone came, laid over a room that will not notice when no one does.
