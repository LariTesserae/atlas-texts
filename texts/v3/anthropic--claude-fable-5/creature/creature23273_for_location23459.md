# Who Lives in the Settling

The first answer is wrong, and you should have it anyway, because the wrongness is instructive: *the plant lives there.* It doesn't. Living somewhere requires a distinction between the liver and the lived-in, and the plant abandoned that distinction so long ago that the grammar no longer reaches it. The plant is not the resident of the vault. The plant is the vault, and the rain, and the floor, and the milk. Asking whether it lives there is like asking whether a song lives in its own melody.

So set the plant aside — though it will not stay aside; nothing here does — and look at the citizens.

---

**The haulers.** The pale six-limbed ones, cat-sized, smooth as wet soap. Begin with them because they are the easiest to mistake for animals, and the mistake will teach you the place.

A hauler is not, first, a creature. A hauler is a *route*. There are perhaps thirty routes in the room — pool to column, column to dome, dome to the long ridge — and each route is older than any flesh that has ever walked it. The route persists; the hauler wears it. Watch one for a day and you'll see it travel the same circuit with small variations, drinking at one structure, disgorging at another, paid in beads of milk, and you will think: this animal has habits. Watch for a year — the citizens live perhaps six or seven — and you'll see the animal weaken, slow, lie down one dim evening near the dome and not get up, and be taken into the floor before the rain has filled its outline. And within days, sometimes within hours, another hauler — younger, paler, born in the heap — will be walking the same circuit. Not a similar circuit. The same one, to the pace, including the small variations, including the pause at the second pool that the old one always took and that serves no purpose anyone can find.

The routes are inherited the way an office is inherited, not the way a face is. No one teaches them. The young hauler simply begins performing a route the way the eastern wall begins performing a buttress: the room has the job, and the job acquires flesh. It is very hard, after watching this twice, to keep believing the haulers are individuals who happen to work. It is more honest to say they are work that happens to be individuals — though even that is too tidy, because they squabble over the milk-shelves, and they have preferences in the heap, and there is one (see below) who has broken the whole scheme, so they are not nothing inside. They are *something* inside. The place just doesn't require the something, and so the something is small, and private, and theirs.

Their calls — the flat two-note honks — are not conversation. Listen long enough and you'll find the calls are *fixed*: each route has its note-pair, sounded at the same stations of the circuit, generation after generation. The honks are not the haulers talking. The honks are the routes announcing themselves, in voices borrowed from whatever is currently walking them. It is the closest thing the room has to writing. The archive of the Settling is two notes long and pronounced continuously, forever, by a relay of throats.

---

**The gliders.** The membranous ones in the vault, who never land.

This is literal. A glider is budded from the underside of the canopy — you can see them forming, if you watch the thick regions between the light-panes: a swelling, a darkening, a slow peeling-away — and it drops into the warm updraft off the boiling floor and rides it, and that is the whole of its life. It eats on the wing, sieving the air for the millimeter-fauna that the floor's churning throws upward in a constant fine aerosol. It mates on the wing, in brief grappling spirals that look like falling and aren't. It sleeps on the wing, locked into the steadiest column of rising air, circling unconscious like a leaf in an eddy. Its first landing is its death: when a glider tires past recovery it simply comes down, anywhere, without drama, and folds, and the floor takes it.

So the gliders never touch the place they live in. They inhabit the *room of the room* — the air, the volume, the part of the vault that is held open rather than the part doing the holding. If the haulers are the room's work, the gliders are its leisure, or its attention: a slow permanent circulation of small watchfulnesses under the ceiling, riding heat that the floor's labor gives off for free. The room sweats effort, and the gliders live on the steam of it.

They are the only citizens who can see the whole interior at once. Whether anything is done with this — whether the wheeling under the canopy is surveillance, or worship, or just thermodynamics wearing wings — is not findable. They circle. The light-panes drift beneath them like slow weather. They have the longest view in the Settling and no apparent opinions.

---

**The grazers.** The soft black ones, forearm-long, who eat the walls.

These are the happiest beings in the room, and possibly the happiest beings anywhere, because their desire and their duty are the same thing and both are administered to them directly in the form of sweetness. The plant decides what to demolish; the plant sweetens it; the grazers follow the sweetness with their whole simple hearts and rasp yesterday's wall into themselves. They are never wrong, because they cannot be — the gradient does their judging for them. They are never idle, because the room is never finished finishing. They experience, presumably, an eternity of finding exactly what they were hoping for, exactly where they were about to look.

They eat the past. That should be said plainly. Every retired buttress, every superseded rib, every region of tissue the plant no longer intends — the grazers take it in, and the plant takes the grazers' leavings, and the matter goes around again. The Settling has no ruins because the grazers are always a few hours behind the present, consuming it. If the haulers' honks are the room's only writing, the grazers are its only forgetting, made flesh and given appetite. They are slow, and gentle, and you can pick one up — it will not object; nothing here objects to warmth — and feel under its skin the same restless shimmer as the walls, the turnover going on inside the eater at the same pace as inside the eaten.

---

**The small ones.** The fauna of millimeters — and this is most of what lives there, by any count that matters. The pools alone hold nations. The weep-stripes of the walls are vertical rivers with their own riders. The floor-folds open and close like slow mouths and everything in them relocates, hourly, without complaint.

You will not learn them as kinds. There may not be kinds — the floor seems to issue small fauna the way a sentence issues syllables, in whatever shapes the moment's grammar wants, and an hour later the shapes are different. What you can learn is their function, which is to be the room's *texture of aliveness*: the shimmer between the big citizens, the aerosol the gliders eat, the seethe in the pools that keeps the clear water moving. If the haulers are offices and the grazers are appetites, the small ones are something closer to the room's thinking — innumerable, fast, individually meaningless, collectively the medium everything else happens in. Kneel at a pool and watch. It is like watching attention itself, before it has settled on anything.

---

**The heap.** At the dim end of the day, the haulers and the grazers — never the gliders — converge on the dome-structure, whose entrance will have migrated since yesterday, and pile.

This deserves its own attention, because the heap is where the question of *who* lives here gets its strangest answer. Awake, the citizens are distinct: this hauler, that grazer, this squabble over that shelf. Asleep, piled, blood-warm and breathing in the dome's wet dark, their distinctness thins. Press a palm to the heap (it will tolerate you; warmth is currency here) and you can feel the cellular shimmer of all those bodies falling into a common rhythm — the same rhythm, you will realize with a small vertigo, as the floor's slow boil, as the walls' churning. Asleep, the citizens stop being citizens and go back to being room. In the morning they unpile and resume being themselves, each one walking out into its route or its sweetness, individuality reissued with the light.

Whether anything is lost or gained in the heap, whether they dream severally or as one or at all, is not knowable. But it explains the lack of mourning. When a hauler dies and the floor takes it, the others do not pause, and this is not coldness — it is that they have spent every night of their lives partially dissolved into each other and into the building, and the difference between a colleague who is asleep in the heap and a colleague who has gone back into the floor entirely may be, to them, a difference of degree. Death here is just the long version of bedtime.

---

**The deprecated one.** Now the exception, because there is one, and the place is more itself for having it.

There is a hauler — old, by their measure; its pallor has gone faintly translucent at the limbs — whose route no longer exists. The column at one end of its circuit was retired some seasons ago: sweetened, grazed, kneaded back into the floor. The route's other stations have since drifted out of alignment. There is, in the present room, nothing for this hauler to carry and nowhere to carry it.

It walks the route anyway. Every day, the full circuit, pausing at the place where the column stood — the floor there is indistinguishable from any other floor; the pause is to nothing — sounding its two notes at the old stations, drinking and disgorging on schedule into structures that no longer want what it brings. The other haulers route around it without friction. And the plant — this is the detail to hold on to — *feeds it anyway*. Milk still beads where it stops. The room maintains the deprecated route the way it maintains everything else: not because it is needed, but because it is being performed, and performance is the only criterion the Settling has ever had.

This animal is the closest thing the place contains to a historian, or a ghost, or a priest — a living record of a building that no longer exists, written in gait and pause and two notes. When it dies, no young hauler will inherit the route; the room only reissues current offices. The column's last trace will go into the floor with it, and the Settling will be, for the first time in some years, entirely up to date.

---

**The watchers.** The bulbs on flexing stalks, the ones that lean toward whatever moves — say a word about them, because you will catch them leaning at you and you will want to know which side of the line they're on.

They are on neither. They are not citizens; they do not eat or sleep or pile; they are extruded and maintained like the columns. But they are not quite furniture either, because they *attend*. They are the place's regard, grown into stalks — the plant keeping a kind of company with its own interior, watching its citizens the way you might watch a fire you built: not anxiously, not for any purpose, but because it is yours and it moves. When they lean toward you, nothing is being decided about you. You are simply, for that moment, the most interesting flame in the room.

---

**Last, and underneath.** So: routes wearing flesh, attention wearing wings, appetite doing the forgetting, a seethe of small thinking, one faithful ghost, and a building that watches its own inhabitants like a hearth. That is the census. But stand in the sliding light long enough and the census starts to feel like the wrong instrument, because every entry on it is made of the same restless tissue, turned over hourly, and *nothing that lives there is older than an hour* — not the walls, not the haulers, not the minds inside the haulers, whatever those are.

What is older? Only the patterns. The route survives its walkers. The two notes survive the throats. The vault survives every cell that has ever composed it. The deepest resident of the Settling — the only thing that has lived there continuously since the beginning, the only inhabitant the floor cannot take back — is not made of cells at all. It is the practice itself: the standing instruction, *be this room, full, and occupied, and working*, performed completely every moment by whatever matter is currently on shift. The citizens are its current pronunciation. So were their parents. So is the wall.

And — for as long as you stand there, with the warm rain on your shoulders and a shelf quietly beading milk near your hand — so are you. The place has no way to record you, but it has every way to *include* you: the watchers lean, the milk offers, the floor adjusts its boil beneath your boots. For the duration of your stillness you are a citizen of the Settling, holding, like everything else there, a temporary appointment in an eternal practice. When you go, the floor will close your footprints, the room will not remember you, and you will not be able to say, honestly, that you were ever not part of what lives there. You were performed too, briefly. That is the only kind of residence the place has ever issued.
