**The Singers of the Condensate Gallery**

Begin with what is obvious, because the obvious will not survive the description.

Obviously, the gallery is inhabited by birds. Thousands of small pale birds, fist-sized, swift-shaped, blind, packed in shingled roosts across the upper half of the chamber. You can cup one in your hands — it permits this, having no concept of being taken, no startle reflex, nothing in it that has ever needed to flinch — and feel its single tone continue against your palms like a small motor that does not know it has been picked up. Its body will be cold or warm depending on where in the roost you took it from: the outer birds run cold as the pipes they grip, the inner ones warm as breath, and the endless shuffling rotation is the colony trading temperature the way a crowd trades places at a fire. Set it back on the pipe and its feet close around the diameter with a click you feel rather than hear, a click like a fitting seating home, and it resumes shuffling as if your hands had been a brief weather it passed through.

So: birds. But hold one a little longer before you put it back, and the word starts to come apart.

The feathers are too regular. They lie in ranked rows, each vane the same width as its neighbors, fine and stiff, less like plumage than like machined fins — and they are fins, you realize, heat-exchange surfaces, the bird dressed in its own radiator. The sealed eyes are not eyes that closed; press gently (the bird does not object; the bird does not object to anything) and there is no orb beneath the skin, no socket, only smooth continuous bone, blank as a bearing race. The beak does not hinge so much as part, a slot through which the tone comes out and the vapor goes in. They drink the condensate that the rib-channels route beneath their roosts, and they breathe the warm steam the grilles exhale, and as far as anyone has determined that is the whole of their diet — the metabolism of a kettle, water and heat in, water and sound out. What they excrete, the floor seams take, and the green-black felt is fed by it, and that is their entire mark on the universe: a film of moss no thicker than paint.

No one has ever dissected one, because no one has ever found one dead.

There is no egg, no juvenile, no molt, no carcass, no sign that the colony has ever been any size other than the size it is. The reasonable conclusion — the conclusion the room itself seems to be patiently waiting for you to reach — is that the birds were never *born* at all. They were installed. They went in at full count on the day of the pour, the way the rivets went in, and you do not ask where rivets come from once they are driven. Run a thumb along a bird's keel and you will half expect to find a casting seam there, a thin proud line like the flash on the ribs of the chamber, and the worst part is that you will not be able to tell yourself afterward whether you felt one or not.

Listen, next, instead of looking, because the tone is where the birds keep what passes for their identity. Each one holds a single fixed pitch, and no two pitches are quite the same. The pitch never wavers when the bird shuffles inward, never flattens in the cold of the outer shingle, never stops when the bird drinks — they sound while drinking, the water simply going in around the note. A bird's tone is its serial number, sounded continuously, and the great chord that fills the gallery is therefore not music and not noise: it is a *roster*. The full complement, calling itself present, every second, forever. If a single bird were ever missing, you could hear the hole — a thin absence in the chord, a gap the width of one frequency. No one has ever heard a hole.

All of which argues that the birds are not inhabitants at all. They are a part. Somewhere, you start to suspect, in a ledger no one has opened in a very long time, there is a line item — *live lagging, cold joint, gallery 9; quantity: one lot; service life: indefinite* — and the birds are simply that line item, doing its duty, a living gasket vibrating at operating frequency in a cold pocket of the machine.

Except.

Watch long enough — hours, the people who stay say, sometimes days — and you will see the part do two things that no part needs to do.

The first is small. Once in a great while, a bird at the outermost edge of a roost will lift its sealed face into the falling water and hold its mouth open. It does not need to. The channels deliver water to its feet; drinking that way is inefficient, awkward, wholly surplus. It holds the position for a few seconds, blind face raised into the rain it has stood beneath since before anyone living squeezed through the gap, and then lowers its head and resumes. A gesture without function. The single least mechanical thing in the room.

The second is worse. Occasionally — there is no schedule, the people who count drips have tried to find one — the shuffling stops. All of it, everywhere, every roost, in the same instant. The chord continues without a flaw, but the motion ceases, the whole feathered architecture going still at once, and for one or two seconds the colony is, unmistakably, *listening*. To the drone below, perhaps. To the *tok* of the loose plate. To you — your heartbeat is, after all, a new percussion in a room that has had the same percussion forever. Then it resumes, and you spend the rest of your visit telling yourself it was nothing, and failing.

So perhaps the birds are the inhabitants after all — a single inhabitant, rather, the colony being one organism the way you are one organism, each pale body a cell in slow circulation through a tissue eight meters across. Or perhaps the true inhabitant is below them, underfoot: the felt in the floor seams, the only thing in the gallery that lives in the way the word usually means. The felt eats, dies in patches, regrows, accumulates. Scrape a sample and you will find ordinary algae, ordinary nothing — a thin film of the visitor's own mortal world, riding the closed loop like a tick on a clock, and the only thing in the room with a *history*. Everything else in the Condensate Gallery is exactly as old as itself. The felt alone has been young.

Or perhaps — sit with the chord long enough and this becomes hard to dismiss — the inhabitant is the chord. It is older than any single behavior in the room; it has been continuous since the pour; the birds may be merely its organs, the way your vocal cords are not you. The chamber's ribs are spaced like the inside of an instrument. The loose plate near the east wall, struck by its eternal drip, may not be loose by accident: it may be the chord's one moving part, its tongue, its slowest voice, saying the only word it has — *tok* — at the only interval it knows. On this reading the room was not cast around the birds. The room and the birds together were cast around a sound, to house it, the way a body is built around a pulse.

And there is one more resident, the most recently installed, and you will meet it on the way out. The ringing in your ears, the one that lasts for days — the one that, people say, never quite changes — does not entirely fade. It drops below attention. It learns to wait. Somewhere, right now, someone who squeezed through the gap years ago wakes at three in the morning in a cold quiet room in winter, and hears, behind the silence, a thin metallic note holding steady at its fixed pitch, faithful as a rivet, calling itself present. The colony does not lay eggs. It nests in ears. Everyone who has ever visited is carrying one note of the roster, and the chord, wherever its body truly is, has never once heard a hole.

A drop strikes the loose plate. A pale body in the third roost shuffles one place inward. The count holds.
