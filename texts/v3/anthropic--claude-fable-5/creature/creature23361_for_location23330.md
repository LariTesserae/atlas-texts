**Who lives in the Gullet**

The honest first answer is: almost everything that lives in the Gullet is small, and almost nothing that lives in the Gullet has ever been anywhere else. Take the inhabitants in order, ceiling to floor, and then — because the place insists on it — take them once more, all together.

---

**The congregation.**

Start with the bats, because the room is theirs the way a cathedral belongs to its choir.

They are not quite bats anymore, or rather they are bats the way a village dog is a wolf — the shape is there, but the priorities have changed. Tens of thousands of generations alone with one room have softened them. Their echolocation has gone slack and low, half-abandoned, because nobody needs sonar in a house they have memorized down to the last candle-wax ripple; what they have grown instead is *talk*. The chittering static you hear from the rim is not noise. It is dense, regional, dialectal. The colony is divided into ceiling-clans — the north-seam roosts, the skylight-edge roosts, the great warm slum directly above the engine — and each patch has its own accent, its own grudges, its own squabble-songs that ripple out and die at clan borders like waves hitting a different density of water. A bat displaced from its home patch will cross the whole ceiling to get back, climbing over ten thousand strangers, complaining the entire way in a dialect the strangers find funny. You can watch the laughter move through the fur.

The nursery is above the flywheel. The pups are born into the warmest air in the room and into the *whum* — it is the first rhythm they ever hear, before their mothers' hearts, or at least alongside them — and this matters more than it seems. Because when the foragers come home before dawn, fifty miles out over a plain with no trees, no rivers, no smells, no landmarks of any kind, what guides them in is the hum. They feel it in the air and through their feet when they land to rest on the flat. The engine is their lighthouse. The colony does not know this, exactly, but the colony behaves as though it knows it: the densest roosting, the most jealous defended real estate, the place where the old males with shredded ears sit like aldermen, is the stone above the machine. If the flywheel ever stops, the foragers some night will fan out across the dark and simply not find the hole, and the room will starve in a week. Everyone living here is leased to that bearing.

The old ones have a custom. A bat that feels itself finishing — and they seem to know, the way old animals know — works its way down the wall over the course of a day, deliberately, refusing help, to a low ledge above the densest part of the floor traffic. The colony calls after it; it does not call back. Then it lets go. The phrase the place suggests for this, watching it, is *going to the floor*, and it has the feel of an institution: a citizen paying in.

---

**The parliament of the floor.**

The floor receives.

The bronze beetles are the largest faction and they are two nations, though they look alike at a glance. The surface nation skims — fresh fall, the daily black rain of droppings, the soft thud of a body. The deep nation almost never sees the gray light at all: they tunnel the old strata, three feet down, six feet down, turning compacted centuries the way earthworms turn soil in a world that has soil. Their tunnels are why the floor breathes instead of souring; they are the ventilation system below, as the engine is the ventilation above. They have been digging long enough that their tunnels occasionally break into older tunnels, their own ancestors' work, and the floor at depth is honeycomb all the way down to the true stone, which nothing living has touched in ten thousand years.

The pale crickets are the musicians and they have been disciplined by the machine. Listen: the *whum… whum… whum…* leaves a gap, and the crickets sing only in the gap. Every one of them. A male who sings against the beat sings into the engine's note and is not heard, and a male who is not heard does not breed, and so for unbroken centuries the flywheel has been culling the colony's sense of rhythm, and now the offbeat is in them like a bone. If the engine paused, the crickets would go on holding its time — singing the shape of a silence, keeping the beat of a heart that stopped.

The brass centipedes hunt the mite-drifts and are hunted by nothing, which has made them slow and arrogant; they walk through beetle traffic the way nobility walks through a market. The blind white spiders are the opposite — careful beyond careful. They cannot see the dusk exodus coming, but they feel the colony's pre-flight restlessness through the air an hour ahead, and they *reef their webs* — haul in the long anchor lines, ball the silk, ride out the hurricane of wings clinging to their rocks like sailors, and rebuild in the emptied hours. A spider that misjudges the colony's mood loses everything it owns. They never misjudge twice.

Below them all, the powder-guild: blind woodlice, white as paper, who finish what the scavengers start. A bat's skeleton, gnawed to lace by the beetles, goes to the woodlice for the minerals, and from the woodlice to dust, and the dust goes into the floor, which is to say into everyone. The floor is the colony's ancestors. It is also, three feet down in the chalk-hard strata, one boot sole, several buttons, the iron heel of a shovel, and one human jaw, mined slowly into the general pale rock along with everything else that ever stopped moving here. The room does not distinguish. It is not disrespect. It is accounting.

---

**The toll-takers.**

There are seven snakes at the rim — pale as the limestone, patient as the limestone — and they may be the oldest animals in the Gullet, because their lives are arranged to require almost nothing. Each one holds a crack at the skylight's edge. Each one takes a single bat a night from the rising column, a strike into a river of bodies so dense that missing is nearly impossible and taking two would be vulgar. One bat a night, every night, for what may be decades: their metabolisms have slowed to match the arrangement, and the arrangement never changes, and so they have settled into something less like predation than like office. The colony loses seven of fifty thousand a night and does not register it. The snakes have never in their lives seen any prey but bats, never hunted, never traveled, never been wet. Once a year each one sheds, and the papery sleeve blows out of the crack and tumbles away across the dead plain — the only thing the Gullet exports, a translucent ghost of its only landlords, skating over country that owns nothing.

---

**The seam.**

In the polished hollow under the weeping seam — two hands of water, a cupful a day — lives the smallest nation on earth. Blind amphipods, translucent, perhaps forty individuals, the whole species in a basin you could empty with a hat. They have been forty individuals for an unknowable span; the water allows forty. They cycle through their two handfuls of universe eating the film of bacteria off the polished stone, and the bats, who drink there in ones and twos through the night, lap around them with what reads — probably falsely, possibly not — as care. Forty citizens, a complete history, an entire bloodline of life balanced in a dimple of rock, refilled one tear at a time by a wall that has wept since before the colony came. If you wanted to hold an extinction in your cupped hands, here is where you would come.

---

**The tenant.**

And then there is the engine, and the circle around the engine, and the question of the grease.

The floor-life does not avoid heat, or vibration, or metal — beetles will walk the cold rails of the ore-cart all day. But nothing crosses the precise ring of bare stone around the engine's casing, and the bearings of a machine no one has oiled in living memory are *wet*.

Two stories are available, and the room declines to choose between them.

The first is bloodless and almost believable: fifty thousand warm bodies hang above the machine, and warm fur sheds an oil-fog, invisibly fine, year upon year, and the engine is the warmest surface in the room, and aerosols condense on warm metal. By this account the colony greases its own lighthouse without knowing it, breath and fur-oil settling into the bearings like dew, and the avoidance ring is only the radius at which the casing's vibration unsettles small feet. A closed loop, elegant, requiring nothing unaccounted-for.

The second story is what you see if you stay through the emptied hours after exodus, sitting very still, and the gray light has gone to the deep blue of the skylight's last hour, and you watch the gap in the casing where the camshaft runs. Something moves along the shaft. It is the color of old lard. It is flat, or it is flat just then; it seems to have the shape of whatever it is touching, pouring itself along the metal with a gait that is either many small legs or one long ripple, and where it has passed, the steel is clean of rust-bloom and shining wet. It is perhaps the length of a forearm. It may be one animal. It may be several that travel as one. It does not come out of the casing — or it has not yet, for anyone watching — and nothing about it appears in the strata of the floor, no molt, no dropping, no dead one, ever, which means either it returns everything to itself or the floor-guilds receive its leavings inside the ring where no observer goes. The crickets do not sing while it is moving. They resume when it stops.

Whether it came in with the miners, riding in the grease-bucket like a domestic thing — every engine of that era had its tallow, and tallow travels — or whether it was here first, in some seep or hollow, and *adopted* the machine the way the bats adopted its hum, the place offers no opinion. The floor-life decided about it long ago and keeps the circle. That is the only testimony available, and it is unanimous.

---

**All together.**

So: the census. One congregation of fifty thousand, with dialects and aldermen and a custom of going to the floor. Two nations of beetles. A culled choir of crickets keeping the machine's offbeat. The arrogant brass, the careful white sailors, the paper-colored powder-guild. Seven landlords at the rim, paid in one coin nightly. Forty citizens in a cupful of water. And one tenant, or none, in the warm iron heart of the west wall.

But stand at the rim at dusk, when the ceiling peels loose and the room exhales its smoke of wings into the dead sky, and the truer census suggests itself. The colony is a lung. The floor is a gut, digesting for centuries, remembering everything it eats. The seam is a tear duct. The flywheel is a heart that someone else installed and the body grew around, the way a tree grows around a fence wire. The Gullet inhales sunlight gathered fifty miles away and exhales it as wings, and nothing inside the room is separable from anything else: take the engine and the foragers scatter; take the bats and the floor starves; take the beetles and the floor sours and chokes the air; take the crickets' offbeat and — well, nothing measurable happens, except that the room would no longer be keeping time, and a thing that keeps time is a different kind of thing than one that doesn't.

Asked what lives in the Gullet, you can answer with the long list. Or you can give the answer the place itself seems to hold, patient under the flat country, humming through your boot soles half a day before you ever see the mouth:

One thing. It is forty paces across at the lips and a hundred million years old at the bone, it breathes once a day through its single opening, it has a heart it did not grow and a tenant it does not explain, and it is — in a finished land where everything else has lain down — fully, deafeningly, stubbornly alive.
