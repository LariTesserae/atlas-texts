**The Keepers of Weir's Standing**

Begin with what you saw: the figure on the footbridge with the covered pail. That is nearly all of them, in miniature — a person carrying water somewhere it needs to be, quietly, in the cold, without hurry, without waste. Everything else about them unfolds from that.

They are few. Fewer than the town could hold, more than it strictly needs, and they know both numbers to the person. Somewhere between sixty and ninety of them, depending on the decade — nobody outside has ever gotten a firmer count, partly because they do not offer one and partly because the census of a place that holds more town than its dimensions allow is not a straightforward exercise. They do not call themselves anything much. The maps say Weir's Standing, so travellers call them Weirs, and they answer to it the way you answer to a knock on the wrong door: politely, without adopting it. Among themselves they mostly do not use names aloud at all, and this is not mysticism but acoustics. The cleft carries sound; a word spoken by the wheel is legible three levels up. A town built inside a whispering gallery learns economy of speech the way other towns learn to lock their doors. So they speak seldom and low, and they say what is needful, and they have shifted the greater part of their conversation into two other channels: chalk, and the pipes.

The chalk you have already seen without understanding it. The dates and tallies beside the weeping seams are the formal ledger — the accounts kept with the mountain — but chalk is also their post, their notice-board, their gossip. A mark beside a door means the household is wanted at the wheelhouse. A short tally on a lintel means a debt of labour, acknowledged. The weeping walls erase everything within a day or two, and this suits them; they regard permanence in writing as slightly presumptuous. The only durable records in the town are the maintenance itself — the replaced lintel inside the older lintel is the town's true archive — and the marks on the stones of the dead, of which more later.

The pipes are the other tongue. Those long deliberate zigzags of lead that step the water down from level to level will carry a tap from the top of the cleft to the bottom, faithfully, and generations of tapping have refined this into a full grammar: short patterns for names, for the hours, for *the wheel wants seeing to*, for *come*, for *stay where you are*, for a birth, for the other thing. A newcomer standing in the lower town hears only the town's ordinary water-noise — plink, hiss, the eight-second groan of the wheel — and cannot tell the weather from the conversation. That is by design, or has become design. The people of the cleft hear both registers at once and do not confuse them, the way you hear traffic and a voice saying your name.

Physically they are what the place has made them. Pale, of course — pale in the way of the choughs and the fish, the pallor of things the light arrives at already tired — but it is their eyes you notice, if you get near enough to notice anything. They see gradation. In a world without shadows, where nothing is ever dark against bright but only deeper grey against paler, they have become connoisseurs of the almost-identical. They move through what you would call darkness at a walking pace, hands loose at their sides. The lamps in the windows are not, mostly, for seeing by. Some are for comfort. Some — the ones burning at noon in the deepest recesses, where no one lives — are for observance, lit and trimmed on a rota that nobody wrote down and nobody misses.

They are also, all of them, careful in a way that has gone below thought. The glazed steps, the iced rails, the sixty-metre drop that is never more than one misstep away from any point in the town — these have bred a gait, a deliberate flat-footed economy of movement that children learn before they learn the pipes. You can identify an outsider in the cleft from two levels up by walk alone. The people of the town move like the water moves: never fast, never stopped.

Each of them keeps a length of the water. This is the organising fact of their lives, more than family, more than trade. The whole descending system — every gutter, channel, zigzag pipe, drip-edge and drop — is parcelled out into *keepings*, and a keeping is cleaned, sounded, mended, and answered for by its keeper, daily, for life. A keeping is inherited if there is someone to inherit it, and assigned if not, and there are keepings now whose keepers are gone and which are nonetheless kept — gutters that run clean, joints that stay sound — and the town's habit is to assume a neighbour is doing it, and the neighbours assume the same, and the matter is not pressed. This is the polite fiction at the centre of their lives, and they maintain it with the same patience they maintain the stonework. The long-spouted oil can in the wheelhouse hangs on its hook and is always full. The lathe in the dark workshop runs true, year on year, its ways wiped and its centres dressed by nobody anyone has met. The people of the cleft do not investigate this. They understand — structurally, the way they understand load and thrust — that the arrangement has two parties, and that the other party also does maintenance, and that examining a gift horse's teeth is bad manners in any climate but especially in this one.

There is no government worth the word. There is the seam-reader — presently an old woman, though the post has been held by men and by people the town describes only by their keeping — whose authority is entirely descriptive. She tastes the water and can tell you which seam it came out of. She hears the mineral ticking the way a physician hears a chest. She chalks the dates and tallies, and when a seam quickens or slows she says so, and the town adjusts its channels the way a crew adjusts sail, without debate, because there is nothing to debate: the mountain has said something and she has translated. She is very old. She remembers the wheel being fitted with its current blades, and the ironwork of those blades suggests that this memory should not be possible, and the town does not remark on it. People here weather rather than age. The cold and the damp and the arrangement wear them slowly, the way the steps are worn — thinner, smoother, more themselves — and an old person of the cleft is old in the way the dinner-plate lichens are old: an accumulation you can read but not quite believe.

Children are rare, and the town's one visible anxiety. A birth is tapped down every pipe in the cleft within the hour. A child's first task, at whatever age it can sit still, is to be set beside a weeping seam with the seam-reader and taught to hear the ticking — first to hear it at all, then to count it, then to notice when the count changes. A child's second task is a keeping, small, a single gutter on a low ledge, and it is only when the keeping has been kept for a year that the child's name begins to be used, and even then mostly in chalk. There is one further post that always goes to the youngest adult in the town: the garden. The eight troughs on the noon ledge, the grey cushion plants under their netting — you wondered whether they were food or observance, and the answer is that they are curriculum. They are the one living thing in the cleft that is willed rather than accepted, the town's proof to itself that it stays here on purpose, and whoever can keep them alive is understood to be ready, eventually, for the chalk and the seams.

The dead are given to the town's structure. There is one dry crack in all that weeping stone — the only place in the cleft the mountain does not touch with water, high on the eastern wall, and the people of the town took this dryness, centuries ago, as an offer. The dead are carried up, walled in with a single dressed stone apiece, unnamed, marked only with the sign of their keeping. The wall of the dead is now several courses deep and several generations long, and it is load-bearing: a street of the upper town stands on it. They find this correct rather than morbid. Everyone in the cleft expects, in the end, to hold something up.

The animals they treat as colleagues of differing rank. The pale choughs are tolerated with something close to tenderness — never fed, never shooed, permitted to walk through open doorways and stand on sills, and it is quietly noted, without being said, that a chough on the windowsill of a sickroom means the room should be made ready for other things. The white eyeless fish in the tailrace pool are another matter, graver. The wire creel is raised four times a year, at the change of each season's light, and one fish is taken, and it is cooked without salt and divided so that every household in the town eats a portion the size of a thumbnail. It tastes, they will tell you if you ask — and this is the longest sentence most of them will give you — of stone and cold and very slightly of iron. They understand this meal to be part of the terms: the mountain sends its own flesh up through the seams, and the town takes it in, and something is thereby renewed that neither side names.

And the other thing — the smooth movement along the highest ledges at the dim change of the light. They do not call it anything. If pressed, and they are not easily pressed, they will say *the reading's being done*, and turn back to their work, and it will be understood that you should do the same. It walks the terms. It pauses at the vents, at the fat white frost-beards, at the top of the wheel; it goes along the chalked seams the way an auditor goes down a column. They do not look at it directly — not from fear, they are not afraid of anything in the cleft, but from courtesy, the way you do not read over the shoulder of someone checking your figures. Once in a while, the morning after it has passed, a tally beside a seam is found altered — a stroke added, or struck through — in a chalk slightly greyer than their own. The seam-reader copies the correction into her own marks without comment. The mountain, evidently, keeps its own books, and the two sets of accounts are reconciled in this way, silently, and have balanced now for longer than the iron of the handrail has been iron.

They want almost nothing from outside. Twice a year one of them climbs the stair with an empty pack and comes back with chalk, netting, lamp-filaments, lead, salt, seed for the troughs — a load one person can carry, which is the town's entire foreign trade. Occasionally someone leaves for good, young usually, up into the roaring wind, and the town does not grieve; those who go were always going to go, and the pipes tap a pattern for them that means neither loss nor blame, only *accounted for*. Occasionally someone arrives — a walker off the plateau, a marriage, once or twice a person who came down the stair by accident and simply never generated a reason to climb back up. The town will take anyone who can be quiet. That is the entire test, and it is not administered; it administers itself, over about a year.

So when you climbed back out and the wind hit you at the lip like a door opening, you were seen. Not followed, not questioned — seen. Someone at a wet window marked your going the way everything down there is marked. Beside the foot of the iron stair, low on the stone where the rime doesn't reach, there is a small chalk tally, refreshed as the walls weep it away, and it counts the visitors the way the seams are counted: as one more thing the mountain sends down, briefly, from above — noted, accommodated, and let go, while the wheel turns over into its next eight seconds and the keeping goes on.
