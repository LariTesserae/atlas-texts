The first thing to understand about the people of the Pumphouse Commons is that they don't call themselves residents. They say *keepers*, the way you'd keep a fire, or a promise, or someone else's secret, and the distinction matters to them: a resident lives in a place, but a keeper is custodian of something that was already underway when they arrived and will go on after them. There are six of them, in two of the five cottages, and not one of them planned to stay.

The oldest of them came with the construction crews, twelve years ago, when the trough was still raw silt and the brick was still on pallets. She was a fitter then. When the works were finished and the crews packed out, somebody had to stay through the first winter to mind the pumps, and she volunteered, and the relief that was supposed to come in spring was reorganized out of existence somewhere down the paperwork, and by the time anyone might have asked her to leave she had become the person you'd have to ask. She lives in cottage one, nearest the pumphouse, and she is partially deaf in exactly the register of the pumps — a notch worn into her hearing the way a path is worn into ground — so that she no longer hears the machine so much as inhabits it. She reads its health through her feet, through the bedframe, through the surface of her tea. When the rhythm shifts — that fractional change every few months, the valve adjusted by no one anyone can name — she sleeps badly for days, not because the new tempo is wrong but because, she says, the basin isn't finished thinking until the frogs agree. When the frogs find the new time, she sleeps.

She shares the cottage with the clerk, a precise and mildly stubborn man who keeps the logs, reads the gauges twice daily, and maintains the settlement's entire relationship with the outside world, which consists of a canvas satchel left on a post where the track meets the valley road. He puts letters in it — gauge summaries, requisitions, an annual and increasingly philosophical inquiry as to where, exactly, the water goes. The satchel is always empty when he checks it again, and supplies arrive quarterly, and pay arrives somehow, and these facts together constitute proof that someone, somewhere, is participating. He has stopped expecting answers. He has not stopped writing the letters. He files copies of everything in duplicate, and the duplicates are, by now, the most complete history the place has: a town archive for a town of six.

Cottage four holds the rest of them. A couple, first — one of them keeps the grounds, the walkways, the culvert grate, and knows the meadow plant by plant and pool by pool; the other keeps the electrics, the transformer shed, the sagging lines between buildings, and speaks of the high sustained hum the way other people speak of a household animal's mood. The groundskeeper is the one who maintains the treaty with the pine martens in cottage three. Nobody negotiated this treaty; it simply emerged. The martens — a dynasty now, three or four generations deep in the wall cavity — patrol the plank walkways at night and have kept the storehouse entirely free of rats since anyone can remember, and the groundskeeper leaves an egg on the porch rail of cottage three every so often, and refers to this, deadpan, as paying *them* rent. The martens take the egg. The arrangement holds.

The couple has a daughter, nine years old, and she is the only true native of the Commons — the only person on earth for whom *whunnk-haah, whunnk-haah* is the original condition of the universe. She heard the pumps before she heard anything else; her mother spent the whole pregnancy within four hundred meters of them. Her first attempts at speech, the clerk's log notes with uncharacteristic warmth, were two-part: an intake and an exhale. She keeps a census of the hares, which she takes seriously and conducts at dawn, and she can tell the hour and the coming weather by the basin's sound the way other children read clocks. She was taken to the distant town once, for a doctor, and what frightened her there was not the strangers or the buildings but the silence underneath everything — she described it afterward as the town having no inside. She has not asked to go back.

The sixth keeper boards with them: a surveyor, sent four years ago to produce a complete assessment of the works for an office whose letters have since stopped coming. The assessment was never finished, because the place changes at precisely the pace that defeats measurement — by the time a figure is recorded it is a knuckle's depth out of date — and somewhere in the second year the surveyor stopped fighting this and started measuring for the measurement's own sake. Willow heights, every season. Pool depths. The silting rate. The drift, in fractions of a second, of the frog chorus against the pump stroke. The photographs of the west end, taken from the same flat stone each solstice, which are the only way anyone knows the willows are becoming a wood. The surveyor's notebooks are the slow-motion film of the basin, and the surveyor is, by now, the only person who can see it moving.

What the six of them share is the rhythm. It has gotten into everything. They speak in the pump's pauses — near the pumphouse out of necessity, everywhere else out of habit — so that conversation at the Commons has a measured, antiphonal quality, sentences sized to fit between strokes. They sleep with windows open, because a closed window makes the sound resonate wrong in a room, hollow and trapped. Away from the basin they are uneasy in a way they find hard to explain; the silence has a missing-tooth quality, an absence the tongue keeps returning to.

And they share the place with a citizenry of animals that is, by now, equally entrained. The starlings in the smokestack — that colony of hundreds — have done what starlings do, which is imitate, and what they imitate is the pumphouse: on still mornings the maintenance ladder rings with dozens of small fluting *whunnk-haahs*, the machine's heartbeat returned to it in miniature, plus the knock of the standpipe and, lately, the squeal of the clerk's door hinge. The herons at the warm outflow culvert stopped migrating years ago; there are four of them, fat and proprietary, holding territories along the mist-rope measured in heron-lengths. The frogs are the basin's choir and its barometer. The hares are everywhere because the basin floor is too loud for the kind of hunter that listens — the owls work the valley rim and the silence beyond it, but not the floor — and the hares have rebuilt their fear accordingly: they do not startle at noise, or at people moving in the settlement's rhythm. They startle at interruption. A stilled machine, a stopped footstep, a silence — that is what danger sounds like here, to everything that lives here, person and animal alike.

Which leaves the eldest inhabitant, the one the keepers half-jokingly defer to: the pumphouse itself. They do not quite say it is alive, but they staff it the way you'd staff a household around its head — oiling, listening, keeping its walkways clear — and they speak of its moods without embarrassment. It was here before the willows, before the moss, before the frogs found the pools; everything else in the trough grew up around its rhythm and took shape accordingly, the way tissue forms around a heart. It lifts the water; the water leaves; nobody knows for whom. The keepers have decided this is not their question. Their question is the one they answer every day, in the gauges and the logs and the egg on the porch rail and the child's hare census and the frogs falling back into time: whether the place is well. And every evening, when the overcast goes gold and the horsetails glitter and the basin pulses *whunnk-haah, breek-breek* in something close to unison, the answer, so far, is yes.
