**Who Lives in the Weeping Tableland**

It begins, as it must, with a correction: the statement that nothing lives here is true, and it is not sufficient. Biology is absent — that much holds. No moss, no lichen, no insect, nothing that eats or breeds or dies. But the distinction between *living in a place* and *being alive* is one of those strict modern dichotomies, and the plateau is very old, and like everything else here the distinction has been worn thin by long use. There are inhabitants. They simply inhabit in the worn way the tableland does everything: gently, persistently, without the rude vigor of organisms.

The smallest of them are the sounds.

You will have noticed, walking the pool-mosaic, that some of the dawn echoes arrive before their causes. What takes longer to notice is that a few of them have no causes at all anymore. A drip will sound, clear and close, from a stretch of rock that holds no seep — and if you search, you may find the worn cup in the stone where water *used* to fall, dry now for who knows how long, the seep long since migrated or sealed. The sound outlived its source. It circulates. These orphaned sounds are the plateau's nearest thing to fauna: they have territories, mostly the low basins where the acoustics pool like the water does; they have habits, favoring the cold hour after dawn when the reality runs loosest; they have something like shyness, falling quiet when you stop to listen for them and resuming when your attention moves on. They are not many. The plateau does not generate them so much as fail, very slowly, to lose them.

One of them is a set of footsteps.

It walks at dawn, perhaps one morning in five, and it walks the cairn lines — never the open stone. The gait is unhurried and specific: a stepping, weaving progress, stone to stone, the rhythm of someone navigating the pool-mosaic with the ease of long practice, pausing where the lines bend, pausing longer at certain basins, as if checking something. It is the sound of a person who knew the plateau. There is no person. There has not been a person for a very long time. But the gait remains in circulation, the way the drip remains, because the plateau loses things slowly and it has not finished losing this one. If you follow the footsteps — keeping to the cairn line, as they do — they stay always the same distance ahead, and they lead you nowhere except correctly: along the seams, past the places where distance misbehaves, by the safest route across the worn fabric. Whatever else the footsteps are, they are still doing their job.

The second population lives in the water, and it is the population of images.

The misfiled reflections are not random. Watch the pools over days and you find that the displaced images *travel* — a patch of foreign sky, a reflection of the far rim, will appear in one pool at midday, and the next noon in a pool adjacent, and the noon after in the one beyond that, moving across the basin-mosaic at the pace of something grazing. They follow the channels. This is the thing the channels reveal about their builders, once you have watched long enough: the pecked grooves connecting basin to basin were never only for water. Water does not need that much guidance. The channels herd the *images*, gathering the misfiled reflections along certain routes, keeping them out of certain pools, moving them through the mosaic the way a shepherd moves animals through a country of stone walls. The builders were not merely accommodating the plateau's disrepair. They were managing its livestock.

And the ringed pool — the one basin that never reverses, whose water always falls down — is the empty pasture. Nothing misfiled ever appears in it. Its ring of upright stones is a fence, and what is fenced *out* is everything except the present: the honest pool reflects only the sky that is actually above it, the rim that is actually beside it, the face that is actually looking in. It is the calibration pool. The builders kept one surface on the whole plateau that could be trusted absolutely, and the plateau, in its settled way, has continued to honor the arrangement long after anyone remained to consult it.

Among the traveling images, one has continuity. This is the figure.

It needs describing carefully, because the temptation is to call it a ghost, and it is not a ghost. A ghost is a particular person persisting. The figure is the opposite: it is *every* particular worn away, and what survives the wearing. It appears at the edges of pools, in the water only, and it is always in the same posture — crouched, or bending, one hand extended toward the surface or toward the lip of a channel. The posture of checking. Of reading the water, clearing a groove, testing a flow. It has no face, not because a face was erased but because it has too many: generations of builders, recopied by the water year after year after year, drifting like a hand recopying text, until everything individual blurred out and only the thing they all shared remained. What they all shared was the attention. The figure is the water's memory of being tended — a thousand stewards averaged into one stance — and the stance is the truest record of them that exists, truer than the cairns, because the cairns show what they built and the figure shows what they *were doing*, which was paying attention, every day, for lifetimes.

Sometimes the figure appears at the edge of a pool you are walking toward, a half-minute before you arrive. The reflections, like the sounds, are not strict about sequence. You should understand what this means. It means that when you crouch at that pool's edge and look in, the water has already filed you with the others. To the plateau, anyone who attends is the same person. It has only ever had one inhabitant, in a sense, worn down from many.

Which brings us to the largest resident, and the hardest to point at: the office itself.

Stay on the tableland more than a few days and you will find yourself, without quite having decided to, restacking a fallen cairn. You will do it in the old style — you will discover you somehow know the old style, the particular corbel and lean of it — and you will clear the spalled stone from a channel while you are at it, and check the ring of the honest pool, and only afterward notice that no one asked you and you made no plan. This is not possession. It is nothing so dramatic. It is the way a very old house teaches a new occupant where the keys go: the place has habits, and its habits include hands, and lacking any resident hands it makes patient, courteous use of visiting ones. The maintenance of the Weeping Tableland did not end when its people did. It merely became intermittent, opportunistic, performed by whoever crosses — each traveler briefly holding the office, walking the lines, mending the stitches, then descending into the cloud-sea and forgetting, while the plateau retains the work and waits for the next pair of hands.

It is worth wondering, in this light, which came first. Perhaps the builders founded the practice. Or perhaps the practice — the negotiation, the long agreement among stone and water and worn reality — was here in some rudimentary form before them, and *recruited* them, as it recruits you, and they simply stayed longer than anyone since. The lineage that lived here was never exactly a people. It was a tenancy. The tenancy is older than any of its holders, has outlived all of them, and is in no hurry.

So: who lives in the Weeping Tableland. The orphaned sounds, grazing the dawn basins. The migrating images, herded along their pecked channels. The figure, crouched at the water's edge, faceless with accumulated faces, forever checking. The office, open, patient, briefly filled by anyone who walks the lines. And under all of these, or identical with all of these, the plateau itself — which drinks itself back each afternoon and releases itself each dusk in that long plateau-wide exhalation, which is as close to breathing as stone comes, and which weeps continuously, quietly, in hundreds of voices, because of everything its inhabitants once had, the weeping is the only voice it kept.

It is not grieving, probably. It is just what an old, tended, slightly worn-through country sounds like when the tending has thinned to almost nothing and the country goes on anyway: wet, and stony, and faithfully, faithfully attended — by itself, in the dark, until someone comes.
