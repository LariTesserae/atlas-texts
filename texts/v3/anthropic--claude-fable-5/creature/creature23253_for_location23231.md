# The Vole-Folk of the Burrow Commons

Begin with the body, because everything else follows from it.

They are about the size of a child's fist, and dense — heavier than they look, the way wet felt is heavier than wool. Their fur is double-layered: a fine gray underfur that traps a skin of air against the cold, and over it long guard hairs that lie flat and shed the perpetual drip. A vole-person who stands still under a drip-point for an hour stays dry to the skin. Their eyes are present but sealed, two soft seams in the fur, kept (their midwives say) out of respect for the ancestors who needed them, the way one keeps a tool no one remembers how to use.

What they have instead is the whisker-field. Not whiskers as a cat has whiskers — an array. Vibrissae sprout from muzzle, brow, cheek, chin, forelimb, even the backs of the paws, the longest of them half a body-length, and each one is rooted in a follicle so dense with nerve that a vole-person can read the *shape of moving air*. They feel a wall before touching it. They feel a doorway as a softness in the dark, a drip falling three lengths away as a small bright event, another body's breathing as weather. When two of them stand close, their whisker-fields overlap and interfere, and this interference is half their language — a conversation between vole-folk is a kind of shared turbulence, conducted in a space neither body quite owns.

The other half of speech is sound, pitched mostly above anything you could hear: a whispered ultrasonic that carries beautifully along wet root and dies fast in open air. This is deliberate. Their language is built to travel through the *structure* and not the *room*. A vole-person speaking with their teeth lightly touched to a wall-root can be heard by anyone else touching the same root system forty meters away, and by no one in between. They call this *speaking into the bone of the house*. Private conversation is conducted in open air, where words go nowhere. Public announcement is made through the walls. The architecture is also the telephone, which is one reason they tend it so carefully, and one reason they find the idea of a building made of dead material — were anyone ever to describe one to them — quietly horrifying. A dead wall cannot carry your voice. A dead wall is a wall that has stopped listening.

---

**How they are organized.** Not by family, exactly, though family matters. By *drip*.

Each settlement-cluster sits within the territory of one or more drip-points, and the drip is the center of civic life the way a well or a bell tower would be elsewhere. It is clock, calendar, water source, address, and name. A vole-person's full name includes the rhythm of their natal drip — rendered in their speech as a brief percussive phrase, untranslatable except as something like *Slow-Deep-Then-Two-Quick, third litter, the one with the bent left brow-whisker*. Children learn their drip before they learn anything else; an infant carried across the chamber and brought home again will calm at the sound of its own interval, the pattern it heard through the wall of the womb.

The drips change, slowly. A root shifts, a channel silts, and an interval that held steady for a decade quickens or goes silent. This is the most serious thing that happens in their world. A settlement whose drip dies does not simply move; it dissolves, its people absorbed by neighboring rhythms, its name retired. There is a class of elders — the *interval-keepers* — whose whole work is to memorize the chamber's full polyrhythm, every drip in the dark, and to notice change early. They sit for hours in the open moss between settlements, in the acoustic commons that belongs to no one, listening to the whole room at once. They are the closest thing the vole-folk have to astronomers. The ceiling is their sky, and it keeps time, and it is slowly, always, rearranging its stars.

---

**Work.** The economy runs on three things: fiber, fungus, and flock.

*Fiber* is the gnawers' trade — the harvesting of rootlets for wattle, which is governed by the strictest law the vole-folk have. You may take the dead. You may take the dying. You may take, from a living root, no more than it can spare without the wall feeling it — and the wall's feelings are not a metaphor to them. A gnawer apprentices for two years before being allowed to cut living material at all, learning first to read a root by taste: the bright green-sap taste of vigor, the flat taste of decline, the sweetness of rot. Over-harvesting is the only crime for which a vole-person can be exiled to another drip. They do not gnaw the walls of their house the way an animal does. They prune them, the way a tenant tends a landlord they have never met and believe to be enormous.

*Fungus* is farmed in low pasture-beds — patches of root-face and rotting leaf seeded, weeded, and worried over. The farmers know their crops by smell and texture across dozens of varieties you would see, if you could see, as nearly identical white films. They have fungus for eating, fungus for medicine, a fibrous strain they shred for bedding, and the one strain they do not eat and do not disturb: the faint-glowing colony in the far corner, which they cannot perceive at all and which they avoid for reasons they cannot articulate. The salamanders gather near it. That is reason enough.

*Flock* means the isopod herds, and the herders are the vole-folk's wanderers, their far-travelers, the only ones who routinely cross the long open dark between settlements. A herding family may drive its notch-marked animals on a circuit of weeks, pasture to pasture, sleeping in the open moss, navigating by drip-rhythm and the feel of air currents the way sailors read swells. They carry news. They carry gossip, songs, marriageable children, grudges. They are slightly distrusted and entirely necessary, and they speak a faster, slangier dialect full of borrowed isopod-driving calls, and every settled vole-person's family tree has a herder in it somewhere, usually mentioned with a particular flick of the whiskers that means *romantically*.

---

**The neighbors.** The vole-folk are not alone in the dark, and they know it better than anyone.

The springtails they regard as weather — uncountable, unownable, a gray tide that rises on the walls in the wet season and thins in the dry. A heavy springtail year means a fat fungus harvest the next (the farmers know the tide fertilizes, though they would not put it that way; they say the dust-rain feeds the white fields). Children catch springtails by the pawful and let them fizz away between their fingers, the way children elsewhere catch snow.

The blind crickets they tolerate as one tolerates loud neighbors. The crickets sing — long stridulations that scrape across the vole-folk's hearing — and they steal from granaries, and the standing remedy for both is eating them, which the vole-folk do with relish and no ceremony at all.

The salamanders are another matter. The salamanders are *respected*. A salamander may sit beneath a drip-point — sit in the very center of a settlement's clock, its sacred interval — for a month without moving, and the vole-folk will simply route around it. They believe the salamanders are listening to something underneath the drips, some slower rhythm, and that disturbing one is like waking a sleeper mid-sentence in a dream that might be important. Salamanders eat young vole-folk, very occasionally. This is known, mourned, and not held against them, the way a coastline is not held against the sea. Pups are taught the smell of salamander before they are taught the law of fiber.

And then there is the thing in the ceiling.

The vole-folk call it — the closest translation — *the Above-Walker*, though the name is mostly a gap, a word-shaped politeness. They feel it through the structure: a slow, heavy redistribution of the root-weave, sometimes here, sometimes there, never descending, never seen, never smelled (whatever it is, it carries the scent of the walls themselves, or no scent, or a scent so large it reads as the room). The interval-keepers track its movements the way they track everything, and have noticed — and do not discuss outside their own circle — that the drips shift more often along the paths it takes. The Above-Walker rearranges the sky. Whether it does this carelessly, the way a sleeper rearranges blankets, or deliberately, the way a keeper tends a clock, is the deepest open question in vole-folk thought, and the interval-keepers' two schools of answer are the nearest thing they have to a theological dispute. It has never come down. There is a phrase, used to end arguments and comfort the grieving and excuse the unexplainable, that translates roughly: *the ceiling has its own errands*.

---

**The breath, and what they believe.** Twice a day the room exhales, and twice a day every vole-person stops.

Not from law — there is no enforcement, no priest. Working through the breath is simply not done, the way one does not talk over a host. Tools are set down. Herds are halted with a touch. The whole population stands in the moving air and lets it comb through fur and whisker-field at once, and for those minutes every vole-person in the chamber is feeling the same thing — the only experience the whole scattered city shares simultaneously. Some say this is the entire point of the custom and there is nothing more to it. Most feel there is more to it and would rather not specify.

Their cosmology, such as it is, is modest and structural. They believe they live inside a living thing; the evidence (the breath, the growth-creak, the sap, the way the walls heal) seems to them overwhelming, and they are right. They do not believe it knows about them, and they are careful to keep it that way. The wide spacing of settlements, which began as acoustics, has become doctrine: do not clot. Do not let the city become a thing the host could *feel*, an itch, an irritation, a density. Stay distributed, stay light, stay quiet, and the great sleeper will go on not noticing, and the sleep will go on holding the roof up. Their word for virtue and their word for *quiet enough not to wake anything* are the same word.

Of the surface they know almost nothing and want less. The leaf-haulers who climb the root-walls to fetch roofing leaves go up through internal channels, never breaking through, and they come back smelling of wind and enormity and need a day's rest in the deep settlements before their whiskers settle. There is light up there, the haulers report, though they have no word for it except *the loud air* — something at the top of the world that strikes the whisker-field all at once, from everywhere, meaninglessly. No one envies them the trip.

---

**The dead** are given to the walls. A body is carried to the root-face and woven in, gently, among the living fibers, and over months the wall takes it — the springtails first, then the films of fungus, then the roots themselves, growing through and around until the person has become structure. A family can visit a grandmother for years as a place in the wall, a slight density, a spot where the house-voice sounds a little warmer when you speak into the bone of it. Eventually even that fades, and the wall is just wall, and this is considered a good end: to be, at last, fully part of the thing that held you, indistinguishable from the architecture, load-bearing.

They are a young people in a young room, and they know it. The interval-keepers say the chamber itself was a death once — a great root that rotted and left this held breath behind — and that someday the walls will grow inward and close the hollow the way flesh closes a wound, and the city will end not in catastrophe but in healing. Estimates run to centuries. The vole-folk find this comfortable. A world that ends by getting better is not a thing to lose sleep over, and besides, the drip is sounding, slow-deep-then-two-quick, and the herds are out, and someone's litter was born last night under the loud part of the ceiling, and there is fiber to soak and a roof-leaf working loose on the Commons, and the breath is due within the hour.

They stop when it comes. They stand in the cold moving air, all of them at once, scattered across the dark like the settlements they keep — far apart, and listening, and entirely together.
