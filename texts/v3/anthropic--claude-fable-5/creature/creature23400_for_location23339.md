Whoever told you nothing lives at altitude has never stood where heat comes up through the floor. The caldera is not merely inhabited — it is occupied at every scale, in layers, the way the canopy is layered, and the honest answer to *who lives here* has to start small and climb.

**The first tenants**

The oldest residents are the ones you mistook for paint. The orange and arterial-red mats fringing the hot pools are not stains on the water; they are the water's population — thermophile communities, sheets of single-celled life stacked in colored strata, each color a different temperature tolerance, each band a different species living one millimeter from a neighbor it could never survive beside if the gradient slipped. They are the valley's true natives. Their lineages were running this exact business — eating heat and sulfur and light at the edge of scald — before there were trees, before there were frogs, before there was anything else on the list. Everything larger that lives here lives, in some sense, by their permission: they fix the energy at the hot margins, the flies eat the mats, the birds eat the flies, and the food web hangs off water that would cook you.

They are also the valley's record-keepers, though they keep the record in real time and discard it instantly. Read the bands and you read the plumbing: a pool whose red ring is widening means the vent below is hotting up; green creeping inward means a fracture has shifted and the supply is cooling. The animals read this. So, you will eventually understand, does everyone else.

**The keepers of the schedule**

Up one level live the creatures who have done something almost no animal on Earth does: they have replaced the sun.

Most living things set their clocks by light. The caldera's light is shadowless, pearly, nearly the same all day under the cloud lid — useless as a clock. But the western vent fires every eleven minutes without fail, and has for longer than any lineage here can remember, and so the valley's fauna has entrained itself to geology instead of sky.

The shore flies that breed at the scald-edge swarm on the *chuff* — the vent's discharge pushes a pulse of warm water across the mats, the larvae rise, the adults boil up off the surface in a gray haze, and the wading birds are already there. Watch the long-toed birds for an afternoon and you'll see it: they do not wander. They stand still through the silent minutes, conserving everything in the thin air, and begin walking the algal mats about forty seconds before the vent fires. They are not responding to the chuff. They are *anticipating* it. Eleven minutes is written into their nervous systems the way dawn is written into a rooster's.

The spectacled lizards on the sinter have made a stranger bargain. Lizards are supposed to be daylight creatures, solar-powered, dead-still by dark. These are the only lizards you will ever meet that are active at night — because their heat source doesn't set. They lie on the warm terraces under the cloud-dark sky, blood-temperature rock under their bellies, and hunt vent-flies by feel and starlight on the rare clear nights, and by nothing at all on the others. Their eyes are going large and pale, generation by generation. The vents do not observe seasons; the lizards have stopped observing days.

And the hummingbirds — here is a small miracle, easy to miss. High-altitude hummingbirds everywhere else on Earth survive the cold nights by torpor: they shut down, drop their body temperature to nearly ambient, and gamble on waking. The caldera's hummingbirds have never made that gamble. They roost in niches above the warm seeps, in pockets of rising blood-warm air, and they are the only hummingbirds in existence that sleep warm — that dream, if hummingbirds dream, every single night of their lives. It has changed them. They are calmer than hummingbirds should be. They sit still longer. They live, the evidence of their worn feathers suggests, absurdly long lives for their size, because they have been excused from the nightly emergency that kills their kind everywhere else.

**The ponds in the air**

The bromeliads — those thousands of sealed terraria stacked up the trunks — are not just water tanks. Each one is a sovereign territory, and each has a sovereign.

The valley's signature frog is thumbnail-sized, copper-backed, and may go its entire life without touching the ground. It hatches in a bromeliad pond three meters up, metamorphoses there, disputes the pond with its siblings, loses or wins, and either stays or makes the single great migration of its life: a journey of perhaps four vertical meters to an unclaimed tank. A female tends her eggs in one pond and ferries her tadpoles on her back, one at a time, to separate ponds — insurance against a predator finding the whole brood — and then returns on a circuit to feed them, laying an unfertilized egg into each tank like a delivered meal. She holds the map of a dozen tree-ponds in a brain the size of a seed, and she runs her route daily, up and down the shaggy trunks, through the moss, in silence.

Because the canopy ponds sit at different heights — some warmed by rising vent-breath, some cooled by the rim air sliding over the cloud lid — the frogs of different strata are drifting apart. The low warm-tank frogs breed in no season at all, continuously, like the vents. The high cool-tank frogs still keep a faint annual rhythm, a fossil memory of the world outside. They can still interbreed. They mostly don't. The valley is in the middle of manufacturing two species out of one, the same way it manufactures terraces: slowly, in layers, without hurry, with no off switch.

The dusk chorus you heard — short economical phrases, call and long pause — is these frogs taking attendance. Each pause is exactly long enough for a neighbor's answer to cross the deadening air. It is less a song than a roll call, and when it stops all at once, something has been seen.

**The riders of the charged air**

On dry-mouthed afternoons, when the big steam columns crackle with their useless blue electricity, look closely at the plume margins and you will see the valley's strangest commuters: spiders, tiny ones, standing on tiptoe on the highest fern fronds with their abdomens raised, paying out silk into the charged updraft.

Spiders elsewhere balloon on wind. The caldera has almost no wind — the bowl shelters itself — but it has static, and silk takes a charge, and a charged thread in a charged plume *lifts*. The spiders of the caldera disperse vertically, riding their own electricity up the steam columns toward the cloud lid, drifting under it like plankton, settling out kilometers away across the canopy. Every plume is a public elevator. The blue flicker you saw at dusk, the discharge that touches nothing and powers nothing — it powers them. The valley industrialized itself a hundred thousand years early, and the spiders found the freight system before anyone could have named it.

**The relict**

Now the dark shape in the fern understory — the heavy-bodied browser that passes once a day, roughly, while the polyrhythm continues around it.

You will want it to be a tapir. For a long time, watching, you will be able to keep believing it's a tapir: the right bulk, the deliberate walk, the browsing pauses. But the pieces accumulate and they do not fit. The browse line on the tree ferns is too high, and the fronds are not bitten but *pulled* — stripped through something hooked, with a strength that takes the whole frond. There are marks on the soft andesite, parallel grooves, far up the outcrops, that no tapir made. The dung, when you find it, is full of a fern that the books say has not grown anywhere on Earth for ten thousand years. And once, at the marsh edge in the pearl-gray afternoon, you will see it clearly for four or five seconds and understand why you've been walking past it for days: it is green. Not greenish — green, mossed, its long coarse coat fogged with the same algae that films the rocks, the whole animal grown over like a lava bench, steaming gently in the blood-warm rain exactly as the canopy steams, indistinguishable from the valley because it has been here long enough to be furnished by it.

The caldera is a refugium. The cold ages came and went around the rim — frost possible at your back, remember — and inside the bowl the temperature never moved, the flowering never stopped, the vents kept their eleven minutes, and a few things that died everywhere else simply did not die here. The browser is one of them: a ground sloth, small by its ancestors' standards, slow by anything's standards, the last subscriber to an extinct world, kept on by a mountain that doesn't observe extinctions any more than it observes seasons. It sleeps in the warm mouths of the old lava tubes. It is not afraid of you. It has not had a reason to be afraid of anything shaped like you for a very long time, and you should sit with what that implies, because it implies it has reasons to be afraid of something else.

**What stops the chorus**

The predator is the one resident you will never see, and the valley's hush is half its doing.

It is feline, or near enough — long, low, dark, perhaps twenty kilos, built for the moss. What makes it the caldera's own is not its body but its clock: it hunts on the schedule. It moves during the chuffs. Eleven minutes of stillness, flattened into the moss that holds its shape like memory foam; then the western vent fires, the valley fills with pneumatic noise for nine or ten seconds, and the predator covers ground — and freezes as the sigh dies. To everything listening, it does not exist between vents. It crosses the whole basin in stolen seconds, a creature that has learned to live inside the punctuation of the geology, and the only evidence it ever existed is feathers pressed into moss and the frog chorus stopping all at once — then restarting a section at a time, in order, away from where it passed, so that if you are quick with a map you can plot its route through the dark by the sequence of resuming voices. The frogs keep better track of it than you ever will.

It does not touch the sloth. There is some old settlement between them, older than observation.

**The ones the moss forgives**

And then there are the people, because there are people, and the reason you found no path, no marker, no cut stone, no wire is not that no one lives here. It is that they build nothing, and the valley erases what little they do.

They live in the intact sections of the lava tubes — the ones whose ceilings never collapsed — warm-floored, dry, dark, curtained at the mouths by tree ferns they did not plant and do not trim. They have never widened an entrance. Why would they? The mountain built better housing than they could, with central heating older than their species, and to a people raised inside it, construction looks like a confession that your home has failed you.

They cook in the pools, and they read the pools the way you learned to, by the color of the mats — they were the original authors of that lesson, though they'd never put it that way. Green-fringed water for steeping and slow ferments; the orange margins for true cooking, fiber nets of roots and frog-meat lowered on cords and counted out in breaths; the red they call *the mouth* and do not use, not from taboo exactly, more the way you don't use a neighbor's tools.

A *breath* is their base unit of time: one cycle of the western vent. Eleven minutes, though they have never needed the number. Meetings happen "two breaths from now." A good sleep is forty breaths. A long life is — they don't count that high; long counting strikes them as a kind of hoarding. They have no calendar, because nothing here observes seasons and so nothing requires one: no planting time, no harvest, no lean months. Follow the implications down and you find the deep strangeness of them — a people with no granaries, because the valley fruits continuously; therefore no stores, therefore no one who controls the stores, therefore none of the architecture of rank that storage builds everywhere else. They have no word for famine. Their word for *winter* is not a time. It is a place — it means the rim, and the outer slopes, and everywhere else.

They go up to the rim deliberately, though. Each child is taken up once, at the cold edge of evening, to stand where frost is possible and look back down at the steaming bowl — so the child will understand, in the body, behind the eyes where the altitude throbs, that the valley is a kindness and not a law. They believe the mountain is alive — not as metaphor; in the plain geological sense you yourself conceded at the rim — and their grammar reflects it: the valley is the subject of most of their sentences. The pool cooked the roots. The vent told the hour. The moss took the footprint. They appear in their own language mostly as objects, things acted upon, kept warm. Their speech is like the frog chorus — short phrases, long pauses, as if the thin air taxes every word — and a conversation between two of them, in the dusk, is nearly indistinguishable from the roll call going on in the bromeliads above their heads, and they consider this good manners.

They keep no records. The moss refills their footprints within the hour; they say the valley keeps no record of them, and they return the courtesy. No writing, no monuments, no count of generations. Asked how long they have been here, they point at the terraces.

This is not evasion. It is the answer. Their dead are carried to the sinter terraces and laid on the scalloped lips where the water films over constantly, and the silica does what it has done for thousands of years to everything left in its path: it covers them, millimeter by millimeter, mineral layer by mineral layer, until what was a person is a pale band in the white and gray and salmon stone. The terraces — the oldest things in the valley, and still under construction — are partly built of them. A woman can stand on a terrace lip and tell you, accurately, that she is standing on her grandmother, and that her grandmother is now part of the foundation that the water is building the next layer on, and that someday she will be a layer that someone stands on, and she will say all this with the satisfaction of a person describing a house being finished properly. The one thing they build, the valley builds for them, out of them, on its own schedule. It is the only record they keep, and it is the most permanent record in the bowl.

They saw you, of course. From the fern understory, counted against the breaths — your altitude-clumsy walk, your habit of pressing palms to the weeping rock, your evenings at the rim. They read visitors the way they read the algal mats: a color at the edge of things, indicating conditions. They concluded you would leave by morning, and they were right, and the moss took your footprints within the hour, which in their grammar means the valley has already finished forgetting you, no offense intended; it forgets them too, daily, and they have decided to find that restful.

**Who lives here, then**

All of the above — and underneath all of the above, one tenant the list keeps circling.

Because the mats run the chemistry, and the flies run the mats' surplus upward, and the birds keep the flies, and the frogs keep the canopy's million small ponds, and the spiders ride the static, and the sloth carries the last of an extinct flora through the fern halls in its green coat, and the predator keeps the count honest, and the people lie down at the end and become terrace — and every one of them is timed, fed, warmed, hidden, and finally absorbed by the same closed loop turning on the same buried heat. The bromeliads were the clue, each one the valley repeated in miniature. It goes the other way too. Each resident is the valley repeated at its own scale: a small hot system that takes in warmth and water and gives back steam, holding its shape for a while before the layers close over it.

What lives in the Caldera of Standing Steam is the caldera. Everything else — the mats, the frogs, the warm-sleeping hummingbirds, the green sloth, the silent cat, the people who refuse to build — is how it lives: the factory with no owner and no product, manufacturing only itself, continuously, in residents, and very good at it.
