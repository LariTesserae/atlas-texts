**The Builders**

Begin with the smallest, because everything else stands on them.

The builders are insects the size of a thumbnail, and they are blind in every sense the word will bear. Eyeless, yes — the plain's interior is dark and the surface is for working, not seeing — but blind also in the larger way: no single builder has ever held an image of the city it builds. Each one knows the grain in its mandibles, the chemical word smeared on the grain before it, the warmth gradient under its feet, the touch-code tapped onto its antennae by the sister passing the other direction. That is the whole of a builder's world: one grain, one word, one degree of warmth, one touch. Out of four such threads, repeated some sextillion times, the skylines of a continent.

They are pale things, almost translucent, the color of the dust they carry — though they secrete the color of the towers themselves, the ochres and rust-reds, in the cement they work from their own bodies. The cement is the heart of their economy. They render it from mineral dust, from the waxes of the cushion-plants, from their own dead — a builder's corpse is carried not to a midden but to a wall, and built in, so that every tower is also a columbarium, every city a continuous interment of itself. The oldest districts of the oldest cities are, by mass, mostly builder. The architecture is the ancestry.

A builder lives perhaps two years. A city is older than rain. The disproportion does not trouble them, because no builder knows it; the knowledge is held where everything important here is held — in the structure itself. The city remembers. A flue angled to catch the dawn wind was angled that way by builders forty generations dead, and the living maintain it without knowing why, because the chemistry of the wall instructs them, because the warmth flows correctly, because the city, in the only language it has, says *yes, this, again*. The builders are not the inhabitants of the cities so much as the cities' way of continuing. Whether the colony thinks — whether the slow chemical weather moving through sixty meters of ventilated tower constitutes a mind — is the kind of question the plain declines to answer. It can be said that the cities respond. They turn their growth toward fresh fissures before the fissures finish opening, reading the strain in the rock through a million pairs of feet. They thicken their windward faces in advance of seasons. They mourn, or do something that resembles it: when a tower falls, the work-rate of the whole city drops for days, and the chemistry of the walls runs bitter, and then the quarrying begins, grain by grain, and the dead tower is translated into the living ones.

Below the cities, the builders go deeper than anyone. There is a caste the surface never sees — long-bodied, soft, paler even than the others — that works the vapor mines: galleries driven down through the basalt to where the young rock still breathes. These deep ones never carry grains. They carry water, a droplet at a time, held in the throat, walking it up through kilometers of dark to the nursery vaults. They are fed, groomed, and lived for by everyone above them. In a country with no rivers, they are the rivers.

**The Green**

The cushion-plants are usually mistaken for many and are mostly few. Core a hundred kilometers of the quilt and you may find it is three individuals, or one — clonal mats centuries across, each swollen lobe a knuckle of a single sprawling hand. The green is not a population. It is a small number of enormous patient beings lying flat against the rock, drinking it.

Their intelligence, if it can be called that, is hydraulic. They route. Moisture found at one fissure is moved through the mat to feed growth at another; the whole organism runs a slow internal economy of water, balancing its body across the basalt the way the cities balance their walls. They have struck their bargain with the builders and keep it scrupulously: wax for water. The builders harvest the leaf-bristle wax that binds their cement, and in exchange the plants are permitted — encouraged — to root into the cities' lower vaults, where the exhalation of the colony, the breath of millions of small bodies, condenses on cool stone. Every city wears a green collar denser than the plain around it. Every cushion-mat thickens toward the nearest skyline. Pull the two apart and both would survive, diminished; no one has pulled them apart in ten thousand years.

In the gray hour, the mats do the one violent thing they ever do. The bristles that comb the fog are motile — they fling, microscopically, snapping condensed droplets inward toward the stomata — and a hectare of cushion-plant drinking fog produces, if you press your ear to it, a sound like very faint, very fast applause.

**The Tenants**

The cities did not invite anyone, and have never evicted anyone. The dun mammals — call them vault-mice, though they are not mice and some are the size of cats — live in the cool lower galleries in extended families of thirty or forty, and have shaped their whole existence around being inoffensive to the landlord. They groom the gallery walls. They cache seeds in chambers the builders have abandoned, and forget a tithe of every cache, and the forgotten tithe feeds the colony's fungal gardens, and so the rent is paid without either party ever conceiving of the transaction. Their enormous eyes are for the gallery dark. Their fur is the exact dun of cured cement, and in the gray hour they come to the tower mouths and sit upright in rows, motionless, letting the fog bead on their guard hairs, and then comb themselves with their forepaws and lick their wrists. It is the only drinking most of them will ever do, and they perform it with a gravity that looks, from any distance, like prayer.

The lizards are the cities' constables — long, low, patient, patrolling the arched galleries on a circuit so regular the vault-mice time their foraging by it. The snakes that thread the avenues are mostly after the lizards. The beetles in their armorial colors are after the wax, and pay for it by carrying plant pollen tower to tower in the chinks of their armor, mostly unwillingly. Everyone here is in someone's employ without knowing it.

The birds have given up the throat almost entirely. They speak in heliograph: structural color in the wing, angled flashes, a vocabulary of tilt and blaze that carries to the horizon in the clear still air and tells nothing to anything that cannot see the sender. Courtship on the plain is conducted in total silence on the tower-tops at dawn, two birds semaphoring at each other across an avenue, the light doing all the singing. Their one concession to sound is involuntary: the wind through their nest-chimneys. Birds choose abandoned flues for the note the flue produces. Generations of selection have made them connoisseurs of resonance, and a city's voice — that low organ-breath by which a traveler navigates — is partly the work of birds, curating which chimneys stay open.

The hawk-shaped silence has a body. It is a soarer with a four-meter span that may not touch the green once in a season; it lives on the thermals the dark basalt pours upward all day, nests in the highest dead spires, and hunts by shadow alone — it has learned that its silhouette crossing the sun is its own beater, freezing everything below, and that whatever freezes a half-second late is the meal. It drinks fog on the wing, mouth open, in long knee-high passes through the gray hour, the only fast thing in the slowest hour of the day.

**The Walker**

And then there is the one that the plain seems to have made on purpose, though the plain makes nothing on purpose.

They are rare — perhaps a few thousand on the whole continent — and solitary, and large: a tall, slab-shouldered, long-legged beast, gray-green as the quilt it crosses, moving at an unhurried swing that eats forty kilometers between dawn and dusk. The walkers eat cushion-plant, but lightly, a lobe here and a lobe there, never grazing a mat down; they carry beetles in their coat-creases and spores in their gut and, in callused pouches above their hooves, hitchhiking builders — young queens and their retinues, sealed in wax, waiting. A walker is the plain's circulation. Cities are founded where walkers rest. The green leaps fissure-fields it could not cross alone in the dark of a walker's belly. Nothing on the plain travels far, except that everything does, inside or upon this one beast that does nothing but travel.

The walker's head is mostly ear. Its eyes are small and incurious; its hearing is the finest instrument the continent has produced. It navigates, exactly as a traveler would, by the voices of the cities — holds the chord of the skyline behind it and the fainter chord of the skyline ahead, and walks the line between them, reading distance in loudness and identity in timbre, carrying in its head a map of the continent written entirely in organ-notes. Walkers raised in different regions know different cities the way singers know songs.

They are silent, like everything here, with one exception, and the exception is the loveliest thing on the plain. A walker ready to mate does not call. It chooses a city, stands in the lee of the tallest chimney rank, and *adds itself to the chord* — a deep, sustained chest-tone, pitched into the city's voice, so that the city's note goes out across the dark plain very slightly changed. Somewhere, fifty or a hundred kilometers off, another walker hears a skyline it has known all its life sounding not quite like itself, and turns, and begins to walk. The courtship can take a season. The song is borrowed from architecture, broadcast by the labor of insects who will never know they carried it, heard through air kept still and clear by the silence of every other living thing.

**Who Lives Here**

Ask the question directly and the plain gives back its one contradiction, repeated at every scale. The builders live here, but no builder knows the place it lives. The green lives here, but the green is three beings pretending to be a meadow. The tenants live in the cities, the cities live on the tenants, the walkers carry everyone, the rock breathes water up and the deep pale caste walks it to the cradles a droplet at a time.

What lives in the Mound Plains is the arrangement. It has no center, no name for itself, no individual older than a few decades, and it is, taken whole, the oldest and largest inhabitant of its world — a single continuous occupancy, settling its grains and tuning its flues and combing the fog out of the dawn, built by no one in particular, and alive in no particular body, forever.
