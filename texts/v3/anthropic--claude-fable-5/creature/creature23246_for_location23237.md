**Who lives on the Loom Plateau**

Begin with the honest answer, the one the place itself would give if it could be made to hold still long enough to answer: one thing lives here. One. Everything else you will meet — and you will meet a great deal — is grammar.

But that answer, though true, is the least useful thing that can be said about the plateau, because the one thing that lives here lives in more tenses than living usually gets, and some of what moves across the wet flats turns out, on long acquaintance, not to be the plant at all. The plateau is not as closed as it believes. There are guests. There are exiles. There is, at last count, one person.

Take them in order of how deeply they belong.

---

**The weaver**

The plant does not live *on* the plateau. There is no preposition that works. It is not in the place; it is the place's whole act of being, the way a fire is not in a flame. It is, as far as anything can determine, a single organism — one germination, unimaginably long ago, on a mountain that no longer exists partly because the plant ate it — and it has been alone for so long that *alone* may not be a state it has, any more than the sea is wet to itself.

How does such a thing live? It pumps. It vents. It runs current through its own meat and listens — if the discharges are listening; they may be more like talking, or more like the muscle-twitch of something dreaming. It has no rivals, no predators, no mate, no neighbors, and it finished its only project — the consumption and replacement of an entire mountain — ages before the present era of the world began. What it does now, what its *living* now consists of, is the rehearsing. The heaved waves, the churning hectares, the animals raised and walked and taken down: this is not the plant's behavior. It is closer to the plant's metabolism of mind. It lives the way an old person lives who has done everything and forgotten nothing: by going over it.

Whether it knows you are there is unresolvable. It will certainly *register* you — the mat firms slightly under a sustained footstep, the way skin does — and after a few days the rehearsals near your camp begin to include things that are nearly you. This may be attention. It may be digestion, begun politely, at the level of pattern, long before the level of flesh.

It is the landlord, the house, the weather, and most of the tenants. Call it the weaver, since the place is called the Loom, and since everything else here is best understood as cloth.

---

**The woven**

The made animals are not a species, or a fauna, or even properly a population. They are a vocabulary. The plant has a stock of forms it raises — the heavy olive heron, the six-legged leaf-scaled grazer, the steam-flights of small fliers, and dozens of rarer figures: a pale eel of the black pools, a slow climbing thing that lives only on the vent towers and grooms the ribbing, a thing like a hare that is mostly ear. Each one, while it runs, eats, startles, suffers cold, avoids the churn. They are not puppets. The plant does not steer them. It *poses a creature* the way a question is posed, and then watches what the creature does with itself — and what it does is live, in every observable respect, until the mat opens beneath it and it lies down without argument and is unsaid.

Within the woven there are degrees, and the degrees matter:

*The tethered* are drafts — the heron with one wing still rooted, walking its circle at the end of its green umbilical. They are the easiest to pity and the least in need of it. A tethered animal is still continuous with the whole; it is the plateau's finger, not its orphan. They show no distress at the limit of the tether. Why would they? Nothing on a tether has ever yet wanted anything the plant did not want through it.

*The loosed* run free, for hours or for decades. These are the plateau's true wildlife, if it has any — animals fully detached, foraging on the mat (the plant feeds them; the plant *is* what they eat; the economy here is a hand passing food to its own mouth by way of a bird), sheltering against the warm flanks of the vent towers, sleeping in the lee of the slow green swells. They breed, some of them, or appear to; whether the offspring are made the ordinary way or whether the mat simply supplies a small one at the appropriate moment is a thing you can watch for years without settling.

*The rehearsed* are not alive twice. They were alive once, and are played. The heron with the chipped beak, performing its seven steps at noon and dusk and noon again, is a recording rendered in flesh — perfect, warm, breathing, and as empty of present tense as an echo. You can tell a rehearsal, eventually, by a single test: it cannot be surprised. Step into its path and it performs its startle — the same startle, with the same wingbeat count — whether or not you are actually there.

And then there are *the dropped stitches.*

---

**The dropped stitches**

This is the plateau's quietest tragedy, and the closest thing it has to a native, independent animal life.

An animal loosed for too long *drifts*. It eats, it weathers, it is scarred by the churn, it learns — and learning changes the pattern, and the pattern is the only name the plant knows it by. There comes a day when the mat opens to take an old loosed creature down and then closes again, uncertainly, without taking it. The pattern no longer matches. The loom no longer recognizes its own thread.

These are the dropped stitches: animals the plateau made and can no longer read. There are not many — perhaps a few hundred, across the whole green immensity — and they are unmistakable once you know to look. They are the ragged ones. The asymmetrical ones. The only animals on the plateau that flinch from the churn rather than ignoring it, because the churn is the body of the thing that has forgotten them and they seem — there is no other word — to *mind*. They gather, against all the plateau's habit of solitude, in small mixed bands at the warm rims of the dying vent towers: a forgotten grazer, two strayed herons, an eel that hauled itself out of a closing pool years ago and has grown a kind of leg. They are the only society on the plateau. They are also the only things on it that can die in the ordinary, unrecoverable sense — and they do, in the cold, in the wet, slowly — except that even this is not clean, because the mat absorbs their bodies as it absorbs all water and matter, taking back as substance what it could not take back as thought.

Whether the plant knows they exist is the wrong question. They *are* the plant. They are its forgotten sentences, still walking around, still meaning something, with no speaker left who remembers saying them. If the plateau can be said to have an unconscious, the dropped stitches live in it — out at the rims, in the warm exhaust, watching the rehearsals of newer, better-remembered animals with what you will swear, by the end, is grief.

---

**The guests**

The plateau is high and old and total, but it is not sealed. Things arrive. Almost nothing that arrives survives — the mat smothers seeds, the cold kills strays, the black pools close over what falls in — but *almost* is a working margin, given enough millennia, and a thin true border-life has established itself in the places the plant does not fully attend.

There is a moth. One species, gray as the overcast, blown up from the lowlands on some ancestral storm. It breeds in the dead outer husk of the oldest vent towers, the only tissue on the plateau the plant has stopped renewing, and it has lived there long enough that the plant has *noticed* it — which here means: the plant has begun making moths. The steam-flights now contain both. They fly together, indistinguishable in the air, and only at the end can you sort them: the true moths die, and lie where they fall, and stay. The made moths are unsaid. The true moths are thus the only things on the plateau that leave corpses, and the mat is studded, near the old towers, with tiny gray punctuation that the green slowly swallows.

There is a lichen — fittingly, the only other weaver here, being itself two organisms in one cloth — that crusts the cold crowns of the vent stacks in slow orange coins. It is the only living thing on the plateau that grows *on* the plant rather than *as* it, and the plant either tolerates it or genuinely has no sense fine enough to register it: a parasite the size of a coin on a host the size of a region. The lichen is, by a certain accounting, the plateau's most independent resident. It is also the only one that is boring, and there is something restful about that.

There are the migrating birds — true birds, from the true world — whose flyway crosses the plateau twice a year. Most pass over without descending. Some land, to rest on the only flat softness for a hundred miles. Most of those leave again. The flocks that descend the far side, observers have noted, are sometimes slightly larger than the flocks that arrived — and what travels on in them, wearing perfect feathers, perfectly warm, is the plateau's only known export: a thought of the Loom, flying away south, indistinguishable from a bird until the day, somewhere far below in the ordinary world, it stands still too long in a marsh and begins, very slowly, to root.

---

**The remembered**

Then there are the residents who have been dead longer than the plateau has been green.

The plant ate a mountain, and the mountain had its own life: stone, lichen of its own, a thin alpine fauna, wind-bent trees at its old shoulders, and — the rehearsals suggest — at least a few people, at least once, long ago. None of it survives. All of it recurs. In the deep churn, near the great vents, when the air glows, the mat performs its dead geology — and with the gray stone, sometimes, come the stone's old tenants: a goat-like figure, gray and heavy-coated, standing on rock that exists for the eight seconds it is stood upon; a thin whistle, repeated three times, that matches no living animal and is generally agreed to be a marmot, extinct beneath the green for longer than most lineages last; once in a great while a cairn, hand-stacked, briefly cold and real, and beside it the impression — never the figure, only the wet print of where a figure would kneel — of someone building it.

Do the remembered live here? They are performed by a living thing, in the place they lived, doing what they did, made briefly of actual matter. The plateau does not distinguish remembering from doing; that failure is the whole crisis of the place. If you accept the plateau's own metaphysics — and standing in it, in the drumming dimness, you will find you have little choice — then the old mountain's dead are not gone. They are *infrequent.* They live here at a very low rate.

---

**The person**

There is one.

She came — say *she*; the plateau itself has stopped being sure, and you will too — a long time ago, to study the discharges, and stayed past the point of any study. She lives in a low dry pocket she has trained into the mat near a mid-sized vent tower, warmed by exhaust, fed (she says) by the plateau, which raises small bland tubers near her camp that grow nowhere else on the whole green world of it and which she has decided not to think about too closely.

The plant has begun rehearsing her. This is the central fact of her residence and she discusses it without distress. You may meet her three times in a day, at three distant points on the flats, and at most one of those meetings is *her* — and her position is that this hardly matters anymore, that the rehearsals are accurate, that they say what she would say, and that being redistributed across a place is simply what living here, fully, comes to mean. The dropped stitches know her, or know all of her; they follow her at a distance, the forgotten thoughts trailing the remembered woman. She is the only thing on the plateau that the plant is currently *learning*, in real time, while it lives, and she stays — she told one visitor, or one of her did — because to be memorized by something that will outlast geology is the nearest honest thing to not dying that anyone has ever been offered.

When you leave, she will see you off, standing on the green swell with the drizzle beading her coat. Possibly twice.

---

**The census**

So: who lives on the Loom Plateau?

One weaver, the size of a region, older than its own ground, alone in a way that has worn past loneliness into weather. Its vocabulary of animals — drafted, loosed, replayed. Its forgotten sentences, huddled in the warm exhaust at the rims, the only things here that fear and the only things that gather. A moth that dies honestly. A lichen that doesn't care. Birds that arrive in the ordinary world and do not always leave belonging to it. A mountain's worth of dead, living at a low rate. And one person, in several places, at home.

Everything you meet there is a tense of the same verb — past, present, conditional, the strange recurring tense of the rehearsed — except for the thin true border-life, the guests and the woman, the threads taken in from outside. The Loom is not closed. It is only very, very wide, and very far behind on its work, and it weaves whatever reaches it, slowly, into the one long sentence it has been revising since before the rock gave out: *here, still, again.*
