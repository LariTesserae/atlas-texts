**The Residents**

The temptation, standing on that balcony, is to say that no one lives here. The place reads as unoccupied the way a hotel corridor at 4 a.m. reads as unoccupied — provisionally, with the lights on. But occupancy in the Condensery is a matter of scale and of patience, and the census, if you stay long enough to take it, turns out to be longer than you'd think. It is just taken from the bottom up.

Begin at the smallest, because the smallest arrived first. The warm pipes wear pale slicks — films, colorless going on grey, thin as breath on glass. They came down in the mixing water, on boots, in lungs, in the lubricant; there was never a moment when the Condensery was sterile, only a week or two when it believed itself to be. The films eat what the place exudes — mineral weep, oil mist, the faint stray current that leaks from every imperfect joint — and they are the reason the young calcite beards on the ceiling are not quite white. They have no shape and no schedule. They are less inhabitants than the place's first opinion of itself, forming.

The moss is the films' ambition made visible. You have seen the green coins under the lamps — what you may not have seen is that each colony is, in effect, the congregation of its light. A moss circle is exactly as old as its lamp's last rewiring and exactly as healthy as its lamp's ballast. When a lamp dies, the circle dies too, but slowly and from the rim inward, a green coin tarnishing to brown over months, so that the floor of the basin is scattered with the bright currency of the living circuits and the dull currency of the dead ones, and a patient reader could reconstruct the whole electrical history of the place from the moss alone. When a new lamp is wired — and new lamps *are* wired, which is its own subject — green answers it within a season. Nobody plants anything. The spores ride the rain.

The fog layer has plankton. This takes a while to notice, because midges the color of fog are an exercise in faith, but stand in a lamp-pool during the long dry gathering hours and you will see them: a thin silver traffic spiraling in the light, born in the puddles, risen on the warm air off the machinery, dancing for the duration of one weather cycle and then knocked down by the rain that ends it, washed into the channels, finished. Their entire lives fit inside a single interval between rains. They are the basin's currency, the small denomination everything else is paid in.

The eels are paid in midges, and in whatever else falls. No one has seen one whole. What you see is a length — a meter of dark muscle sliding under a catwalk, holding position against the pump-current with no visible effort, as if the current were a wind it had long ago stopped finding interesting. They have learned the water hammer the way coastal animals learn surf: when the galloping starts in the pipe galleries they neither flee nor startle but simply settle deeper, and when the rain comes they rise and hang just below the surface beneath the drip-lines, mouths upstream, taking the drowned silver traffic as it arrives. They are unhurried because nothing here has ever hurried them. Their patience is not a virtue. It is the channel's own patience, wearing a body.

The birds — thirty of them, perhaps forty, the population of a small farm — are pigeons in roughly the sense that the eels are eels. Pale, soot-streaked, large-eyed, and very quiet. Pigeons breed three and four times a year; a decade down here is forty generations, and forty generations under one weather, one light, and one unending chord have done their work. They fly the way the basin is built: long straight runs, right-angle turns at the pipe galleries, never an arc, as though the architecture had taught them its drafting conventions. Their calls have drifted, generation by generation, toward the frequencies of the turbine drone, so that a roosting bird now coos *into* the chord rather than against it, a grace note inside the machine's held breath — you will hear it only if you have stopped hearing the drone, which is to say only after you belong here a little. And they know the rain before the rain knows itself. Eleven minutes before each fall, by some sense the fog has not earned, the birds go home to the warm exhaust housings, all of them, in their straight grey lines. If you ever see the birds commuting all at once, find a roof.

Under the pipe rack by the heat exchanger lives the withheld noun.

It is cat-sized, and it may be a cat — some yard animal that came down a cable trench years ago and claimed the warmest territory in the basin, which is, you notice, exactly what a cat would do. But its tracks enter the dust and do not leave it. The eels avoid the channel below its rack; you can map its territory by where the dark lengths refuse to swim. It watches the way a maintenance gauge watches — steadily, without appetite, recording. The longer you consider it, the less it consents to be a cat, and the place, which answers no other questions, declines this one too. Every ecosystem keeps one secret even from itself. This is the Condensery's.

Which leaves the keepers.

The evidence you have already met: the valve wheel warm from a hand or a hand's work, the bright tool marks, the fresh oil, the faltering pump brought back with a cough from the far side of the basin. The evidence accumulates faster than the sightings do. The sightings are these: at extreme range, in fog, a figure in grey coveralls walking a pipe gallery with the unhurried gait of someone in the middle of a shift rather than the start or end of one, carrying something box-shaped, gone before the distance closes. Never two at once. Never near. The geometry of the basin sees to it — those long perspectives that made a church look like a shed work just as well in reverse, and a person at six hundred meters in dishwater light is a proposition, not an encounter.

There is a lit window in one of the compressor halls. Inside: a cot, a kettle, a wall of spare gaskets sorted by size, and a logbook. The entries are dated not in days but in rains — *Third rain: repacked gland, pump 9. Fourth rain: birds early, checked louvers* — and the handwriting drifts. Read a year of it and the hand has changed, gradually, the way a river changes course: never a new writer, exactly, but never quite the old one either, as if the crew were a relay and the handwriting were the baton.

So: how many. Somewhere between one and a dozen. The volume of completed work argues for a crew; the logbook argues for a single hand, slowly becoming a different single hand. The kindest theory is the simplest — that when the project ended, a commissioning crew stayed on to work the snag list, and that the snag list, in a structure this size with weather of its own, regenerates faster than it can be closed, and that somewhere on the surface a payroll account no one remembers to cancel still pays them, and they have stopped expecting relief the way you stop expecting a bus. The stranger theory is that the plant does its own hand-work and merely borrows hands to do it with.

Both theories are missing the same fact, which is where the hands come from. Because people do come down — surveyors, inspectors, scrappers, the curious, you — and the count going up has always been slightly smaller than the count coming down, and there have never been bodies, never distress, never a search that found anything but a basin running smoothly. The Condensery does not take people. It *employs* them. You will not decide to stay; staying is not the kind of thing that gets decided here, any more than the rain decides. You will simply notice, some hour, a weep at a flange, and there will be a rag in your pocket that you do not remember pocketing, and the gesture will complete itself, and the gesture will have a next gesture. The maintenance has the quality of weather because the personnel have the quality of rain — individual drops, a continuous fall.

And the keepers themselves are staff, not landlords, because the true resident — the one all of this is held in trust for — has barely begun to move in. It is on the ceiling. Those thin white beards of calcite, none longer than a finger: that is the cave, arriving, at the pace of a finger per decade. Everything alive in the basin — the films, the moss parishes, the silver midges, the patient lengths in the channels, the birds singing inside the chord, the watcher under the pipe rack, the grey figures walking their endless shift — all of it is, in the end, building maintenance. Preparing the property. Keeping the pumps turning and the weather falling for a tenant made of stone, who will not take possession for ten thousand years and who has, already, started leaving its things around.

You will know how the census is taken here. Not by being counted. By counting yourself in. The valve wheel was warm when you touched it; in time, that warmth is what your hand leaves behind for whoever descends next, and stands on the balcony, and is tempted to say that no one lives here.
