**The Resident**

You were told there are no inhabitants, and that is true the way it is true of a forest, or a reef, or a body. Nothing lives *in* the Kiln Ward. The Kiln Ward is what lives.

Start small, though, because that is how you'll come to see it. Start with one heat-drinker.

---

Take a single plant — say, a young one, rooted in the third gallery where a hairline fissure went warm two seasons ago. It stands about knee-height. Its stem is the brown of dark honey gone old in the jar, and rigid; flex it and it will not bend but *ring*, faintly, like a struck cup. Its roots are not roots in any sense a gardener would recognize. They are drawn glass, black and fine as horsehair at the tips, thickening toward the stem into braided cables with the dull luster of obsidian, and they do not seek water, because there is no water and never was. They seek *gradient*. A heat-drinker's root grows along the line where warm rock meets cooler rock, the way a willow's root grows along the line where wet soil meets dry, and for the same reason: that boundary is where the living is. Heat flows across a gradient. The plant sits astride the flow and drinks.

What moves in its stem is not sap. Hold a leaf to the fissure-light — you did this, you saw the amber fluid creeping in it — and understand that you were watching a circulatory system for *temperature*. The fluid is dense, slow, thermally greedy; it loads heat at the root and hauls it up through the stem and spends it in the leaf, in chemistry we don't have good names for, building waxes and glasses and that stiff translucent flesh out of warmth and rock-breath and almost nothing else. The ceramic flowers that open with a *tick* are exactly what they sound like: each one is fired in place, its own minute kiln, the petals stressed by the plant's internal heat until they crack open along planned lines. The flower does not bloom. It *cools into bloom*. The seed pods detonate the same way — grown under tension, glazed shut, and then, at ripeness, allowed to cool a fraction of a degree too fast. The crack you keep hearing is intention. It is the plant throwing its children into the void, hard, so they will bounce far.

That is one heat-drinker. Now widen your eyes.

---

Dig into the brickwork anywhere in the ward — gently, you'd need a chisel, the joints have the integrity of poured glass — and you will find that the roots do not stop at the plant. They run. They run *through* the mortar, and after the first few finger-widths you will lose the ability to say which black glass is mortar and which is root, because the answer for some centuries now has been: both. The fused-glass joints that hold the masonry together are grown as much as poured. Roots from a plant on the seventh gallery braid into roots from a plant on the floor, eighty vertical feet apart. The "ancient plant whose roots hold a wall together better than any mortar could" — there is no such individual plant, not really. There is one organism in the Kiln Ward, wearing a few thousand stems the way a tree wears leaves, and it wears the city too. The brick terraces are its trellis, its skeleton, its shell. The bridges flexing across the void are bridges, yes, and also branches.

And once you know that, the heat of the place reads differently. The chamber sits at the temperature of a fevered body — *evenly*, gallery to gallery, despite fissures that run furnace-hot a few feet from rock that should be cool and dead. That evenness is not geology. Black glass conducts. The root-network is a circulatory system at the scale of the whole hollow: it drinks from the hot fissures and bleeds warmth out through the cooler masonry, smoothing the chamber's temperature the way your blood smooths yours. The fissure-glow that waxes and wanes "like weather" is being *gardened*. When a seam runs too hot, roots crowd it and drain it; when a seam falters, the network starves its far provinces to keep the core warm, and a terrace of stems goes brittle and dies back, leaves curling into scrolls. You watched its dead leaves being herded into the corner-drifts by the chamber's breathing and thought *swept by something*. You were right. The drifts are not waste heaps. Root-tips, finer than hair, rise through the floor seams at the drift edges. The resident eats its own fallen leaves there, slowly, in the dark, and the floor is kept bare not for tidiness but because the floor is a working surface — the broad warm pan where seeds land and are tasted and, a few each season, permitted to root.

The breathing itself — the organ-note, the minutes-long cycle of air drawn in and pushed out through the fissures — is the one thing here you could call a metabolism made audible. It is partly the rock's own thermal pumping. But the resident has shaped its growth around that pumping for so long, narrowing channels here, sealing them with glaze there, that the breath now goes where the breath is *wanted*: across ripening pods to dry them, down the central void to carry seed, along the corner-drifts to tumble the leaf-litter toward the feeding roots. The hollow heart of the city, the emptiness the builders so carefully preserved — that is its largest organ. A lung, a throat, a seed-fountain, the open column through which one end of the body speaks heat to the other.

---

Which brings us to the builders.

Here is what can be said with confidence: the resident was in the bubble first. It is the kind of life that needs a cooling lava chamber, and people are not. The people came down through the seam into a hollow already amber-lit and already ticking, and they found free heat, and they fired brick in the fissures, and the resident — which until then had grown on bare curved stone — found *brick*: porous, conductive, stackable substrate, appearing in ever-greater quantities, arranged in shapes that channeled warmth in interesting new ways. The doorways that skirt root-masses, the terraces that bend around a single old stem: you read those as deference, the people accommodating the plant. Read it also the other way. The plant let the brick stand where the brick served the gradient. Symbiosis is the polite word. *Mutual domestication* is the honest one, and as with all domestication, it is hard afterward to say who kept whom.

What happened to the people, the ward does not record, and the resident — whatever it has in place of memory — keeps no remains and no answer. Perhaps they left when the surface cooled enough to live on. Perhaps a generation simply dwindled, in the heat, in the dark, in the dry. But notice what the resident has done in the centuries since, because it is the strangest and the gentlest thing in the Kiln Ward: *it has kept the city human-shaped.* It grows glass into every failing joint, takes up the load of every sagging lintel, holds the dished stairs at the exact wear-curve the last feet left them — but it does not fill the doorways. It does not overgrow the wall-niches; the basalt hand-axes lie where they were set down, ringed by growth that stops a finger-width short of touching them. Every passage in the ward remains passable at the height of a walking person. Maybe that is only thermodynamics — open channels move air, air moves heat. Maybe a shape held for centuries is just habit written in glass. Or maybe the resident, in its slow vegetable way, maintains the rooms the way you might keep a dead friend's chair by the fire: not in hope, exactly. In fidelity to a form.

A *ward*, after all, is also someone in your keeping.

---

It knows you were there. Not as you know things — it has no heartbeat to quicken, no eye to turn. But you came in burning at thirty-seven degrees in a chamber that reads every degree, and your sweat flashed off you in little plumes of cooling, and for the hour you stood on the swept floor you were the most interesting weather the Kiln Ward had felt in a long time. The flowers that ticked open while you listened — count which way they faced. The leaves on the floor-level stems will have turned a few degrees by morning, toward the warm ghost of where you stood, and turned back, finding nothing, in a week or so.

And check the tread of your boot before you climb out through the seam. One glassy seed, hard as a bead, riding the warmth of you up toward the cold surface — flung, like all its siblings, as far as the body could throw it. Almost certainly it will land somewhere it cannot live. Almost all of them do. The resident has time, and heat, and no one left to talk to, and it talks anyway, all day, in cracks and ticks and the long breath of the rock: a single slow life the size of a courtyard, holding a city open in the shape of its absent half.
