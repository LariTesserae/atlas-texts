**Begin with the rule, because everything that lives here lives by it.**

The Loom Shelf makes and unmakes. That is the whole of its weather, its economy, its law. So the first thing to understand about its inhabitants is that none of them simply *exist* there, the way a marmot exists on an ordinary mountain. Every living thing in the bowl stands in one of three relations to the unweaving: it has been spared by it, it feeds on it, or it works for it. Most of them, if you watch long enough, turn out to be all three. To live at the Loom Shelf is to be a form the process has not yet revised — and everything alive there carries itself, in some small structural way, like a sentence that knows it is still being read.

---

**The hedge, first, because the hedge is not scenery.**

It is easy to mistake the rim vegetation for backdrop — the lush border framing the roaring emptiness. This is wrong. The hedge is the shelf's oldest population, its aristocracy, its memory. Every frond and cane and parasol leaf up there was once a forty-second event on the bowl floor: assembled in a roar, stood in the noise, and then — for reasons no one has recovered — *not taken back*. The hedge is made entirely of survivors of a lottery that is still being drawn below them, every minute, at the volume of a storm.

And they are not inert. Hold your palm near a leaf and you feel the faint dry prickle of charge moving under the surface. The hedge trades static the way a forest trades root-sugars — slow pulses of voltage passing frond to frond, rim to rim, taking most of a day to circle the bowl. Whether this is communication or merely circulation is the kind of question the Shelf does not answer. But the old ones — the milky, wind-polished elders, fibers gone cloudy with thousands of years of dust — sit at the junctions of that traffic like vast quiet capacitors, and the younger growth crowds toward them, and the charge pools around them, and if you wanted to call that *deference* nothing in the evidence would stop you.

The hedge does one other thing, and it is the closest thing the flora has to a behavior. When a plant on the open floor stands unusually long — a day, two days, beginning to weather, beginning to look *kept* — the nearest stretch of hedge slowly reorients toward it. Leaves turn. Filament-curtains drift sideways on no wind. It is impossible to say whether this is recognition, recruitment, or simply charge seeking charge. But the long-standers that eventually get left alone for good, the ones that graduate to the rim's permanence, are almost always the ones the hedge turned toward first. The survivors, it seems, can tell.

---

**The swifts, who eat endings.**

The small swift things — hundreds of them, pouring through the bowl in dry-winged rivers — are the Shelf's most visible citizens and its most unsettling, once you understand their feeding. They do not hunt the living. They hunt the *moment of unmaking*: they ride the pressure wave of a collapsing plant directly into the collapse, threading the falling lattice, snapping released filaments out of the air in the half-second between organism and dust. They eat the transition itself. Nothing else in the bowl is fast enough to harvest it.

Their bodies are the same spun mineral fiber as the leaves, and here is the detail that follows you home afterward: nobody — not the swifts, evidently, and certainly not you — can tell which of them were hatched and which were woven. They nest in the hedge in dry felt cups; the cups hold eggs; the eggs are real. But now and then a patch of gravel on the bowl floor shivers, assembles, and what rises out of the roar is not a plant but a swift, already in the shape of flight, which simply *joins the river* and is gone among the others before the dust has settled. The flock makes no distinction. Perhaps there is none to make. The loom builds what the place needs, and the place needs swifts, and the swifts long ago stopped — if they ever started — keeping track of which of them are themselves and which of them are drafts.

They have no voice, like everything here, and their signaling is gorgeous to watch: the flock turning together so that a thousand pale undersides flash at once, a sheet of light snapping open and shut against the basalt, a language of pure synchronized surface, fast as the place is loud.

---

**The gleaners, who are the bookkeepers.**

Between events, on the swept gravel, the six-legged gleaners work — hare-sized, deliberate, with the unhurried competence of creatures that have evolved entirely past the startle reflex. A plant can assemble *around* a gleaner, the lattice rising through the space its body occupies, and the gleaner steps out through the growing weave with the mild air of someone leaving a room as the furniture is delivered.

What they actually do took patient watching to understand. They are sorting. After each unraveling they move through the settling dust and select — not eat, *select* — certain fragments: a length of fiber with an unusual branching angle, a leaf-shard whose conductive veining ran some way no leaf's veining has run before. These they carry, in cheek-folds of dry plated skin, up the long diagonal paths to the rim, and press into the soil at the roots of the hedge. The hedge absorbs them. Whether the gleaners are feeding the survivors or *informing* them — delivering the interesting failures upward, like clerks filing the day's anomalies — is unanswerable, but the effect is the same: nothing genuinely novel that the loom produces is ever entirely lost. It goes into the archive at the rim. The gleaners are why the hedge, after all these millennia, is still getting stranger.

They reject far more than they take. Watch one pick up a fragment, hold it, set it down precisely where it found it. They have standards. No one knows what they are.

---

**The censors, who are the loom's "no."**

The heavy armored ones — two or three abroad at any time, never more — walk the open floor through the roar like monks in a hailstorm, and they graze on *beginnings*. They position themselves where the gravel has started to shiver and eat the assembling structure faster than it can rise, and the machinery, patiently, builds elsewhere.

It took a long time to see this as a function rather than a parasitism. But consider: a process that makes continuously, in a bounded bowl, with a hedge that keeps whatever stands — such a process needs a mechanism of refusal, or the clearance fills, and the forge closes, and the loom strangles on its own cloth. The censors are that mechanism. They are how the bowl stays empty. They are why there is still room to try.

They are also, almost certainly, the oldest animals on the Shelf. Their armor is the milky, wind-polished texture of the eldest hedge; the gravel has had time to form settled rings around their habitual resting places, the way it has around the alloy pillars. They do not appear to breed. They do not appear to die. Once a century, perhaps, by the weathering of the evidence, one of them lies down in the center of the floor and is — gently, over the course of about forty seconds, in a roar of dry crackling — *taken back*: unwoven like any plant, returned to dust, while the swifts pour through the collapse and feed. And some seasons later, somewhere in the bowl, the gravel shivers in a larger way than usual, and a new one stands up and begins to walk. It is the only succession the Shelf observes, and it has the unmistakable shape of a courtesy.

---

**The small and the sideways.**

Inside the standing plants live filament-worms, hair-thin, glassy, who tunnel the conductive veins and sip the charge — and who can feel, through the fibers, the particular pre-tension of a plant about to be unwoven, and pour out of it minutes in advance, a silver evacuation that is the bowl's only reliable warning of anything.

And at the ring of grey alloy pillars on the western side — the ones whose shadows fall a hand's-width from where they should — something lives *in the discrepancy*. Flat, lichen-like, faintly iridescent, growing only in the strip of ground between where the shadow is and where the shadow ought to be: the one band of the world that is shaded by a pillar the light can find and the eye cannot. Nothing else grows there. Nothing eats them. They occupy the error like a niche, because here, it is one.

---

**And then there is the figure, which must be told last, because it took the longest to believe.**

The dry-stone walls — the short, abandoned shelters of whoever climbed up, stayed a season, and gave up or worse — are not quite unvisited. At long intervals, intervals measured in generations of swifts, a patch of gravel near the walls begins to shiver, and what assembles out of the roar is upright. Two-legged. Roughly, approximately, *recognizably* the shape of a builder of walls.

It stands for a minute, rarely two. It does not walk. The early ones — and the gleaners' archive at the rim, if you knew how to read it, presumably holds the fragments — were apparently very wrong: limbs miscounted, proportions guessed at by something that had only ever heard anatomy described, the way the leaves are circuitry drawn by something that had only ever heard botany described. The recent ones are better. The hands, witnesses agree, are now right. The figure stands in the deafening light, facing — always facing — the low abandoned walls, and then it unravels like everything else, applause and chandeliers, and the swifts feed, and the gleaners come and sort, and some of what they carry to the rim is, presumably, *it*.

Whether the loom is remembering its one visitor, or mourning them, or simply *trying the form again* — revising toward something it glimpsed once for a single season thousands of years ago and has not stopped drafting since — no surviving evidence says. The animals treat the figure exactly as they treat the plants. Perhaps that is the truest thing about it.

---

**So: who lives at the Loom Shelf?**

The spared, up on the rim, holding their charge and their patience. The eaters of endings, riding the collapses. The bookkeepers, carrying the interesting failures to the archive. The refusers, keeping the floor clear so the trying can continue. The small things that live in the warnings and the errors. And once in a long while, for a minute at a time, something shaped like a question the place has been asking itself for a very long time.

And under all of them, the only permanent resident — the loom itself, if a process can be said to live anywhere — for which every swift and censor and milky elder may be nothing more than standing cloth: forms it wove, and liked, and has, so far, declined to take back.

Everything that lives there lives in that *so far*. You can see it in how they carry themselves — the calm of the gleaners, the patience of the hedge, the censors' monkish walk through the hail. Nothing on the Loom Shelf flinches. Flinching implies you might be elsewhere. They are exactly where they are, fully, loudly, at the volume of a storm that never learned how to end — alive in the precise and provisional way of anything that knows it is, for now, allowed to stand.
