The census of the Drowned Cordillera is short. You could write it on one hand: the green, the mats, the glitter, the grazer, the far swimmer, and the small nations of the turf. But each entry runs deep, and the first one runs deepest, so begin there — with the resident that owns the place, and reorganize your sense of the word *inhabitant* as you go.

---

## The Green

The forests are not the habitat. That is the mistake the eye makes on the first day and unlearns slowly afterward. The forests are the inhabitant — possibly *the* inhabitant, singular — and the mountains are what they live in.

Pull at the question of how many of them there are and it comes apart in your hands. A drowned valley holds what looks like ten thousand trunks of braided green, but the trunks rise out of a continuous turf, and the turf is continuous with the trunks, and where one colony ends and another begins is not a line anything here has bothered to draw. The likeliest answer — the one the structure of the place keeps suggesting — is that each valley's forest is a small number of very large individuals, or one, clonal and braided, a single body wearing ten thousand columns the way a wave wears whitecaps. Wound a blade at one end of a cirque and there are reports — slow reports, days in the arriving — written in pigment at the other end: a flush of deeper red moving through the turf like a thought taking its time.

So the green has behaviors, if you let the word stretch. It tracks the sun — the whole canopy of a valley turning together through the day, too slowly to watch, too uniformly to be accident. It holds the ruled line at the surface with absolute discipline: not one blade, anywhere in the whole cordillera, has ever broken into the air. This is not inability. The canopy lies a hand's depth under the bright ceiling, pressed against it, tiled against it, and stops. Whatever the air did to its ancestors, or never did, the refusal is total and looks almost principled.

And it climbs. The growth front advancing up the bare black rock — a few centimeters a year, the pace of a fingernail — is the green's one campaign. There is no urgency in it because there is no competition. The summits will be taken. The only question is the century.

The green talks to itself, or at least the chemistry of the place behaves as though it does. The decades-stable currents that thread the valleys carry more than warmth: a forest upstream blooms a certain amber, and weeks later, valleys away, downstream forests answer in kind, a season arriving by water rather than by sun. Whether this is conversation or only a weather of molecules, the place keeps its own counsel. But spend long enough in the green-gold dimness under the canopies and the distinction starts to feel like yours, not theirs.

What the green does not do is notice. It has no reactions to spare for anything on the animal scale of time. Swim through it for a season and you will register on it exactly as much as a single afternoon registers on a glacier. This is not coldness. It is a difference in clock so total that *attention* stops being a meaningful demand.

## The Mats: The First Tenants

In the hot seams, where the water shimmers over the fissures and the bubbles rise in single file, there is an older lineage. Pale mats — white shading to faint sulfur-yellow, soft as wet felt — lining the heated cracks down where even the frantic seam-gardens thin out. They do not eat light. They eat the mountain: the chemistry of the young rock's breath, the dissolved heat-stuff coming up through the plumbing.

They were here first. They had to be — they are the kind of life that needs nothing but a planet's interior leaking into water, and this place has been leaking since before the green arrived, or evolved, or simply happened. The forests above them are, from the mats' position, a recent and irrelevant development: a gaudy newcomer dependent on a sun the mats have never required. If the light failed — if the bright ceiling went permanently dark — every blade and column in the cordillera would die, the glitter and the grazer and the far swimmer after them, and the mats would continue exactly as they are, the last tenants as they were the first, keeping the range's oldest relationship: the one between the water and its own heat.

They are also, in their unglamorous way, the foundation. The sweetness in the water's taste — that not-quite-mineral note the young rock is still deciding — passes through the mats on its way to becoming the green. The whole bright country upstairs is, distantly, their work.

## The Glitter

The small transparent swimmers are the cordillera's travelers — the only residents for whom the geography is an itinerary rather than an address.

Seen poorly, which is the only way they can be seen, a school is a patch of organized distortion: a region of water, a meter or two across, in which the light bends in coordination. Each animal is a finger-length of near-nothing — a gut, a faint silver thread of nerve, a pair of eyes like pinheads, and around all of it a body the water shines straight through. They are visible the way a flaw in glass is visible. Lose sight of a school and you have lost it; you find it again by accident or not at all.

They live on the fine economy of the place: the oxygen-bead films on the filament-curtains, drifting spores, the slow snow of shed canopy tissue. Thin fare, and they live thinly on it — there may be only a few schools to a valley, and a valley is a country.

But it is their travel that matters. The glitter moves between valleys along the roads of light — the drowned ridges lying just under the surface, glowing their startling mineral green. A school will rise out of one valley's canopy, cross a sunlit saddle in open formation, exposed for an hour in water bright as air, and descend into the next forest. Why they cross at all is not obvious; the valleys are not different enough to be worth the journey. Unless the journeys are not theirs. The green's spores ride on them — caught in the faint stickiness of their skins, ferried valley to valley along the bright roads. The glitter may believe, insofar as a silver thread of nerve believes anything, that it is following food. It is also the postal service of a forest, and has been for as long as both have existed, and neither party has ever been consulted.

They never enter the deep blue of the open valleys. The roads or the canopies, nothing else. Whatever the open water means to them, they have voted on it unanimously.

## The Grazer

The pale, slow, forearm-long thing in the hot seams is the cordillera's only animal of leisure.

It is soft-bodied, unhurried, and very nearly blind — its eyes reduced to a scatter of light-sensitive freckles, because in water this clear, at noon brightnesses that reach the valley floors, there is nothing it needs to see. It distinguishes turf from not-turf, warm from warmer, and that is its entire visual philosophy. It moves along the heated fissures rasping at the fleshy seam-colonies, which grow faster than it can eat them. It is the only resident of the range that lives in surplus, and it shows: there is no caution in it, no speed held in reserve, nothing in its design that has ever needed to flee or chase. It is what an animal becomes when nothing has hunted its lineage for the whole of its history. Approach it and it continues grazing. It does not lack a response to you; it lacks a category.

There may be very few of them — a few dozen, perhaps, in the entire drowned country — and they may be very old, on the scale of the long-lived things, decades into centuries, each one alone on its own seam for most of its life. How they ever meet is one of the place's quiet logistics. The best guess is the currents again: a chemical sentence released into the decades-stable circulation, read months later and valleys away, and a grazer turns from its seam and begins a journey that takes years, navigating by warmth, crossing the floors of drowned cirques at the pace of resting heartbeat, toward a rendezvous arranged by water. Whether they arrive in time, whether the other waited, whether *waiting* is even the word for what a grazer does — the place doesn't say. But there are young ones, occasionally, small and paler still, so the system works, at whatever rate it works.

## The Far Swimmer

Then there is the shape at the limit of clarity, and here the census stops being confident.

It lives in the one habitat everything else refuses: the open volumes, the empty deep blue of the drowned valleys, the water between. It is large — genuinely large, whale-scaled in the glimpses, though the misbehaving distances of this water make every estimate a confession of doubt. Large because slow, most likely: a body built to bank its metabolism like coals, warmed for free by the blood-adjacent water, spending almost nothing, because almost nothing is what the open blue has to offer. It filters as it goes — spore-fall, drift-tissue, the rare dead of the glitter — a diet so thin that the animal is less a hunter than a kind of patience with fins.

It circuits. That is the strongest impression the sightings leave: not wandering but *rounds* — a route through the range, over the deep saddles, valley to valley, a circuit that may take years to close. If that is true, then the far swimmer is the only living thing that has seen the whole cordillera: every cirque, every column-forest, every road of light from below. The green uses it, of course, the way the green uses everything that moves — spores ride its skin in slow gardens of their own, and a forest may owe its founding, generations back, to one pass of that enormous unhurried shadow.

How many there are is unanswerable. Sightings come years apart, from valleys far separated, and the descriptions agree suspiciously well. It may be a sparse population. It may be three. It may be one — a single animal, decades or centuries old, swimming the same long loop through warm blue emptiness, the entire open-water fauna of a mountain range compressed into one unhurried body. The water's clarity, which shows you everything, will not settle this; the far swimmer lives precisely at the depth of blue where clarity becomes rumor, and it has never once, in any account, come closer.

It does not surface. The bright ceiling is its sky, and it has no more business there than you have in yours.

## The Small Nations of the Turf

By number, almost everything alive in the cordillera lives in the turf and never travels a body-length.

Part the dense purple-black lawn on any valley floor and there is an interior: a millimeter-scaled fauna of grazers and burrowers and filter-fans, translucent specks with too many legs or none, things that spend entire lives inside one handsbreadth of turf the way other creatures inhabit continents. They are the decomposing crew — the slow snow of shed canopy blade falls into the turf and they unmake it, and what they unmake feeds the lawn that houses them, and the loop closes in a space smaller than your palm.

They are the only residents with ordinary lifespans — days, weeks — and so they are where the cordillera keeps its quick time. All the speed the place is missing at the visible scale is down here, hidden in the cloth that covers the mountains, billions of brief urgent lives proceeding at a pace the green above them would not register as motion at all.

## What Doesn't Live Here

The air belongs to no one. The black rock above the ruled line is the emptiest habitat in the range — barer than the open blue, barer than the deep clefts. No lichen, no stain of algae, nothing: the rock is too young, the wind too scouring, the line too absolute. The wind crosses the whole cordillera without passengers.

Once in a long while something arrives anyway — an insect blown up from a green country beyond the horizon, a seed that has crossed unthinkable distance to land on the one surface in the world that cannot grow it. The water holds it on the bright ceiling like an exhibit. Nothing rises to take it; there is nothing here that knows what it is. Eventually it waterlogs and sinks, and becomes a flake of the snow that feeds the turf, and in that sense — and that sense only — the cordillera accepts immigrants.

And you don't live here either. You feel that within the first hour, and the feeling never softens: you are the only thing in the whole drowned country operating at your speed, and the residents arrange themselves around that fact by ignoring it completely. The green will not notice you. The grazer cannot. The glitter will pass through the canopy gap on its own schedule whether you are hanging there or not. You can stay a season and leave no mark — the place has never held a mark — and the only record of your visit will be the spores that leave on your skin, the green making one more use of one more thing that moved.

## The Question of One

Hang long enough in the sunlit middle of a drowned valley and the census starts to want simplifying. The mats feed the water; the water feeds the green; the green feeds the snow; the snow feeds the turf and the glitter and, thinly, the far swimmer; the glitter and the swimmer carry the green to new rock; the grazer keeps the seams in trim. Pull on any resident and all the others come with it. It may be truest to say that the Drowned Cordillera has one inhabitant — a single slow composite organism, green at its surfaces, pale at its heat, glittering at its edges, with one large dark thought circling its open interior — and that this inhabitant's single ongoing act is the one you can feel on all sides at once: growing toward light, too slowly to see, too thoroughly to doubt.

Or it may simply be a short list of patient strangers, sharing warm water, owing each other everything, acquainted not at all.

The place, as ever, keeps its own counsel. The forests turn with the sun either way.
