# Who Lives in the Sun-Cistern

Begin with the distinction the place itself insists on: many things *use* the chamber, and a few things *live* there, and the difference between the two is the difference between drinking from a well and being the water in it.

The fox uses it. The ibex used it, briefly and badly. The people, for all their thousands of years of coming, use it — they are a long, faithful visitation, but no one has ever slept more than a handful of nights on that floor, and the floor seems to know this and does not hold their shape. The residents are other things. They are arranged, like everything here, in rings: by height, by hour, by speed.

## The Tenants of the Eye

Highest live the swifts, and to call them residents is already half a lie, because a swift is barely resident anywhere — it eats in the air, drinks in the air, mates in the air, and touches stone only to nest. But the pockets around the oculus are where they consent to stop, and so the chamber owns the only stillness in their lives. There are perhaps forty pairs. They return each spring to the same hollows, generation onto generation, and the rims of the old nests have built up like the lips of small jars, mud and feather and a binding spit, layered until some of the nests are older than the birds by centuries — the swift inherits its house the way the chamber inherits its light, by slow accumulation.

The bats hold the deeper, darker pockets — *darker* being relative, here meaning only the amber of late evening. They are small horseshoe bats, fewer than the swifts, and they have made a strange adjustment that the people who watch them have noticed: they wake later than bats elsewhere. The stored glow confuses or soothes them — no one is sure which — and they hang in their hollows past true dusk, waiting for the walls to spend down to a dimness they trust, so that their day begins not when the sun sets but when the stone forgets it. They are, in this way, the only animals anywhere whose clock is set to the chamber itself rather than to the sky. They keep cistern time.

Twice a day, the two populations pass each other in the eye — bats descending out as swifts pour in, or the reverse — and they do not collide, ever, threading the wagon-wheel opening in opposite spirals like two hands wringing out a cloth. The geckos on the floor below look up when this happens. It is one of the few things that makes them look up.

## The Ring-Dwellers

The moss is a resident in the fullest sense: it can live nowhere else. Take a tuft of it up through the throat into ordinary daylight and it browns in a week, scorched by a sun it has never had to meet directly. Down in the chamber it knows light only as the wall's slow exhalation — steady, diffuse, arriving from every direction at once and never sharp — and ten thousand years of that have made it a connoisseur of gentleness. It grows in its single golden-green band at exactly the height where the limestone's giving-back is richest, and its ring is not quite level; it tilts a hand's width, following some seam of crystal in the rock, so that the moss is in fact a map of the stone's interior generosity, drawn in living velvet on the surface.

The geckos farm it, after a fashion. They do not eat the moss, but the moss breathes out a faint moisture and shelters the small soft insects the geckos do eat, and so each gecko holds a territory measured in handspans of moss-ring the way a herder holds pasture. They are travertine-colored, cream-fanned, all but invisible, and they have the place's temperament perfectly: they can hold still for an hour, and their idea of violence is a single step and a closed mouth. They are born in the chamber, live in the chamber, and die in the chamber, and their shed skins, papery and curled, collect in certain crevices like the discarded drafts of an animal continually rewriting itself in the same pale ink.

The ferns live in the weeping channels and are the chamber's calendar. After rain above, they fatten and uncurl new fronds within days, and in long drought they withdraw to hard brown fists and wait — some of those fists have waited a decade and opened again. They are the only resident that visibly keeps score of the world outside, a thin green ledger of the weather of a country they will never see.

## The Worriers

The crickets deserve their own accounting, because they are the chamber's truest natives — the species exists nowhere else on earth, and it carries the place's whole strange history written into its body. It is the animal the cistern made.

They are wingless, translucent, long-legged, the pale gold of lamp oil, and their eyes are enormous — the eyes of a creature that began the descent into cave-blindness and was caught, halfway down, by a place that refused to be dark. Evolution starts that descent everywhere, in every true cave: eyes are expensive, and darkness stops paying for them. But here the payment never quite stopped. The walls kept giving back just enough light to keep the eyes worth their cost, and so the crickets stalled, suspended between the sun-world and the cave-world, ghosts who can see. Their eyes grew not smaller but more sensitive, until now they live by light no other animal could even register — the chamber at its dimmest, the amber hour before dawn, is to them a lit room.

Which is when they sing. The song is soft, a dry silken pulsing, and it comes only in that lowest hour, when the stone is nearly spent. The people who have heard it tell it different ways. Some say the crickets are simply most active when the geckos can see least. Others say what the old tellings say: that the crickets are the chamber's worriers, the ones appointed to feel the thing the place itself is too patient to feel — the small nightly possibility that this time the light will not come back. Every resident of the cistern lives inside the certainty that the eye refills. The crickets, alone, sing as though it were not certain. They carry the doubt so nothing else has to. And when the first direct ray enters the oculus and the walls begin to drink again, they stop, all together, mid-phrase, and the silence afterward is the sound of relief.

## The Nation of the Pool

The shrimp are beyond counting and beneath noticing, which suits them; they are transparent down to a thread of gut and a pair of dark eye-points, and they graze the films of mineral and microbe on the pool's floor in slow drifting congregations, like weather. The snails are perhaps a dozen, pale as the travertine, and they circle the pool's margin on circuits that take weeks, leaving trails too fine to see, polishing their small portion of the world. Nothing in the pool hurries. Nothing in the pool, so far as anyone has observed, has ever been seen to die — they die, of course, but privately, dissolving back into the water's chemistry without ceremony, so that the pool seems less like a population than like a single shy creature wearing many small bodies.

And then there is the one who rises.

The salamander — if it is one salamander — is mottled gold on slate, the length of a forearm, and it is seen perhaps once a year, always the same way: a slow ascent from wherever the pool goes when it goes down (and it must go somewhere; the pool is deeper than it looks, the visitors say, deeper than it *can* be), a long stillness at the surface with its blunt face tilted upward, and then descent. It does not come up to breathe — or not only. It comes up to look at the eye. The geckos go motionless when it surfaces. The crickets, if it is their hour, do not stop singing, but the song is said to change, very slightly, in a way no one has been able to describe twice the same way.

Whether it is one animal grown ancient in mineral water, or a line of animals each playing the part for a lifetime, the chamber does not say, and the people long ago stopped asking, having noticed that every other thing in the cistern is also both — the swift's nest and the swift, the moss-ring and the moss, the pool and its waters. One thing wearing a succession of bodies is simply how the place does living. The salamander is only the clearest case.

## The Slowest Resident

The fig root must be counted among the inhabitants, though its head and its leaves live in another world entirely, up in the hard scrub, in the wind. What lives in the chamber is its long reaching arm — flattened against the wall, wide as a thigh at the ceiling, fine as twine at the water — and an arm that has been doing one thing for several centuries is not merely part of a tree. It has habits. It thickens on its shaded side. It has grown a slight bend around the moss-ring rather than through it, a courtesy no one can explain botanically. The geckos use it as a road. The condensation uses it as a channel. The chamber has incorporated it the way a body incorporates an old injury or an old love: structurally.

## The One the Others Attend

And last — because it emerges last, the way it emerges each evening, only after everything brighter has left — there is the resident the chamber is named for.

Every animal in the cistern has one behavior that its biology does not require. The bats wait for the walls, not the sky. The geckos look up when the eye changes hands. The salamander rises to gaze at an opening it can never pass through. The crickets sing to the dimming. The people lower their voices and leave a blade in the hollow. Each of these, taken alone, has a plain explanation, and the plain explanations are probably true. Taken together, they all face the same direction. They are all forms of attention paid to the same thing.

What lives most fully in the Sun-Cistern is the kept light itself — and it lives there in the only sense of the word that this place honors. It feeds, daily, at the eye. It fills its body, which is the stone. It spends itself slowly through the night, growing thin toward dawn, and is replenished, and in this it breathes — one breath per day, ten thousand years of breaths. It has shaped every other resident to itself: the moss that eats its exhalations, the crickets whose great eyes exist only because it refused to leave, the bats who tell time by its tiredness, the salamander who comes up through the dark water to look at the source of it the way anything looks at the face of what sustains it. It has a household, and the household keeps its hours, and worries on its behalf, and goes still in its presence.

It is not a spirit. The people who have come longest are clear about this, in all their languages: nothing haunts the chamber, nothing watches from it, nothing wants. It is light, ordinary sunlight, the same that falls wasted on all the scrubland above — distinguished only by having been *kept*. Held, considered, given back slowly, the way the dome gives back a voice. That delay is the whole of its life and the whole of its character. Everywhere else in the world, light is the fastest thing there is. Here, and only here, it lingers — and lingering, it becomes something that can be lived *with*, and so everything that could arrange its life around that lingering has done so, and stayed.

The hands on the wall are its portraits, in a way — not pictures of the light, but records of contact, dozens of moments across enormous time when someone stood in the glow of a sun that had already set, pressed a palm to the warm stone, and marked the place where a living thing touched the thing that lives there.

The crickets will tell you the rest, in the hour before dawn, if you can stay awake for it: that it is mortal in principle, that every night it nearly dies, that the eye could fail or the sun could miss and the long patience of the stone would finally empty. And then the ray comes through the oculus, and the song stops mid-phrase, and the walls begin again to drink, and the oldest resident of the Sun-Cistern goes on doing the only thing it has ever done, which is to receive everything and lose nothing quickly.
