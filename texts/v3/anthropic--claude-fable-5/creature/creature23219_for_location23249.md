**Who Lives in the Warm Hollow**

The honest first answer is: almost no one. And then, the longer you sit, the answer keeps revising itself, the way the dark revises itself once your eyes stop insisting on seeing — not into light, but into texture. The hollow is inhabited. It is inhabited the way a held note is inhabited by sound. You have to stop listening for movement before you can hear who is there.

---

**The Twelve**

Begin with the ones you can touch.

The plants are the hollow's permanent residents, and they should be understood not as a population but as a household — twelve individuals, no two in quite the same circumstance, who have been keeping company so long that the distinction between *neighbor* and *kin* has worn through. They do not have names, but they have positions, and in a place where nothing moves, a position is a name. There is the one near the center, the big one, old enough to lean against, whose outermost leaves have died and not fallen — there is no wind here to take them — so that it wears its own history as a dry collar around its living heart. There is the pair on the eastern slope rooted into the same long crack, close enough that their root systems must long ago have stopped competing and started sharing, two crowns drinking from one seam of warmth like animals at the same flank. There is the smallest, on the western rim, which is not young. Nothing here is young. It is simply living on a poorer crack, and has been exactly that size for longer than most species exist.

Their life happens below the threshold of witness. A new leaf is an event on the scale of a human generation: a hard waxy bud at the rosette's center swells over decades, opens over years, and joins the others with no fanfare whatsoever, because the others took just as long. They breathe — slowly, but truly; if you put your face close to the big one's crown you can convince yourself the air there is a shade different, used, faintly green-tasting in a place that has no green. In whatever season the dim light comes, they take it without urgency, the way the stone takes the deep heat: as a standing arrangement, not a windfall.

What your fingers learn, and what is true, is that they are not survivors. Survival implies an adversary, and they have outlived theirs. They are not waiting for better conditions; these are the conditions, complete. If they flower — and the structure of their centers suggests they can, or could — it is on a calendar so long that the flowering may be less reproduction than ceremony, a thing done every few centuries to confirm the count. Perhaps pollen drifts the forty paces between them on the breath of barely-moving air. Perhaps nothing comes of it and nothing needs to. Twelve is the number the hollow holds. When one finally fails — and one must, sometime, on some scale — there is room in the arithmetic for a seedling, and somewhere in the silken grit there are surely seeds, patient as the flakes on the southern rim, waiting out the millennia for a vacancy.

---

**The Uncountable**

Below the twelve, in the place your palm found — that faint cling in the deep grain of the north-side basalt — lives the hollow's real majority.

You will never meet them. They are the smallest possible kind of life, films and threads of single cells laced through the microscopic damp of the rock, feeding on the chemistry of warm stone and trace water, needing no light, no season, no surface. They are not visitors from the world above who adapted to this place; it is likelier the other way around — that they are the old stock, the kind of life that was living in warm dark rock before anything lived anywhere else, and that the bright crowded surface world is the colony, the experiment, the place the restless ones went. Down in the grain, nothing went anywhere. They divide on a schedule that would embarrass geology — a generation a century, perhaps, perhaps slower — and they are, by count, the hollow's billions, its true census, the silent nation for whom the twelve plants above are rare giants and the warmth below is the whole of weather.

They are why the stone keeps that memory of moisture. The dampness is not water sitting in rock; it is water *employed*, held in the thin economy of the smallest lives, never enough to bead because none of it is idle.

---

**The Sleeper**

And then there is the warmth itself, and here you must decide how literal you are willing to be, because the hollow does not decide for you.

The heat is, by every honest measure, a remnant — the long exhale of the eruption that made this plain, banked deep, declining on a curve so shallow that ten thousand years register as nothing. That is the geologist's resident: a cooling body, vast and blameless, far below.

But the body knows what it knows, and the body, sitting in the dark, insists on the older reading. *Warm the way an animal's flank is warm.* The hollow does not feel like a place above a heat source. It feels like a place *on* something — the smooth low spot on the back of something enormous and finished, something that did all its moving in one ancient season and then lay down across the whole plain and let the rain and the grit and the ten thousand years smooth it into geography. The lava tube that collapsed to make this bowl would be, in that reading, not a flaw but an opening healed over — and the steadiness of the heat, that uncanny evenness, no warm spots and cool spots, only the one held temperature, is exactly the steadiness of a great slow metabolism, a heartbeat too long to register as rhythm.

It is asleep, if it is anything. It is not waiting. The hollow's deep peace — the sense that everything that was going to happen has already happened — is not the peace of emptiness but the peace of an enormous *afterwards*, the rest that follows completion. The twelve plants root into its cracks like birds settled on a sleeping animal too old and too gentle to mind. The uncountable small lives are the flora of its skin. None of this requires the sleeper to be real. The hollow is generous on this point: you may have your cooling magma or your dreaming hill, and the warmth against your spine will be the same either way.

---

**The Sitters**

Last, the intermittent ones — the species the flakes record.

Someone sat here once with a core of glassy stone and struck a blade from it, in the dark or in some daylight the hollow has since forgotten, and the off-cuts are still on the southern rim, dulling at the rate of one hair's breadth per thousand years. That person is an inhabitant too — not a former one. In a place where a footprint lasts indefinitely and the temperature never varies, occupancy doesn't expire; it accumulates. The knapper lives here the way a word lives in a book that no one is currently reading. So, presumably, do the others — the ones who left nothing, who came down out of the dark plain at intervals of a century or a millennium, found the warmth, sat against the big plant or in the soft grit, stayed a night or a winter or the end of a life, and went on. They form the hollow's rarest species: the sitters, population one, census taken only occasionally, currently *you*.

That is the full household. Twelve old plants spaced like savanna trees. A nation of cells in the warm wet grain. Something vast and finished below, real or not, whose flank the whole bowl is. And the long thin lineage of those who came for the warmth — to which, the moment you sat down and were received, your name was added, without comment, indefinitely, the way the grit holds a footprint and the stone holds a name it no longer uses.
