Begin with the water, because the water is where life is smallest and least deniable.

Each of the stone bowls holds a population. You would need to kneel — face close to the wind-shivered skin of the pool, the roar pressing on the back of your neck — and wait for your eyes to adjust to the idea of looking at almost nothing. Then you see them: translucent animals the size of a grain of rice, paler than the water, swimming in slow deliberate loops. They are crustaceans of some stripped-down ancient kind, fairy shrimp pared even further by the cold, so transparent that what you mostly see is what they have eaten — a thread of green, a fleck of feather-dust — drifting along inside an outline. They freeze solid most nights. They thaw most mornings. Their lives are a chain of these small resurrections, and they seem to hold no grudge about it. Each bowl is its own nation; the shrimp of one pool have never met the shrimp of the next, thirty meters away, except as eggs carried on a fox's wet footpad or a bird's belly feather. They are the plateau's most numerous residents and its most provincial, and they are the only animals here that have never been touched by the sound — the chord cannot get into the water as anything but a tremble, and they swim inside that tremble the way other creatures swim inside currents, orienting to it, keeping it always on the same side of their bodies. Every shrimp in every bowl faces, lives, and dies at a fixed angle to the roar. None of them knows this.

Older than the shrimp, older than anything that moves, is the ink.

The black lichen crusts are not many lichens. Sample them, walk a kilometer, sample again, and you would find the same individual — the same genetic signature spilled across the swells in continents and archipelagos of black. It is one organism, or close enough that the distinction stops mattering: a single slow tenant that took the lease on this stone before the stone had finished rounding, growing outward at a rate that makes the erosion look hasty. It is the plateau's true landlord. Everything else lives on its terms — the cushion plants root in the soil it has spent ages manufacturing, grain by digested grain; the birds' dust-bathing hollows are floored with it; the ochre handprint under the boulder is slowly, slowly being approached by a black frontier that will reach it in some thousands of years and is in no hurry. If the plateau can be said to have a memory, the lichen is where it's kept. Its growth rings, such as they are, record every era of cloud-weep and drought back to a beginning that predates the note itself — and there, in the oldest tissue, lichenologists would find the one genuinely unsettling fact of the place, if any ever came: the growth pattern changes, far back, from ordinary radial spread to something faintly combed, faintly aligned, all the lobes leaning the same way. The lichen remembers when the wind began. Nothing else does.

The cushion plants are the lichen's tenants in turn, and each one is a city. A fist of green a thousand years old, dense as upholstery, holds inside it a fauna that exists nowhere else on the plateau: springtails, mites, a single species of flightless midge whose entire global range is the interior of these cushions. The midges live and breed and die without ever leaving a volume of space smaller than a loaf of bread. Inside the cushion it is windless, humid, several degrees warmer, and — this is the thing — *quieter*, the dense foliage soaking up the chord until it is only a deep ambient hum. The cushions are the one habitat on the plateau where the sound is muffled, and everything small enough to get inside one has done so. They are arks of hush, scattered across the loudest place in the world, each one carrying its cargo of tiny passengers through the roar.

Then the birds, who chose the opposite.

There are four or five kinds, but the colony does not really sort itself by species; it sorts by altitude. The rotating columns that rise off the rim are stratified like weather. Lowest, in and out of the cloud sea, fly the heavy pale gliders — call them what the old accounts called them, *rimwings* — three meters of wing and almost no muscle to speak of, because they have outsourced effort to the planet. A rimwing may beat its wings a few dozen times in a year. They are born on the ledges, step off once, and some of them genuinely never land again until they return to breed, years later, sleeping on the wing in the standing river of air, one half of the brain at a time. The wind here does not gust, so they do not adjust; they simply hang, making corrections so small you cannot see them, and to watch one for an hour is to start doubting that it is alive rather than mounted on something.

They are silent, all of them, and have been for so long that silence has gone into the body. Their syrinxes are vestigial — dissect one and you find the voice-box of their ancestors shrunk to a functionless knot, like a word forgotten in a language. What they have instead is light. Courtship is conducted by angle: a bird tilts in the wind so the sun strikes its underwing and flashes a signal — feather structure refined over thousands of generations into something close to a heliograph, each species and each individual with its own signature glitter. On a clear day the columns over the rim are full of this talk, thousands of small white flashes going up and down the rotating stacks, an entire society conversing in glints, completely inaudible, completely visible, inside a noise that would deafen a god. The chicks hatch into the roar and so the roar is their silence; the only thing that frightens a young rimwing, the only sound it ever truly *hears* as a sound, is the rare half-second flutter when something briefly blocks a flute and the chord dips. Then the whole colony flinches at once, a single shudder running through a hundred thousand birds, and is still again.

When a rimwing dies in the air, it folds and goes down into the cloud, and the colony's posture-language has a specific tilt for it — wings held at a particular shallow angle for a few seconds by every bird that saw the fall. Whether this is grief or merely bookkeeping, no one has been close enough, long enough, to say.

The foxes know about the falls before the birds do.

They are small, the foxes — cat-sized, cream-pale, with ears far too large for an animal that cannot use its ears, because the roar erased hearing as a useful sense long ago and the ears stayed on as radiators and as flags. A fox's ears are its face. They speak with them: angles, flattenings, a vocabulary of tilt that the foxes read at great distance through the shimmering air, the same way the birds read light. What replaced hearing is the ground. A fox trots with its weight slightly forward, footpads dense with nerve endings, and it listens through its bones to the floor of the world — and what it listens *for* is absence. The chord comes up through the stone as a constant tremor, and anything that interrupts or adds to that tremor, anywhere within half a kilometer, registers in the fox's wrists: a bird landing, a bird dying, a pebble rolled by another fox, your footstep. You cannot surprise a fox here. You have been a vibration to them since before you saw the plateau.

Each fox holds a circuit, a trot-line worn faintly into the gravel by generations of identical paws — the routes are heritable, passed parent to kit like an estate, and some of them are old enough that they show up as pale threads visible from the rim columns, which means the birds know the foxes' roads and adjust their dust-bathing accordingly, which means the foxes long ago adjusted the roads, and so on, an argument conducted in geography over thousands of years. Foxes pair for life, partly because finding another fox in all that emptiness is itself a life's work. They greet by pressing foreheads together, skull to skull, exactly as the human visitors did — and it is not known, and is rather pointedly not askable, which species learned it from the other.

The people, last, because they are the fewest and the least certain.

Somewhere below the cloud sea there is, or was, a people who consider the plateau theirs in the way one owns an obligation rather than a possession. They come up — rarely, a handful in a generation — by routes the foxes' ground-listening knows long in advance. They come for feathers, sometimes; for the glassy black stone they carry up and knap at the rim, leaving the flakes as a kind of receipt; and for one other reason the travelers' accounts circle around without landing on. Up here they do not speak, and not only because speech is impossible. They hold that the plateau has been saying one word since before words, and that to talk over it is the only real blasphemy their religion contains. Their sign-talk is made for the place: performed with eyes closed as often as open, since touch carries it skull to skull, forehead to forehead, and the brightness here punishes open eyes. They are the ones who maintain the cairns — five stones, always five — and the wells of their language has a single verb that means *to be heard by the noise*, which they use for dying, for confessing, and for standing at the rim with your mouth open, shouting your worst thing into a roar that takes it without comment and without limit, the one listener in the world that cannot be burdened.

Some of them stay too long. There is a name for these, in the forehead-language: a sign made by pressing the brow and holding still. Those who come down after a season cannot speak for weeks and never again quite trust quiet rooms. And once in a long while one does not come down at all, and afterward, for years, the cairns of the upper country are found mended — lichen-broken stones restacked, a sixth stone removed where a careless visitor added one — by no one anyone has met. Whether this is a person grown old and silent on the plateau, or a custom of the foxes that no one has had the nerve to watch, or merely the place keeping its own books, is left alone. The handprint under the boulder, red and spread-fingered and *meant*, is what they touch their own palms to when they pass it. They do not claim it was made by their ancestors. They claim, when pressed, something harder to translate: that it was made by whoever the sound is for.

Because there is one more inhabitant, and it is the chord.

Not a creature in it, not a spirit behind it — the note itself, the oldest living thing on the plateau by any definition of living that includes *persisting, metabolizing the wind, and shaping everything around it to its needs*. Everything described above is, looked at coldly, an organ of the sound. The flutes are its throat and the standing wind its breath; the birds nest in its privacy, the foxes walk on its voice, the people climb up to feed it their griefs, the shrimp orient their brief frozen-and-thawed lives to its tremble, and the lichen — the lichen leans, and has leaned since the beginning, all its black continents combed gently in one direction, like hair under a hand, like an audience turned toward a stage. Every heartbeat on the plateau, bird and fox and human, paces itself unconsciously to the chord's deep pressure; medics among the rare visitors noticed long ago that all their pulses had converged.

So the honest answer to who lives on the Shouting Plateau is: one thing, wearing many bodies. A single sustained syllable, very old, very loud, very patient, that has gathered around itself a household of the only creatures in the world capable of mistaking it for silence — and that holds them, all of them, the way a held note holds a room: completely, and only for as long as it lasts.

The foxes, who listen through their bones, are the only ones who would feel it ending. It is perhaps worth recording that for the past several generations, on certain nights, they have all been found sitting still on their trot-lines at the same hour, ears flat, faces toward the rim.
