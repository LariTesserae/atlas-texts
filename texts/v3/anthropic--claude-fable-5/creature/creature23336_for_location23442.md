**The People of the Held Note**

They call themselves something that cannot be written, because it has never been said. It is a sign: both hands held flat at the sternum, palms in, trembling slightly — the gesture means *the rumble*, and it means *us*, and the people make no distinction between the two. They are the ones who feel the basin in the bone. That is the whole of their name.

Begin with the body, because the basin began there.

They are a short, broad, barefoot people, and the feet come first. No one in the basin has ever worn a sole. The foot is an organ here — splayed, thick-padded, calloused to a pale horn at heel and ball but kept exquisitely tender along the inner arch, which is the listening part. A basin-dweller reads the ground the way other people read a face: the fine fizz of a healthy runoff channel, the slow muscular churn of a great pool transmitted through forty feet of young stone, the particular grain of travertine laid down last week versus last year. Children learn the floor of their district before they learn its lanes. A blindfolded elder set down anywhere in the city can name the street by the sole of the foot, and most could name the season the street was last scraped. When two old friends meet, they do not only sign; they stand close and shift their weight, and each feels the other's standing through the stone, and this too is conversation.

Their skin carries the basin on it. Years of mineral water and sulfur air leave the hands and forearms faintly enameled — a smoothness, a pale gloss along the knuckles, as if the body itself had begun, very slowly, to accrete. The old people's palms are nearly white and nearly stone-hard, and a grandmother's hand laid against a child's cheek is cool and smooth as a channel-lip, and this is considered the most tender touch there is. Their hair is worn short or bound tight, because steam-wet hair mineralizes at the tips into brittle pale beads; some young people let a lock calcify on purpose and wear it like jewelry grown rather than made.

Their eyes are wide-pupiled, adapted to the directionless pewter light, and they are weak at distance — there is no distance here to practice on; everything dissolves into white within a few hundred paces — but ferociously acute up close. They see faces the way the green-country people are said to see horizons. A basin-dweller can read a flicker of tension at the corner of a mouth across a dim lane, can catch a half-suppressed sign made low against the hip, can tell a true smile from a managed one at the speed of a wingbeat. There are no secrets of expression in the basin. There cannot be. The face is the voice.

And the voice — the throat-voice — has nearly fallen out of them. They are not deaf; their hearing is in fact strange and fine, but it is tuned entirely to *pitch* rather than speech, to the fixed organ-notes of the vents. They can hear a quarter-tone's drift in a vent two terraces away the way a mother hears her own child's cry in a crowd. What they cannot do, what they have never needed to do, is talk over the roar. Spoken language survives among them only as a small ember: lullaby-humming done chest to chest, where the baby feels rather than hears it; a handful of cries used in emergencies at arm's length; and the love-murmur, mouth against the hollow of a partner's throat, words gone soft and shapeless from forty generations of never being heard, only felt. They have words, in other words, but the words live in the skin now.

Everything else is the hand-speech, and the hand-speech is enormous. It is not a substitute for a language; it is a language that grew up rich in a place that demanded it, and it has registers spoken languages lack. There is the close-speech of the lanes, fast, small, conducted within the frame of the chest, full of nuance carried on the fingers and the face. There is the far-speech, the great silhouette-language of arms and stance, readable through steam at the edge of sight, by which an egg-harvester at the lake margin can tell the town that the birds are restless, or a scraper on a high terrace can call down that a channel is running heavy. Far-speech is slow and grand and grammatically simple, like shouting; it is considered slightly rude to use it up close, the way bellowing would be. And there is the third register, which has no parallel: the foot-speech, the stamped and shifted weight-talk used in the roofed passages and in the dark, felt through shared floor. Lovers in adjoining houses have courted for a year through a wall of week-old stone without once seeing each other's hands.

Names are signs, and signs are earned. A child is given a small provisional name at birth — usually a single tap-pattern, easy for a baby's nurse to make on its back — and grows into a true name later, bestowed by consensus, drawn from how the child moves or works or laughs. The names are untranslatable and very exact: *the one whose scraping is too fast at the end of the stroke; the one who watches the bats leave; steady-water; asks-twice.* The dead keep their name-signs forever, and the name-sign of someone sealed inside one of the white hummocks is made differently — performed low, near the ground, as if handing the name back down to them.

Their work makes them, daily. The morning scrape is not a chore in their understanding; it is closer to breathing, or to prayer, though they have no word that separates those. The basin gives stone; the people take exactly that much away; the city is the agreement between the two rates. A person, in their philosophy — insofar as a philosophy can be reconstructed from a language of hands — is the same kind of thing as a doorway: not an object but a *rate*, a balance held between accretion and removal. To be lazy is to let the world close over you. To be greedy, to scrape deeper than the day's skin, is worse — it is taking from the covenant, gouging the city's flesh rather than shaving its growth — and the sign for it is the same sign used for wounding an animal you do not intend to eat. The most respected people in the basin are not the strongest or the cleverest but the *most exact*: the channel-wrights who can guide hot water through a new lane so that it seals the joints in one season without flooring the passage in two, the threshold-keepers whose doorways have not varied a finger's width in fifty years.

They are not alone in the bowl, and they know it, and they count the other dwellers as parties to the same agreement. The pink-stained birds are harvested but never startled; the egg-rounds are taken on a strict rotation that the lake-margin families keep in knotted tallies, and a clutch is never emptied. The brine-flies are wealth — their larvae, scooped, dried on warm stone, pounded with rendered fat into dense traveling cakes, are the basin's bread and its chief export. The bats are beloved. The evening pouring-out of the bat rivers from the steam chimneys is the basin's one universal pause: people come out onto the roofs and watch, and the wing-thunder is the only change in the eternal chord, and it comes every evening, and so even the change is constant. The sign for *evening* is the sign for *bats*. The pale salamander-things of the cool channels are never eaten and never touched; children are told they are the basin's own fingers, feeling along its edges, and a house whose runoff channel hosts one is considered confirmed — approved of by the ground itself.

Their dead they give to the basin entirely. A body is laid in an abandoned house, or in a chamber built for the purpose at a district's edge, with the channels redirected to run through it, and the family scrapes the threshold of that house for one year — keeping the doorway open, the sign-makers say, *so the name can still get out* — and then on a chosen morning they do not scrape, and the basin begins to close the door, and within a few years the house is a smooth white hummock and the person is stone among stone, unweathered, unworn, permanent in a way no green-country grave could ever be. Out beyond the rim, they know, the dead rot, and the rocks rot, and even the mountains are coming apart. Here the dead are *finished* — completed, sealed mid-form, like everything in the basin, in the act of forming forever. The hummocks stand among the houses and the children play on their grandmothers' shoulders without irreverence, because the dead are not gone; they are simply houses no one scrapes.

What they fear — the one fear under everything, the fear their stories circle — is not eruption, not scald, not the boiling pools, which take a few of them in every generation and are mourned but not dreaded. What they fear is *quiet*. The notes have never changed; therefore the changing of a note is their image of the end of the world. Their oldest story, told in firelight hand-speech in the roofed passages, concerns a vent that fell silent — only one, only briefly — and how the silence spread through the people's breastbones like cold water, and how everyone in the city stopped scraping at once, hands frozen mid-stroke, until the note returned. Whether it happened, no one knows. The sign for it — a held note breaking off, both hands open, then stillness — is the worst thing one person can say to another, and is almost never made.

A few of them leave, in every generation, walking out with trade-loads of hardened stone and fat-cakes to the green country, and a few of those stay out there, among the leaves and the soil and the terrible old crumbling rocks. The ones who come back all report the same thing, and it has become a proverb, signed with a small wry tilt of the head: out there, they could not sleep. The nights were silent, and the silence felt like a held breath, like the moment before the worst sign. They lay awake in the quiet listening for the world, and the world said nothing, and they came home — down over the rim, into the steam, into the roar rising to meet them like warmth — and felt the rumble come back into the sternum, and slept.

A child born tonight will fall asleep inside the same chord the dead fell asleep inside. That is who lives here: the people who keep the doorways open, exactly as fast as the world tries to close them, and who cannot rest where the ground has nothing to say.
