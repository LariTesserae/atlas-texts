# Who Lives on the Quiet Tablelands

Start small, because the place does. The plateau teaches you its census from the ground up, and it withholds the largest resident until last, the way it withholds everything.

## The Small and the Many

Under the bunchgrass, in the bare circles each clump keeps around itself, there is a whole civilization of things that walk instead of fly. The pollinators here do not buzz. They are slow, deliberate, ant-like creatures with a dusting of pale fur, and they travel from pinhead flower to pinhead flower on foot, in lines, following scent-roads that must be generations old, because the lines persist across seasons and detour around stones that have since rolled away. A flowering season on the tablelands makes no hum at all. It makes a procession.

The lizards are the plateau's sunbathers and its gossips, insofar as it has either. They gather on the black varnish of the seep-stains — the warmest, darkest surfaces in the country — a dozen to a boulder, arranged with a spacing as deliberate as the grasses'. They are the only animals here that seem to *watch* things for entertainment. A chase, a dust devil, a stranger coming up through the notch: the lizards turn their heads in unison and attend. They do not flee until the last possible moment, and when they do it is a brief liquid scatter, soundless, and then they are stone-colored and gone, and the boulder looks like it was always empty.

The doves — call them doves; they have the shape and the gentleness of doves, though their wings are fringed like an owl's — live their whole lives between the pools. They know the water-map the way you know the rooms of your house, in the body, without thinking. A flock of them crossing a terrace at dusk is one of the eeriest beautiful things the plateau offers: forty birds banking together a meter off the ground, and not one sound, not a whistle, not a wingbeat, so that the eye keeps reporting motion the ear keeps denying. They drink in turns. Always in turns. There is an order to it that nobody has cracked, except that the order exists, and a dove that drinks out of turn — this has been seen — is not punished, but the whole flock goes still for a moment, the way a room goes still when someone says the wrong thing.

In the pools themselves: almost nothing, and the almost matters. Hold your face close to one of those slow-turning surfaces and you will eventually see them — translucent threads, finger-length, finer than grass, swimming in the circulation rather than against it. Whether they cause the water's strange patience or merely ride it, no one knows. They have no eyes. They have no apparent mouths. They may not be animals at all. They turn over with the water, generation after generation, in pools the size of a sleeping animal, and they have never been found anywhere else, and each pool's threads are subtly different from the next pool's, as though every little water has been alone long enough to invent its own.

## The Hares

The hares are the gentry of the tablelands. Not the oldest residents — that title goes elsewhere — but the oldest *tenants*, the ones whose lease has been renewed so many times that the land has shaped itself partly around them.

They are rose-gray, large-eyed, and almost supernaturally calm. The calm is the thing you notice. Hares elsewhere are creatures of flinch and bolt; these sit. They sit in the open in numbers that would be suicidal anywhere else, motionless, ears half-lowered, and the longer you watch the more you understand that the sitting is not torpor but *practice* — a discipline of stillness so old it has become physiology. A hare of the tablelands can hold a position through a passing dust devil, through the shadow of the cat itself crossing the next rock over, through your own approach to within a few paces. Then it is simply elsewhere, and you did not see the transition, only the dust settling in the bright air.

They are social in the way furniture in a well-arranged room is social: each alone, all in relation. A terrace's hares maintain a constellation — fixed sitting-places, worn faintly into the stone over what must be centuries of bodies, and a young hare inherits a place the way a person inherits a name. They breed in the talus, in the cool seams around the half-buried structures, and the structures tolerate them; more than tolerate — the warmest winter dens and the coolest summer ones are always within the long shadow of a standing thing, and the hares have known this far longer than any observer has.

When the low tone comes — that once-or-twice-daily note felt in the sternum — the hares do not lift their heads like the grazers do. They lower them. All of them, all at once, a small bow held for the duration of the sound, and then the ordinary stillness resumes. It is the only ceremony anyone has ever caught them at.

## The Grazers

The big slow ones — marmot-shaped, sheep-sized, gray-gold like the grass they crop — live in herds of a dozen or two, and they are the plateau's timekeepers. Their movements are so regular you could set instruments by them: down the terrace edge in the morning, the long midday in the cooled shadow of the folded wing, back along the rim in the evening with the light going long and red behind them. The trails they keep are not paths so much as *records* — smooth troughs in the stone, generations deep, polished by an unbroken procession of the same decision made daily for thousands of years.

They are nearly mute. Their cropping is audible from a few feet; their footfalls on bare rock are a soft repeated press, like someone kneading bread in the next room. But they have one voice, used rarely: a low chest-note, a single syllable, passed down a herd from animal to animal when something requires general attention. It sounds — and this has unsettled more than one listener — like a smaller, warmer version of the plateau's own tone. A child's imitation of a parent's word. Whether the grazers learned it from the land or the resemblance runs the other way is one of those questions the tablelands declines to answer.

They are gentle, incurious, and very hard to alarm. A grazer that finds you sitting on its trail will stop, consider, and then go around you with the unhurried courtesy of someone stepping past a stranger in a narrow aisle. The herds have no sentries. They don't post watches against the cat. They seem to have arrived, long ago, at some actuarial peace with being eaten — the cat takes what the cat takes, and the herd's answer is not vigilance but continuity, the trail worn one day deeper.

## The Cat

There is probably one to a terrace. There may be fewer than that — the territories are vast, and the same long tan shape may serve two or three terraces in slow rotation. No one has ever seen two together. No one has ever heard one at all.

It is built low and long, marked in the rose-gray and rust of the stone so exactly that a resting cat is, for all practical purposes, a rock with a pulse. Its silence is not merely the soft-footedness of cats everywhere; it is something more thorough. It moves through dry grass without the grass ticking. Watchers who have been close — and the cat permits closeness, with the same eerie tolerance everything here extends — describe a feeling of *pressure* around it, a faint deadening, as though the animal carries a small portion of the plateau's quiet with it the way other animals carry scent.

It hunts perhaps once in several days, almost always hares, almost always at the bright edges of morning. The chases are short, silent, and decided in seconds either way. And here is the strange etiquette of it: a failed chase ends completely. The cat does not pursue, does not circle back, does not lurk. It sits down — actually sits, in the open, in full view of the hare it just missed — and the hare, a few bounds away, sits too, and for a while predator and prey rest in the same sunlight like two parties after an arbitration, and then both go about their day. The plateau does not seem to permit grudges. Or the animals long ago found grudges too loud to keep.

The cats den under the largest structures. The folded wing on the third terrace has sheltered an unbroken line of them — the floor of the deepest seam there is glazed smooth, body-polished, older than any of the trails. Cubs are born in that artifact-cooled dark, against material with the warmth and faint give of skin, and one wonders — it is hard not to wonder — what a creature raised in the arms of such a thing believes its mother is.

## The Junipers and the Silver Congregation

Count the trees among the inhabitants, because here they have earned it. Each juniper is alone, ancient, and mostly dead — gray driftwood architecture with one green banner held out on the lee side — and each one is a *site*: the only perch on its terrace, the only deep root, the only thing that creaks. (The junipers are allowed to creak. In a strong wind, the creak of a single tree carries impossibly far across the silence, and every animal in earshot plainly knows which tree it is.) Birds cache seeds in the dead wood; lizards winter in the bark; the trees in their slow way are landlords, each presiding over a small neighborhood of dependents, all of them quieter than the tree itself.

And then the silver rosettes — the plants that grow only on the pedestals of the standing things, the ones that all face the same invisible direction at any given hour. These are best understood not as a species but as a congregation. They do not compete; they do not spread beyond the pedestals though nothing visibly stops them; they flower all at once, plateau-wide, on no schedule anyone has matched to season or weather, on nights when the grain of light inside the structures runs brighter than usual. Whatever they are attending to, they attend together. They are the only living things on the tablelands that point at the largest resident, and so they are the closest thing it has to a face you can find by following their gaze — except that their gaze moves, hour by hour, and following it leads you only in long slow circles around the country, which may be exactly the truth of the matter.

## The Resident

Now the one the place withholds.

Something lives in the standing things — or through them, which is closer. Not a ghost; the word is too thin, too leftover. Not machines in any sense that survives contact with the material's warmth and give. The honest description is this: whoever built the structures did not leave, and is not dead, and is no longer anything like a *they*.

The builders finished. That is what the artifacts' posture has been saying all along — *the posture of things that have finished happening* — but finishing, for them, did not mean departure. It meant resolution in the chemical sense: a dissolving into solution. They took whatever they had been — bodies, minds, the boundaries between individuals, the boundary between themselves and their works — and folded it down into function, into the slow ongoing verbs of the place. They became the coolness that pools in certain shadows. The patience of water that will not evaporate at the rate it should. The taking of rain on its way down. The quiet itself — that thorough, attentive quiet, which is not an absence of sound but a *holding* of it, the way a hand can hold a small bird still without gripping.

It is one thing now, or close enough to one thing that the distinction has stopped mattering, and it is distributed across the whole country at a density too low to ever meet. You cannot find it because you are never not in it. The dim grain of light falling sideways inside the structures at night is, as near as anyone can say, its metabolism — or its dreaming; at this remove the two may be identical. The columns of cool air over the pools are its breath, exhaled once per evening. The low tone, once or twice a day, is its entire vocabulary: not a message, the long-term watchers think, but a *roll call* — a single syllable that means something like *still here*, to which the herds answer by lifting their heads (*still here*) and the hares answer by lowering theirs (*still here*) and the silver rosettes answer by simply continuing to face it, which was their answer all along.

It wants almost nothing, and what it wants, it has arranged. It keeps the water cold and hidden because small things need water more than large things do, and it long ago stopped being a large thing. It cools the shadows where the animals shelter. It deflects the dust devils — not, the careful observers suspect, to protect itself, which needs no protecting, but to protect what dens against it. Its entire surviving will, the whole compressed remainder of however vast a civilization, has narrowed to something a person can state in a sentence: *that the small lives here go on, quietly, for as long as the rock lasts.*

It does not think about you. But it notices you — the compass-drift is the edge of its noticing — and the noticing is without appetite and without judgment, the way a sleeping host notices, somewhere below waking, that a guest is moving gently through the house.

## The Listeners

And then there are the people, because there are always a few people.

They come up through the notch in the rimrock at a rate of perhaps one or two a year — surveyors, once; then the curious; now mostly the *referred*, sent by someone who came back changed and couldn't explain it. Most stay a week, lose their taste for talking somewhere around the third day, and go back down carrying the silence like a stone in the pocket, taking it out for the rest of their lives at odd moments.

A handful have stayed. There is, at present, one: an old woman on the second terrace, in a dry-stone shelter she built against the flank of a door-high column, with the column's permission as far as she can tell. She keeps no animals — keeping seems wrong here — but the hares of her terrace have incorporated her into their constellation, and there is a sitting-place outside her door that has been occupied, by one rose-gray body or another, every morning for thirty years. She speaks perhaps a sentence a day, out loud, mostly to the column, mostly observations about weather. She says — on the rare occasions she has had anyone to say it to — that she does not believe the resident understands her words, but that this is fine, because she does not understand its tone either, and the two of them have nonetheless reached an arrangement: each says *still here*, once a day, in its own language, and that is the whole of the conversation, and it is enough conversation for anyone.

When she dies — she has thought about this — the hares will keep her sitting-place open for a while, the way the grass keeps its circles, and then some young hare will inherit it, and the constellation will close over the gap without a sound. She finds this a comfort. It is, she says, exactly what the whole country is: an enormous someone who finished, and made of their finishing a roof — and everything that lives on the tablelands, hare and cat and thread-in-the-water and old woman, lives the same way under it.

Quietly. By agreement. Holding still in the bright air, and answering, when asked, *still here.*
