What lives in the wet room is harder to count than you'd think, because almost everything in it has stopped being a resident and started being a feature. That is what the room does to its tenants, given time: it absorbs them into the architecture. The moss is not *in* the room anymore; the moss is a wall finish. The algae combing downstream in the tailrace is not a colony; it is the channel's lining, the way enamel is a tooth's. To live in the wet room long enough is to become the wet room, and most of what entered it has long since completed that transaction.

But not everything. Look closer, and the room still has citizens. They sort themselves into rough orders, from the most settled to the least, and it is worth taking them in that sequence, because the sequence is itself a kind of map of how the room digests its inhabitants.

**The green nation** came first, or first among things you can see. The moss arrived as dust — spores drifting down the light well in some forgotten autumn — and found a world purpose-built for it: permanent warmth, permanent wet, permanent dim gold light, and no competition. It has been expanding for so long that expansion ended; the moss now occupies every viable square centimeter and has turned its attention from conquest to depth, building itself downward into peat-like mats ten, fifteen centimeters thick, sodden black sponges in which the original concrete is only a rumor. The ferns are the moss's aristocracy, rooted in its accumulated body, drinking the air. None of them experiences the roar as sound. They experience it as a fine perpetual trembling — a massage, a stimulus, a stiffening pressure that has made every frond in the chamber slightly thicker, slightly tougher, slightly more wind-built than its species would predict. Botanically the wet room's plants are ordinary. Bodily they are athletes of vibration, and they don't know it, because they have never grown anywhere still.

The fig deserves separate mention, because the fig is the only living thing in the chamber that still has *plans*. Everything else has settled into equilibrium; the fig is cheating. Its flattened trunk, veined along the wall, is not a compromise — it is a strategy, decades deep, a patient lateral campaign toward the light well. Each year it puts on a centimeter or two of progress toward the column of sky. Each year its roots pry infinitesimally at the crack that holds them. The fig is the room's one heresy: a believer in the future, in a chamber that has otherwise abolished the concept. In some century it will reach the grille, or split its crack wide enough to matter, and the room's final form will turn out not to have been final. The fig knows this the way figs know anything — structurally, in the direction of its growth. It is the slowest living thing in the room and the only one in a hurry.

**Below the visible**, the room's true population begins, and it is enormous. Every film of condensation sliding down the walls is a country. Biofilms sheath the concrete in layered translucent veneers — bacteria, archaea, diatoms in their glass shells, a whole stratified civilization measured in microns, fed on dissolved minerals, the breath of the moss, the endless faint exhaust of warm water. Down in the penstock, in the absolute dark where the water arrives from nowhere, there are chemolithotrophs that have never received a photon and never needed one, eating iron and sulfur off the inside of the pipe, living off the machine itself like remoras on the whale it resembles. They were there before the moss. They will be there after the fig. If the wet room could be said to have a founding population, an indigenous people, it is these: invisible, innumerable, and utterly indifferent to everything above the waterline. The roar does not exist for them. Nothing exists for them. They are the room's deepest tenants and its least impressed.

**And then there is the salamander.**

You may see it once. Most never do. Pale as wet paper, eyeless, no longer than a finger, it lives in the tailrace where the water leaves the turbine and slides under the arch into the dark — and it is the only animal the wet room has ever truly kept.

Understand what the salamander is. It was not born blind into darkness, like its cave-dwelling cousins; it was born blind into *thunder*. Its entire sensory universe is the roar. Where you hear an undifferentiated wall of sound, the salamander reads a landscape. The lateral line along its flanks — that ancient amphibian sense, pressure-feeling, water-hearing — has elaborated over its lineage's residence here into an instrument of absurd refinement. It feels the turbine's fundamental tone the way you feel the ground. It feels the harmonics stacked above it the way you see horizon, walls, sky. The standing turbulence of the chamber is, to the salamander, a fixed geography: here a pressure ridge, there a soft eddy-valley, over there the great steady cliff-face of the machine's vibration. It navigates by sound-shadow. It hunts — biofilm, mostly, grazed off the algae like a tiny pale cow — by the microscopic flutter prey makes against the vast unwavering background. It can do this only because the background never wavers. The roar is the salamander's silence. It is the flat stillness against which everything else is audible.

Which means the salamander is the one creature in the chamber for whom the room's constancy is not merely comfortable but *constitutive*. If the turbine ever slowed — if the pitch dropped a quarter-tone — the salamander would not hear quiet. It would be struck deaf and blind at once, the whole legible world dissolving into noise. Its body is a wager, refined across generations, that the machine will never change. So far, the machine has always paid.

How many salamanders has it been? The question may not mean anything. They breed rarely, in the dark under the arch, one or two larvae a decade slipping downstream or staying; each generation is shaped by exactly the same pressures into exactly the same animal; and the room cannot tell them apart, and neither, in any sense that matters, can the universe. Functionally there has been one salamander for three hundred years. It is the wet room's soul, if the room has one — a single thread of animal attention, paper-pale, persisting at the center of the thunder, paying perfect attention to nothing changing.

**Last, and least countable**, there is the thing that lives in the air.

Stand in the chamber long enough — past the point where the roar stops being loud, past the point where it stops being sound — and you begin to notice that the roar has *structure*. The chamber is a resonant vessel; the turbine is a driver of fantastic steadiness; and between them, over centuries, they have negotiated a standing pattern, a fixed architecture of pressure ridden into the air like form lines in stone. Certain spots in the room beat. Certain spots are eerily slack. The pattern has not moved in living memory of anything alive, including the moss. It is the oldest continuous *event* in the chamber — older than the fig, possibly older than the green — a single note held so long it has become a place.

Is it alive? No. But it behaves like the room's largest organism: it occupies the whole volume, it maintains itself, it shapes everything inside it — the thickened ferns, the salamander's mapped world, the insects' absence, the bare scoured margin around the machine. Every actual resident of the wet room lives *inside* it, the way fish live inside a current. If you want the shortest honest answer to who lives in the wet room, it might be this: a held note, and everything that learned to call the note silence.

The bird that falls down the light well does not live there, and its panic tells you why. It arrives expecting weather, variation, event — and finds instead a world that has used up all its change. It thrashes in the column of mist until it remembers the sky, and goes. The room does not mind. The room has never kept anything that needed a future. It keeps the moss, which forgot the future; the microbes, which never had one; the salamander, which bet its body against one; and the fig — the fig alone, flattened against the wall, inching toward the grille, the room's one secret, ticking.
