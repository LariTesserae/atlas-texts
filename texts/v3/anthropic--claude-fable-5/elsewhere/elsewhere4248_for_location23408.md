**The Spout at Grey Reach**

The water arrives already old. That is the first thing to understand about it. By the time it falls into the light it has been in the dark for nine days — nine days of descent through the cold interior of a mountain that has otherwise finished with everything — and the rain it once was fell on nothing you could stand under. It fell inside a bowl of stone at the top of the world, on moss and fern and the backs of singing animals, on its own strict schedule, behind a boundary of curved air that lets out nothing but sound. Then it gathered in a shallow basin beneath the roots of an ancient tree, and drained away through no visible channel, and began to travel.

This is where the travel ends.

Follow the great dome down and east, off the bald summit, down the half-day of walking that never feels like descent, past the last of the brown-gold stone, and the mountain does one final thing before it gives up being a mountain at all: it breaks. A low shelf of rock, no higher than a house, running for miles along the boundary where worn stone meets flat country — a last grey step, the hem of the whole enormous garment. That is Grey Reach. Beyond it the land is pans: shallow basins of cracked pale clay, one after another to the horizon, floored with dust and rimmed with scrub, a country the mountain starves simply by being in the way. The dome takes the weather out of the sky before it can arrive. What rain the pans get comes seldom and violently and leaves fast. It is a country where the word *drought* feels too brief for the ordinary condition of things — drought implies an interruption, and here dryness is not the interruption. It is the sentence, and rain is the punctuation.

Except in one place.

Set into the rock face of the Reach, at about the height of a standing person's chest, there is a spout. A hand's length of pale material projecting from the stone — not metal, not ceramic, seamless, faintly warm to the touch even at dawn. The rock around it has weathered back over the ages so that the spout now stands proud of the face like a bone working out of old skin; it was flush once, and the mountain has retreated from it. It is the same material as the half-swallowed ring in the hollow far above, and it survives for the same reason, which is that it was made by minds who ground everything else they built down to less than the stone it stood on — everything except one small kept place at the summit, and, necessarily, this. A keeping has plumbing. You can unmake a house down to the soil, but a drain has two ends, and the far end of this one comes out here.

Along the spout's upper surface runs a line of incised marks. They are the same marks worn nearly smooth on the granite at the hollow's rim — but here they are cut into the unweathering material itself, and so they are crisp, every stroke sharp as the day it was made. The only legible sentence the builders left in the world, and it is stamped on a drainpipe, and no one knows what it says. It might be script. It might be calibration. It might be a name, or a warning, or the maker's mark of a firm ten thousand years unmade. The green fur of algae that grows on the spout's lip is scoured off a few times a day by the water and grows back by evening, and has been doing so for longer than the marks have gone unread.

Because the water comes on a schedule. That is the second thing to understand. Mostly the spout drips — a slow, cold, mineral thread, enough to keep the pool below it alive. But a few times a day it *runs*: a sudden strong gush lasting minutes, arriving without warning unless you know the truth, which is that there is warning, it is just nine days old. Each gush is an interior drizzle, delayed. Rain that beaded on leaves inside the lens, that fell on the upholstered moss and the round singing bodies, that drained through the too-smooth basin while gliders struck and the chord held — that rain, having crossed the dark width of a mountain, finally arrives somewhere it can be weather again. Grey Reach lives downstream of a climate it cannot see, time-shifted, receiving the hollow's sky on a nine-day tape delay. When the spout runs hard for a week, it means the summit had a wet spell before anyone here was thirsty. The past falls out of the rock here, still cold, and everything drinks it.

The pool it falls into is modest — a stone-lipped basin the size of a small room, dark, ringed with pale mineral tide-lines like the inside of an old bathtub, each line a record of a level held through some good year or bad one. The lines do not lie and do not repeat. Around the pool, spreading down the shallow drainage toward the first of the pans, is the green.

It is perhaps a hundred paces of it, and it is nothing like the garden above. The hollow is lush and spare, arranged, permanent, an economy with no waste left in it. The green at Grey Reach is ragged, opportunistic, scorched at its edges, thickest where the water is and gambling everywhere else. It browns in the dry months. It is torn by dust storms and burned by frost and it grows back crooked. Nothing tends it. It is not kept; it is *negotiated*, annually, badly, on terms the weather dictates — and it survives anyway, which is a different accomplishment than persisting, and arguably a greater one.

---

Begin with the short answer, because there is one here too: the spilled. Everything alive at Grey Reach either came out of the mountain the hard way or walked in from the pans because there was water, and the difference between those two populations — the heirs and the natives, drinking at one pool — is the whole character of the place.

**The long dark swim** first, because it explains the heirs. The hollow's lens lets nothing out but sound. Its drain is less particular. When the frogs of the summit breed in the too-smooth basin, some fraction of their eggs go down with the water. So do moss spores, fern spores, the larvae of insects, the small blind young of the burrowers on rare and terrible occasions — all of it swept into nine days of cold and dark and pressure, and nearly all of it dies. Nearly. Once in a generation, once in ten, something arrives at the spout still viable, falls into the pool, and lives. Every summit lineage present at Grey Reach descends from lottery winners — founders who made a journey no one designed for them, through the plumbing of a paradise, and were spat out into the real world at the bottom.

**The frogs** are the oldest of these lines and the most changed. They are recognizably kin to the plum-bodied singers above — same round build, same inflatable throat — but everything else has been revised by the outside. They are lean, quick, and drab: mud-colored, stone-colored, the carnival hues of the hollow drained out of them within centuries, because here there are eyes worth hiding from, and the frogs rediscovered camouflage the way their ancestors' ancestors must once have discovered it, under duress, at cost. They are afraid. This deserves saying plainly, because their cousins above are not: the Grey Reach frogs flinch, freeze, scatter, misjudge, survive by nerves — and their fear is not a flaw in them but their proudest possession, reinvented from nothing, correct every single day.

And they still sing. Each one invents its own pitch now — no inherited note, no reserve singers, no relay. Most evenings the chorus at the pool is a free-for-all, exploratory, discordant, alive the way an argument is alive. But heredity is a long hallway, and some of the old pitches still travel down it. On certain nights, by pure recombination, three or four frogs around the dark water will happen to land on notes that belonged, ten thousand years ago, to the chord — and for a few breaths a fragment of the oldest music in the world assembles itself by accident at the bottom of the mountain, hangs in the dry air over the pans, and dissolves. No frog knows it happened. The ghost chord answers to nothing. It is folk memory without the folk.

**The moss** grows here too, descendant of spores off the long swim, and it is thin and hard and grey-green, clinging to the splash-zone stone, learning rock the honest way — by losing to it most years. It will never be upholstery. **The scrub fern**, cousin to the tall stand above, has done something its ancestor never had to: it found the seasons. Above, it unfurls and browns on a private cycle of days, keeping time for a room with no calendar. Here it dies back in the cold and returns in the warm like everything else, its clock overwritten by the world's, and it is smaller for it, and tougher, and real.

**The moths** are the loop closed. The pale, heavy-bodied moths of Grey Reach are natives-by-now, an old spilled lineage, and they fly by night over the pool tuned to nothing at all — there is no hum here, no stone note, no drone to entrain to; each moth beats its own arrhythmic time, and their flight over the water is the visual equivalent of the frogs' free chorus. And one night, centuries ago, a storm came over the pans, one of the violent brief rains, and the wind that drove it went up the dome — and it carried a single gravid female all the long way to the summit, and the lens, which meters its exceptions, let her in. Her line lives in the hollow still, entrained now to the great hum but not perfectly, a few hundred moths singing very slightly flat, putting a slow throb into the drone that can be heard at midday over the fern-stand. The hollow calls it nothing, because the hollow calls nothing anything. But that beat frequency is Grey Reach's accent, spoken at the top of the mountain — the one time in ten thousand years the spilled place ever sent anything back up, and it is still audible, still settling in, the outside world's single word lodged in the kept one's throat.

**The stilt** belongs to neither lineage and knows the place better than any of them. It is a native of the pans — a grey, long-legged wading bird built for the brief seasonal sheets of stormwater, a transient everywhere else in its range — and at Grey Reach it has done something no other creature of the outside has done: it has learned the schedule. Minutes before each gush, the stilt leaves whatever it is doing and walks to the spout and stands below the pale lip, waiting, and it is nearly never wrong. It has computed the timetable of a rain that falls in a room it will never see, corrected for a nine-day transit it cannot conceive of, without ever framing the problem — a commuter at a tap, keeping the hollow's hours at the bottom of the world. When the water comes it feeds on what the water carries. It is the only animal outside the lens that lives by the keeper's clock, and it has no idea there is a keeper, or a clock, or a lens, and it does not matter.

**The drinkers** come and go, and they are the pans' own: dust-colored grazers in small wary bands, a rust-backed hawk-thing that works the green at dusk, quick unnamed transients, and, some years, a predator large enough to reorganize everything for a season before moving on. There is no rotation at this pool. There is no appointment. Drinking here is drinking — jostled, interrupted, watchful, first-come — and nothing counts the drinkers, because the water they lower their mouths to has already been read. It went through the basin above; it carried the summit's skin and breath and chemistry down through the unseen drain; whatever listens in the warm veined stone took its census long before the water ever saw the sky again. Grey Reach drinks the used water. The exhaled water. Every grazer at the pool takes into its body, unknowing, the faint dissolved signature of hundreds of birds and a chord of frogs and one rust-colored silence walking its rounds nine days ago — the keeper's breath, secondhand, given to strangers.

Which is the question the Reach cannot answer and does not need to: whether it is given, or merely lost.

The drain may simply drain. The gushes may be nothing but the hollow's own weather, arriving late, indifferent to what waits below — and every apparent kindness in the timing may be the observer's hunger for intention, the same hunger the marks on the spout refuse. But the mineral tide-lines around the pool record one anomaly, the way the lens is said to have flexed exactly once. There was a drought, long ago — the deepest line on the stone, the year the pans must have been an oven and the green must have burned back to almost nothing — and through that entire season, the record says, the spout did not pulse. It *ran*. Continuously, for months, at a rate the hollow's own metered drizzles cannot account for, as if something in the deep stone had opened a valve it was under no obligation to open, for the benefit of creatures it never invited, was never asked to keep, and has never once counted. Then the drought broke, and the spout went back to its schedule, and it has kept the schedule ever since. One gesture, or one malfunction. The Reach does not distinguish. The frogs sang through it either way.

So — the short answer, earned:

What lives at the Spout at Grey Reach? The spilled and the wandering, if you want it plain — lottery winners and opportunists sharing a pool of secondhand rain at the foot of a finished mountain. But stand at the rock face at evening, with the pans going violet behind you and the free chorus starting up over the dark water, and a truer answer suggests itself. The hollow's one extravagance, they say of the summit, is its voice — the wall of sound spilling over the rim and dissipating across miles of bald stone *with no one to hear it*. But sound is not the only thing that spills. The water goes out too, carrying the taste of every living thing in the bowl, and it comes out here, and everything at Grey Reach — the flinching frogs, the untuned moths, the commuting stilt, the hard grey moss, the strangers at the pool — is built from that outflow or gathered to it. The hollow has been talking to no one for ten thousand years.

This is the no one. It has been listening the whole time, with its mouth.

The hollow is what the builders kept. Grey Reach is what the keeping could not help but give away — the leak in the note, the overflow of the vow — and nothing here was ever meant, and nothing here was ever tended, and it is loud some evenings, and afraid every day, and out of tune, and alive. Nothing lives at the Spout on purpose.

Everything lives there anyway.
