# The Gullet

The valley is old the way a scar is old. It had finished being a wound long before anyone now living, and had settled into the shape wounds settle into — broad, soft-shouldered, floored with the deep brown soil that only ten thousand patient years can make. Then, three hundred years ago or less, the land to the west of it stood up.

The people here do not call that event the rising. They call it the burying. When the plateau came out of the earth, a third of the valley went under the rubble of its birth — fields, orchards, the upper town, and the river. The river had a name, and the name survives, attached now to nothing: it is what old women call the wind out of the west, and what the marsh at the valley's foot is called on maps, and it is a common name for daughters. The river itself went into the ground in an afternoon, swallowed whole where the rock broke, and for eleven years the valley was a dry country grieving beside a fresh cliff two kilometers high.

In the twelfth year, the cliff began to speak water.

Not the river back. Nothing so kind. What came out of the base of the escarpment — first a seep, then a mouth, then dozens of mouths, then the hundred and some mouths there are today, though the count changes, because the springs seal themselves with their own stone and new ones tear open beside them — was the river's ghost, changed by where it had been. It comes out warm. Blood-warm, a held-hand warmth, having spent its years of descent among rocks that are still, in their depths, remembering the heat of the event that made them. It comes out white, or nearly: so burdened with dissolved young mountain that it is cloudy as milk glass, and bitter, and it cannot keep what it carries once it touches air. It lays its burden down. A fingernail's thickness of new stone a season, on everything it touches.

And it comes out *loud*.

---

That is the fact of the Gullet, the way brightness is the fact of the plateau above. A hundred mouths of warm water falling out of a cliff face, day and night, without variation, for near three centuries. The roar is not a sound in the ordinary sense — a sound is something that arrives and departs. The roar does not arrive. It is the medium. Babies here are born into it and sleep in it and are famously difficult to settle anywhere else; travelers from the Gullet complain that quiet rooms keep them awake, that silence has a pressure on the ears, like depth. The roar is measured at the Assay House daily and has not varied meaningfully in eighty years, which the Gullet finds satisfying in a way it does not examine. Above, the flywheel keeps the hours. Below, the roar keeps them, all at once.

Voices are useless within half a kilometer of the shore. So the Gullet does not use them. What the Gullet uses is stone — because sound behaves here precisely opposite to how it behaves above. The plateau's rock is fractured and moss-wrapped and will not carry a vibration an arm's length. The Gullet's stone is the densest, truest, most continuous stone anyone has ever built on, because the water lays it down wet and seamless, and it *rings*. A knuckle rapped on a doorpost can be felt in the floor of a house three streets away. And so the Gullet talks by knocking: a knuckle-speech, rhythms tapped on walls and rails and the rims of basins, with a grammar and idioms and registers of politeness, and a coarseness available to it that appalls visitors once translated. A polite person knocks softly. A famous storyteller here is called a *good hand*, exactly as a fine lamp-sender is called above, and the two languages, light and knock, have been trading vocabulary for three generations by way of the people who move between — until the plateau now flashes a phrase that began as a drummed joke about a tax collector, and the Gullet knocks a blessing that began as a keeper's dusk-signal, and neither place quite remembers which words were originally whose.

---

The town climbs itself. It has no choice.

Everything the water touches, it stones. The terraces below the spring-mouths — great frozen weirs, lips and basins and aprons of travertine, white shading to ochre to soft grey with age — advance down the valley a hand's width a year, and the town, built on the older terraces, rises with the ground it stands on. Doorsills climb until the doors are stoned shut and new doors are cut a storey up. Every house has an older house beneath it, filled and sealed. The oldest quarter is a reef: you can see, in the cliff-like face where the terraces have been quarried back, the cross-sections of former streets, the arch of a former gate, a former window with the white stone standing in it like frost that never left. Below all of it, everyone knows though no one can visit, is the upper town of before the burying, and below the springs somewhere is the true bed of the river whose name the daughters carry.

The dead are given to the water. There is no other phrase for it here either. A body is laid in one of the whitening pools on the high terraces, and within two seasons the water has dressed it in stone and made it part of the shore, and the shore goes on advancing over it, and in the faces of the older terraces you can see them if you know how to look — smoothed, simplified, white forms in the white ground, the ancestors holding the town up in the most literal way a town has ever been held. The water takes everything. The Gullet finds this consoling rather than otherwise. It is, one notices, the same consolation as above, arrived at from the opposite direction: there, the moss closes over you and the green deepens; here, the stone closes over you and the town stands on your shoulders. Both places have concluded that being kept is better than being marked.

---

The bright pipe from the plateau ends here, at a square stone building on the highest terrace called the Sweetwater House, and only at the Sweetwater House does the whole economy of the high country finally make sense. The Gullet is the wettest town in the world and cannot drink a drop of itself. Its hundred mouths give bitter water, fit for the terraces and the dyeworks and the warm public baths, and for nothing that lives. What the Gullet drinks is combed out of the fog two kilometers overhead, a droplet at a time, and sent down the shining line — *high water*, it is called, and it tastes, people say with real reverence, like nothing at all, which in the Gullet is the rarest taste there is. Infants get it, and the sick, and the dying get a last cup of it, and oaths are sworn over it, and no one in living memory has watered it for profit, because the town has decided as one body that this would be the crime beneath all other crimes.

The current comes down the same line, and lights the lamps: the Gullet burns, in its evening thousands of windows, electricity made from air falling cold through the high clefts. And back up goes everything else, on the Counterweight — the water-lift bolted to the escarpment, where a tank of spring water descending hauls the goods-stage up: grain, oil, tools, coin, the twice-yearly rotation of people. The plateau's own water, having fallen three hundred years through the mountain, does one last job on its way to the marsh: it lifts bread to the people who combed it from the fog. The engineers of both towns regard this arrangement as merely sensible. Everyone else regards it as slightly holy, and no one argues, because the two views require no different maintenance.

The two towns sort people between them, and every family here has someone up there. The ones the plateau sends down within a season — the ones who need to hear themselves — mostly wash up in the Gullet and thrive, and you can pick them out years later by the way they still glance up when a door slams, hoarding the startle like a delicacy. The ones the Gullet sends up are the opposite kind, the rare children born under the roar whom the roar wears at, and the plateau receives them the way high ground receives floodwater, without comment. Physicians note that the Gullet's resting heart rates would alarm a cardiologist on the plateau, and that neither population will trade. The body consults the local clock. Here the clock is a hundred mouths, all speaking at once, forever.

---

The census of living things is long here, where the plateau's is short, because this is old soil and the web finished knitting itself ages ago — reeds and frogs and dragonflies in the cool marsh at the terraces' foot, where the water, having laid down its stone, spreads out at last mild and almost sweet; eels in the marsh channels; pale blind shrimp in the flooded seams behind the spring-mouths, which the Assay House naturalists have examined closely, because they resemble, more than they resemble anything else, the small translucent rowers that the keepers' ledgers record appearing in the black pools far above.

And the grey birds nest here. That is the plain, kept fact of it: a colony of them, some dozens, long-winged, in the spray-wet ledges beside the loudest of the mouths, where nothing that hunts by ear can work. The naturalists have logged them for sixty years, as carefully as the keepers log their one. And their ledger shows what the keepers' ledger cannot: that each year one bird, and never two, leaves the colony on still afternoons and makes the high crossing, and comes back with the dusk; that the crossing passes down, when a crosser dies, to a single younger bird, by no election anyone has witnessed; and that the crosser goes up with marsh mud on its feet and comes back clean. The naturalists have their suspicions about the black pools, and about what the mud carries, and about who, exactly, has been writing the first sentences of the plateau's ten-thousand-year book. They have tried twice to tell the keepers, in relayed knock and translated flash, and both times something essential fell out of the message on the way up, and they have decided, with the mildness common to both towns, that the plateau will work it out in its own time, which is the only kind of time the plateau has.

There is one more entry, unlogged here as it is unlogged above. On certain nights the springs surge — all the mouths together, the whole shore lifting its voice and the warm pools rising a finger's width and falling back, irregular the way breath is irregular. The Gullet calls it the swallow, and children are taught to lay their palms flat on the terrace stone when it comes, out of a courtesy no one explains. Asked what it is, a Gullet man will say the mountain is young, and young things move in their sleep, and then he will change the subject with a small dry double-knock that is the local shrug — the identical shrug, note, to the one flashed above, because some things a language keeps through any translation.

---

At dusk the mist over the terraces goes gold, then grey, and high on the black rim of the world the orange points come out one by one, the constellation the Gullet has looked up at for eighty years the way other towns look at stars. The Gullet cannot answer in light; its own spray scatters a lamp within a street. So it answers the only way sound has ever crossed between the two towns. At full dark the warden of the Sweetwater House lays a hand on the bright pipe — the beautifully maintained pipe, and now you know why it is so beautifully maintained — and knocks the goodnight up it, slow and formal, the rhythm every child of both towns knows before they know words in any language. Two kilometers up, in the silence where a shout dies at the lips, keepers stand at intervals along the shining line with their palms flat against the metal, and feel the loudest town in the world say its one quiet thing.

What the mountain swallows in silence, it says again below, at the top of its voice. The plateau is where the world is worn away a droplet at a time; the Gullet is where the world thickens, a fingernail a season, laying down in advance the old age the high country is too young to have. One throat, two ends of a single breath — the bright quiet drawn in above, the warm roar let out below — and neither town in any hurry at all, because between them they can hear, better than anyone, how long the breath is going to be.
