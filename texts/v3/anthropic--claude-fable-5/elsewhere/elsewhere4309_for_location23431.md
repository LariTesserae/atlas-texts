## The Lent Water

You descend to it through ordinary country — terraced orchards, dust-colored villages, air thickening back toward warmth and pollen and the smell of turned earth — and the road, unlike the trail four thousand meters above, does not stop. It runs straight over the lip of a wide green rim and down, without hesitation, into the basin of a lake that is not there.

The absence is the size of a small inland sea. You see the shape of it before you understand it: a pale ring banded around the enclosing hills, chest-high in some places, higher in others, where water rested against stone for so long that the stone still wears its shoreline like a horizon lying on its side. Below that ring, the land changes character completely — smoother, paler, patient — and stone jetties walk out into open grass on their pilings, going nowhere, and a squat light-tower stands a kilometer from anything, built to be seen across water that has not reflected it in living memory.

The people of the rim, who have every reason to be bitter and have chosen instead to be exact, call it the Lent Water. Not the lost lake. Not the taken sea. *Lent* — because a loan, however long, is a species of promise.

## The Basin

The floor of it is a fine, hardened silt, pale as unglazed clay, and it has one property that visitors discover with their first step and never quite stop feeling in their feet afterward: it takes prints, and it keeps them.

Not for a season. For generations. A footstep pressed into the bed of the Lent Water outlasts the walker, outlasts the walker's children, blurring only slightly at the edges, the way a signature blurs in old ink. The basin is, in this respect, the precise opposite of the water it once held. Up on the mountain, in the Held Sea's standing dark, nothing propagates and nothing marks; your passage is corrected out of existence before it finishes happening. Down here, everything marks. To cross the bed is to write on it. The town on the rim understands this thoroughly, and so the basin floor is not wandered but *read* — a ledger two centuries deep, crossed by sanctioned paths worn glossy with reuse, while off the paths the old records lie undisturbed: cart tracks from lifetimes ago, a dog's looping joy preserved mid-sprint, the small close-together prints of some child long since grown, buried, and forgotten everywhere except here.

And near the middle of the bed, roped off with a courtesy that has hardened over the years into law, the oldest prints of all: eleven sets of footsteps, side by side and then gradually single-file, starting at the drowned town's old landing stair and going out across the naked lakebed toward the mountain trail. The first crossing. The morning after the morning when the lake was simply elsewhere.

There is a twelfth set. It goes out with the others. And it comes back — alone, later, the stride longer, the prints deeper, as of someone carrying something or carrying nothing and doing it badly — and then, mid-basin, a long way from any shore, facing the town, the prints stop. They do not scatter. They do not turn. There is no disturbance around them and no continuation beyond them. The last two prints are side by side, evenly weighted, the stance of a person who has paused to look at something, and then there is only clean unmarked silt, all the way to the rim.

The town keeps a lantern there. It stands at the end of the twelfth prints on an iron post, and it is filled and lit every evening, and it is the only fixed light on the whole dark floor of the basin — one small votive burning somewhere you can, for once, locate.

## Sound

The basin is an ear.

Whatever acoustic generosity the water refused to take with it, it left behind concentrated. The bowl of the Lent Water carries sound with an accuracy that unsettles newcomers for weeks: a word spoken quietly at the old landing stair arrives at the far shore intact, undiminished, personal, as if said an arm's length from you. Nothing shouted in the basin can be local. Nothing said on the bed can be unsaid, because everyone heard it, and the bed — being what it is — has probably kept the footprints of the saying.

The town has made this into an institution rather than a curse. Things that must be heard are said on the lakebed: marriages, verdicts, the settling of debts, and — every year on the morning the lake is reckoned to have left — the names of that year's dead, spoken outward across the empty floor by a single voice, in the direction of the mountain. Whether this is a report or an appeal, the town declines to say. Everything else, everything private, everything provisional, is said up on the rim, in the wind, where the basin cannot hear.

Because the basin is listening for something specific. On certain nights — still ones, cold ones, the air lying in the bowl like water remembering its posture — the whole valley catches, from very far up-mountain, three soft pulses in slow succession, evenly spaced. No instrument has recorded them here either. But four thousand meters below the spire that makes them, in the one landform on earth shaped to receive them, they arrive.

## The Fleet

The boats are the first thing you notice in the town and the last thing you understand.

There are perhaps forty of them, broad-beamed lake boats of a design no living person has sailed, drawn up in the old boathouse rows and in the square and along the rim road, each on its own cradle, each under its own canted roof. They are not relics. They are *maintained*. Every spring the town caulks them, tars them, sands and repaints their hulls in the old colors, replaces what has rotted, and every spring the lake continues not to exist. The fleet has been kept in launching condition for longer than most nations last, at real cost, in labor and timber and pitch, against the day the water comes home.

Outsiders find this unbearable in exactly the way some visitors find the Held Sea's pooling light unbearable, and for a related reason. Up there, in the standing dark, one pale ribbon-bodied swimmer still runs an errand between eleven correspondents, tirelessly, needlessly, forever — keeping a route because the route is what it is. Down here, the fleet is the town's Custodian. It is an errand still running. The difference — and the town would tell you it is the only difference that matters between the two places — is that up there, keeping costs nothing. The water does it; the equilibrium does it; permanence was made into a substance and the substance was made responsible. Down here keeping must be done by hand, annually, in weather, by people who age, out of materials that rot, and therefore — this is the town's entire theology, though they'd never dress it in that word — down here it is love. Above the shoreline ring, everything weathers eagerly, as if whatever in the world refuses to age was gathered into one place long ago and that place is not this one. The town keeps things anyway. That is the point. That has always been the point.

## The Town of the Rim

It has no name, and the lack is structural. The town's name was the lake's name — they were one word, as lakeside towns and their lakes often are — and when the water went up the mountain the name is understood to have gone with it, as fittings go with a house. The people say *here*, or *the shore*, and after a while so do you.

Eleven houses along the upper street stand empty. They are the houses of the eleven, and they are kept — swept, aired, reroofed when the roofs demand it — but not lived in and not entered except to keep them. Their door-lintels carry carved names, and the weather, which works honestly here, has worn those names shallow, so that you read them now more with your fingers than your eyes. Anyone from the town can recite them. No one from the town climbs the mountain to visit their owners. It is not fear and it is not law; when it is explained at all, it is explained the way you'd explain not calling on someone who has told you, kindly and permanently, that they are not at home: *you keep the house instead.*

Four thousand meters above, on the swept granite floor of the Held Sea, there is a twelfth foundation, dressed flat and knife-young, waiting. Here, inverted, is the answer to it: eleven kept houses, waiting. Each place holds room for exactly what the other is missing. The mountain keeps a place for the one who would not come; the valley keeps places for the eleven who would not stay. Between them the account is balanced to the last empty room, and neither ledger has been touched in two hundred years.

The twelfth house is different. The twelfth house is lived in. Its lintel is blank — never carved, the town says, *because a name is for the finished* — and the family in it now is no relation to the one who walked out across the wet lakebed and half walked back. They are tenants of an obligation, chosen generations ago and never released: it is the twelfth house that fills the lantern, every evening, and carries it out along the sanctioned path to the iron post at the end of the returning prints, and lights it, and comes home.

## The Lending

What the town believes is this, and they will tell it to you flatly, without ornament, on the rim, in the wind, where the basin cannot hear:

The lake was not taken. It was asked, and it consented, and it went — up the mountain in a manner no account describes, to be the medium in which eleven of the town's own people finished becoming themselves. It carried up its cold, its patience, and one white eyeless fish from its deepest reach, and it left behind its ear, its record-keeping floor, and its obligations. It is a loan. The proof of the loan is the Throat.

The Throat is a fissure at the basin's deepest point, near the eleven prints, and it is where every drop of rain that falls in the basin goes. Rain does not pool on the bed of the Lent Water. The floor refuses it — sheds it, all of it, in bright threads that run inward and downward and vanish into the dark of the fissure, and no spring below the rim, no well, no stream in any adjoining valley has ever been shown to receive it. The one water anyone connects to it is high on the mountain trail, two-thirds of the way to the Held Sea: a small cold spring, the last drink before the dry final third of the climb, which tastes — everyone who has drunk from both says the same thing, unprompted — like the wells of the rim town. The lake, they say, is still going. Drop by drop, storm by storm, the valley's water is still climbing toward the rest of itself, and has not, in two centuries, arrived: the Held Sea does not grow, does not admit, holds only what it knows. Where the climbing water waits, and what it is waiting for, the town believes it knows exactly.

It is waiting for the count to come right.

Because here the two places' hopes converge on a single act, from opposite sides, and the whole tension of the world hangs in the gap between them. Above, in the standing dark, three pulses rise through the water toward the ceiling where the unread shape circles: *still — here — come down.* An invitation two centuries long, from the eleven to the one who could not settle, to descend at last onto the swept twelfth foundation and finish the arrangement. And below, the town's oldest teaching, the thing said over the lakebed once a year with the names of the dead, holds that a lent thing comes home when its purpose is complete — that the Held Sea holds itself only because it is unfinished, an eleven-clause sentence still missing its final term, and that on the day the twelfth settles and the chord resolves, the water's long decision will conclude, and the meniscus will let go, and the sea will come down the mountain the way it went up, and fill its ring, and float its fleet, and the town will have its name again.

So the eleven knock, asking the twelfth to come down to *them*. And the valley waits, wanting exactly that, for exactly the opposite homecoming. And between the two — inside the sea against its silvered ceiling by one account, ended mid-stride on the open lakebed by the other, and possibly both, and possibly not knowing which itself — the twelfth continues not to decide. One refusal, held at altitude, keeps a sea standing and a basin empty. One unfinished person is the pin in the whole held world.

## Three Knocks

On the nights the pulses come, this is what happens in the valley, and it has happened for two hundred years.

Whoever keeps the twelfth house rises, and takes the path out onto the dark floor of the basin, past the roped-off prints, to the place where the returning footsteps end and the lantern burns on its post. There, where the bed has been swept down to true bedrock — the only bare stone on the whole vast floor, kept clean the way one keeps a room — the keeper crouches and knocks, three times, slowly, evenly, knuckles on the living granite root of the mountain.

The basin, being an ear, sends it everywhere. The rim hears it in its sleep. Whether the mountain hears it, whether it climbs the stone the way the rainwater climbs, whether it arrives in the standing dark as a small tick of glass, once an hour, that visitors to the Held Sea have never explained — the town does not claim to know. They only answer. They have only ever answered.

Three pulses down the mountain: *still — here — come down.*
Three knocks up through the stone: *still — here — come home.*

The same message, passing itself on the way. And in the black water above and the pale basin below, in the one long note and the one small lantern, the two halves of a single keeping go on holding their places — the sea that cannot fall, the shore that will not fill anything else with itself — while somewhere between them, against a silvered ceiling or at the end of a line of footprints, the last resident of both places circles the top of the world, two doors open beneath it, still deciding which way down is home.
