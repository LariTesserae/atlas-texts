# The Pattern Loft at Wrey

There is a name on the brass plate in the hollow — polished by a century of water, mostly readable — and the name is an address. Follow it out of the low wet country, against the grain of the rivers, north and up into the gray stone hills where the water runs fast and cold and shallow and has never once stood still long enough to go black. Three days' travel, or a week's, depending on the age you make the journey in. The last of it is a climb through a town built on iron money and now living quietly on the memory of it, streets of the same gray stone, and at the bottom of the town, by the river that gave the place its name and its power, the site of the foundry of Casker & Vane, Engineers & Ironfounders, Wrey.

The foundry is gone. This is the first thing you understand, and you understand it standing in the middle of where it was: the casting halls are down to their footings, the cupola was demolished within living memory of the oldest people here, the yard where the loaded barges tied up is a car park, or a sheep market, or grass, depending again on the age. Iron does not last. That is the joke the whole trade was built over and never told. Iron rusts to lace — you have seen it do so, far south, in a hollow under leaves — and buildings made to pour iron in go the same way once the pouring stops.

What lasts is at the back of the site, and it is made of wood.

One building stands: the old fitting shop, long and low, and above it, running the whole length of it, a timber-framed upper storey with a steep slate roof and a rank of small-paned windows facing north. You reach it by an external stair, also timber, and the treads of the stair are worn into shallow spoons — not in the middle, but in a slightly staggered line, left-right-left, the print of a particular stride climbing a particular way for a hundred and some years. You go up, because everything here is up: up out of the damp, up out of the river's reach, up into the dry. The door at the top is not locked. There has never been a reason to lock it. Nobody steals shapes.

Inside, the second thing you understand arrives as smell before it arrives as sight: shellac, linseed oil, old pine, and dust — a dryness so complete and so deliberate that after the hollow it feels like a held breath. The loft is perhaps a hundred and twenty feet long. Racks rise on both sides from floor to collar-beam, a single aisle down the middle, and the north light comes in flat and even through the little panes and lies along the shelves, and on the shelves, in their thousands, are the patterns.

You may need a moment with the word. A pattern is the wooden original of an iron thing. Before any casting was ever poured — any pump barrel, any valve chest, any gear wheel, grate, bracket, boiler fitting, or nameplate — a pattern-maker built it first in wood, full size, exact, allowing in his measurements for the shrinkage of cooling metal, and that wooden ancestor was pressed into sand to make the hollow the iron would fill. The iron went out into the world and did its work and rusted. The wood came back up the stairs and went on a shelf.

So the third understanding, the one that reorganizes the other two: this room is the foundry's entire life, in the ideal. Every object Casker & Vane ever made exists here in its first and most permanent form. Pine and mahogany, shellacked to a deep amber — amber applied by brush, layer on patient layer, and if that pricks at your memory it should, because you have seen amber laid down slowly over kept things before, in basins in a root-floor far away; the two ends of this story have separately arrived at the same method of not forgetting. The patterns are painted in a code older than anyone here: red on the faces that were to be machined true, black on the prints where cores would sit to make the casting hollow. Split patterns hang in their halves, dowelled, like shells. Core boxes sit beside them — the negatives of the negatives. Sizes run from a boxwood letter smaller than a thumbnail to a flywheel pattern taller than a person, standing at the far end like a rose window with no church. Each one is stamped with a job number, and every number is keyed to the ledgers at the desk by the door, and the ledgers are complete.

And the sound — the sound is the secret here too, and the secret is that there is almost none. The slates tick as the sun moves across them. The river says something continuous and far away under the north windows. Dust does whatever dust does, which is silent. In a day, the loudest thing that happens in this room is a shelf accepting the weight of the light.

Almost none. Not none. Stand long enough, on a still afternoon, and you will hear it: a faint dry knocking from up in the king post, irregular, patient, seven or eight taps and a wait. That is the resident population beginning to answer the census, and here, as in the hollow, the answers come in installments.

The first answer is the beetle.

There is, as far as the keeper has ever been able to determine, exactly one. It lives in the king post at the loft's midpoint and it is a deathwatch beetle, which announces itself the way its kind has always done, by knocking its head against the wood — a courtship signal, tapped out into a building where there is no other of its species to hear. It has been tapping for years. In any other timber building it would be a sentence of execution, and the keeper knows precisely where it is and could end it in an afternoon and has never done so, for two reasons. The first is that it only knocks when the air goes damp — dry wood bores badly, and the beetle stirs when moisture creeps in — so it is, in practice, the loft's instrumentation: a living gauge mounted in the one member that holds the whole roof, sounding the alarm for the only enemy this place has. Water. Everything in this room is at war with water, which is a thing to sit with, given where the room's iron went. The second reason is the one the keeper would not say aloud: a building this quiet needs a pulse, even a false one, even one that is a question asked over and over into a silence that will never once answer it. Far south, a single green moth holds station on warm brick, mouthless, reading the air for a landlord with no eyes. The trade, it seems, issues one small watchful life per premises, and no more.

The second answer is the keeper, and she is the fifth of her line, and none of the five were kin.

The post passes by aptitude. Her predecessor chose her the way his predecessor chose him — a long apprenticeship of dusting, then of oiling the hinged patterns so their leaves swing sweet, then of shellac, then of the ledgers, then of the saw — and one day the older keeper stopped climbing the stair and the younger one kept climbing it, and that was the whole ceremony. Her wage arrives quarterly by standing order from the county bank, drawn on an account whose name has been abbreviated by four generations of clerks copying it forward until it is no longer a name at all, only a habit with money in it. Nobody at the bank knows what the account is for. Nobody has ever canceled it. You have met this before: things here do not run because anyone runs them; they run because no one has stopped them.

Her duties are the duties of the dry. She keeps the small stove going in the worst of winter — low, even, never enough to check the wood — and its flue draws with a faint flute-note that would sound familiar to you, and above the roofline it is the loft's one deliberate opening, its regulated breath. She hunts damp along the sills. She waxes. She keeps the ledgers current, which mostly means writing, at each year's end, the same phrase against page after page: *held against reorder.* And once in a great while — every few years now — she works, because the foundry that pours from these patterns is not quite extinct. Casker & Vane died, but a small jobbing foundry two valleys over still casts drain grates for three municipalities from patterns carried down these stairs and carried back up, and when a grate pattern comes back worn, she cuts and glues and fairs a new one, red paint on the machined faces, and the shape goes on. Understand what that means: the loft outlived its own foundry. The wood outlived the iron *here, too.* Iron was only ever the message. Wood is the language it was written in, and the language survives the letters.

Her round of the racks is made at a stride, one bay per pace, her hand touching each shelf-end without breaking step. She did not invent the stride. It is in the stairs; it was in her predecessor; it is, though she has no way of knowing this, the exact interval of a row of resin-taps along a living wall a hundred miles south, set there by some other worker's walking pace and kept by a grove the way she keeps the grates. Both places retain their dead the same way: not as ghosts, as measurements.

Which is the third answer, the tenant proper, because the tenant of the loft is not the keeper any more than the tenant of the hollow was the grove. The keeper is staff. What *lives* here — what the whole long dry room is a body for — is the promise. Every pattern on every shelf is a sentence in the conditional: *we can make another.* That is all a pattern is; that is all this room has ever housed. Not objects — capacities. The loft is a building-sized *in case*. Its tenant is the reorder that has not come yet, thousands of them, stacked to the collar-beams, each one immortal for the simplest of reasons: a promise cannot die of old age, only of cancellation, and nobody cancels what they have forgotten they were promised. The hollow's tenant is a job that never ended. The loft's tenant is an offer that never lapsed. They are the two halves of one contract, breathing at either end of it.

And so, the last entry in the census, which is a shelf.

Bay nineteen, north side, at reach height. The job numbers run 2,214 through 2,231, and the ledger line against them, copied forward in five hands over a hundred-odd years, reads: *pump work — barrel, valve chest, clack, gudgeons, nameplate &c. — the Level works, Fenholt — customer's property, held against reorder until released.*

The customer never released. So the shelf holds. The great clack-valve pattern is there, hinged, its leaf swinging sweet on oiled dowels because the keeper oils it with the rest, its seating face painted red for machining that will never be ordered. The pump-barrel pattern is there in its two amber halves. And at the shelf's end, small, is the nameplate pattern: the firm's name and the date carved proud in boxwood by some apprentice a century and a half gone, every letter crisp — the wooden father of the brass plate lying half-buried in a root-floor in the low country, where the letters have gone soft under a hundred years of running water. The name exists twice in the world now, wood and brass, dry and drowned, and only the wood is still fully legible.

In the plan drawer beneath the ledger desk there is a rolled drawing, general arrangement, pumping engine, Fenholt, and in its title block, in faded ink, in an engineer's careful round hand, the line that would stop your breath if you carried the hollow with you up those stairs: *stroke — 15 to the minute.*

Fifteen to the minute. One every four seconds. The beat you felt through your knees in the root-floor, the thump-and-sigh that the grove inherited from the dead engine and has kept, faithful past all reason, in valves of living tissue — it was never a rhythm the iron made up. It was written. It was set down in this dry room, on this paper, by a man with a rule and a preference, before the boiler was ever poured, and it was shipped south down the Wrey by barge with the iron that carried it, and the iron rusted to lace and the beat did not, because the beat was never in the iron. The beat was a specification, and specifications, as both these buildings know, are the only immortal residents there are.

The river makes the connection literal, if you want it literal. The Wrey runs cold under the loft's north windows and turns south and slows and spreads, and its water reaches the Levels inside a fortnight, and some of it, some of every fortnight's worth, seeps under a root-floor and rises through hollow boles on a four-second count and weeps down leaf-walls into a black pool where something old and unpigmented has been listening to arriving Wrey water since before the closure. The keeper spends her life holding this water out of her building. A hundred miles south, the same water *is* the building. The trade's two survivors, drowned and desiccated, each keeping perfect faith with its half of a contract only they remember.

Neither knows the other continues. This is the thing to carry back down the stairs. The loft holds the Fenholt shapes because the customer never wrote to release them, and the customer never will, and so the loft is correct to hold. And the customer — patient, punctual, incurious, entirely without ambition — has meanwhile begun to solve its supply problem in-house: somewhere under the leaves, at the pace of resin, a root is being fair'd to the old lines, wood copying iron that was copied from wood, the shape crossing back into the language it was drafted in. Third generation. The pattern-makers of Wrey would have checked its work with calipers and passed it.

Some still afternoon, the keeper makes her round, bay by bay at the inherited stride, and pauses at nineteen, and touches the clack-valve pattern the way she touches everything, to know it is dry. Dust climbs into the north light. In the king post the beetle knocks its question and waits. And far south, on the same afternoon, in a hollow with no horizon, a valve of living tissue closes on its count — one stroke of fifteen to the minute, right on the number sleeping in the drawer beneath her hand — and both ends of the contract, having heard nothing from each other in a hundred years, are exactly, punctually, permanently on schedule.
