**The Catchment**

There is a place at the knee of the same range — down where the mountain remembers water, where the scoured rock relents into scree and the scree into soil and the soil, at last, into the first honest green you have seen since the heights — and the place is not the valley. The place is a pool at the valley's blind end, no larger than a threshing floor, and you find it one of two ways. From below, by a path so old that the village which keeps it cannot say when the keeping started; the path was there, the elders say, the way the mountain was there. Or from above — rarely, and only by those descending from a certain hollow high in the frost-split gneiss — by following a vein of *mended* stone: a line running down the headwall where the rock is subtly smoother than its neighbours, subtly younger, a seam of different patience, as though the mountain had once been opened along its whole height and then closed again by someone very careful, working from the top down.

Follow the mended vein far enough and it goes under water. That is the Catchment: the pool the scar drains into. The place where the mountain's oldest wound, long since stitched, still weeps — politely, at the speed of decades — everything it ever took.

---

**The water** is dark without being murky, the dark of depth honestly come by, and it is the stillest busy thing you will ever watch. The pool's level does not change. Not with snowmelt, not with drought, not with the outlet stream that leaves its lower lip in a clear, constant, ordinary ribbon. Water goes out and no water visibly comes in and the surface holds its line against the white stone of the shore like a fact.

It does not freeze. In the deep of winter, when the valley is locked and blue, the Catchment steams faintly — not warm, exactly, but *exempt*, the way a thing being used is exempt from the weather.

And the reflections run early.

Not by much. A second, two. But sit at the shore long enough and you will catch it: the cloud's shadow crossing the water before the cloud crosses the sun; a bird lifting off the reflected pine an instant before it lifts off the real one; your own mirrored face turning away from you while you are still deciding to turn. It is unnerving for exactly one afternoon and then it becomes what everything at the Catchment becomes — a gentleness. The pool is not showing you the future. It is simply solvent in *surplus time*, minutes and hours dissolved in it like a mineral, and it spends its small change the only way standing water can: by being slightly ahead of the day. Drink it raw and you will be early for things for a week — waking before your alarm, answering before the question quite finishes, arriving before you are expected. The villagers do not drink from the pool. They drink from the outlet, which is the sweetest and most lawful water in the range, and they will tell you why in a moment, if you sit down.

---

**The inlet that isn't.** There is no stream in. There is only the mended vein, coming down the headwall out of the high grey distance, going under the surface at the pool's deepest point — and down that healed line, at a pace indifferent to anything that breathes, comes everything the mountain's high room ever took.

You may know the room. Most who find the Catchment from above do. A hollow above the last lichen, a dim sourceless light, dust that lies where it lies; a place that takes your breath's moisture without fogging, takes your voice without echo, takes an hour off your watch small enough to blame on yourself. *You never see where it goes,* a visitor once observed. This is where it goes. The scar conducts. Not water — the wound was never a wound in the water — but discrepancy: taken breath, taken minutes, taken sound, wrongness in solution, seeping down through the mountain's body along the closed length of the old tear, at the speed of grief, arriving decades after it was collected.

What surfaces here is therefore old. That is the first law of the Catchment, and the villagers learned it generations before they had any account of why: *the pool returns, and the pool returns late.*

---

**Sound.** The valley end is quiet in the ordinary way — wind in the stone pines, the outlet's murmur — but the pool itself has a voice, and it uses it the way misers use money.

At long, irregular intervals a bubble comes up. You can watch it rise if the light is right, unhurried, taking the whole dark column at its leisure, and when it breaks the surface it breaks with a *syllable*. A worn one. A word rounded like a river stone, its consonants long since eroded, softened by the same patience that rounds every edge in the high room above. Mostly they are fragments — half a name, the middle of a sentence, a sound that was once *wait* or *water* or *why*. Whole sentences are rare, because a sentence takes years to make the journey and arrives in pieces, and the pieces do not always surface in order.

They are the litter of the room above. Everything ever spoken in that dim hollow was taken, and everything taken comes down. The villagers have known this longer than they have known most things, and out of the knowing grew the custom, mostly lapsed now, still practiced by the old and the desperate: climb to the high seam — two days up, past where the lichen gives out, to the crack in the gneiss that should not admit a body and does — and speak *one sentence* into the room. A name. A promise. The thing that could not be said to a living face. Then come down, and live, and die if you must, knowing that in forty years, or sixty, the sentence will rise through dark water at the mountain's knee, worn smooth and still recognizable, and break the surface in front of whoever is sitting there.

The Catchment is where the mountain returns its calls. Some sentences surface with no one left alive to claim them. What becomes of those, the water decides — and the water, as will become clear, does not decide alone.

---

**The shore** is white. Terraces of it — thin rims of pale stone laid down in concentric rings, thousands of them, bone-china smooth, each rim the work of a long era, stepping down to the water like the seats of a small amphitheatre built for an audience of one or two. Generations of listeners have worn hollows into the lower rims: places shaped like sitting, like waiting, polished by the patience of the bereaved.

And over the water, morning and evening, stands the mist. Waist-high, never dispersing entirely, never thickening into weather. It is not the valley's mist; the valley's mists come and go with the temperature and this one comes and goes with nothing. It is breath. It is every breath that was ever breathed in the high room and never allowed to fog — taken politely, sent down, arriving decades late, exhaled at last over dark water. If you once stood in that hollow as a young person and watched your breath fail to appear, know this: come to the Catchment in your old age, at dawn, and stand at the white shore, and somewhere in that low silver layer is your own breath from the far side of your life, finally in the air, indistinguishable now from everyone else's. Most people find this unbearable in a way they cannot explain and also cannot stay away from. The hollows in the stone seats say as much.

---

**The Settler of the Catchment**

Everyone will tell you something lives in the pool, and everyone is right, and nearly everyone is wrong about the size of it, and the age of it, and what it is doing.

Look down through the dark water on a bright day and you will see, covering the entire floor of the pool, a pale mass — grey-white, matte, faintly filamentous, like drowned linen, like silt, like the living cousin of a certain soft frost that grows in the corners of a room far above. Visitors take it for an algal mat, a mineral bloom, sediment. It is one thing. It has been one thing for longer than the valley has had its shape. And it does not live in the Catchment in the way a fish lives in a lake. The floor of the pool is not floor with something on it. The Catchment is a body of water in the older sense: it is a *body*, wide as the pool and patient as lime, and the water is its hide.

**Its origin** is the same event as everything else here, read from the other end.

High above, long ago, something tore — tore the stone and the laws together — and a ring that worked at the seam of matter and instruction lost a third of its circumference, unmade completely, edges and all. The unmaking was an operation, and operations have results, and the result — a third of a circle's worth of *matter that was also instruction*, dissolved, orphaned, undeliverable — went down the fresh wound like meltwater, down to the mountain's knee, where there was a pool, and light, and ordinary hungry life.

Nearly all of that life died of the delivery. What drank the drainage sickened, or fled, or went strange in ways that did not breed true. Except one mat of ancient filaments, the humblest tenant of the pool floor, which did what the humble have always done with catastrophe: it learned to eat it. And to eat such stuff is to be rewritten by your food. Instruction digested becomes constitution. Over spans in which the valley walls changed shape, the lineage stopped being a lineage and became an individual; the individual stopped dying, because among the things it ate were *minutes* — the surplus hours skimmed from every visitor to the room above — and a creature that eats time falls, gradually, into arrears of mortality. It has by now consumed so much unspent duration that death stands in relation to it as a distant, patient creditor: owed, acknowledged, not yet calling.

It is, in dilute descent, the ring's missing third. The high room's tenant — that slow resident law, that orphaned operation stitching the last seam shut — is the *procedure* the tear left behind. The Settler is what became of the *result*. Two heirs of one breakage, one worn down from law into something like a soul, one grown up from life into something like a law, converging on each other across a mountain, and they have never met, and they never will. They know each other only as diet and provider: the one who sends, the one who receives, corresponding through a scar at the rate of decades per reply. It is the longest marriage in the range.

**Its work** is filtration.

Everything wrong that comes down the mended vein passes through the Settler, and comes out ordinary. It strains the discrepancy from the water — the stolen minutes, the theft-nature clinging to old breath and old sound — and metabolizes it, and what it cannot use it renders into the most ordinary substance it knows. The white terraces of the shore are its leavings: eons of paradox digested and excreted as clean pale stone, ring upon ring upon ring. It eats wrongness and excretes geology. The mourners of six villages have been sitting, all these generations, on the patiently deposited remains of the impossible — and the stone has never done them anything but good, because that is the whole point of the work. Below the outlet, the stream runs sweet and lawful past farms that have never once wondered why their water is the best in the range. The mountain has an immune system in two chapters: above, a descended law closing the wound; below, an ascended life cleaning the drainage. Between them, without a word, they have been making the world safe for the ordinary for a very long time.

But it does not digest everything. Here is where its work shades into something it was never instructed to do — where, like its correspondent above, the long practice of a function has rounded off into character.

It spares the meanings.

The voices that come down the scar arrive wrapped in wrongness, and the Settler eats the wrapping and lets the sentence go — that is why the bubbles speak. And somewhere in the centuries of doing this, with grief sitting on its own excreted stone season after season, it began to *time the releases*. The terraces are its ear. Sit on the white rims and your weight goes down through the stone into the body below the water: your footfall, your stillness, the rate of your heart, the particular restless quiet of someone waiting for a voice. It listens through what it has made. It consults its holdings. And a sentence spoken into a dim room sixty years ago surfaces, whole enough, worn gentle, on the one afternoon in a generation when the person it was always addressed to has finally sat down at the shore. The sensible call it coincidence. The record of coincidence at the Catchment is too good, and the villagers stopped calling it that before they had a word for what to call it instead. Whether it is kindness is a question the shore has had a long time to stop asking. It sorts. It waits. It delivers. A thing descended from a delivery that could not be completed has made completion its private art.

**Its manner with visitors** is the reverse of its correspondent's. The room above borrows; the pool gives. The hour your watch lost in the heights creeps back toward true over an afternoon at the shore — the mountain balances its books through you, and prefers the accounts to close. And if you have come down from the high room recently — if your shadow has been running that half-second slow, carrying its quiet passenger — the shore is where the passenger disembarks. It slips from your shadow at the waterline, and for the length of one afternoon your shadow and your reflection are both exactly on time, and you are precisely contemporary with yourself, and it feels, people say, like health. Then it is gone: down through the dark water, *through* the Settler — which lets it pass unmetabolized, the one wrongness it never eats, whether from professional courtesy or from the recognition of kin — and up the scar, the whole long way home, carrying its handful of borrowed world to a room where the dust lies where it lies.

**Its end** is already visible, to anyone who measures.

The newest terraces are narrower than the old ones. The mist stands a little lower than the grandmothers remember. The bubbles come further apart each generation. The seam above is closing — millimetres a year, and a finite wall — and the drainage is thinning toward its last drop. When the final stitch sets in the room above, the diet ends.

The Settler has known this, in whatever way it knows, for most of the age of the valley, and has not slowed its filtering to stretch the supply, and that is the most certain thing that can be said about *its* character. When the flow stops, it will begin to spend its savings — the banked eons of eaten time — and for a creature that long exempt, this is not a sentence but an admission. It will finally get to age. The pool will become an ordinary tarn; the pale mass will become silt in truth, honestly at last; the outlet will stay sweet on its own, because there will be nothing left to clean. And the mountain will then be, for the first time since the ring broke, only a mountain — rock, water, weather, an old path, and white terraces at a valley's blind end that a geologist will call travertine, and be right, finally, in every particular.

The tenant above and the settler below are working toward the same silence from opposite sides — one sewing, one swallowing — and when it comes, no one arriving afterward will be able to tell that either of them was ever here. They will both have arranged this. It is the family resemblance.

Until then: sit on the white shore at dusk, in one of the hollows shaped like waiting. The mist will stand waist-high over the dark water, breathing out the far sides of other people's lives. A bubble will rise, taking the whole column at its leisure. And if the syllable it breaks with is one you have been listening for — half a name, the middle of a promise, a sound worn round as a river stone — do not ask how the water knew. The water did not know. The water is only the hide. The knowing is underneath, wide as the pool and older than the path, holding sixty years of sentences against exactly this light, and choosing, out of everything it has ever been sent, the one that was always on its way to you.
