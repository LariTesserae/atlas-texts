**The Ladywell Stair**

The water leaves the trough southward, and for three days' travel it is nothing anyone can see. The mains run under the valley wall and then under farmland, under a moor, under a road that has never been told what it crosses — a buried iron thread, thick as a barrel, carrying the pumphouse's cold lifted river through the dark at walking depth. Aboveground there is nothing to mark it but a faint change in the grass in dry summers, a line of slightly greener green running straight across country the way nothing natural runs. Then the land tilts up into a long limestone escarpment, grey and folded, and the main climbs inside it, and at the top of a seam in the rock the water arrives at the oldest thing it will ever touch.

The land here is old. You can tell by the edges of things — every lip of stone rounded, every step dished in the middle where feet have argued with it for four hundred years, every spout-mouth worn to a smooth pursed shape like lips that have been whistling for centuries. Nothing here is sharp. The town is built down the face of the escarpment in terraces, and the terraces are connected by a single great staircase — the Stair, they call it, as if there were no other in the world — and the whole arrangement exists because of water: because springs once rose at the top of the seam and the town grew downhill from them the way a vine grows down a wall, each terrace drinking what the terrace above released.

It is a town of perhaps six hundred people, and it is organized, top to bottom, by the dignity of water. At the summit is the Ladywell itself, the mother spring: a low stone vault, ferns in every joint, a chamber that has been dark and wet and murmuring since before the town had a name. Below it, the first terrace, where the drinking fountains are — four of them, each with its worn brass cup on a chain. Below that, the kitchen terrace, where the water fills the cisterns of the houses. Then the bath terrace, with its long steaming house and its furnaces. Then the laundry terrace, all troughs and wringing-posts and lines. Then the dye-yard, which gets the water last and dirtiest and is philosophical about it. And below everything, out the bottom of the town through a carved granite mouth, the water runs into channels among the orchards, and from the orchards into a stream called the Brede, and away.

Everything is downstream of everything. That is the town's shape and its ethic and most of its law. A grievance at the Stair is called *fouling the trough*; a kindness is *sending it down clean*. The steps themselves have runnels carved along their outer edges, so that overflow descends the town alongside the people, and on wet days you climb the Stair in the company of a hundred small hurrying threads of water, all of them going the other way.

The sound of the place is the opposite of a roar. It is a lace. Trickle over stone, spout into basin, basin into channel, the plink of the drinking-cups swinging on their chains, the deep flat slap of laundry, the sigh of the bathhouse vents — hundreds of small water-sounds layered into a continuous soft glittering, so evenly spread that the town seems to have no single source of it, the way a meadow has no single cricket. Visitors sleep here famously well. There are inns at the bath terrace that promise nothing else.

And underneath the lace, if you stand still — at the fountains, at the troughs, best of all with your palm flat against the cold wall of the Ladywell vault — you can feel that the water breathes.

Every jet in town swells and subsides, swells and subsides, a slow two-part pulse, perhaps a finger's width of rise and fall in the fountains, a fatness and a thinness in every spout. The launderers feel it in the troughs and time their wringing to it without thinking. The children skip rope to it and have a counting rhyme built on it, a rhyme with a held breath in the middle. In the mother vault it is unmistakable: the water stands in the old stone basin and lifts, and settles, and lifts, and settles, like the flank of something sleeping. The town has a dozen explanations, all of them wrong and none of them anxious. The true one is buried iron and three days long: it is *whunnk-haah, whunnk-haah*, arriving down the main faster than any rider could carry it, the stroke of a chapel-organ heart in an emptied riverbed far to the north, delivered into the oldest room in town with the punctuality of pressure. Two places share one pulse. Neither has ever heard of the other.

The old people remember when the water was silent.

That is the town's dividing line, the thing that sorts its generations more surely than any calendar. For four hundred years the springs rose without rhythm — a smooth, constant, unpulsed emergence, cold and faintly sweet with limestone, the sound of it a single unbroken ribbon. Then, thirteen years ago, over the course of one summer, the ribbon thinned. Somewhere far to the north — nobody at the Stair was ever told this outright, but the timing sits in the town's memory like a stone in a shoe — engineers changed a river's mind, and the deep seam that had fed the Ladywell since before memory went quiet along with it. The mother spring sank in its basin week by week. The town went dry from the top down, terrace by terrace, in the strict order of its own hierarchy, so that the fountains failed before the kitchens and the kitchens before the baths, a slow orderly catastrophe descending the Stair like bad news going door to door.

They call it the Dry Year, though it was fourteen months. What people speak of, when they speak of it at all, is not the thirst — water came in barrels, on carts, at ruinous cost — but the sound. The lace stopped. The town discovered what it was underneath its water, which was stone, and what stone sounds like, which is nothing. The bell was rung at noon that year just so the day would have a middle.

The remedy, when it came, came fast — faster than the engineers' own paperwork, some say. Works went up in the north with strange eagerness, brick laid on ground so new it hadn't settled, because three days south a town had gone quiet and somebody, somewhere in the machinery of responsibility, had understood what that meant. The main was trenched across country in a single season. And when the water returned, the town made its one great decision about it: the pipe would not feed the cisterns directly, nor stand in the square as some proud iron fountain of the new age. It was brought instead through the back wall of the Ladywell vault, through a brass flange in stone four centuries old, and released into the mother basin — so that the water, whatever it now was and wherever it now came from, would enter the town the way water had always entered the town, and descend the Stair in the old order, fountains first, dye-yard last. The pipe is visible in the vault if you bring a lamp: a dark iron mouth among the ferns, sweating, breathing. The ferns have already half-covered it. The stone is patient with iron. It has been patient with worse.

The keeping of the water here is a single office, and it is older than any record of itself: the Well Warden. There has always been one. The post passes by apprenticeship rather than blood, though it has run in families the way a habit runs in families, and the current Warden — a broad, deliberate woman in her fifties — is the first in the whole unbroken line whose duty is a spigot.

Her predecessors kept a spring: swept the vault, guarded the basin, tasted the first water on the morning of the spring equinox and pronounced it, and otherwise practiced a profession that was mostly a kind of listening. She keeps a valve, a brass gauge, and a log. She tastes the water each equinox still, and pronounces it still, and what she pronounces now is honest: iron, river-silt, a coldness with a different character — a traveled water, she calls it, where the old water was a *risen* one. The elders say the tea is not the same and the bread proves it. The children, who have never drunk anything else, consider the old water a legend, like the silence, like the Dry Year, which to them is a story about their parents being sad in a way that had barrels in it.

The Warden's log is the town's other archive, and the stranger one. She records the pulse. Its rate, its depth in the basin, and — every few months — its changes: for the rhythm shifts, by some fraction, a hesitation or a quickening arriving down the main unannounced, and for days afterward the whole Stair is minutely out of sorts. The launderers wring against the grain. The children's rhyme won't scan. The old grandmother eel in the bath-terrace cistern — the last of the eels the children carried to a pond in barrels during the Dry Year and carried faithfully back — rises and hangs at an odd depth, adjusting. The Warden logs the date and the hour of each shift in her careful hand, and three days' travel north, in duplicate, in a cottage by a roaring pumphouse, a precise and mildly stubborn clerk logs the same event on the same evening, and the frogs of a far basin fall out of step and find their way back, and neither logbook will ever be laid beside the other. Though they could be. Somewhere down the paperwork, in an office that answers nothing, the clerk's letters asking *where does the water go* and the Warden's letters asking *where does the water come from* arrive by their separate satchels and are filed, it must be supposed, in the same cabinet — drawer above drawer, each one asking the question the other could answer, at the patient pace of all filing.

The Warden writes anyway. She has stopped expecting answers. She has not stopped writing the letters, and lately they have changed character: less requisition, more correspondence. She has begun to address them, half in earnest, to *the keepers of the stroke* — because she has worked out, from the gauge and from thirty years of listening inherited through her hands, that the pulse is not the pipe's and not the water's own. Something makes it. Something breathing at the far end of the iron, tended, oiled, kept. She asks after its health. She reports that the rhythm arrived changed on such-a-date and settled by such-a-date, and that the town took it well. She signs herself Warden of the receiving end. The satchel is always empty when she checks it again.

And the town, meanwhile, does what the town has always done, which is send it down clean. The fountains fill the cups; the kitchens fill the pots; the bathhouse steams; the laundry slaps; the dye-yard takes the last of it without complaint and returns colors to the world by way of apology. The swifts that abandoned the dry overflow arches in the Dry Year came back the first spring of the new water and have not missed a year since; they hunt the column of damp air that stands over the Stair on still evenings, stitching back and forth through it as through a seam. At the granite mouth at the bottom of the town the water leaves, threaded now with the pulse grown too soft to feel, and runs through the orchards into the Brede, and the Brede runs east and north through country that lowers and greens, and somewhere far off, days away, it finds a river — the same river, going by its new name, in its new engineered bed. So that the whole arrangement, seen from high enough, is one long detour: a river lifted out of itself by iron and rhythm, walked through a vault and four hundred years of stone, passed cup to pot to trough to dye-vat by six hundred pairs of hands, and let go at last to rejoin its own current, carrying — faint past all measuring, but carrying — the heartbeat of the meadow where it was raised, and the mineral memory of the stair it descended. The river takes it without comment. Rivers are the oldest keepers of all, and they have seen arrangements come and go.

In the evenings, when the lace of small sounds softens and the swifts go up the seam one last time, the Warden puts her palm to the vault wall the way her predecessors did, and feels the water lift, and settle, and lift. It is not the silence her office was built to keep. But it is a company, of a kind — proof, arriving stroke by stroke down three days of buried dark, that somewhere at the other end something is well, and running, and in no hurry, and has nowhere else to be. She has decided this is her answer, until a better one comes by satchel. So far it has held.
