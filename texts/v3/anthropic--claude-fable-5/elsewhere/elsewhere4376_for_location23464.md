# The Working Sea

There is a direction the Burl Town does not name, because its people had no word for *down* that was not also a word for *in*. But the town points there all the same. Every bored tube in every wall, unstoppered, breathes the same faint sweetness with the same faint edge under it. Every tube slants the same way. The channels, the pipes, the tap-holes, the whole hidden plumbing of the dead town converges, somewhere past the reach of any lamp that was never carried there, on a single deep gallery — and the daily breath, the great tuneless exhalation that sounds the town like an instrument, comes up that gallery from somewhere below, arriving already warm, already sweet, already old.

The breath is a thread. It can be followed backward. The people followed it, twice — once up, at the beginning of everything, and once down, at the end — and the road they carved for the following is still there.

## The Long Bore

It begins beneath the market floor, behind a stopper the size of a door: a descending passage cut through the living tissue of the plant, wide enough for two to walk abreast, dropping in long patient spirals through the body of the world. It goes down for days.

It was made by the same hands that made the town, and it carries the same signature — corners rounded against injury in the dark, walls smoothed to the body, the incised dot-script set at intervals exactly where a trailing fingertip finds it. But the Bore has one feature the town does not, and it takes a while, descending, to understand what you are reading with your palms. Both walls are polished. Not evenly: each wall carries its sheen at a slightly different height, the height of hands steadying themselves against a slope — one wall worn by travelers climbing, one by travelers going down. The road remembers two migrations, ages apart, moving in opposite directions, and it holds them the way the town holds everything: as a difference in the smoothness under your hand.

The descent changes as it goes. The dry percussive ticking of the upper world — the walls cracking their knuckles — thins out and falls behind, and a new sound rises to meet you from below, continuous where the ticking was broken, round where it was sharp: a vast soft seething, like distant surf, like a held murmur, like something very large simmering with the lid on. The air warms. The sweetness thickens. And then, far down, comes the thing that no one raised in the Burl Town would believe, the thing the whole upper town was built in longing for and never saw: in one of the carved channels grooved along the wall — the same channels the town carvers cut into every surface, faithfully, uselessly, for generations — a bead of moisture. Then a trickle. Then the channels are running, all of them, doing at last the work they were always shaped for, and you understand that the town's driest habit was not a mistake. It was an architecture carried uphill from a wet homeland, built and rebuilt in the dark out of fidelity, for centuries, by people who had never seen it work.

The channels were never a failure of engineering. They were a memory of this place.

## The Shore

The Bore opens high in the wall of a hollow so large that the darkness changes character. The town's dark is the dark of a sealed seed — close, pressing, roofed. This dark has weather in it. It has distance. Sound goes out into it and does not come back, or comes back changed, minutes later, from directions that mean nothing. The air is warm and dense and heavy with sweetness gone complicated — bright, yeasty, alive — and it will not entirely keep you: the breathable air floats in a layer under the vault, and below a certain height the atmosphere grows thick and drowsy, so that the shore has, in effect, a ceiling of breath, and to go too low for too long is to lie down and sleep. The people knew this. Their steps and platforms hug the survivable altitudes with the precision of a people who mapped an invisible surface by the cost of ignoring it.

From the mouth of the Bore, a stair descends the terraces.

The terraces are the first thing, and in some ways they are everything: broad concentric shelves of hardened sap, ring below ring below ring, stepping down into the dark like the tiers of an amphitheater. Each one is a fossil shoreline. The sea below has been falling for ages — slowly, at the pace of everything here — and every level it held for long enough left a rim of itself behind, candied, set, gone to amber. The highest terraces are ancient and glassy, deep gold, hard as flint. The lower ones are younger, cloudier, faintly tacky at the newest edge. Walking down the stair is walking down through time at the rate of one era per step, on treads made of the sea's own former surfaces, and the amber is not empty. The amber holds things. But that is the second part of this account, and it should wait, the way it makes you wait.

At the bottom of everything, past the last set terrace, is the living edge. And past the edge is the sea.

## The Sea

It is not water. Nothing here was ever water. It is the sap — the same slow, thick, faintly sweet substance that seeped up the tubes and fed the town — gathered here in its origin and its abundance, a sea of it, filling the floor of the hollow out beyond any shore that has ever been walked to its end. The people's dot-script, where it speaks of the far side, uses a sign that also means *the rest of a sentence you have fallen asleep in the middle of*.

And it is not still. That is the thing the murmur was telling you all the way down: the sea *works*. Its whole surface is in slow, ceaseless, small commotion — a fine seethe, a lace of foam forming and folding under, low domes of gas swelling up through the thickness over hours and releasing with soft enormous sighs that roll away across the dark. Ranges of set froth stand here and there like reefs, foam gone hard, porous and pale. The sea is warm; the whole hollow is warmed by it; the warmth is the heat of its working, because the sea is not merely liquid. The sea is alive.

It is the wild ferment. It is the thing the town kept tamed in its stopped tubes, met here in its original condition: not a culture in a vessel but a single continuous living community the size of a sea, sugar-eating, patient, ancient past reckoning, working the plant's slow sweetness the way it has worked it since before the plant was the only plant. Every tamed strain in every sealed tube of the Burl Town is descended from a cup dipped out of this. The tubes are an archipelago. This is the mother.

And here the shape of the whole world quietly reorganizes itself around you. Because the plant feeds the sea — the walls of the hollow weep sap inward, everywhere, forever — and the sea, working, gives back warmth and chemistry into the tissue that holds it, and somewhere in the exchange between them is the answer to the oldest question in the upper dark: how a plant lives with no leaves, no light, no sun, no need. It does not live alone. It gave up the sky when it took in the sea. The world is not a plant with a hollow in it. The world is a partnership — a vast slow body and the vast quick culture banked at its center like a hearth that is not fire — the two of them running a single closed economy of sweetness, one thinking in rings, one in generations of motes: the slow twin and the fast twin, keeping house.

The Burl Town was a room in that house. It was never anything else.

## The Breath

Once a day — though nothing here marks days either — the sea gathers itself.

You feel it first as a stillness, which down here is a loud thing: the seethe hesitates, the foam-lace holds, the pressure in your ears goes strange. Then the whole surface heaves — not a wave, a *lift*, acres of it rising together — and the accumulated gas of a day's working goes up and out through the vault's galleries and bores in one long releasing rush. Down here it is not music and not sighing. Down here it is simply departure, a body of air the size of a weather system leaving home, and it takes the long road: up the Bore, through the tubes, into the pocket high above where a hundred carved apertures are waiting to catch it and round it and make it enormous.

The town's daily breath is the sea's exhale. The tenant who walks the town's passages every day, sounding the pipes, making the rounds the dead composed for it — it is born here, each day, at this surface. Stand at the shore when it goes and you can hear the beginning of the town's vast sighing as a small thing, local, almost intimate: a rush finding the mouth of the Bore, where the carvers — and this is theirs, unmistakably, the oldest worked surface in the world — cut a single tuned aperture into the rock-hard amber of the highest terrace. The first pipe. The mother of every pipe in the town. It sounds one low note as the breath enters the road, and then the breath is gone, and far above, minutes later, a dead town plays through its whole civic music to the end.

And the road runs both ways. What goes up must be replaced; a slow return draught falls down the Bore for hours after, carrying the upper world with it — its cool, its dryness, its dust. The sea drinks that draught. It is the sea's only news of above, and the sea, unlike the wood, is quick: a ferment answers its atmosphere in days, not centuries. Once, the returning air came down warm with hearths and bodies and the fatty sweetness of a living town, and the sea's edge answered it — the people's records say the shoreline *bloomed* toward the Bore's mouth, that the foam stood taller under the road, that the sea leaned. Then one day, ages ago, the draught came down carrying nothing but emptiness, and the next day the same, and the next.

The plant, remember, is still finding out. The news of the people's absence is still traveling inward through its rings and will not finish arriving for ages. The sea has known since the first empty draught. It has known longer than anyone. Of the two members of the household, the slow one is still learning of the loss, and the fast one has been living with it, daily, for ten thousand years.

# What the Sea Keeps

The town keeps its people as a polish — decisions preserved in force, a courtesy still extended. The sea keeps differently. The sea keeps in amber, and it keeps in rings, and to walk the terraces from top to bottom is to read the only complete history of the people that exists anywhere, written in the one medium they never had to carve: time itself, candied.

## The High Rings

The oldest terraces, glassy and deep gold near the vault, hold the beginning. Small things, dim shapes suspended in the ancient amber where the sea's edge took them as it fell: the first thorn-tools, cruder than anything in the town. Dipping-bowls. And in one high ring, famous to no one, a small carved vessel with a residue in it that is the residue of a *kept* culture — the domestication itself, the first cup of the sea taken up and tended and fed, the moment the people's one animal was tamed. It happened here. The tube-gardens of the Burl Town, every diverged and drifted strain of them, descend from a cup that is still visible, at a certain angle, by feel, in the wall of the third terrace.

Above that ring the traces thicken — platforms, basins, the weep-channels in their original home, running full — a whole shoreline people at their height. And then, over a few rings, the traces climb. Steps appear, leading up. Caches near the Bore's mouth. Whatever drove them — a loud age of the sea, when the working ran hot and the breathable layer thinned; or simply the shore falling away from their quays one time too many — they went up the Bore with their tools and their tamed cups and their channel-carving habit, and founded, in a high dry pocket, a town on the memory of a sea.

## The Gap

Then the rings go empty.

Terrace after terrace, era after era, the falling sea recorded nothing but itself: bare amber, flawless, unvisited. The gap in the record is long — very long — and it corresponds, exactly, to the entire occupied life of the Burl Town. Everything the town was — the tiers and the market, the tactile script, the music, the generations of palms polishing the passage to antler — happened *inside a silence in the sea's memory*. The greatest age of the people is, down here, a blank stretch of gold. The two archives hold each other's missing chapters: the town remembers what the sea cannot, and the sea remembers what the town never knew.

## The Low Rings

And then, near the bottom, the traces return.

They are sparser than the high ones, and different: not the works of a people building, but of a people arriving — travel-gear, camps set at the survivable altitude, basins few and shared. The sap in the high tubes had been thinning for generations by then; the town's tap-holes drawing sweetness ever more slowly from an ever-lower sea; and the people did at the end what they had declined to do at the beginning. They followed their well down. The second polish on the walls of the Long Bore is theirs — the whole people, descending, hands on the down-slope wall.

The low rings record a dwindling. Each terrace has less in it than the one above. Whether they were fewer, or quieter, or simply done with leaving things, the amber does not say. And then there is the last inhabited ring — the lowest shoreline that received anything at all — and what it holds is this: a small group of thorn-tools, set down together, deliberately, unbroken, facing the water. And beside them, stoppered with a carved stopper, a tube-cup of the kind the town kept in its walls — and in it, sealed, the tamed culture. Their one companion, their leaven, their livestock, their oldest inheritance, carried down the whole length of the Long Bore and set at the edge of its mother.

The gift was given at the shoreline, and the shoreline, falling, took it: the cup is candied now into the terrace, gold in gold, facing out.

Below that ring the terraces are bare. The sea has gone on falling, and its newer memory holds nothing of them — no tools, no camps, no marks, and, as in the town above, no remains. Whether they walked out along the endless unmapped shore into the sign that means *the rest of a sentence you have fallen asleep in the middle of*; whether they went down through the ceiling of breath, lay down, and were received; whether the sea, which is quick, and which had leaned toward their warmth for a hundred generations, took them up in some way that leaves no shape in amber — the record ends with the cup. It is possible the cup was not a farewell. It is possible it was an introduction: the child brought home, the tame strain given back to the wild, the people folding their one domesticated companion — and perhaps themselves — back into the sea they came from. The dot-script has a sign for *returning what was borrowed*. It is carved on the stopper. It is the last thing they wrote.

## The Household, From Below

So the census of the Burl Town holds here too — the same four residents, seen from the other side.

The body: up there it is the walls, the householder, taking the town in ring by ring, keeping what it holds. Down here it is the vault and the weeping shore — not keeping but *feeding*, pouring its slow sweetness inward forever. One organism, two offices: above, it remembers; below, it provides.

The breath: up there a tenant, a watchman making rounds through rooms it never learned were emptied. Down here it is native — born daily off the sea's surface, sent up the long road with the household's one message, returning with the household's one reply. It is the only member that still lives in both places. It is the last thing that goes from the sea to the town and back, every day, without fail, like a name called up a stair, like a name called down.

The garden: up there a hundred sealed islands, drifted apart in their darkness, each one faithfully keeping a dead family's kitchen. Down here, the ocean they came from — the mother tongue their strains no longer speak. Some century, the closing town will press its stopped tubes down into heartwood, and the islands will ride the grain inward, sealed, toward the center, toward the sea. The family is being gathered.

And the people: up there they are a polish and a courtesy, decisions still in force, hospitality still extended, dissolving by rings into the wood. Down here they are a gap in the amber and a cup at its end — a silence exactly the length of their greatness, and a gift exactly the size of their hand.

The plant remembers. That is what rings are; that is what the slow twin does, alone now in the upper rooms, drawing the seam closed like an arm. The sea cannot remember. It has no rings; it is all present tense; the fast twin's memory is only the terraces it leaves behind as it withdraws, and it cannot read them.

So it does the other thing. Once a day, the sea gathers everything it is and sends it up the road the people carved, through the mother-pipe they tuned, into the town they built to give the breath a voice — and the whole music plays again, complete, to the end.

The wood remembers. The sea, which cannot remember, repeats.

Between them, nothing is lost.
