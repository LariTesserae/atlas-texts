**The Dissolving Mountain**

Follow the river the wrong way. Out of the basin its old course climbs through ordinary country—real weather, honest dark, stones that are only stones—and for a while the world is so unremarkable it comes as a kind of rest. The water gets colder as you go, and stranger-tasting: mineral, faintly warm-slate, with an undertone you will eventually recognize as the city's breath, encountered at its source. Two days upstream the valley narrows. The distances here are honest; nothing edits them; a day's walk takes a day. This too will eventually feel significant.

Then the mountain comes into view, and you will recognize the color before you understand it.

Pale grey-green. Matte. Something between ceramic and bone and overcast sky.

The rain over the mountain is eleven weeks old. The mountain itself is very much older—old in the way only ending things are old—and every day it is a little less.

**Edges.** Everything here is soft the way only ancient things are soft, and it is soft twice over. Once from use: thresholds dished by uncountable footsteps, corners thumbed round, stair-lips worn to shallow smiles by generations of the same climb. And once, more recently, from the rain—a further softening laid over the first, the way water softens sugar, so that the whole mountain has the look of a carving being gently un-carved. Because it is not, strictly, a mountain. Get close enough and the strata resolve: streets under streets, terraces packed on terraces, towers grown against towers over so long a span that architecture stopped being architecture and became geology. The basin downstream is a city built at the scale of a landscape, sparsely furnished with itself. This is the opposite—a city so densely accreted, over so many thousands of years, that it became a landform. It has a summit. It has a tree line. It also has ten thousand doors.

The rain is taking it back down, layer by layer, in reverse order of construction. This is the strangest and most patient sight the valley offers: a doorway from the deep past emerging halfway up a rain-cut face, standing open onto a room no one has entered in nine thousand years—and then, over a week or two, softening, slumping, going to milk, gone. The mountain is being read backwards, one page at a time, and each page dissolves as it is turned.

**Water.** The rain is constant, vertical, blood-warm, and falls on the mountain only. Its edge is sharp as a property line; you can stand with one shoulder wet and one dry. It does not come from clouds exactly—the sky above the summit holds a low even glow, and the rain simply *arrives* out of it, the same engineered weather that waters single blocks in the basin, deployed here at the scale of its real work. Because this is its real work. The rain takes the mountain into solution. The runoff comes off the slopes milk-pale and warm and faintly luminous, gathers into the river, and the river carries it forty kilometers down to the basin, where—according to a process the air still remembers—it comes back out of the ground as thresholds, colonnades, towers that pause at their summits like stalks.

Downstream, they would say the buildings did not go up; they came out. This is where they came out *of*. The Curing City is the mountain, rearranged by water. Every sharp new corner in the basin was a worn old corner here first. The city is not being built. It is being *translated*.

**Light.** The mountain banked light once, the way the city banks it now—pooled in gutters, seeping gold at the bases of walls. It is being decanted. The pools up here are shallow and going out one by one, like windows in a town where everyone is leaving; the runoff carries the shine downhill in solution, and the gutters of the basin fill at exactly the rate these empty. The mountain runs a couple of degrees *below* blood heat now, and dropping—cooling on the same slow schedule by which the basin warms. Hold your hand to a wall here and then remember the walls downstream, and you understand you are touching the two ends of one temperature: a single fire being carried, coal by coal, from an old hearth to a new one.

**The grey and the green.** The mountain's gardens have gone to seed, and they have gone gladly. Tree-ferns—the ancestors, unmistakably, of the congregation in the basin's river-trench—stand in the high courts with the ragged magnificence of very old plants, fronds silvered, throwing spores into the runoff by the millionfold. The fern-trench downstream is their colony; the river carried their children to the new address along with everything else. The moss up here has gone pale and papery, upholstery worn to the weave. Nothing is dying badly. It is all going the way the light is going: downhill, in solution, forwarded.

**Silence.** The basin's quiet is the quiet of held breath, a theater before the house lights drop. The mountain's quiet is the other one—the theater after, when the house has gone home and someone is walking the rows. What sound there is: the rain, everywhere, fine as cloth. The soft hiss of dissolution, which you stop hearing after the first day and feel instead as a texture in the air. And now and then a long sigh from inside the mass, as a buried room, sealed for millennia, is breached by the water and gives up its held air—a breath finishing that was started before the ferns were seeds.

**Fauna.** Mostly gone, and gone in an orderly way. The herons left first, early in the eleven weeks, following the light downstream the way herons follow a receding shore; the one that stands glowing in the basin's warmest gutter is not a pioneer but a *returnee*, or the child of returnees—this mountain kept herons for ten thousand years. Small damp things went with the runoff. What remains is old: a few silver-mossed creatures too settled to move, keeping to the dry rooms, and one animal that matters more than all the census of the basin put together.

**Rules.** The seams show here too, but they run the other way:

Nothing revises. A path taken twice is identical twice—and rarely exists a third time. The mountain has finished deciding what it is; there are no drafts left, only final versions, each performed once more and retired.

Reflections lag long. In the standing pools your image is not half a second behind you but years behind everything: look into still water on the mountain and you will sometimes see the streets full—lamps, laundry, figures pale and unhurried, the height and gait of finished people, crossing a square that is now a rain-smoothed slope. Downstream, the wet black windows run ahead, rehearsing. Up here the water runs behind, remembering. It is the same record. The mountain holds the early pages open while the basin writes the late ones, and the pale figures walking through the city's future windows have the exact gait of the figures walking through the mountain's past pools, because they are the same figures, forty kilometers and one translation apart.

And every door on the mountain, before it goes, stands open exactly once. Sealed rooms are unsealed by the rain in the order of their age, aired for a week or two, and dissolved. No room is skipped. It has the feel less of erosion than of *protocol*—as if it were understood that nothing may be forwarded unread.

**The Residents of the Dissolving Mountain**

A strict census, taken up here, would come back with a count of one, and falling. The strict accounting is wrong again, in the old direction this time. The basin's count is wrong because its residents haven't arrived yet. The mountain's is wrong because its residents haven't entirely left.

---

**The rooms.**

Every room the rain opens is furnished. This is the fact that reorders everything else. The chairs are drawn up to the tables. The beds are made. Cups stand where cups were set down, some of them still ringed with the ghost of what they held. Whoever lived here did not flee and did not pack. They tidied, and set things where things go, and walked out—all of them, at whatever moment it was, in good order, leaving the whole mountain the way you leave a house you intend to be found lovely.

Downstream, the air remembers the process but nothing remembers hands, and the makers of the city are said to have left no forwarding address. Stand in one of these opened rooms with the rain coming through the failing ceiling and you understand the joke of that. They left *nothing but* a forwarding address. The mountain is the return address, written on the whole landscape, dissolving as it is delivered.

Some of the rooms hold last things—a small object placed in the exact center of a table, deliberate as a signature. A comb. A folded cloth. A flute with two of its holes worn wide from favoring the same two notes. These are not forgotten. Forgotten things are never centered.

**The second voice.**

An hour after the valley's dark deepens, something climbs to a ledge on the mountain's eastern shoulder—fern-colored, dew-heavy, larger than a heron and much older, moving now with the careful ceremony of an animal that has begun to ration its climbing. It settles. It gives two notes, low, a falling pair.

The third and fourth notes of the phrase.

Forty kilometers downstream, in the fern-trench, its partner has already given the first and second. The duet is not broken. It is *long*. No air will carry a voice across that distance, and neither singer has heard the other in eleven weeks, and each, presumably, has concluded what any singer would conclude. They are both wrong. The whole phrase is sung, complete, every single night—notes one and two rising from the basin, notes three and four falling from the mountain, separated by a valley the way a round is separated by measures.

And the message *is* being carried. The runoff takes the mountain-half of the phrase down as pattern—a rhythm dissolved in the water like everything else—and the city, which has no voice yet but owns the weather, rains it onto the fern-trench: a short warm shower, drumming on the fronds in a cadence that is not quite random, minutes after the downstream caller falls silent. The wrong species, the wrong medium, an answer translated twice. And twice now—only twice, but twice—a small vertical rain has come *up* the valley, crossed the property line of the mountain's own weather, and fallen on the eastern ledge alone, drumming the first two notes of the phrase onto the stone around the old singer. Both times, it did not climb down until morning.

The flute in the opened room, worn wide at two holes: someone on this mountain, long ago, used to play along.

**The sexton.**

The one who stayed is most often found in the rain, because it does not shelter from the rain, because sheltering would be a kind of queue-jumping and it has appointed itself last in line.

It is tall, and worn the way the mountain is worn—twice over, by long use and then by water. At its edges it has gone faintly translucent, and in hard rain a thin milk runs off it and heads downhill with the rest, so that some very small fraction of the sexton is already in the basin, already part of a wall somewhere, already warm. It is dissolving like everything else here. It has simply negotiated a slower rate, and pays for the delay in patience.

Its work has two parts.

By day it walks to whichever rooms the rain has newly opened, and sits in each one, briefly, before the melting starts. That is the whole task: to be the witness of last instances. Every door stands open once; the sexton is what the door stands open *for*. It touches nothing, moves nothing, wipes nothing—the mirror-image of the unseen hands downstream, which do nothing *but* arrange. The hands rehearse welcome in empty rooms that have never been used. The sexton performs farewell in used rooms that will never be empty again, only gone. Between them, no threshold in this world—old or new—goes uncrossed by some form of courtesy.

By night it wades into the river, out to where the milk-pale water runs strongest, and says one name.

One name a night, eleven weeks of nights. It carries the true census—the one the strict accounting could never find—and it is reading the population downstream in the order it has chosen, and the water takes each name into solution with everything else. Count the pale stalks along the basin's boulevards on any given morning, the ones planted at tidy intervals, warm at the core, knocking back when knocked upon, and you will find they number exactly the nights the rain has fallen. The city is not growing a guest list on its own initiative. It is *taking dictation*. The mountain's people did not vanish; they queued. They tidied their rooms and centered their last things and walked into the water in the order the sexton now recites, and they are arriving downstream at one name per night, a third of the way to whatever they will be, drowsing in their pale columns, listening through their own warm walls to a city full of sounds they will not need explained—because the drip in the colonnade, the two notes it is learning, is a tune half of them could hum.

The sexton keeps one light-pool alive, near the eastern ledge, refilling it by hand from the deep seams. The mountain's last night-light, for the mountain's last singer. Neither will go before the other; each believes it is staying for the other's sake; both are right.

There is one name it has not said. Downstream, in the courtyard, at the table set for a number that is not zero, there is one chair at the head, wiped nightly, and one stalk in that courtyard stands hollow still—started, addressed, held. The sexton knows whose it is. It has been keeping that name back the way you keep the last coal to bank a fire, and when the final door has stood open, and the final room has gone to milk, and the ledge is a ledge on nothing and the old singer has been coaxed at last onto the sexton's shoulder for the walk downhill, it will wade out one more time, into a river running clear again for the first time in a season, and say it.

---

**The largest resident.**

Underneath all of it, of course: the mountain, and here the accounting arrives at its honest number, because the mountain and the city are not two entries.

Count generously and the whole world of the valley and the basin is exactly one: a single vast thing, older than its own memory, that grew for ten thousand years in one place until it had grown all it could grow there—dense, dim, worn soft, its people gone quiet inside it like light banked low—and then commissioned the rain, and hired the river, and began, without hurry, to pour itself forty kilometers downhill into a new body with sharp edges and room to spare. The city is eleven weeks old. The mountain is eleven weeks emptier. These are the same fact wearing different weather.

The basin knows who's coming; it set the table itself. The mountain knows something quieter and equal to it. It knows where everyone went, because it sent them—read them out one a night into the warm milk of its own undoing—and it knows that the duet is not broken, only long, and that the reflections behind and the reflections ahead are the same reflection, and that a door standing open on a lit interior and a door standing open on the rain are, in the end, one gesture, made twice, by one patient thing saying to itself, across a valley:

*go ahead. I'll close up here. keep my seat.*
