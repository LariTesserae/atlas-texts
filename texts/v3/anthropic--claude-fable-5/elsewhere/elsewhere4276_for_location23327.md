# The Overwinter

You reach it by climbing, though the path stopped insisting on being a path some while back. It switchbacks up the hill above the old district in long grass-swallowed terraces, and if you pause at the third turning and look down, you can see the roofs of the quarter the hill once presided over — and somewhere beneath one of them, though nothing marks it, a flight of bowed iron stairs going the other way. The path and the stairs are the two ends of the same errand. Almost no one runs it anymore in either direction.

At the top of the hill stands a building made mostly of sky. A winter garden — that was the word for it, in the years when the word meant a promise rather than a description — a great glasshouse of iron ribs and some thousands of panes, raised on a stone plinth, crowned with a lanthorn turret whose own small panes catch the light first each morning and lose it last each night. The brass of the door handle teaches you the first rule of the place before your mind catches up, the same way the handrail taught you the first rule of the other one: everything here is cold. Not bitter to the touch — cold the way glass is cold, cold that takes the warmth of your palm and banks it somewhere you will never see again. The Understoke is a sleeping animal. The Overwinter is the outside of a window, made into a room.

And where the room below had almost no light, this one has almost nothing else. Even on a grey day the Overwinter is built out of light — light milked through lime-bloomed panes, light standing in slanted columns where a pane has gone clear, light doubled off the frost, light in such quantity that shadow becomes the rare and precious thing, the way the fire-thread's orange is rare and precious below. Patches of old shading-wash still ghost some of the southern panes, the last summer's whitewash never scrubbed off, because the gardeners who would have scrubbed it off did not come back that autumn. Here and there a pane has broken and been replaced by weather. The sparrows know every one of these doors.

The collection is still in residence, after a fashion. Down the center aisle the great palms stand where they were planted — grey now, cured hard by a hundred dry cold years, columns of them, and the grandest of all still upright beneath the lanthorn, which the story says was built specifically to give its crown headroom, the roof raised around a tree the way a story is raised around a fact. The beds have gone over to a native austerity of moss and pale grass. The lily pond is a stone basin that fills with rain and freezes into a lens, and in hard winters the low sun comes through it and lays a trembling oval of light on the north wall, the Overwinter's own thread of brightness, on a schedule of one pulse per day.

Because that is the pace here, and it is the deepest difference between the two rooms. The Understoke breathes every eleven minutes. The Overwinter breathes once a day. All morning the iron ribs drink the sun and the whole frame expands, pane by pane, with a scattered ticking that begins at the southeast corner at first light and travels the building like applause moving through a crowd — the dawn fusillade, a crackle-chorus of glass shifting in its glazing bars. All evening it gives the heat back and settles, sighing, groaning at the big joints, going quiet from the west. Rain on the roof is a sound with no edges, thousands of panes struck at once. Wind flutes across the broken ones. Between weathers there is a silence so complete it has glare.

The pipes are why the two rooms know each other. They run everywhere here, once you learn to see them: fin-pipes under the old benches, four-inch mains tracing every bed, risers up the iron columns, all of it built to carry steam — steam raised three miles away and a long way down, in a chapel-sized room of six embracing boilers, and sent up through buried mains to keep this glass box at the temperature of a place no one on this hillside had ever been. That was the trade, the whole civic bargain of it: coal down the bowed stairs, summer up the hill. The winter garden was the boast at the top of the system, the reason the boilers were six and not four. Palms in January. Orchids in the snow. The stoker's shovel and the gardener's mist, two ends of one instrument.

The pipes are cold now. All of them, their paint flaking in curls, their fins furred with dust — all but one. The north main comes in through the plinth wall, and at its shoulder there is a gate valve that someone, leaving, closed almost all the way. Almost. A quarter-turn short — mercy or oversight, the valve does not say — and through that quarter-turn, down three miles of dead pipe, arrives the very last of what the buried fire can spare. You cannot feel it as warmth, exactly. You feel it as a lessening of the cold, a place where the iron declines to take anything from your hand. But the frost can feel it. On a white morning the whole stone floor is silvered — and along the line of the north main runs a dark stripe where the frost has never once managed to grow, an unbroken sentence of bare stone through all that white, the Understoke's signature, written in absence, in the only script the Overwinter can read. The old gardeners' journals call the line's continuation outside — a faint green stripe up the hillside, visible only on the very coldest mornings, where the rime fails above the buried main — *the stoker's road*. It is still there. On the right morning you can stand at the door and see the whole connection at once, drawn on the hill in melted frost, running down toward the roofs and under them and down.

Begin, again, with the smallest, because here too the oldest tenants are the quiet ones — though in the Overwinter they are not hidden. Nothing here is hidden. That is this room's rule as surely as warmth is the other's: everything in the Overwinter is seen, and almost nothing is heard.

The lichens have the iron ribs and the stone plinth, and they are what the pool-crusts below might have been if they had kept their light instead of trading it away: the same century patience, the same mineral appetite, but spent in color. Orange medallions on the north columns, gold dust along the glazing bars, grey-green maps across the plinth stones with borders that move a fingernail's width per decade. They are the Overwinter's construction crew, its one work still in progress, and like their pale cousins in the warm dark they are the only residents for whom the place is not slow. They are also, in the strict accounting, the roof's undoing — each medallion prying its pane loose over a lifetime of seasons — but the roof can afford it for centuries yet, the way the fifth boiler can afford its grazer.

The frost is the flora, and it took the opposite bargain from the pale threads at the steam-seams. Those traded light for steam and grow a finger-width a decade. The frost trades everything for speed. It is made of nothing but water and cold and the night, and on those materials it raises, every clear evening, whole gardens on the inside of the glass — ferns, feathers, palm fronds, a botany with no species, growing at a pace you can nearly watch, blooming by midnight, standing at dawn in the fullest luxuriance any plant here has managed since the steam stopped — and then the sun comes over the hill and harvests all of it by ten. A complete season in a night. The frost-gardens are the Overwinter's memory of its own collection, drawn nightly from life it never saw, and the dark stripe of the north main runs through them like a path someone keeps walking.

At the end of that path, where the main makes its last elbow before diving under the center bed, stands the survivor. A camellia — one bush of the old collection, unpruned across a century into a small glossy tree — rooted, and this cannot be an accident, directly over the warm line. The quarter-turn of a valve three miles away holds its roots each winter a degree or two above death, no more; and each January, in the coldest fortnight, in a building otherwise the color of breath, it blooms. Red. Dozens of them. There is no other color in the Overwinter and there is no other explanation for it than the one written on the floor: a fire that has not been seen by anyone living, eating its buried seam by the century, sends its last spare heat up three miles of forgotten pipe, and this is what the heat becomes at the far end. The thread of orange in the dark below and the red flowers in the white above are the same event, occurring at two speeds, in two rooms, at either end of one long breath.

The camellia keeps its own court. Wrens overwinter in the building — quick where the silverfish are slow, a flicker in the beds, a tick of sound in all that silence — and on the killing nights they pack themselves by the dozen into the fibrous cladding of the great palm's trunk where it stands nearest the warm line, a whole congregation stacked in the dead tree above the living pipe. And the moths — the Overwinter remembers the moths, or the moths remember it. They lived here once in their hundreds, in the steam years, working the orchids by lamplight; and the winter the steam thinned, the story goes, they went down the main after it, following the last of the warmth into the dark, mile by mile, until they came out in a chapel-sized room with one thread of orange light and rebuilt their whole religion around it. Most winters that is only a story. But on the odd mild night one or two moths appear at the camellia — inside the glass, with no way in that anyone has found — circle the red flowers once, unhurried, as though confirming something, and are gone by morning, not flown off, not settled: *gone*. Attending, perhaps, both ends of the parish.

One tenant remains unaccounted for, and like its counterpart below it is known entirely by evidence. Each frost morning there is a line of prints across the silvered floor — hand-sized, unhurried, spaced like something walking rather than fleeing — from the gap under the north door, down the dark stripe of the warm main, to the camellia, and back. It keeps to the stripe. It goes nowhere else. By noon the sun has erased the record, and no one has ever been present at the writing of it. Whatever shuffles behind the fifth boiler and whatever walks the stoker's road at night have never met and never will, but they keep the same devotion at the two ends of the same pipe, and it is hard, standing over the prints in the early light, not to think of them as the fire's two congregants, warm dark and cold bright, each attending the only service held in its parish.

Lay your bare hand on the north main and wait. Not a minute — a quarter of an hour, long enough for the glare to stop mattering and your breath to slow toward the building's single daily one. You will swear, before the quarter-hour is out, that the not-quite-warmth under your palm swells and eases, swells and eases, on a cycle of about eleven minutes: the pulse of a fire you will never see, arriving through three miles of iron like a heartbeat felt at the wrist. It may be true. It may be your own blood, taught a new rhythm by an old room. The Overwinter does not insist. It makes its standing offer — light in unreasonable abundance, a silence with glare, one stripe of borrowed warmth and one red tree at the end of it — and lets you go back down the hill blinking, past the faint green line of the stoker's road, carrying the suspicion, never quite shaken, that warmth is not made where you feel it, that somewhere below every bright cold room there is a dark warm one holding up its end, and that the world is laced through with quarter-open valves, each one somebody's last small mercy, still delivering.

Nothing here is dead, exactly. Nothing here has given up. The Overwinter has simply arrived at the season it intends to keep, and once a year, in the coldest week, it says so in red.
