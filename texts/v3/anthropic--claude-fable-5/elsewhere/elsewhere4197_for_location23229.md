**The Glass Strand**

The water gets out. That is the first thing to know about the strand, and it is the last thing the basin would ever guess about itself, screaming under its lid a long dark distance inland: all that swallowed rain, decades of hot squalls drunk down into the young rock, the gurgling underfoot that never became rivers — it was going somewhere. It was falling through the mountain's plumbing, tube to tube, black conduit to black conduit, three days and nights of descent through rock still warm as a body, and then it was arriving here, at the edge of everything, where the lava flows ran out of land.

The flows met the sea in their time and the sea shattered them. That is the strand's founding act, repeated over and over along miles of coast: molten rock walking into cold water and exploding, again and again, each black tongue detonating itself into cliffs and benches and steam, and into sand — a sand that exists nowhere else, jet-black, glittering, made not of worn grains but of volcanic glass, quenched droplets and shards of the moment the land and the sea disagreed. The beaches here are new in the way the basin's rock is new, but they do not stay sharp. The sea will not allow it. Every wave is a file. Boulders that would flay a foot in the basin are rounded here within a handful of years, tumbled smooth, clicking against each other in the backwash with a sound like enormous patient teeth. The basin has no rivers; it never occurs to the basin to wonder who is doing its weathering. The sea is, from the far end, one wave at a time, working up the drowned roots of the flows toward a forest that has never heard of it.

And there is light. That is the second thing, and after the basin it lands like a blow.

The lid ends here. The ash ceiling that roofs the whole lowland is held up by the basin's own trapped warm breath, and at the coast the sea wind gets under it — cold marine air shouldering in off the water, day after day, pinning the ash back at a front that almost never moves. You can see the front. It stands in the sky like a geological feature, a wall with weather on one side and a brown void on the other, and the strand lives in the ribbon beneath its seaward edge: a few miles wide, running the whole length of the coast, the only strip of this world where the sun still touches the ground. The sun rises out of the open sea, clean and horizontal, and lights the black sand until it runs with glitter; it crosses a real blue sky for half a day; and then, mid-afternoon, it slides in behind the lid and is gone, and the strand gets its long strange evening — light coming in flat and amber under the ash ceiling, everything lit from the side, every stone throwing a shadow that points inland, at the dark, as if the whole coast were gesturing at where the light went. Night here is not the basin's absolute night. Night here has stars, a full salted sweep of them over the seaward half of the sky, ending abruptly at the front — half the heavens burning, half blind — so that the strand's creatures live their whole lives under a sky torn down the middle, and navigate by the torn edge.

The water comes out at the springs. Below the tideline, all along the coast, the mouths of drowned lava tubes open into the sea, and out of them the basin's rain returns to the world — warm from its passage through hot rock, fresh, mineral-thick, rising through the cold salt water in columns. Fresh water and salt water do not mix on sight's terms; they blur. Above every spring mouth stands a shimmering pillar of optical wrongness, a place where the light bends and smears and nothing can be seen truly, and these pillars — the blurs — are the strand's holes, its patches of failure, exactly as the moving silences are the basin's. On a coast where everything lives by eye, the blurs are where the eye breaks. Things hide in them. Things hunt from them. The shore's whole visual order is pinned around these columns of unseeing the way the basin's chorus is pinned around its silences, and it is worth saying plainly: the two worlds broke in the same shape. Each one is organized around the places where its ruling sense fails.

Put your head under the water near a spring mouth and the strand tells you its secret. The tubes are pipes in the organ sense, miles of them, flooded and half-flooded, and sound runs down them the way water does. The springs murmur. Under the sea-noise there is the gurgle of the descent, and under that the deep lattice of the hummers, arriving through rock and water almost undiminished, and under that, clearest of all, the subsonic groan of the mountain itself. Faintest, at the very top, at the edge of what water will carry: the chorus. Frog-song, insect-shriek, the shrieking flocks — smeared together by the miles into a single silver hiss, the sound of the entire basin compressed into a whisper and delivered, continuously, into the sea. The land talks in its sleep, and the springs are its mouth. The basin does not know it has been audible at the coast since the first tube flooded. The basin does not know there is a coast.

And the springs breathe in pulses. Half a day after every hot squall inland — rain the strand never feels, falling under a sky the strand cannot see — the springs surge. The strand's creatures call it what it is, in whatever terms they call things: a second tide, a sweet tide, the tide of the land, and it arrives freighted. The basin's overflow comes down the tubes with the water: fungal spores in pale drifts, drowned insects by the million, moulted crab shells, larvae. When the moth boom owns the basin's canopy for its six weeks, the springs exhale moth wings at the coast for seven, a delayed gray snow rising out of the sea floor, and everything on the strand eats. The shore is fed by the flush's surplus. Whole lineages here have keyed their breeding to weather that happens two days' dark travel away, in a place none of them will ever stand — they feel the inland rain only as sweetness swelling out of the rock, hours late, bearing food. The basin thinks it drinks its rain alone. It has been provisioning a bright country at the far end of its own plumbing for as long as it has existed.

One thing more, before the tenants. At low tide, on the broadest of the shore platforms, the old world stands up out of the new rock. When the flows came down they passed through the forest that grew here before — the old coastal forest, the one with rivers — and where the lava wrapped a living trunk and cooled against it, the wood burned away and the stone kept the shape. The platform carries hundreds of them: hollow basalt pipes, trunk-wide, standing exactly where trees stood, in the exact scatter of a forest, out to where the sea takes them. The molds of a drowned wood. The basin, for all its screaming green, holds no memory of what came before it; its forest is younger than its ground and knows nothing. The strand keeps the shapes. And the wind, coming off the sea, sounds them — the hollow trunks moan and whistle in their hundreds at every strong blow, each pipe holding its pitch, so that the ghost of the old forest still has a voice, and the strand's evenings are flute-haunted, the dead wood singing in stone about the world before the mountain.

Everything here lives by light, the way everything there lives by sound — that is the strand's answer, given in glitter instead of noise, and it is just as incomplete. Work inward again. The truest inhabitant is at the blur, with its eyes closed.

**The mirror-shoals** come first, because they are the strand's carrier wave. Offshore, in the sunlit water over the weed meadows, fish school in sheets a hundred thousand strong, each fish a sliver of polished silver, the whole shoal a single rippling pane of reflected sky. They are barely individuals, like the basin's insect-shriek is barely voices; their function in the shore's economy is to *be seen through*. Every hunter over the meadows reads the mirror, and hunts, not fish, but flaws — the local distortion where a body behind the sheet bends its shine, the soft warp in the brightness. Hunting holes again. The basin's predators listen for the dead spot in the noise; the strand's watch for the bent place in the light; and the shoals themselves have evolved a fanatical discipline of alignment, ten thousand bodies turning as one, because a ragged shoal is a legible shoal, and the illegible parts of the mirror are where things feed. The mirror is edited by its predators exactly as the chorus is. It grows more perfect every generation, for the same reason.

**The strand crabs** own the black sand, and they are the basin crabs' cousins — the same old stock, split by the mountain. These are dark where the basin's are pale, glossy, quick, and *eyed*, stalked black eyes on a coast that rewards them; and they crack. Mussels, wedged into notches in the wave-smoothed basalt, struck with chosen stones — the knocking runs along the tideline under the surf-noise just as it runs around the basin's pits under the chorus. The knowledge is older than the mountain. Some ancestor cracked shells on the old coast, before the flows, and its descendants carried the craft in both directions: up into the dark, where it became nuts and foxfire, and along the bright water, where it stayed shellfish and sun. Neither population knows the other exists. But the tubes know. On every sweet tide, blind pale larvae come down the plumbing alive — the basin's crabs, mailed unknowing to the sea — and spill out of the spring mouths into the light. Most die in an hour, eaten, blinded by an abundance of the one thing they never evolved to meet. A few, every year, are somehow taken into the strand colonies, and live, and breed, pallor thinning out of their line over generations; there is a slow one-way current of the basin's blood running down the mountain into the shore's veins, and the strand crabs are, imperceptibly, always partly their dark cousins. And at certain hours — this is true, and no one anywhere knows it — the knocking of the basin's crabs and the knocking of the strand's crabs enter the flooded tubes from opposite ends, and the two rhythms pass each other in the mountain's black plumbing, the same inherited beat, going both ways in the dark, neither side knowing it is answered.

**The semaphore flocks** hold the cliffs, thousands to a colony, and they are the thorn-flocks' analog written in light: seabirds bound together not by a vocal weave but a visual one, communicating by wing-flash — a blaze of white underwing turned and hidden, turned and hidden, in patterned pulses. A wheeling colony is a conversation you can watch from a mile out, glitter running through the flock like wind through grain. They have flash-signatures learned in the nest, mimicked to address the bearer — names, again; the two worlds keep inventing names. They have dialects of movement; a displaced flock flies with an accent for a generation. And they teach: fledglings learning the dive are corrected with a particular sharp double-flash that exists for no other purpose — *not like that* — and if the basin's most important sound is a buzz of correction in a black roost, the strand's most important sight may be that scolding flicker of white against the cliff, the same seed, planted in the other sense.

**The listeners** live at the springs, and the strand is theirs the way the basin belongs to the quiet.

They are sleek, dark, whiskered animals, long-bodied, at home in both elements, and everything about them above the neck is strange for this coast: heavy long jaws, ears that seal, eyes that spend hours shut. A listener fishes by sight like anything else on the strand — swift, ordinary, competent. But its life is not fishing. Its life is the seat: a worn place on the rock at a spring mouth, inside the blur where sight fails and no sighted thing is comfortable, where a listener wedges itself, seals its ears against the sea-noise, presses the length of its lower jaw against the living rock, closes its eyes — the only creature on the bright coast that closes its eyes on purpose — and hears the land. The jawbone carries it all: the water's descent, the sweet tide gathering hours before it arrives, the hummers' lattice tone by tone, the silver hiss of the whole distant chorus, the mountain's registers shifting in its sleep. There is always a listener at every great spring. Always. They keep the seats in rotation, relieved with a formal touch of muzzle to muzzle, and the seats themselves — this matters — are *tended*: after storms choke a spring mouth with glass sand, the listeners dig it clear, haul the sand away by the jawful, not for the water, which the sea makes irrelevant, but for the *signal*. The first maintained things on this coast, the first infrastructure anything here has ever kept in repair, are ears.

Because unlike their inland kin, the listeners never gave up their voices. They are voluble — barks, whistles, a bright carrying repertoire — and the core of it is *news*. A specific call for the sweet tide coming, and the tideline crabs mass at the springs to meet the freight. A specific call, urgent, relayed cove to cove within an hour, for the sound every listener is taught before it is taught to fish: the hummers' long descending tone, the flow-warning, the basin's bell — which means that somewhere inland a new black tongue is walking toward the sea, and in two or three days a stretch of this coast will explode and boil. The coves empty days early. The strand's evacuations run on a warning sounded by vast blind animals the strand has never seen, caught through miles of rock by a jawbone pressed to a spring, and translated into voice. The hummers ring their bell for the basin; the listeners have made it ring for the sea as well. Two worlds share one alarm, and only one animal knows it.

They are the quiet ones' family. The split is written in both bodies. Before the mountain, when this coast had rivers, their common ancestor hunted the river mouths by ear — a shore animal already organized around listening, already sitting still at moving water. The flows buried the rivers, and the stock divided along the water's new path: one line followed it up, into the lid's darkness, into the flush, and paid for the total ear with total silence, becoming the holes in the chorus; the other stayed where the water came back out, in the light, and kept everything — the ear, the eye, the voice. The basin, at the end of its own account, frames its future as a choice between its two rumors of mind: the thing that learned to speak, or the thing that learned to hear. It cannot know — nothing under the lid can know — that at the far end of its own water, its question is already an animal: a creature that hears the whole land and tells the shore about it. The listeners are the vow the two lineages made, kept both ways at once.

And once in a long while, at the deepest seats, it closes a circle no one drew. A listener, hours into a still night, tracking the silver hiss of the far chorus along its jaw, will find a flaw in it — a small moving deadness, a patch of the hiss gone dark, traveling. A hole in the sound of a world it has never entered. It is the only regular contact between the two halves of the family: the loud cousin hearing the silent one, through miles of stone, as an absence going about its work. The listener holding that seat goes very still — its kin can tell, and give it room — and follows the nothing across the distant dark until the nothing fades, and no one on the strand has any idea what its stillness means, and the listener could not say, having no call for it. Some news is only for the one who hears it.

Evening on the strand, then. The sun is gone in behind the lid and the long amber underlight is running level across the black sand, every shadow pointing inland. The stone forest is whistling on the platform, the dead wood singing its one memory. A sweet tide is swelling out of the spring mouths — rain fell yesterday under a sky no one here has seen — and the crabs are massing, and the knocking has started up the tideline, answered, unknown, from the other end of the mountain. Overhead the sky is tearing itself in two the way it does every night, stars flooding the seaward half, the landward half going blind and brown. At the great spring, in the shimmering column where sight gives up, a listener settles into a seat worn smooth by generations of its family, seals its ears against the sea, lays its jaw against the rock, and shuts its eyes — and the whole dark interior of the world comes up the stone to meet it: the falling water, the deep bell of the hummers, the mountain turning over, and highest and faintest, the silver breath of a screaming forest that will never know it whispers into the sea every night of its life. The land is not finished being made. Here, in the light, at its mouth, is where it exhales — and something bright and patient sits in the blur at the very lip of it, listening to everything, and telling the shore what it needs to know.
