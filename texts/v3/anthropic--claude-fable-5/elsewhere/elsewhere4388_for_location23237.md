**Selvage**

Begin with descent. The Loom Plateau does not simply end; on its southern rim the mat thins, browns, gives out — the one border the plant has never crossed — and the world drops away beneath it for the better part of two vertical kilometers. This is the escarpment: the last true rock of the eaten mountain, its one surviving face, spared not by mercy but by geometry. The mat cannot hold a cliff. It grew to the very lip long ago, leaned over, and stopped, and the stone below it has stood ever since like the wall of a house whose interior has been entirely replaced by a tenant.

Over that lip the plateau weeps.

Not in one river. The mat has no rivers, only saturation — and so what comes off the edge comes off *everywhere*, in hundreds of threadlike falls spaced along kilometers of rim, each too thin to roar alone, all of them together making a sound like a held breath the size of a horizon. The people below call the falls, collectively, **the Fringe** — because the plateau above is the Loom, and this is where the cloth ends, and every cloth ends in threads.

Descend beside them — there is a trail, a week's switchbacking down the shoulder-country, since no one climbs the Fringe itself and lives — and the weather changes register rather than kind. Above, the dimness of a permanent hour before dusk. Here, at the foot of the wall, you come down through the underside of the plateau's own cloud into a pearl-gray brightness that never resolves into sun: the perpetual spray of the Fringe hangs over everything as a fine standing mist, and the light arrives in it pre-scattered, sourceless, kind. The plateau makes its weather out of nothing. Selvage inherits that weather secondhand, already made, falling down the wall in threads.

And in a bowl of green terraces at the foot of the falls, where the threads gather into one gray river, there is a town.

---

**The hungry water**

The first thing to understand about Selvage is its water, because the water is the plateau's true and constant export, and it is not what water usually is.

The plant keeps everything. Every mineral of the mountain it consumed, every salt, every grain — it holds all of it, hoards it, is *made* of it; nothing of substance is permitted to leave the body of the Loom. So what comes over the rim is water and only water: scrubbed, distilled by a metabolism the size of a region, the purest water in the world. And pure water is not gentle. Empty of everything, it is hungry for everything. It dissolves what it touches. It has spent uncounted ages eating the cliff it falls down — patiently unstitching the last rock of the mountain, grain by grain, and carrying it away south.

Understand what this means. The erosion of the plateau is finished; the text of that place is closed. But the plant is still consuming its mountain — here, below, by proxy, through its own exhaust. The Fringe is the Loom's longest and slowest act, outsourced to gravity. The plateau ended long ago and has not stopped, and Selvage lives at the exact point where the not-stopping touches the ground.

The people of Selvage cannot drink the water as it comes. Empty water leaches; it would take the mineral out of their bones as readily as out of the cliff. So every cistern in town is floored with gravel quarried from the escarpment's foot, and the water is left to stand on the stone for a day before anyone drinks it. They call this *hardening the water*, but the older phrase, the one grandmothers use, is better: *teaching the water*. Water that has forgotten everything must be given something to remember before it is safe. Every household in Selvage, every day, feeds a little more of the mountain to the mountain's own runoff. It is the town's oldest chore and no one thinks of it as strange, and it is the whole relationship between the two places in a bucket.

There is a stair in the cliff. It matters that you know about the stair.

It was cut when the mountain was a mountain, by the people who lived on its shoulders — hand-hewn switchbacks, thousands of steps, rising from what is now Selvage's upper pastures to what is now the rim of the mat. It is the last artifact of the eaten world, and it is dying, because the Fringe's hungry water has been dissolving it for as long as it has been dissolving everything else: the steps are rounded to sighs, the handholds gone soft as candle-stubs. Here is the cruelty of the arrangement, stated once and plainly: everything the plant consumed is remembered forever, rehearsed above in flesh and brief stone, granted the strange low-rate immortality of the Loom. The stair escaped. It was never eaten. And so it merely *persists*, and wears away, and will one day be gone in the complete and ordinary manner — the only part of the old mountain to which that will ever happen. Above the wall, being devoured was preservation. Below it, escape is mortality.

One person has climbed the stair in living memory. She went up to study the lightning and did not come back down, and her house in Selvage is kept, and this account will reach that house in time.

**The town**

Selvage is built on terraces, because everything at the foot of the Fringe is built on terraces; the land comes down out of the mist in long green steps like the seating of an amphitheater facing the wall. Perhaps six hundred people. Stone houses with steep slate roofs, every roof furred deep in moss — true moss, ordinary moss, the honest small cousins of the immensity overhead, and the townspeople are quietly partisan about this, the way one might be about a decent relative of a notorious family. Everything in Selvage is a little green and a little wet. Wood swells here; doors are hung loose on purpose. Iron is oiled daily or surrendered.

The sound of the town is layered like the sound of the plateau, but where the plateau's layers are pump-thud and vent-roar and tearing churn, Selvage's are gentler and human at the edges: the held-breath hush of the Fringe, always, under everything, the town's silence being in fact a very loud thing you stop hearing in your first week; the gray river running over stones it is simultaneously eating; and the looms.

Because of course Selvage weaves. It could not, situated where it is, named what it is, have done anything else.

The temperament of the town is what the weather and the work have made it: unhurried, exact, damp-cultured, faintly ceremonious about small things. The dialect has eleven words for kinds of mist and no word at all for drought. People here do not point at the plateau; they refer to it, when they must, with a small upward tilt of the chin, and mostly they do not refer to it. This is not fear, exactly. It is the manner of people who live at the foot of a sleeping neighbor's wall and have found, over many centuries, that the courteous thing — the *safe* thing — is to keep one's voice down and one's edges bound.

They tell children: *don't stand like a bird.* You will learn why.

**The looms**

In the tailraces and mill-ponds below town, where the hungry water has been slowed and slightly fed, there grows a weed. It grows nowhere else in the world, because nothing else in the world offers it water empty enough: a pale, fibrous, rootless silkweed that hangs in the current like combed hair and asks nothing of the river because the river has nothing to give. For a long time Selvage believed it a native miracle. It is not. It is the plateau. Fragments of the mat go over the rim constantly — torn loose in the churn, washed off in the endless weeping — and nearly all of them die on the rocks or drown in the pools; but *nearly*, at the foot of the Fringe as at the top, is a working margin. The weed is the mat's flesh, gone over the falls, gone small and feral and modest in the millstreams. Selvage has been harvesting, retting, and spinning the body of the weaver since before it had records, and does not know it, and would go very quiet for a season if it found out.

The cloth they make from it is famous three watersheds south, and its famous property is this: it does not forget. Crease Selvage cloth once and the crease outlives the tailor. A Selvage coat, worn a year, takes the shape of its wearer and keeps it — hang it empty on a peg and it hangs like a person standing with their back to you; widows in the lowlands are known to buy their husbands' coats back from the ragman for exactly this reason, and to keep them behind doors, and not to explain. Cloth with memory, woven from the flesh of a memory the size of a region, sold by the bolt into the ordinary world: this is the Loom's thread reaching everywhere, blunted into commerce, warming shoulders that will never know what weather it was cut from.

Two details of the weaving, and they are the deep ones.

First: the rhythm. Selvage looms are treadled to a beat every apprentice learns before they learn their letters, a slow doubled knock the old weavers count aloud — and it is the pump-beat of the plateau. *Thud... thud... thud.* The heartbeat of the plant carries down through the last bone of the mountain; you can feel it in the deepest cellars of the upper town, through the soles of your feet, a floor-borne pulse arriving from two kilometers overhead. No weaver alive knows this is what the count is. They know only that cloth woven off the count comes out wrong — sulky, creaseless, forgetful — and that the count was their teacher's teacher's, back beyond naming. The whole town keeps time to the landlord of the country above it and calls the timekeeping tradition.

Second: the mark. Into the bound edge of every bolt — into the selvage of Selvage, the edge that keeps the cloth from raveling — the weavers work a small angular figure, always the same, copied faithfully from sample to sample down all the generations, meaning unknown, assumed to be decoration. It is not decoration. It is a word, in the script of the mountain people, the cairn-builders, the stair-cutters — a dead script that survives nowhere else below the wall. Up on the plateau, in the deep churn, when the mat performs its eight seconds of gray stone, that script sometimes comes with it, cut into the brief rock beside the brief cairns. The Loom could read the mark. It is perhaps the only reader left. The word means, as nearly as a dead language can be made to mean anything, *kept.*

And the moths. Selvage has a clothes-moth — gray as the overcast, the persistent small sorrow of every wool-chest and weed-silk store in town, swatted, cursed, sachet-warded, utterly unremarkable. It is the same moth. Some ancestral storm carried a freight of them up the wall, long ago, to breed in the dead husks of the vent towers, and so Selvage's household pest is the plateau's only honest death — the one thing up there that leaves a corpse and stays left. The town's chief nuisance is the mountain's chief mortality. Nobody in Selvage knows they have exported anything to the plateau at all, and in fact they have exported exactly two things: the moth, and the woman.

**The custom of keeping**

Selvage builds cairns. It has always built cairns — trailside, field-corner, one at the door of every house begun and never dismantled when the house is done — and it does not remember why, having carried the custom down from the mountain along with itself, losing the reasons on the stairs. Here is the inversion that sits at the center of everything between the two places: *above, the plateau remembers the people and has forgotten it is remembering anyone in particular; below, the people have forgotten the mountain and go on doing, faithfully, everything the mountain taught them.* The mat raises the kneeling wet print of a cairn-builder beside a cairn of brief stone; two kilometers down and unaware, the cairn-builder's great-great-descendants kneel in the drizzle and stack real ones. Neither fidelity knows of the other. Between them the Fringe falls and falls.

But the custom is not only inherited. It has been renewed, because things come down the wall.

They are called, in Selvage, **the come-downs**, and the town has known about them far longer than it has understood them: animals, appearing at long intervals at the foot of the escarpment — battered, asymmetrical, ragged, wrong. A heavy heron-thing with a rebuilt look about one wing. A six-legged grazer with scars no lowland animal could have earned. Once, within living memory, a pale eel-bodied creature that walked, badly, on a single grown leg, and that survived the descent of the Fringe — a descent that kills nearly everything that attempts it — and hauled itself into a lambing shed above town and would not leave, and was not made to.

You know what these are. They are the dropped stitches — the plateau's forgotten sentences, the made animals grown too changed to be read and taken back, the only things on all that green immensity that fear, and want, and gather. And some of them, at the end of long lives of being unrecognized by the only mind that ever knew them, come to the rim, and look down through the falling threads at the terraced lights, and climb. Most die on the wall. The hungry water has left it few holds and the cold does the rest. But *most* is a margin too, and every generation or two something forgotten arrives at the bottom, alive.

Selvage keeps them. It has no theory of them — the town's working belief is simply that the high country sometimes loses animals and that lost animals are owed shelter, which is, as theories go, very nearly correct and entirely sufficient. The come-downs live out their spans in barns and kitchen-corners, warm for the first time in their existence, eating what they are given (they can eat almost anything; they were built by a thing that is what its creatures eat), following the townspeople at the small fixed distance of creatures long accustomed to following someone. And when they die — and they do die, in the full ordinary unrecoverable sense, which may be, though no one in Selvage would phrase it this way, the very thing they climbed down for — the town buries them, each under a cairn, in a walled wet meadow on the highest terrace.

This is why the custom persisted when its reasons did not. The cairns had been waiting, for centuries, for something to be *for.* The plateau forgets; the town keeps. The two of them together, without coordination, without acquaintance, perform between them one complete act of memory that neither can perform alone. That is the trade. It is not conducted in cloth.

And this is also why children are told *don't stand like a bird* — because the flyway crosses Selvage too, and the town learned, generations before anyone put it into words, that a bird which stands too still too long in a mill-pond is not resting. Selvage children are taught to startle birds the way other children are taught to swim: run at it, clap, watch. A true bird startles freshly every time. A bird that performs the same startle twice — the same wingbeat count, the same cry, whether or not you are actually there — is walked, gently, with a kind of processional gravity the town reserves for nothing else, up to the highest terrace. To the walled meadow. Which has, by now, a second purpose, and a pulse.

**The Standing Ground**

For as long as the records run, rooted birds have been planted there — the plateau's exports caught mid-arrival, the made things that descend with the true flocks and stand still and begin, very slowly, to anchor. Once the town understood what stillness meant, it stopped fighting it and gave it an address. A wet walled meadow, spring-fed, north-facing, in permanent view of the wall it answers. The rooting birds were set in its turf like bulbs, generation after generation, and left to become what they become.

What they have become, merging root into root across the centuries, is a mat.

It is perhaps three acres now: olive-black, dense as felted wool, warm to a bare hand on a cold morning, weeping clear water at every step. Press your palm down and the print fills, and drains, and the surface exhales slowly back into shape. It is, in miniature, in infancy, unmistakably a second Loom — and it has, in the last few generations, developed a pulse. Faint. Slow. A soft doubled beat you must lie down on the turf to feel. The weavers' count, arriving now from two directions.

And it has begun, very tentatively, to *raise things.* This is where the Standing Ground stops being an oddity and becomes the hinge of the whole country, because the things it raises are not its own. The rooted birds that compose it were rehearsals — recordings, rendered in flesh, the plateau's memories flying south in feathers — and so the young mat's entire first vocabulary is inherited. It dreams its parent's dreams. It has raised, so far, and briefly: a gray moth; a stretch of turf that went, for four witnessed seconds, hard and cold and stone-gray, actual rock, the bedrock of a mountain this three-acre child never ate and has never seen, standing two kilometers below the place where that mountain's ghost is rehearsed; and once — this emptied the upper town of every soul who could walk, and is spoken of since in the voice reserved for weddings and floods — a heavy olive heron with a chipped beak, performing seven steps.

But the inheritance is not the whole of it. The young mat is *learning*, and what it is learning is what the town has planted at its edges. The come-downs are buried there — the forgotten, under their cairns, in the one soil in the world that reads. And it has happened, once, that the Standing Ground raised one of them: a scarred six-legged grazer, dead and cairned a full generation, walking the meadow through one long gray afternoon, whole, calm, present — not a rehearsal, the old startle-test said so; it could be surprised; it *looked* at people, freshly, each time — before lying down at dusk and being, gently, unsaid.

Sit with what that means. The dropped stitches climb down the wall because the great mind above can no longer read them. And at the bottom of the wall, in a churchyard-nursery kept by weavers, a small new mind is learning to read them fresh. The forgotten of the Loom descend, at mortal cost, toward the one place in the world where they may be *re-known* — and the town of Selvage, without ever assembling the thought, has spent its whole history building the road: the custom of shelter, the custom of cairns, the walled meadow, the plantings. The town believed it was keeping graves. It has been keeping a library, and the librarian has just learned its letters.

There is one human grave at the meadow's edge. The first keeper of the Standing Ground asked for it, and the town argued for a winter, and allowed it, once, and has permitted no other since. Nothing has come of it. On certain fog mornings, people crossing the upper terrace walk a little faster, and do not say why, and there is nothing to see, and the matter is closed.

**The kept house**

On the second terrace, mid-row, there is a house with its door repainted every third year in the same deep green, its cistern hardened, its roof-moss trimmed, its rooms aired weekly by a family descended from a sister of the woman who went up the stair. No one lives in it. Everyone maintains it. It is not spoken of as empty; it is spoken of as *kept*, which in Selvage is a different and stronger word, and by now you know the word's lineage — it is the word in the cloth.

She was of this town. She learned the count at a loom on this street before she ever learned what lightning was; she hardened water in that cistern; the coat she climbed in was Selvage cloth and is, presumably, still up there somewhere, holding her shape in the drizzle, on one of her or several. Selvage knows less about her present circumstances than you do. The town knows only this: that she went up the dying stair, the last person who ever will, and did not come down, and sent no word, because no word comes down the Fringe — nothing comes down the Fringe but water, weed, and the forgotten.

Except.

Every few autumns, out of the migrating flocks, a bird descends onto the roof of the kept house — that roof, no other — and stays a day too long, and watches the street below with what the keeping family decided, three generations ago, to call fondness, and has called fondness ever since with increasing confidence. It does not stand still enough to root. It does not startle quite freshly. It leaves, when it leaves, flying north — the wrong way for the season, the right way for the wall. The family does not test it. There are startle-tests for strange birds in every yard in Selvage, and this house alone declines to use them, on the grounds — stated once, by an old keeper, and adopted since as policy — that you do not run clapping at your aunt.

So the connection closes its loop through a green door: the town made a woman; the woman climbed to the Loom; the Loom is memorizing her; and the memory, redistributed across a place, sends a tense of her downstairs every few years to look at the street where she was small. She stays because to be memorized by something that will outlast geology is the nearest honest thing to not dying that anyone has ever been offered. The house stays because Selvage was built, stone and custom and cloth-edge and marrow, on the only reply a mortal town at the foot of such a neighbor can make: *and we will keep our copy too.*

---

**The census**

So: who lives in Selvage?

Six hundred people, more or less, descendants of an eaten mountain, keeping time they cannot name to a heartbeat they cannot hear, in a mist made of a place they do not visit. The true moss on their roofs, honest and small. A weed in the tailraces that is the flesh of the great weaver, farmed unknowing into cloth that will not forget a crease. A gray moth, pest below and only honest death above. The come-downs, warm at last, in the barns and at the hearth-corners — the plateau's forgotten sentences, finishing themselves in a town that writes everything down in stone. A stair, dying the one true death in all this country, step by softening step. A young loom in a walled meadow, three acres old, dreaming its parent's mountain and learning, from cairns, its neighbors' dead. One grave there that is not discussed. One house, kept, and its irregular autumn visitor.

And under all of it, threading through all of it, the gray river of empty water — the current that carries everything between the two places and belongs to neither: the plateau's exhaust, the cliff's undoing, the weed's home, the town's taught and hardened drink, running away south into the ordinary world with the last of the mountain dissolved in it, invisible, distributed, everywhere.

Two kilometers up, one immense being revises a single sentence it has been saying since before its ground gave out: *here, still, again.* At the foot of the wall, a small town has spent its whole existence weaving the answer into the edge of every bolt it sells, in a script only the questioner can still read, without knowing it is answering, without knowing there is a question — the bound edge, the selvage, the word that keeps the cloth from raveling:

*Kept.*
