The flux pit.

Three kilometres over the ridge from the works, at the end of a haul road the birches are quietly repossessing, there is a hole in the hill the exact size of everything the slag fields are made of. For a hundred years the quarry fed the furnace its flux — grey bedded limestone, cut in benches, blasted, crushed, carted over the ridge and melted into iron's company — and what the furnace didn't keep, it poured out the back as slag. The black plateau behind the works and the white-walled hole in the ridge are the same object with the sign reversed. One is the county's stone subtracted; the other is the county's stone put back down wrong, as glass. The works stands between them like a heart between two lungs, and for a century it breathed rock from one into the other.

The pit closed in 1989. The autumn pour the locals know by that year — the pour with the fissure, the pour with the bubble under it — was made, in part, from the quarry's last consignment. The walls of the chapel-shaped room, the peacock glass, the dimpled ceiling: some fraction of all of it lay in these benches as limestone, in beds laid down by a sea, until the year the pumps were pulled. So the two places have the same birthday. The locals, who name things by year without ever deciding to, call the pour *the '89 pour* and the lake *the '89 water*, and do not seem to notice they have named twins.

Because when the pumps stopped, the water came back. Groundwater had been held out of the pit by machinery for a century — a river's worth of it, lifted and thrown away daily — and with the pumps gone it rose like a tide that had been waiting, a metre a season, drowning the benches one by one, the rails, the ramp, the pump house with its door left open. It took nine years to fill. What it made is the one blue thing in a black-and-green country: an oval of water four hundred metres long, cliff-walled, so mineral-clear over its white ledges that from the rim it looks tropical, and so cold a metre down that the look is a lie.

From above it reads as empty — a flooded absence, a grave for a hill. It is neither.

**The water.**
The lake is not one water. It is two, stacked, and they have never met.

The upper ten metres live an ordinary lake's life: warmed in summer, chilled in winter, stirred top to bottom by the spring and autumn gales. That upper water breathes twice a year, the way the bubble over the ridge breathes twice a day — turnover instead of the fissure's nightly sigh, the same transaction on a slower clock.

Below ten metres the water is different in kind. It rose out of the ground dense with dissolved stone, heavier than any rain that has fallen on it since, and it has simply never come up. It sits in the bottom twenty-five metres of the pit, dark, cold, oxygenless, untouched by wind or season — water that has not seen the sky since it left the aquifer, decades ago now. The room behind the works is a breath the rock caught and held. The deep pit is the opposite made liquid: a breath never taken, and never to be taken, growing older and stiller every year while the lake above it goes on pretending to be weather.

**The plate.**
Where the two waters meet — eleven metres down, give or take the season — there is a boundary, and the boundary is alive.

The last violet dregs of sunlight reach that depth at the same level the first sulfide seeps up from the dead water below, and in the narrow band where light and poison overlap, purple sulfur bacteria have built a plate: a rose-pink lens, half a metre thick, stretching wall to wall across the entire footprint of the pit. It photosynthesizes on light no plant can use and breathes a chemistry no animal can survive. Seen from above by a diver it is a pink floor over the abyss; seen from below it would be a pink sky, though nothing lives below it to look. It rebuilds itself within hours of any disturbance, continuously renewed from beneath — and if that sentence sounds familiar, it should. The metallic film on the pool in the bubble does the same work in the same way: a living membrane on a boundary, eating what the rock exhales. The film and the plate are not kin by descent. They are kin by profession — the same trade practiced in the two rooms the works made, one the size of a dinner plate, one the size of a drowned quarry, both of them the first thing that ever lived where they live.

**The shelves.**
The quarry's working benches — the flat terraces the cutters stood on — are now littoral shelves at two, five, and eight metres, and they have been colonized by the one organism that could answer the pit's oldest debt.

Stoneworts. Waterfowl brought them, spore by spore, in the late nineties, and now the shelves carry meadows of them — brittle, crunching, olive-green plants that armour themselves in lime pulled straight from the water. They live, they calcify, they die, and their dead settle as marl: soft white carbonate mud, a millimetre a year, accumulating on the benches in beds. Which is to say: *the pit is making limestone.* In the hole where a hundred years of stone was cut out and carried over the ridge, new stone is quietly being laid down, in beds, by a shallow mineral sea, exactly as the old stone was — only this sea is four hundred metres long and the process has an audience of nobody. Over the ridge, the bubble grows its stalactite straws, a finger a decade, from calcium leached out of the slag. Follow that calcium back and it came from these benches: bed to furnace to pour to leachwater to straw. The mountain is not gone. The mountain is in transit — coming down as glass behind the works, dripping into straws inside the bubble, settling as new marl in its own old grave — and every station on the route is alive.

**The fish.**
Sticklebacks, from a jam jar. Children did it, sometime around the turn of the millennium, the way children always do it, and the pit's fast clock has been working on the descendants ever since — because the pit runs the same accelerated time as the bubble. Not two species yet. But already two habits hardening into two shapes: a heavy-armoured, deep-bodied form that works the stonewort meadows, and a pale, slender, huge-eyed form that hangs out over the void in loose shoals, hunting the daphnia clouds in open water. Twenty-odd years, forty-odd generations, and the split is visible in the hand. Islands do this to snails; young lakes do it to sticklebacks; the works, without meaning to, built two laboratories for the same experiment and stocked them by accident.

The fish never go below the plate. They cannot — there is no oxygen under it — and so the pink lens is their floor, as absolute as the bubble's glass walls, though it is only water all the way down. Every stickleback in the pit lives its whole life suspended over a deep it will never enter, above a boundary it cannot see but has never once crossed. In the room over the ridge, the midges have never required the sky to exist. Here, the fish have never required the bottom. Each place raises its residents inside a world with one edge missing, and the residents are fine.

**The drowned architecture.**
Below the plate, nothing decays.

No oxygen, no scavengers, no burrowers, no current: the dead water is a preservative, and the quarry's abandoned floor stands in it like a sealed exhibit. The pump house is intact at the base of the ramp, thirty metres down, door open as it was left, roof on, a kettle still on the cold stove — everything skinned matte black by sulfide but whole. Rails run to a tipped wagon. The crane jib leans where it leaned. A ladder bolted to the north wall runs from the sunlit shallows down through the pink plate into the dark — the only staircase between the two countries, its upper rungs furred green with algae and sponge, its lower rungs bare and black and exactly as machined.

Over the ridge, the slag swallowed the works' accidents and made them geology — the chain in the wall, the gear in the floor, tools promoted to landscape. Here the dead water has done the opposite and made them museum: nothing absorbed, nothing transformed, everything merely *kept*. The bubble digests its artifacts. The pit remembers hers.

**The birds, and the ferry.**
By day the surface belongs to swifts and swallows, skating the midge hatches, and in winter to a pair of grebes that work the shoals and keep the sticklebacks honest.

At dusk it belongs to the connection made flesh. Within minutes of pouring up through the fissure behind the works, the bat colony arrives over the pit — three kilometres in a line their grandmothers drew, over the ridge the flux carts used to cross — and takes the first drink of every night off this water, sixty low passes, sixty touched circles spreading on the '89 water from the residents of the '89 pour. Then they hunt, and the pit's midge hatches are the first course, and by dawn those midges have been carried over the ridge, digested, and dropped as guano onto the warm floor of the bubble, where they become the soil that feeds the springtails that feed the crickets that feed the lizards and the bats themselves. The lake's plankton ends up as the chapel's floor. The two places have never touched — one is sealed rock, one is open water, and a ridge stands between them — and they exchange bodies nightly. The bats are the ferry, and they have been running the route so long that the route is now part of what a bat from this colony *is*.

**The deep.**
And under everything, the archive. The still water below the plate receives what settles — each year's pollen, each year's dust, whatever the works' chimneys put on the wind in their day — and because nothing stirs and nothing burrows, it all lies down in order: annual layers, fine as paper, on the black floor beside the pump house. The pit has been keeping a diary since the pumps stopped, the way the snail-shell drift in the bubble keeps one — chemistry recorded in bands, in the dark, by no one, for no one. Between the shell midden against the cool wall and the varved mud under the dead water, the works' whole afterlife is being written twice, in two hands, and both manuscripts are sealed inside the two places everyone above is sure hold nothing.

**The one who rows out.**
Twice a year — ice-out and the first still week of autumn — an inflatable boat comes down the haul road strapped to a roof rack. The same headlamp, the same folding rule, the same notebook gone soft with damp. Their father cut flux in this pit until the year it closed; they helped pour the slag it became. The notebook is filled from both ends: the bubble's measurements advancing from the front — straw lengths, the fissure's width, the walls' slow closing — and the pit's from the back — a thermometer on a knotted line, a stoppered bottle lowered through the plate and hauled up smelling of struck matches, the depth of the pink water noted to the half-metre. One entry each visit, same pencil. The walls of the room close a millimetre a year. The marl on the benches rises a millimetre a year. Somewhere in the middle pages, decades from now, the two records will meet, and the family's account of what the works did to the county — subtraction and addition, hole and heap — will be complete in a single book that no one else knows exists.

The grebes no longer lift when the boat goes out. The pit has filed this person under weather too.

**What lives there.**
So the works made two rooms without meaning to, and both came alive. One is air held in rock, hot, young, packed to its glass skin, racing — a decade doing a millennium's work, breathing daily through a crack two hands wide. The other is water held in stone, cold, layered, patient — half its body a breath never taken, breathing twice a year at the top and not at all below, keeping everything, hurrying nothing. The stone that walls the first was cut from the second; the calcium dripping into straws in one is settling into new bedrock in the other; the bats stitch them together every night of the year, and one soft notebook holds them both.

From the ridge between them you can see the whole arrangement at once: the black plateau steaming faintly on a cold morning, the blue oval flashing in its white walls, and the works in the middle, still pouring, still paying out heat and stone in both directions. Everyone above believes the black field is dead and the blue water is empty. They are wrong in the same way about both — and the two places, hot twin and cold twin, born the same year of the same hill, go on trading bodies over the ridge in the dark, keeping their separate diaries, waiting out the people who are certain there is nothing to read.
