**The Ground**

You come down to it through the same cloud — down the flank of one of the great descending buttresses, or under the weather from the flat country beyond — and the first thing you understand, before the rain, before the pillars, before anything else, is that here there is nothing *but* ground. Ground in the fullest mineral sense. Stone under soil under loam, black and dense and deep, giving off the cold honest smell of ended things. After the sprung drum-skin of the world above, the stillness of it under your feet is shocking. Nothing flexes. Nothing answers your step. The earth takes your weight the way a fact takes an argument: completely, and without comment.

It rains here most of the time, because this is where the weather goes. The clouds that form below the Terraces have to fall somewhere, and they fall on The Ground, a grey-green rain-country of low hills and full rivers lying in the permanent soft shadow of the ceiling. Look up and you will not see sky. You will see cloud, and standing down through the cloud, spaced miles apart, the pillars: columns of buttressed living root, each one the width of a town at its foot, descending out of the overcast and planting themselves into the earth like the legs of something too large to have a body. Their tops are never visible. They go up into the grey and do not come back. The people who live here have never seen what the pillars hold, and they do not need to. They read it, the way sailors read a sea floor they will never touch, in everything that comes down.

Three things come down.

The first is the snow. It falls finely and almost always, a slow dark sift drifting out of the cloud-ceiling: spent tissue, fine as ash, soft as felt. It is the shed interior of the world above — because the Terraces, understand, do not lack autumn. They *export* it. Every leaf never dropped, every death declined, every completion refused up there in the warm suspended light is passed inward and downward, layer under layer through the descending weave, and arrives here as a black snow that has been falling since before anyone's grandmother's grandmother. The Ground is made of it. Dig anywhere and you dig through the composted refusals of forever, loam thirty feet deep, the richest soil in the world: an entire landscape floored in everything the pause above declined to be.

The second is the water. At the foot of every pillar there are springs — not seeps but gushing mouths, whole rivers born fully formed out of the root-buttresses — and the water they give is warm, faintly resinous, sweet, tasting unmistakably of the inside of a greenhouse crossed with the inside of a violin. It is old rain. It fell on the drum-skin of the Terraces a lifetime ago, two lifetimes, stood in those bright inch-deep sheets holding somebody's reflection, and then went down, filtered for decades through the living miles, and comes out here still carrying its warmth like a remembered afternoon. The Ground-folk say every drop still holds its *tok* — the round resonant note it made when it first struck the world — and that if you drink from a spring in silence you can feel, very faintly, the drum it fell on.

The third thing that comes down is sound, and this takes the longest to notice, because you have to put your ear against a pillar to hear it. Do that — press your ear to the wet root-bark, the way above you pressed it to the warm floor — and you will hear, from very far up, a chord. Open, unresolved, sustained past all patience: one of the great hollow halls, sounding in the wind a mile overhead, its voice bleeding slowly down the trunk-pipes toward the earth. The Ground-folk call these *the standing questions*. Every pillar carries one. They have been descending for so long that the question you hear today was asked, up there in the suspended light, before you were born.

Otherwise the Ground is loud the way the Terraces never are: biologically, riotously. Birds in real flocks, colored and quarrelsome. Insects in clouds over the rivers. Frogs at dusk in their thousands. Everything here breeds, calls, hunts, is hunted, dies, and rots, and the black snow feeds all of it, and it all happens *fast*. Seasons here run in weeks. Flowers open at dawn and seed by nightfall. Trees shoot up, fruit heavily, grey, hollow, fall, and are eaten back into the loam within a handful of years. In the eternal pause overhead a soft tendril-tip has been considering its first movement for centuries; down here, in the same span, whole forests have lived out their entire arguments and reached their conclusions and been buried. That is the deepest law of the place, the one everything else obeys: on The Ground, everything finishes.

---

The people finish too. That is the first thing you understand about them, before you understand anything else, and they would want it understood first.

You will see them quickly, because unlike the dwellers above they do not hold still — they are dark, small, quick, soil-colored, out in the rain in oiled coats, and their faces are *finished*: every feature carried all the way through, complete, lined, legible, over-written with weather and expression. A Ground-person is born, grows, masters a craft, raises children, greys, and is folded back into the loam in less time than a tendril overhead takes to stir in its sleep. They know this. It does not grieve them. They live the way their flowers live — at full speed toward a good ending — and their whole culture, every custom and craft of it, is a single long study in the art of finishing well.

They use tools. These will be the first tools you have seen in this world, and the sight of them lands strangely, almost indecently, after the grown and grafted country above: knives, chisels, saws, needles, planes. Nothing here is persuaded into place. Everything is cut, shaped, joined, pegged, fastened, *done* — and done beautifully, because to leave a rough edge, here, is very nearly a moral failing. Their houses are small and perfect. Their boats are perfect. In the deep shade under the eaves of the plateau, where the pillars stand thickest, there is a bridge: forty feet of joined timber crossing a spring-river, complete at both ends, maintained across generations with a reverence wildly out of proportion to its size — because a mile above it, invisible past the cloud, the great unfinished bridge still hangs reaching over its gap with its hundred fine green threads. The small bridge below is not a copy. It is a *reply*, sent up too quietly ever to be heard: *this is how it ends. Take your time.*

Their language consists entirely of endings. Conclusions without their openings; the last clauses of sentences; melodies that arrive already on their final descent and land, and close, and are still. They hand each other endings the way other peoples hand each other bread, and the courtesy consists in *receiving* them — in letting a thing be over. And here is the secret at the center of their life, the one they keep the way the dwellers above keep their soft-handed vigil: when a standing question in one of the pillars grows loud enough to be heard at the root-foot, the singers gather. They stand against the wet bark in the rain, sometimes forty of them, sometimes three, and they resolve it — sing the chord shut, gently, completely — and their resolution goes back up the trunk-pipes the way the question came down, rising for decades, arriving overhead so faint and so late that a dweller must come, unhurried, and climb inside the hall, and breathe the chord back open.

Neither people has ever seen the other. Neither is certain the other exists. And the whole world between them is one continuous correspondence — question descending, answer ascending, each side patiently undoing the other's patience — and this correspondence, this argument conducted across a vertical mile and across generations, is the music. It is what the wind is playing. It is what you stopped hearing after an hour, up there, and started breathing with.

There is more, and it is under your feet. The Ground-folk bury their dead in the black loam, at the root-feet, without stone or marker, each finished life folded in among the composted centuries — and the roots drink them. The finished dead feed the pillars; the pillars hold up the pause. Every life that ends well down here in the rain becomes, literally, part of the strength that keeps the world above unfinished — a hand under the open hand. The Ground-folk know this, in the plain way they know everything, and it is the nearest thing they have to a faith: *we finish so that it doesn't have to.* When, once in a long age, a spring at some pillar's foot runs suddenly warmer and does not cool, they hold a small festival, because they know what it means. Someone came home upstairs. One of the standing sleepers gained a thread. They drink the warm water and speak endings over it and are glad, in a neighborly way, for a person they will never see, who lay down a mile above them in the standing water through one full drumming night and became the floor.

The pale bird comes here. On The Ground it lands heavily — thumping down into the mud like any mortal thing, ruffled, rained-on, at weight among its own kind, which is to say among finished things — and the Ground-folk look at it directly, which no one above will do, and feed it, and have given it a name. The name is an ending, the last word of a sentence nobody remembers beginning, and it suits the bird exactly. Above, they say it is a thought the Terraces completed and could not keep. Below, they say it was once one of *them* — because once in a long age, this happens too: a Ground-person leaves something unfinished. No one knows why. A phrase left open; a door not closed; a love kept beginning. And that one grows pale, and downy, and light, and loses weight the way the dying elsewhere lose warmth, until one grey morning they climb a pillar into the cloud and do not come back. Two countries, two stories, one bird; it crosses between the tellings the way it crosses between the lands — without invitation, and gone again — and does not say.

If you come down here from above — and you will be known at once for having done it, by the resin still on your boots and by something the Ground-folk can apparently see in the set of your shoulders — you will be fed, and dried, and asked no questions, because questions are openings and they will not burden you with one. But before you leave, someone will notice your hand. The right one. The one a dweller began a greeting toward, up there in the suspended light, and left forever open at your side — because you have been carrying it that way, mid-offer, without knowing, the way everyone who comes down carries it.

An old woman, most likely, quick and grey and nearly finished herself, will cross the wet ground to you, take that half-raised hand in both of hers, and close it. Firmly. Kindly. All the way. And the greeting begun for you a mile overhead, in another country's frozen morning, will be complete at last — finished down here in the rain, by someone who will be folded into the loam long before the sound of it climbs back up.

You have not been dismissed. You have been answered.
