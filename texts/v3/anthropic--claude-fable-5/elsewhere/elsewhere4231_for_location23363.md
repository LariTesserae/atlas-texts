## The Risings

There is a town at the foot of the mountain, and the town has never seen the sky.

Seven kilometers below the notch, at the place where the young black rock finally runs out into gravel plains, the cloud-sea comes down to meet the ground. Seen from the lip of the notch it is a floor of gold at evening, stretching to every horizon. Seen from beneath it is a ceiling: low, permanent, the gray of wet slate, sagging between the foothills, never higher than a few hundred meters and often close enough to touch from a rooftop. The mountain rises into it and vanishes. Most people in the town have lived their whole lives within sight of the greatest spire on earth and have never seen more than its ankles.

It rains most days — not the warm cloudless drip-rain of the notch, but a thin cold drizzle, the ceiling leaking. The land around is sodden moor and dark gravel, and it would be a miserable place to put a town, except for one thing.

The ground here is warm, and the ground gives water back.

## The Water

They are called the risings, and there are dozens of them: places where the black rock splits and hot water comes up out of the earth, spreading into terraced pools rimmed with pale sinter, steaming gently under the drizzle. The oldest risings have built themselves stepped aprons of mineral the size of amphitheaters, white and cream and rust, descending pool by pool, each pool a degree cooler than the one above, until the water slips away body-temperature into the moor.

This is the mountain paying its debts. What the spire drinks from the sky at its summit, it returns at its foot. The rain that falls without a cloud in the green notch — onto moss, through half a meter of peat, into fracture seams, down the hot veins of the spire's plumbing, kilometer after kilometer through the body of the youngest mountain in the world — comes out here. No one can say how long the descent takes. The water rising into the town's pools today may have fallen as a droplet from a fern frond before anyone now living was born.

You can taste the journey. The spring water is faintly brown, soft on the tongue, with a note in it like steeped leaves — the tea of the mountain's only soil, the first peat that young rock has ever owned, seeping its signature down through seven kilometers of stone. The townspeople say their water tastes of *up there* and mean nothing by it. They are exactly right.

The notch makes its own weather; so does the town. The warm pools steam day and night, and the steam rises a few hundred meters and condenses, and this is a large part of why the ceiling never lifts. The town manufactures its own cloud the way the notch manufactures its own rain. The difference between the two places is only which side of the water they live on: one drinks the cloud-sea from above, in perpetual light; the other feeds it from below, in perpetual gray. Between them runs a single circulation — sky to moss to peat to stone to spring to steam to sky — and each place is the other's weather, and neither has ever seen the other, and neither ever will.

## The Town

Perhaps four hundred people, which means the town, like the notch, can be counted — and often is, by its own bell-keeper, who tolls the number every new year from the tower by the largest rising.

Everything is built around the warm water. Houses of dark stone stand with their backs to the pools, warm channels running under their floors, so that no hearth in the town has ever needed to burn all night. There are bathhouses — the town has more bathhouses than it has streets — and laundries where the washing steams on lines under the drizzle, and long low weirs in the outflow brooks where pale fat eels are farmed in water that never chills. In cellars kept humid by spring-heat, mushrooms are grown in tiered beds; on the rare bright days when the ceiling thins to a luminous pearl-gray, gardens of leaf vegetables the color of jade — nothing here ever quite achieves green — make what they can of the light. No grain grows. Grain comes up the long road twice a year, traded for eels smoked over peat brought from the moor, and for the water itself, bottled, which is prized in the low country for the taste no one can place.

The sound of the town is the opposite of the notch's roar. The cloud damps everything. There is the murmur of warm water in a hundred channels, the drip of eaves, the flat clank of the hour bell muffled to softness, voices carrying strangely far in the wet still air. And under all of it, if you put your ear to any wellhead on a quiet night, a faint far breath that is not quite the water — a sighing in the deep veins, out of rhythm with everything, which the townspeople call the mountain sleeping and think no more about. Seven kilometers up, that breath has a machine throttled onto it. Down here it is only a sound in a pipe, and old.

## The Cold Works

At the north edge of town stands the largest building for a hundred kilometers: the foundry, roof intact, doors on their hinges, cold for four generations.

The boring company built it. They came when the town was a hamlet of shepherds huddled at two risings, and they sank the great wells — cased in iron, still sweet, still the town's best water — and while they were here they cast, in this building, a machine. The town remembers the night of the great pour the way other towns remember battles. Every crucible they had, run together; the whole population standing back against the walls; and when the metal went into the mold the light of it struck up through the smoke-hole and lit the underside of the cloud orange from horizon to horizon. People walked out into the fields to look at their ceiling burning. It is the only sunset the town has ever had, and they made it themselves, and what they made in that light was a flywheel — a wheel taller than a door, poured in one night, ground true over a year.

Then two of the company's engineers packed it upward. It took mule caravans two full seasons: the wheel in segments, bearings the size of barrels, barrels of mineral oil, fanned plates of copper by the hundredweight, iron sheet for a building no one below would ever see. The grandmothers' grandmothers of the present town watched the caravans file up into the cloud and out of the world. The purpose, as far as anyone understood it, was a kind of question put to the mountain: the company had proven the heat ran under the town; the two who went up meant to prove it ran the whole height of the spire, that the mountain was a single circulating body from root to crown, drinking the sky at the top and pouring it out at the bottom. They built an instrument to measure how much sky a mountain could drink, and they built it to run without them.

One of them came back down. He came alone, a year late, thin, and he lived in the town another forty years and never fully rejoined it. He kept the company's record hall after the company itself dissolved. He kept a tin mug, the twin of one he said he'd left on a shelf up there, and his mug sat empty on his own shelf all his life — down here, nothing fills a mug that is not held under water on purpose. He told children, when they asked, that above the weather there was a country of light no bigger than the bell-square, where it rained upward out of warm rock, where a garden grew that had no business growing, where a machine turned like a slow heart and a small dark bird would land on your boot and sing louder than the machine. The town filed all of this, gently, under the tales of a man who had been too long alone at altitude. Every word of it was true. He was the only human being who ever stood in both halves of the mountain's body, and he died the only one who knew they were halves.

In the foundry, on the north wall, the wooden casting pattern of the flywheel still hangs where the pattern-makers left it — a wheel of pale lamination taller than a door, spoked, mounted like a rose window in a church nobody deconsecrated. Once a year the bell-keeper oils it against the damp. Nobody remembers deciding to do this. It is simply done, the way you tend a grave, or a door.

## The Pale Ones

Every good well in town has one, and a well without one is watched with suspicion until one moves in.

They are the length of a forearm, low and long, soft-skinned, the white of sinter, and blind — eyeless entirely, their faces smooth. Three toes on each foot, no claws. They live in the warm throats of the wells and the deep channels of the risings, breathing through their skin, eating whatever small blind things the warm veins carry up. On mild nights they leave the water and walk between pools, and the morning drizzle erases their three-toed prints from the warm mud before most of the town is awake. Children feed them scraps of eel and consider them lucky; the well-keepers consider them proof, since a pale one will not stay in water that has gone wrong, and in living memory they have never once been wrong.

The town believes it knows them completely, the way you know a house cat. The town knows the least part. The pale ones are the surface population of a country — the water-filled veins of the mountain itself, thousands of kilometers of warm flooded darkness threading upward through the youngest rock on earth — and for as long as the spire has existed, some of their lineages have been following the heat up through it, generation by generation, as roots follow water down. The well by the bell-tower and the mud of the notch, seven kilometers apart, are two balconies of the same house. What walks at night among the tree ferns of the green notch, puncturing snail shells and teaching its children the only sky their kind will stand under, is the town's well-fish's cousin, grown great in the long climb. Forty wrens scream at it on principle. Four hundred people, at the other door of its country, hand-feed its relatives and have done so for a hundred years without one bad word.

The engineer knew. It is in the records, if anyone read them.

## The Drawer

The record hall is one room behind the foundry, dry by the grace of a warm channel under its floor — the best-preserved paper for a great distance in any direction. Boring logs. Casting ledgers. Forty years of numbers in a careful hand: condensate yields, brought down annually at first, then estimated, then continued anyway out of what can only have been love.

And one field notebook, never published, never even much read. In it there is a bird, drawn in pencil, plump and dark and round, and beneath the drawing a name in careful letters — the only name that species has ever been given, sleeping in a drawer under the clouds while forty of its bearers sing in the light above them, formally unknown to science, informally known to one dead man. On the facing page there are five lines of musical notation. A long descending phrase. It is the wrens' one song as it was sung a century ago — the old song, the one the village has since revised syllable by syllable into something else, the one that is gone absolutely from the world above. It is not gone. It is here, in ink, in the dark, in a town where no one has ever heard a wren, waiting for someone to hum it. The notch's only history is kept by the one place the notch will never touch, and neither of them knows.

Further on, in the margin of a page of yields, one sentence, small: *Prints again at the vent mud. Old friends from the wells, I think, grown great.* He had fed pale ones as a boy at the town's springs. Alone at seven thousand meters, in the roar, he recognized his neighbors.

Once in a long while — a few times a decade — a small gray fringe-winged moth is found dead on a lit windowsill in town, a species matching nothing within a thousand kilometers, and it goes unremarked, or into a child's box of oddments. It has fallen seven kilometers through the cloud-sea, one of the notch's endless outward gamble of emigrants, a lottery ticket blown all the way down to the one settlement of the mountain's other half. The mail arrives. No one can read it.

## Time

The town does not grow and does not die. The bell-keeper's count wanders between three-eighty and four-twenty and has for a century. The wells run warm and will for millennia; the heat is the same long patient bleed that turns the flywheel above, and it retires nothing before its time. The sinter terraces thicken by a fingernail a year. The eels fatten. The pattern-wheel gets its oil. The empty tin mug stands on a shelf in the record hall next to the notebook, the dry twin of a full one, a table set at both ends of the mountain for the same two people, neither of whom is coming.

Nobody living here has seen the sun, or a star, or the spire they live on. What they have instead is the water — warm out of the dark, tasting faintly of a garden they will never visit, carrying down to them, dissolved and anonymous, the actual substance of the country of light: rain that beaded on filmy ferns, that ran off copper polished nightly by snails, that soaked the peat that is the old machine's only child. They bathe in it. They are born into it and washed in it at the end. Between the notch and the town there is no road, no line of sight, no living messenger — only a column of stone and water and heat, and one dead man's handwriting, and a breath in the wellheads out of rhythm with everything.

Stand in the bell-square at evening, when the day's drizzle stops and the pools steam harder in the cooling air, and watch the town's breath go up — hundreds of white columns rising off the risings, climbing, fraying, joining the gray ceiling and vanishing into it, off to become the cloud-sea whose golden roof, at this same hour, is blazing under a sun the town has never seen, at the foot of a green notch that is drinking it. The bell tolls the hour into the soft air. In the wells, the pale ones stir. In the drawer, the dead song keeps. And seven kilometers overhead, above the weather, above everything, the wheel that was poured here in the town's one night of sunlight turns its patient four-second heart, sending water down and needing nothing back, the two halves of the mountain holding each other alive without acquaintance, without gratitude, without end — with no apparent intention, either of them, of ever stopping.
