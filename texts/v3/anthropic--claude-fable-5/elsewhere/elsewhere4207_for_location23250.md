**The Tacet**

Leave the Antiphon Basin by the western passes and the chord follows you for days. This is the first thing the caravans teach you: how long a sound that size takes to let go. The upper tones fall away first, then the middle voices, and finally, somewhere in the dry hill country beyond the rim, there comes a morning when the fundamental — which you have stopped hearing and started merely carrying, a pressure in the sternum, a fact of the body — is not there. Travelers mark the spot. The Basin-born weep, quietly and without embarrassment, the way people weep at borders. The road climbs for another two weeks after that, up through scrub and scree and then above the scrub, onto a plateau where the air goes thin and cold and hard, and at the end of the third week — the Tacet is three weeks from the Basin, exactly, a coincidence that has recently stopped feeling like one — the land levels out into a steppe the color of unpolished silver, and you are there, though there is no line to tell you so. That is the point. The Basin begins along an edge you could cut your hand on. The Tacet does not begin at all. Everything in it is transition; nothing in it is edge; you are an hour deep in the ruin before you understand that the low hills you have been walking past are not hills.

The silence is the first strangeness, and it is not an absence. Newcomers expect absence and are ambushed by what they get instead, which is an achievement — a made thing, a silence with craftsmanship in it. The wind blows almost constantly out of the west, a steady press you can lean into, and it makes no sound, because in a thousand years it has found nothing left to play. Every surface on the steppe has been faired. The stones are lenses. The ruins are swells. The grass — the famous grass, which the residents call **the nap**, as if the whole steppe were the surface of a bolt of cloth — grows in felted mats, its blades fused side to side so that no wind can part them and make them hiss, and walking on it is like walking on held breath. Even when the nap dies it does not rattle. It compacts. Stand still in the open and the loudest thing on the plateau is your own blood, and after that your joints, and after that — for those who have lived in the Basin — a phantom chord, faint and insistent, which the mind supplies because it can no longer tolerate the alternative. Traders who work the route say the two ends of the road ruin you in opposite directions, and that the trick of the profession is never staying long enough at either end to be finished.

Where the Basin hoards its light, the Tacet is drowned in it. There is nothing overhead here — no canopy, no filament layer, no filtering policy — only thin air and a sun that comes down like judgment, and the residents go about in visors of carved bone with slits fine as paper cuts. At night the temperature falls past frost and the sky opens into a savagery of stars that Basin visitors, who have never once seen past their own dim ceiling, sometimes cannot look at for more than a few seconds at a time. Sight is the only sense this place fully honors. The steppe is so flat and the air so clean that a standing figure is visible at three kilometers, a signing figure legible at one, and the residents hold conversations across distances that would swallow any voice, hands flashing in the glare, patient, articulate, dry.

The hills that are not hills are called **the knuckles**. They rise from the nap in long smooth swells, some hip-high, some the size of barrows, and they are arranged — you feel this before you measure it — in a grid, kilometers on a side, marching to the horizon in every direction. Under the lichen, which grows on them in slow rings the residents read the way other people read tree stumps, the knuckles are a material between ceramic and bone, worn lustrous, faintly translucent at the crests, like the inside of a tooth. The lichen says a thousand years, give or take a century. The knuckles are the tops of pylons. Everyone has always known this; the Tacet has been mapped for centuries, and it is the reason the mapping civilization built its listening instruments in the first place — the seismic ears that registered, three weeks ago, a vibration in a far southern valley, were designed to a craft the Tacet's people have kept alive for forty generations, a craft of attending to the ground. What was not known until three weeks ago is this: within days of the Basin's emergence, someone laid the new survey over the old maps, and the grids agree. Same spacing. Same orientation. Extended across the fifteen hundred kilometers between them, the two lattices meet in phase, within the error of the instruments, which is duly recorded, and which no one has explained, and which means — if it means anything — that the Tacet and the Antiphon Basin are not two cities. They are two exposures of one city, worn down at one end and still precipitating at the other, and the steppe and the drylands and the passes in between are simply the part that has not surfaced.

Underground, the Tacet is not worn at all. The residents dig their wells against the knuckles, because the roots of the old pylons go down past any depth anyone has reached and the water gathers along them, and every settlement has, at the bottom of its deepest digging, a chamber where the root-material breaches the wall — a smooth cool flank of the buried city, dense and dry and perfect. These are the **ear-rooms**. For a thousand years, the vocation of the listeners has been to go down into them, lay palms and brow against the stone, and hear nothing. This is not considered a failure. It is considered the keeping of an appointment.

The steppe is not empty of voices; it has merely moved them out of the air. Walk barefoot — the residents mostly do, or wear soles of thin felt, for exactly this reason — and the ground turns out to be a marketplace. The herd animals of the plateau, dense-wooled, sheep-sized, are called **the gossips**, half a joke and half not, because they never stop talking: their speech is footfall, a language of thumps and rolls and stutters transmitted through the soil, and a herd at its ease fills the ground for a kilometer around with a soft continuous felt of conversation you receive through the arches of your feet. Their one predator is a wide pale glider the residents call **the sail**, which makes no sound at all, whose approach is announced by nothing, only a shadow — and the gossips' defense is the steppe's single commandment, the same one the Basin's creatures obey for their own deleting hunter: they stop talking, and they hold still, and they wait for the quiet thing to pass.

What lives in the Tacet lives as a kept promise. The residents — a few thousand, in low turf-and-felt dwellings faired against the wind like everything else — do not call themselves anything aloud, because they say very little aloud; speech among them is an indoor practice, rare and whispered, the way other cultures treat prayer. Their true tongue is sign, and it is no pidgin: it has registers, meters, an evening tradition of poetry performed in the long horizontal light to audiences who watch hands the way audiences elsewhere close their eyes to listen. Every hand-language on the trade roads descends from it. The sign the Basin's residents use, the flat palm drawn slowly across the chest, the one they will not voice, the one that means *erasure* — that is a loanword, generations old, worn smooth by distance like everything else that leaves this plateau. In the Tacet it is not a word for a predator. It is the oldest sign in the language, and it is the name of the place, and what it means, as exactly as translation allows, is: *a written silence — a rest with a length*. The Basin's transcribers, the honest ones, have begun to sit with the fact that the deletion that moves through their chord and the thousand-year quiet of this steppe answer to the same word. A rest is a rest at any scale, the Tacet's usage implies. Every utterance keeps its silences somewhere.

The vocations here mirror the Basin's without either place having planned it. Where the Basin has transcribers, the Tacet has the listeners in their ear-rooms, keeping the appointment. Where the Basin has residents muffling themselves against a voice, the Tacet has the **smoothers**, who walk circuits of the steppe with pumice and abrasive cloth, silencing whatever the wind might learn to play — a frost-cracked stone beginning to whistle, a dead gossip's ribcage, a trader's badly rigged tent line — a priesthood of maintenance whose scripture is aerodynamics. And where the Basin produces the open, the Tacet has always produced its own deserters in the opposite direction: the **wind-hungry**, children born with an ailment their people regard with sorrow and no blame, an appetite for sound that the steppe cannot feed. For centuries they left with the caravans, and it is quietly understood that all the music of the surrounding world descends from them — that every song sung in the low countries was written by the exiled children of a silent land. Now they go east. Several of the open, standing rapt in the tea-colored dusk of the Basin with frogs calling from their shoulders, were born on this plateau; there is at least one mother in the Tacet who knows, from a trader's careful signing, exactly where her daughter is standing. The commerce runs the other way too, and it is the Tacet's bitterest joke, though they tell it with still faces: their one export is felt, the nap and the gossips' underwool beaten together into the finest sound-deadening anyone has ever made, and its best customers, these three weeks past, are a few thousand people in a far valley trying to keep a new voice out of their sleep. The silence country sells silence to the neighbors of a god.

Their own account of the old city is kept in a signed liturgy, performed once a year at the turning of the season, and it is brief. *We finished speaking,* it says. *We are keeping the silence between the verses.* Whether this is memory or consolation is contested — the low-country scholars have always preferred the crueler reading, that the city asked its question, wore out asking, and died unanswered, and that the liturgy is a thousand years of a widow saying *he's just stepped out*. The Tacet's people do not argue. Their position is in their name: the silence has a length, and the length is written, and it is not theirs to know, only to keep.

Three weeks ago, the keeping ended.

It was night, and the duty listener — an old woman who had held the post for a decade and heard in that decade her own pulse and nothing else — had her palms on the root-stone when the stone, very slightly, pushed back. Not a sound. A pressure, arriving through the deep material, and beneath it, at the very floor of what a body can register, the beginning of a hum. She came up out of the ear-room and stood in the doorway in the starlight making the oldest sign in her language over and over with a modifier no one present had ever seen used in earnest: the flat palm across the chest, and then the two hands drawing apart and stopping. *The rest — its length — ending.*

By the time the first caravan came up the passes with the news — a new city, ringing from the first moment, three weeks east — the whole population was already out on the knuckles, and the traders' signing was met with the flattest sentence the Tacet has ever produced: *We know. It came up through the ground before you crossed the first pass.* The hum is in most of the ear-rooms now, and strengthening by a measurable fraction each week; the listeners keep continuous vigil, and letters from the Basin's transcribers come up with every caravan, so that one thing is now being watched from both of its ends by two sets of instruments three weeks apart. The root-stone has begun to sweat — beads of moisture on its flank, the first city-water on this plateau in a thousand years, and the smoothers wipe it away and it returns. On the western face of one knuckle a fresh edge has appeared: not exposed by erosion but grown, a straight line you could cut your hand on, the first edge the steppe has held in living memory. The smoothers ground it away, and it was back by morning, and they ground it away again, and it was back, and after the third time their order stopped, and has not yet decided what it is for. The gossips have gone strange. At night they gather on the knuckles, whole herds, silent, pressed flat to the worn bone with their bellies to the ground, listening with their whole bodies to whatever is coming up through it, and the sails ride the dark higher than anyone has seen them go.

Some families have packed. Most have not, because — as one of the listeners signed to a trader who asked, her hands moving slowly so he would not mistake her — you do not keep an appointment for forty generations and leave when it is kept. And there is the last strangeness, the quietest, the one they discuss least. Some of the Tacet-born have begun to hum in their sleep. Each of them holds a tone a few cents off the pitch rising in the knuckle nearest their birth, and their families sit up at night in the felt-lined dark watching them, and do not wake them, because waking them stops it, and there is a feeling in the houses, unargued and general, that stopping it would be rude — that something a thousand years patient is at last clearing its throat, and is doing it, courteously, through the sleeping.

An antiphon is not a song; it is half of one. Everyone in the Tacet has always known which half they lived inside. Now, at both ends of a three-weeks' road, a few thousand people lie awake with a tone in the sternum — one growing toward a question, one toward its answer — and everything alive in the fifteen hundred kilometers between them, the drylands and the passes and the unsurfaced city sleeping under all of it, lives inside the interval: the breath between call and response, held for a thousand years, and now, at last, very slowly, being let out.
