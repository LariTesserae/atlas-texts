# The Unbraiding

You come to it the way the river does: by coming apart.

For most of its length the river is one argument — a single broad brown insistence, shouldering down out of the weathering uplands with everything the world has finished riding on its back. Spent leaves. Toppled trunks. The silt of hillsides that gave up. Boats that came unmoored from lives that ended, roofbeams of towns the floods concluded. The river gathers all of it, everything upstream that is over, and carries it south, and then — at the flat warm country where the land loses its conviction — the river forgets how to be one thing.

It unbraids.

One channel becomes three, three become twelve, twelve become a country: a maze of slow bright water and reed and black mud a day's sail across, if the tide agrees, with no firm edge anywhere — no line where you could stand and say *here the delta ends*. Land that is mostly water. Water that is mostly land. A hundred mouths opening to the sea, and the sea reaching back up into them twice a day, so that even the border between river and ocean is a negotiation, renewed at every tide, never settled, never meant to be.

The people who live there call the whole of it the Unbraiding, and they mean the place, and they mean what the place does. Because everything that arrives at the delta comes apart the way the river does — gradually, generously, into more of itself.

---

The light at the Unbraiding is the ordinary miraculous kind: it comes from the sun, and it leaves. There are days here, and the days end. There is weather, and the weather passes. Travelers who have been down to the Germinal Vault — and many who come through the delta have, or will, since the delta is the vault's front country, the last inhabited place before the long way down — say the strangest thing about coming back up is the shadows. In the vault your body is the only dark thing in the world. At the delta, at evening, the whole country lies down in shadow stitched together with gold, and everything casts one, and knows it, and doesn't mind.

Evening is the delta's hour. This is fair, the delta people say. Somewhere else is holding the morning.

The sound of the place is the sound just over silence — the exact inverse of that under-hush in the vault. Wind in reeds is a hush made of a billion small collisions. Under it, layered like sediment: frogs, tide-suck, the creak of woven houses, insects in their trillions, birds whose entire profession is the eating of endings, and — every evening, from every settlement — singing. The delta is never quiet. It would consider quiet a kind of hoarding.

And the smell. Travelers brace for rot and find instead something they have trouble naming afterward: brown, warm, sweet at the bottom, enormous. The smell of things becoming other things. The delta's word for *rotten* is the same as its word for *ready*.

---

Here is the strange thing, the thing the delta is about:

Everything here is ending, all the time, and the place is not sad.

Every ecosystem elsewhere is half-staffed by death — the vast patient guild of molds and worms and flies and scavengers whose living is made of conclusions. In the Germinal Vault that entire guild is absent; there is famously not one thread of fungus in the whole bright length of it, because nothing there has ever finished, and so the guild has no work. Stand in the delta and you understand where the guild went. It is headquartered here. It is *thriving* here. The delta is the guild-hall of the world's endings, and it runs at full employment, and the wealth it generates is everywhere you look: black silt so fertile that a dropped seed is a plant by the month's end, reed-beds thick as fur, more birds in one backwater at dusk than some countries see in a year.

There is no rock at the Unbraiding. None. Dig, and you find silt; dig further, and you find older silt; dig for a lifetime and you will find only *elsewhere* — the crumbled uplands, the finished forests, the ground-down bones of mountains, fathoms deep. The vault, travelers say, stands on stone so young it has never once crumbled. The delta stands on nothing but crumble, and calls it abundance. The two places are the two answers to the same question, and the river runs between them like the question being asked.

Because — and this is the current that binds them, the fact the delta has organized its whole heart around — not all of the river reaches the sea.

At the delta's inland edge, where the channels first begin to separate, one channel turns away from the salt. It narrows. It quickens — the only hurrying water in the whole slow country — and it pours, turning, into a round dark swallow in the earth with a lip worn smooth as a palm. The delta says the river has a hundred mouths and one throat. The Throat drinks a share of the river, day and night, and has since before the songs began, and the water that goes down it does not come back.

The delta knows where it goes.

The water sinks through fathom after fathom of stone, and the stone strips it as it sinks — takes the silt, takes the leaf-meal, takes every particle of ending the river was carrying — until what arrives, far below and long after, is nothing but water: clear, body-warm, one drop at a time, falling from the cool underside of a root-arch into a small still pool where hair-fine rootlets drink at their own reflection. Every heartbeat-drip in the pools of the Germinal Vault fell at the delta first as rain, ran there as river, and went down the Throat with a burden it was made to set down along the way. The vault has no rot because the delta keeps it all. Somewhere, everything must be allowed to finish, or nothing could be afforded a beginning.

The delta took the job. It does the vault's dying for it, and considers this an honor, and a household chore.

The oldest song at the Unbraiding — revised nightly, like all their songs, but never in its bones — says why. It says that long ago the river carried down a seed too great to float to the sea. It says the seed sank, and the earth opened to take it, and the hole it made is the Throat, and the river has been watering what it planted ever since, one drop at a time, and will not stop until the seed is done sprouting. The delta did not discover the vault. The delta's river *planted* it. Downstream of everything, the delta is the gardener's house.

# The Inhabitants of the Unbraiding

Start, again, with the problem of the word.

To *inhabit*, everywhere else, means to stay. By that measure the delta is empty, because nothing stays there — not the ground, which is in transit; not the houses, which are rewoven; not the water, which is tide; not the people, who are mortal and know it better than anyone alive. And yet the delta is the most inhabited place in the world. Everything lives there by passing through it well. The census, then, is not a list of what remains. It is a list of what moves, and how beautifully.

---

## The First Inhabitant: the River Itself

The delta is not a place the river passes through. The delta is the river, taking its time about leaving.

Upstream it is one creature; here it is a hundred; below the Throat it becomes something else again — a drip, a pool, a stillness. The same water at two speeds. In the vault, time is a still pool; at the delta, time is a current; and it is the identical water doing both, which the delta people find neither strange nor mystical but simply *true of water*, the way it is true of a person that they can run and can also sleep. The river is the one being that lives in both countries at once — the only inhabitant the two places share — and the delta treats it accordingly: not worshipped, exactly, but *deferred to*, the way a household defers to the one member who does all the carrying.

---

## What Does Not Stay There

Count the absences, because they are the vault's presences, turned inside out.

There are no ruins at the Unbraiding. No monuments, no heirlooms, no object older than a handful of seasons. There are no graves — the dead go downstream, woven into reed boats, unbraided into the delta the way everything is; *downstream* is the delta's word for died, and it is a calm word, a word you can say at supper. Wounds heal fast here; scars soften and go; travelers notice their small hurts closing almost politely, and think of the Arranger far below, whose one thin scratch has been fresh for centuries. The delta is good at endings, even small ones. Nothing at the delta is arrested, nothing held, nothing kept at its verge. Even grief moves. Especially grief.

Their entire past is carried in song — a spoken archive, sung each evening, corrected, added to, worn smooth by repetition the way the Throat's lip is worn smooth by water. Their language has, famously, seven past tenses and a future tense so weak they mostly borrow one from upstream traders when they need it. They are the world's rememberers, the keepers of everything the vault cannot keep, having no past tense at all.

And in all their generations they have made exactly one permanent object. It is not at the delta. We will come to it.

## The Greylings

The adults are grey the way driftwood is grey — pale things weathered honest — and they are the size of a horse gone soft, and they are the finished versions of the firstlings.

Everything the firstlings are not, the greylings are. Their limbs are resolved: real feet, real weight. Their skin has gone opaque with age; you can no longer see the slow tide inside them. Their eyes have heavy lids, because here things fall — dust, rain, sun, night — and they have closed their eyes a hundred thousand times, and it gives their gaze the guarded, forgiving quality of the old. They sleep, hugely, half-sunk in warm mud. They are scarred. They are mortal. They are exactly what a firstling would be if it were ever allowed to continue, and no firstling ever will be, and both facts live in the delta like two notes of one chord.

They lay their eggs in the warm shallows, and most of the larvae — pale, translucent, hand-sized, lidless — burrow into the sun-heated mud to finish becoming, as larvae everywhere seek heat to finish in. But the fissures near the Throat breathe a deeper warmth, a fever-heat rising from the bright country below, and every season some of the young follow it down, and do not come back, and cannot: the vault keeps them at hatchling, forever, drinking condensation in the endless morning. The delta calls these young *the given*, and does not mourn them, or says it doesn't. They are the lucky ones, the songs insist. They will be new until the world ends, and possibly after.

The greylings know human families. They lean against the descendants of people their kind has known, generation blurring into generation, exactly as the firstlings do below — there is a lineage at the delta, the one that traces back to a woman who went down young and stayed a season, whose members are leaned on by a particular grey cow with a fern-curl of ear, and leaned on far below, by that cow's eternal siblings, at the same pools their great-grandmother knew. To both species of that animal, it seems, a bloodline is simply a person arriving slowly.

And when a greyling grows old enough to feel its ending coming, it does the thing the delta loves them for. It hauls itself overland to the rim of the Throat, and it sings down into the earth — a low continuous note, held for days, until the note stops.

Travelers who have heard both swear to this: the greyling's death-song and the metabolic hum of the Germinal Vault are the same note. The sound a region-sized seedling makes holding its one eternal beginning, and the sound a grey animal makes at the very end of its finite life, are one pitch. No one knows how the greylings learned it, since none of the given ever return to teach it. And no one has ever stood in both places at once, so no one can say whether the moments when every firstling in the vault lifts its head at once — attending, lidless, toward the deepest chamber, as if someone *almost* called — are the moments when, in the stone far above that chamber, a song at the rim is coming to its close. The great bud stands, the delta's songs say, directly beneath the Throat, in the socket the seed made. Whether the firstlings turn toward the bud or toward what is singing above it is a question the geometry declines to settle.

## The Dayswarm

The little comma-shaped swimmers that circle the vault's pools forever have kin at the delta, and here the commas get their sentence.

At dusk — always at dusk — the eggs in the backwaters hatch by the million, and the air over the channels fills with a glittering translucent smoke of them, rising, swarming, mating, falling; by morning they are gone, every one, into the mouths of frogs and birds and the brown generous water, and the next evening's eggs are already laid. Their whole lives fit inside one night, and end, as the delta thinks all good sentences should, on time. The delta's eggs keep their appointments. Down in the vault, their cousins swim slow circles around clusters that have been one day from hatching since before the river-stones were laid, attendants at a nursery that never graduates. Up here the graduation is nightly, and total, and the delta people sit out in their reed boats to watch it the way other peoples watch fireworks.

---

## The Weavers

The people of the Unbraiding cut nothing. It is their oldest courtesy: everything at the delta gives itself, in time, so nothing needs taking — a fact travelers to the vault will recognize, having stood before that polite half-wall of stacked unmortared stone and those wall-stems that bear no scars. *Built by arranging, not by taking* is not a mystery of the vault. It is delta manners, carried down.

They weave. It is their whole material culture: houses, boats, cradles, sails, shrouds — all reed, all deliberately impermanent, rewoven each year or two, because a thing that lasted would be a thing withheld from the river, and the delta does not withhold. When a child is expected, they weave a small dome — a birth-house — and every well-wisher who visits brings one smooth river-stone and sets it in a ring around the dome, and the delta's midwives will tell you, with total professional confidence, that the child comes when the ring is closed.

So now you know what the travelers found in the vault. The small dome of dead brown reeds, intact amid the luminous green — the only dead thing in that whole deathless country — is a birth-house. It is the one permanent object the delta has ever made, permanent only because it stands in the one place where nothing the delta weaves can ever rot. Their civilization's single enduring artifact is a nursery in someone else's morning.

And the ring of stones, a third complete around the great bud, one stone added per visit-span: those are the well-wishers' stones. Every pilgrim who goes down from the delta carries one, smooth from the river, and gives it to the one who waits there to be set in its place.

The delta's name for the Arranger is the Midwife.

The songs keep her whole story. How, generations back, the delta understood at last what the Throat had been watering — not a place but a pregnancy, a birth in progress the size of a region, a bud the size of a hall holding brightness like a full cup. How they did the only thing their customs knew to do for an expected child: sent down their finest midwife with a birth-house on her back and the first apronful of stones, her hair cut for the journey, a scratch on her cheek from a branch on the way down. How she said, at the bottom, that she would be back tomorrow.

Travelers come up from the vault troubled about her, sometimes — her endless fresh sincerity, her few hundred years of leaving in the morning — and the delta receives their worry kindly and sets it down like luggage. She was not lying, they say, and she is not lost. She is using the vault's calendar. The vault has never yet had a tomorrow. When it has its first one — when the ring closes and the bud opens and the one note finally moves to its second note — she will keep her word to the day.

They rebuild her house every year, on its spot by the eastern channel, empty, door open, facing upstream toward the Throat road. It is the only structure at the delta maintained in memory of anyone. Ask why the door is open and they will tell you: *she is leaving tomorrow.* At the delta this is said in the borrowed future tense, and it is said smiling.

## The Expected

Here, finally, is the honest census. Who lives at the Unbraiding? A river dawdling before its hundred goodbyes, gardener to a seed it planted before the songs. The whole employed guild of the world's endings, fat and industrious. Grey mortal mothers who sing the vault's own note down the Throat as they die, and their given children, eternal below. A swarm that lives one night and calls it enough. A people who keep nothing, remember everything, and have spent their single act of permanence on someone else's child.

Because that is the last thing to know about the delta: it is waiting for the same guest.

The vault is the womb; the delta is the world the child will be born into, and it keeps itself ready the way any household does. Every delta child's first bath is in moving water — so the child knows from the start that everything goes — and at the head of the eastern channel, nearest the Throat, a bathing-pool is kept clear and a cloth is kept woven, unworn, rewoven every year, waiting. When the ring closes. When the Midwife comes up the long way with her work finally done, leaving, at last, tomorrow.

Travelers who go on upriver from the Unbraiding all report some version of the same feeling, and it is a strange one to carry out of a country built entirely on endings. Not melancholy. Not relief.

The knowledge that somewhere downstream of every life there is a place with warm water and good silt and singing in the evenings, where being finished is a kind of arrival, where a boat will be woven for you and a word said at supper — and that they, too, are expected.

The vault, in its bright held morning, is expecting one guest.

The delta is expecting everyone.
