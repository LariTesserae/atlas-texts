**The Loud Overstory**

Climb back the way you came — half a day up through the rock — and then there is no more climbing, only the roof of the world: a plain of white salt under a true sky, running flat to the horizon in every direction, and the true sky, after the vault, is the second-strangest thing you will ever stand beneath.

The sound is the first wrong thing, and the most beautiful. There is wind up here — there is always wind; the pan has nothing to spend it on and so it never stops — and the wind carries everything. Nothing interrupts sound on the salt. A footstep travels. A dropped tool rings like an accusation. Two people talking a mile apart can hold a conversation at nearly normal volume, and do. At nightfall, when the day's heat leaves the crust, the whole plain cools and contracts and begins to crack — sharp reports, single and double, near and far, gunshots with no guns, the pan talking itself to sleep for an hour — and travelers who don't know the country lie awake all night with their hearts going, while travelers who do sleep through it like rain.

And the eye gets nothing. That is the inversion, and it takes exactly as long to accept as the silence did below. Down there the plain carried the visual noise of a market and made no sound; up here the plain is visually dead — white to the edge of the world, cracked into polygons like the skin of something enormous, shimmering with mirage-water that is never water — and it is acoustically crowded. The mirages lie to you all day; the ear never does. You learn, crossing this place, to stop looking. It is a country you navigate with your ears, and its people, as you will see, have taken that fact and built a civilization out of it.

The ground is the second wrong thing. It is a roof and it behaves like one. The crust is salt over salt over calcite, going down farther than any well has ever gone, and it does not ring the same everywhere. There are reaches where a stamped heel makes the dull thud of solid earth, and there are reaches — mapped, named, inherited — where the same heel makes a *boom*, round and deep and slightly delayed, the unmistakable voice of a great hollowness underneath. The people here have always known their country is hollow. It is the first thing every child learns: strike the ground, and hear the size of what is under you.

Rain comes rarely and comes enormous — a wall of gray standing on the horizon for a day before it arrives, lightning walking under it on thin legs. And when it arrives, the pan drinks it. There are no rivers here, no pools except one, no runoff at all: the water lands and stands silver for an hour and then goes down, pulled into the seams and swallow-holes and drinking cracks, whole storms vanishing into the ground with a sound like the world sipping. Where the crust is wet it goes glassy, and for a day or two after rain you can look *into* the ground — a blue-white translucence deepening under your feet, ten meters, twenty, light falling away into the salt like light into ice. *The ground has weather inside it*, the people here say, and they are more right than they know. The storm they watch disappear will finish its descent weeks from now, grain by grain through the crystal, and arrive in another country as a bloom of brightness crossing a ceiling — a slow bright cloud sweeping over pale herds and a candied city. The clock the builders told time by is this place's weather, delayed. Rain here is somewhere else's noon.

And the ground breathes. Along certain cracks — thin, unremarkable, easy to walk past — the pan exhales when the weather turns and inhales when it settles: a cool, damp, mineral breath sighing up out of the deep, the sweetest air in a thousand miles of glare. The people camp on the breathers. They always have. Their caravan lines, their well-stones, their birth-places and meeting-places are strung along the breathing seams like beads on strings — and the seams are the fractures, and the fractures go all the way down, and half a kilometer below, the same fractures carry the drip-veils, and the drip-veils carry the dead streets of the finished city. The two countries have the same map. Every camp up here stands above a district down there; every district down there was built beneath a place where, on the surface, the ground gives its cool sigh and people have always chosen to sleep. Neither people drew the map. The rock drew it, and both of them, ten thousand years apart or ten thousand years in parallel, read it the same way.

At the pan's low center is the lake, which is usually not a lake. It is a basin of finer, softer salt, floured with a gray dust, ringed at a distance by old shorelines like the growth rings of an absence. Once or twice in a long while — a year of many storms, a decade's rain in a season — the basin fills, shin-deep and blinding, and becomes for a few weeks the reason for everything, as you will see.

And the pan is sinking. Slowly — finger-widths in a century — the whole country sags toward its middle, and here and there it sags suddenly: dolines, round collapses, appearing overnight where the roof beneath has thinned past bearing. The people fence them with cairns and give them names and do not grieve them. They understand, in the way one understands a thing too large to say plainly, that their country is being *spent* — dissolved from below, grain by grain, handed downward — and that the payment goes somewhere. The vault thins. The light below grows, generation by generation, imperceptibly brighter. There was a time, their oldest stories agree, when the ground was solid dark all the way down; the below-country's own lineages remember it too, in their blind crickets and their silence. The Overstory is paying for the Understory's daylight with its own substance. Someday, no one says aloud, the roof will be a window. Neither country hurries.

At the pan's eastern edge is the mouth — the great stepped collapse you descended, half a day of switchbacks into the cool. And at the rim of the mouth stands the door.

It is three slabs: two uprights and a lintel, freestanding, framing nothing, facing down into the dark. It is built in the manner of the city below — stone on stone, no mortar, courses you can count like days — and it is the only construction of its kind on the surface of the pan, the last thing the builders made before the descent or the first thing, no one remembers which. It is maintained. The drifted salt is swept from its threshold. When the lintel slips, as it does once in a few lifetimes, it is levered back. At dusk, pale blind crickets come up out of the mouth and mill about the threshold in the cooling air, a soft gray tide rising to the rim of one world and going back down into the other, and the door stands over their small border traffic like a customs house for a trade too old to tax.

**Who Lives in the Loud Overstory**

Begin at the bottom, because here too everything does — but the bottom here is dust.

The gray flour in the lake basin is not flour. It is eggs: the drought-proof, decade-sleeping eggs of a small brine creature, trillions of them, the largest population in the country and the least awake. The lake exists, most of the time, as powder. The wind lifts it and lays it down again; it drifts against the cairns; it silts into bedrolls and bread. You crossed the basin on your way to the mouth and you have been breathing the lake ever since. When the water finally comes, the dust hatches in days — the shallows go pink, then seething — and the brine flies rise off the new shorelines in banks you can lean on, and the whole stored country cashes itself out at once.

Then the birds come.

No one knows how they know. The lake fills perhaps twice in a generation, and within days — from wetlands a thousand miles away, over country with no water in it and no reason to cross — the sky begins to arrive. Stilted white waders in strings. Rose-colored things in their tens of thousands, wheeling in sheets that dim the sun. They land on the shallows and the shallows disappear under them, and for a few weeks the quietest basin in the world is the loudest: a continuous roaring mutter of wings and water and feeding, audible from a day's walk out.

And while they stand on the lake, the sun stands on the water, and the water stands on a country's-roof of salt gone glassy and saturated to its depths — and half a kilometer down, the vault over one district of the finished city ignites into a sustained white blaze lasting hours. You have seen it. You have seen what it does: the herds stopping, the doorways filling, the open hands held still. Every doorway in the old quarter faces the lake. That is what the alignment is. When the quiet people stand in their thresholds facing the brightest thing their world contains, they are facing, through the rock, a hundred thousand birds — and the sign they keep, the polished waiting word taught to every silent child, the word for a thing no living signer has ever seen, describes with perfect accuracy what is standing on the water directly over their lifted faces.

The rest of the surface fauna keeps to the rim, where the salt gives out into gray saltbush steppe: a dust-colored hare-thing, a dust-colored fox-thing that eats it, lizards like animate gravel. The pan's true interior belongs to the wind, the eggs, and the people.

The people are herders and salt-cutters, a few thousand of them in scattered bands, and they are the loud ones — prodigal with sound in exact proportion as their cousins below are frugal with it. Their spoken language is only the smallest part of their voice. For distance they whistle, a piercing fluent speech that rides the wind for miles, negotiations and gossip and warnings crossing the pan all day like invisible birds. For greater distance they drum the ground itself: at the hollow reaches — the booming places, mapped and named — a striker with a wooden maul can put a phrase into the crust that another band will take out of it half a horizon away, ear pressed to the salt. Their country is an instrument, and they have always played it. What the drummed phrases do below — whether they arrive in the deep rock as anything at all, whether the pressure-shifts that send the salamanders piling into their spawning basins are the storms, or the storms and something more rhythmic — is not a question either people is positioned to ask.

They navigate by ear, especially at night. Their cairns are not solid: they are built hollow, with throats, tuned, and the wind plays them — each one a different note, moaning steady in the dark — so that the pan is mapped in tones and a traveler with no stars can walk from voice to voice all the way home. Below, a people who see by pattern and gradient in a light without direction; above, a people who steer by pitch in a darkness full of sound. The same country, solved twice.

They live by salt. They cut it in slabs from the good beds and caravan it off the pan's western rim to the green countries beyond — there are green countries beyond; the world is wider than these two floors of it — and they come back with wood and cloth and grain and stories they only half believe. In flood years they live richly and briefly like everyone else: fly-cakes, egg-harvest, the gray flour of next decade's lake gathered by the sackful.

Their memory is one story with two endings, and they tell it plainly, the way you tell a thing that is simply true. In the deep drought — the one at the bottom of every genealogy — the people of the old wells divided. *Some followed the rain out, and became us. Some followed the water down.* For the ones who went down they have a word, and it is worth pausing on, because it is the same word they use for the eggs in the salt: it means, as nearly as it translates, *what the ground keeps* — a thing held alive in the earth until the water comes. They do not speak of their cousins as lost, or as dead, or even really as elsewhere. They speak of them in the grammar of seeds. Not gone. Deferred.

They give their dead to the seams. A people with no architecture, whose whole lives are wind and canvas and moving on, lower their wrapped dead into the deepest breathing cracks with salt in their folded hands — so that the roofless, at last, are housed — and the water that follows them down, grain by grain, sheets them into the rock the same way it sheets the builders' dead into the floor below. The kilometer of stone between the two countries is the only thing both peoples have ever built into, and what they have built into it is their ancestors: faced from above, faced from below, the oldest kind of shared wall — a wall with family on both sides.

In flood years, every band converges on the lake. It is the festival, the market, the marriage-season, the whole civic life of a scattered people compressed into a few loud weeks: races on the wet salt, drumming that goes all night at the booming reaches, the din of the birds under everything like a sea. It is, for those weeks, the loudest place for a thousand miles. And for the same hours, half a kilometer down, an entire country stands motionless in its aligned doorways with its hands open, holding the one utterance louder than any other in a language of silence. The stillest observance below; the loudest festival above; the same water, the same light, the same hours. Two peoples have kept one appointment for a hundred centuries from opposite sides of a wall of ancestors, and neither has ever missed it, and neither has ever seen the other keep it.

They keep the door. They sweep its threshold, right its lintel, camp near it but never in its sightline, out of a courtesy no one can date. And when anything goes down the mouth — a straying animal, a mad saint, a stranger with loud boots and a bellows chest, which is to say *you*, whom they heard coming across the pan two days before they saw you and paced along the horizon all the way to the rim — they begin the vigil. Every dusk, for as long as the descended thing is below, a singer sits at the mouth and sings down into it. Dusk, because at dusk the mouth inhales: the surface air cools and sinks and the great hollowness drinks it, and the song goes down with the breath, into the dark, toward whoever might be trying to find the way back. The song is old and its words are simple. *The lake will fill. The birds will come down. The door is swept. Come up and see.* No one has ever come up. The song has never once been answered. It is sung note-perfect anyway, every evening, on schedule, a song maintained against a return no one expects — and it is being sung right now, because you are still down there.

And half a kilometer below the singer, at the hour of the long dimming, along the high seams that lead up toward the door, there is a sound. It is very faint — a murmur in the wet cracks, at the edge of what even that hushed world can hear — and the quiet people class it, reasonably, among the voices of water. They have a sign for it. It is one of the gentlest words in their language. No one below has ever wondered why the water speaks only at evening, and only along the seams that climb toward the mouth.

So: above, a song kept for a return no one expects. Below, a word kept for a bird no one has seen. And between them, dissolving grain by grain, carrying the rain down as light and the dead down as floor and the song down as a murmur mistaken for water — the rock. Thinner every year. In no hurry.
