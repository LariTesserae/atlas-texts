# The Race

Two days downstream of the basin, the land runs out of patience. Everything the Grown Quarter's canopy holds back — the mist that burns off by noon, the rain that takes a minute to finish leaving the roofs, the drip-by-drip discharge of thirty patient buildings — has to go somewhere, and where it goes is over the basin's southern lip through a single notch, into a stream that spends its first day doing nothing much and its second day gathering tributaries and opinions, until it arrives at the escarpment and drops two hundred feet in a quarter mile, down a broken staircase of falls the maps call the Stair, and the town built into the Stair calls, simply, the water.

The town is the Race. It hangs on the fall-line like laundry: terraces cut and built and re-built into the escarpment face, structures stacked so that one house's floor is its downhill neighbor's weather, everything joined by stairs, ladders, and the flumes — timber aqueducts on trestle legs that carry stolen threads of the falls overhead through the whole town, so that a person walking the Race walks under running water more or less constantly, the way a person in the Quarter walks under leaves. Where the Quarter is wide and half-empty, the Race is crowded and vertical; it has never in its history had enough flat ground, and it has responded to this the way it responds to everything, by building faster.

Because nothing here lasts. That is the first fact of the place and the source of all its character. The spray is perpetual; the escarpment throws it back up as a cold standing haze, and in the morning sun the lower town wears rainbows the way other towns wear smoke. In that spray, wood goes grey in a year and soft in ten. Iron weeps. Rope has a season. The Race is therefore a town in a permanent state of replacement — at any given moment two or three buildings are half-skeleton, coming down or going up, and often it is hard to say which, and the Racefolk do not find the question interesting. Nothing here is healed. It is replaced, which is faster, and remembered better.

## The Works

There are forty-one wheels. This number is known, argued about, and out of date by the time anyone finishes saying it. They stand at every level of the Stair, overshots and undershots and one enormous breastshot called the First Wheel — currently in its fourteenth incarnation, and still the First Wheel, because a wheel, the Racefolk will tell you, is the same wheel the way a river is the same river, and then they will laugh before you do, because they have made the joke before and they talk fast.

The wheels themselves are stately — a big overshot turns perhaps ten times a minute, hardly faster than the Quarter's engine strokes, and this is a comparison the Racefolk make themselves, with a certain edge of pride, because the wheel is not the point. The gearing is the point. Inside the wheelhouses the slow dignity of the water is translated upward through pinions and lay-shafts into genuine speed: saws that scream, trip-hammers going like anxious hearts, grindstones, fulling stocks, lathes. The water only falls, the saying goes. Everything else we do to it. The Race grinds the plains' grain, saws the hill country's timber, fulls cloth — including, profitably, the Quarter's bark-cloth, which comes down stiff as board and goes out to the cities soft as anything — and above all it brokers. The Race sits at the one workable break in the escarpment, and everything moving between high country and low passes through it, pays it, and is improved by it, and the Racefolk have built their entire civic self-regard on being the place where things change speed.

The finest thing they handle they do not work at all. Three or four times a year — when it happens, which is when the tree is ready, a scheduling philosophy that has been driving the punctual Racefolk quietly out of their minds for three centuries — a barge comes down from the basin carrying leaf-board, resin casks, bark-cloth, and, sometimes, crated in a sprung cradle packed with wool, a membrane pane. A day ahead of the barge the Quarter sends a float, a carved wooden leaf sealed in resin, and the children of the Race watch the head-pond for it — the Catch, the pond is called — and the first to spot the leaf holds it until the barge arrives, and this is a genuine office, taken seriously, held once in a childhood. When a pane comes down it is portaged past the falls on foot, by relay, along a path the whole town clears beforehand; it is the strongest carriers' honor and their dread; a pane has been dropped twice in living memory and everyone in the Race can tell you both dates, unprompted, in a lowered voice.

## Sound, and the Lack of It

The Race roars. Not loudly, exactly — a person raised there would tell you the falls are quiet today — but totally, a sound with no gaps in it, and the town's whole acoustic culture is built inside that sound. Racefolk speak loudly and fast and on top of one another; interruption here is a courtesy, proof that you are keeping up, and their conversations braid three or four voices at once in a way that reduces visitors from slow country to polite despair. On the wheel floors, where voices fail entirely, they use the hand-talk — a full gestural dialect, quick and dry, capable of technical instruction and of jokes, with signs for every wheel by name and a famous sign for *up-basin* that involves letting both hands settle very, very slowly, like leaves.

The old go deaf, most of them, and are not much troubled by it; the hand-talk carries them, and they say the roar keeps them company from the inside by then anyway, lodged in the bones.

Silence is the alarm. A wheel that stops unplanned makes a hole in the roar that the whole town feels before it hears, and heads come up everywhere at once, the way heads come up in other towns at a scream. And silence is the rite: when a Racefolk dies, the wheel they worked is braked and chocked for one full turn of the tally-house water-clock, and for that hour there is a gap in the sound of the town shaped exactly like one wheel, and everyone in the Race knows whose. Then the wheel is released, because the water does not wait and the dead of the Race would be insulted if it did. The name goes into the ledger under a blue line. That is all, and it is understood to be a great deal.

## The Tally-House

One building in the Race is never torn down.

It stands at the head of the town, beside the Catch, square and low and almost aggressively out of fashion, and it is, in point of construction, barely a Race building at all: sheathed in Quarter leaf-board, sealed with Quarter resin, roofed in slate that came up from the plains before anyone's grandmother, and windowed — this is the thing, this is what visitors are brought to see — in membrane. Eleven panes of Quarter-grown membrane, amber-green, each of them older than every other structure in town, each bought at auction prices by some past generation of clerks and installed with ceremony, each bearing, scratched minutely in a corner, the name of the clerk who ordered it — orders placed eleven years ahead, several of them by clerks who did not live to see the pane arrive, which the Race regards as the most honorable form of purchase there is. Inside, the light is Quarter light, green-gold and slightly botanical, falling on the ledgers.

The ledgers are the Race's leaf-book, though no one on either end of the river would put it that way out loud. Gauge readings going back centuries; the flood marks; the record of every building the town has ever raised and razed, because when a Race structure comes down, one beam is saved and carried into whatever is built next — the carried beam, set somewhere visible, and some beams are in their ninth or tenth building now, black as jet with age and lamp oil, and a family's carried beam is its lineage in a town where nothing else is allowed to get old. The oldest beam of all is in the tally-house, over the clerks' long desk, and is claimed to be original, and the clerks, who are professionally incapable of asserting anything they cannot document, will say only that no record exists of its ever having been anywhere else, and will look pleased.

The still point of the fastest town on the river, in other words, is built almost entirely out of the slowest, and the Racefolk know it, and it does not embarrass them. Every town, the senior clerk has been heard to say, keeps its memory in imported material.

## The Racefolk

Begin with the hands, because that habit travels upstream and down. Race hands are read in scars, not stains: the wheelwrights' missing fingertips, worn like service medals; the flume-keepers' rope-burn stripes; the catchers' palms cross-hatched from barge lines; the gaugers' hands gone pale and water-soft from a lifetime in the spray; the clerks' right forefingers ink-black to the second knuckle — ink, the only stain in town, and they are vain about it.

And here and there, on the wheel floors and above all in the packing rooms: hands stained amber at the fingertips.

Because people run both ways on this river, even if the water doesn't. The Race is where the Quarter's leavers come — this is fast country, or the nearest edge of it — and every generation a handful of quiet young people with resin-tipped fingers arrive at the Catch, pause too long before answering questions, and get hired within the week, because the Race learned long ago what they are worth. You cannot hurry them, and in a packing room, with a pane in the cradle, that is the whole job description. They are loud by Quarter standards and silent by Race standards, and they cluster mildly, and they marry in, and their children talk as fast as anyone. Some go home in their forties, worn shiny, and their houses put out green leaves over the door. Some stay, and when they die a wheel stops for them like anyone, and the Race considers this exactly right.

The traffic runs the other way in winter. The Lock, the Racefolk call it: three to six weeks when the falls thin to ropes and columns of ice, the wheels are chocked, and the roar — for the only time in the year — goes out of the town like a tide. Racefolk endure the Lock variously. Some love it and will not admit it. Many find the quiet physically oppressive, describe it as a pressure on the ears, and a good number go up-basin for the duration, to the Quarter, whose engine is idling at two strokes a minute anyway, and where a loud fast visitor in the dead of winter is received with amusement and put to useful indoor work. The two towns' idles coincide, is the thing; their busy seasons never do; and three centuries of Lock visits have laced the two populations together with cousinships that neither place keeps track of and both places rely on.

**The flume-keepers** are the young trade, the rowdy element, running the trestle-tops in all weather with clearing hooks, and they stand to the Race exactly as the trimmers stand to the Quarter — the crews that keep the town's arteries the shape the town needs — except that where a trimmer negotiates, a flume-keeper *chases*: debris, ice, leaks, once famously an otter. The otters, it should be said, are tolerated staff, employed against the fish that would otherwise clog the intakes, and the town's cats are employed against the rats, and the rats are not tolerated at all, and the Racefolk, who have heard that the Quarter has no vermin because its very walls object to them chemically, consider this the single most enviable fact about slow country and would never say so.

**The gaugers** read the river, and they are the Race's listeners: the trade of the trained sense, the trade that takes thirty years to enter and improves until death. There is always exactly one senior gauger, by no rule anyone can cite. The current one is a long, folded old man, deaf as a post in the ordinary way, who reads the water through the handrails with the flat of his hand and through the floor with his feet and by the color at the weir lip, and who has predicted every serious rise for forty years, most recently by walking through town at dawn making the hand-sign for *up, tonight* at anyone he passed, which emptied the lowest terrace by noon and was correct by midnight. He and the senior listener of the Quarter have met four times in their lives, at Lock-season, and are reported to spend those meetings standing side by side at the frozen weir saying almost nothing, in what both towns describe, respectfully, as a very fast conversation.

**The clerks** keep the tally-house, and their deformation is the mirror of the membrane growers': where a grower is hopeless within the week and infallible at ten years, a clerk is infallible backward and helpless forward — they can tell you the flood mark of any spring in three hundred years and cannot tell you what they intend to do about the coming one. The town accommodates this. The gaugers face forward, the clerks face back, and the Race stands between them like a wheel between the water and the work.

**The catchers** hold the head-pond, receive the barges, and carry the panes, and are quietly the town's aristocracy for one reason: they are the trade that touches the Quarter with its hands. A catcher who has carried a pane down the Stair walks differently afterward. Everyone says so. No one can say how.

## Evening

The lamps of the Race burn Quarter resin — they always have; nothing else stays lit in the spray — and so every evening, as the light goes and the lamps come up terrace by terrace down the fall-line, the whole roaring town begins to smell faintly of the basin: that cool, green, resinous cellar-smell, the smell of slow country, laid over the thunder of fast water like a hand on a shoulder. The Racefolk do not remark on it. They have breathed their upstream neighbor every night of their lives.

At the Catch, the heron stands. There is always a heron at the Catch — the gaugers say the same heron, the clerks say there is no documentation — and it is the settled belief of every child in the Race that the old heron in the Quarter's engine-house cistern fledged here, went up-basin, and stayed, and that the two birds are therefore in the same relation as the two towns, which is to say: the same water, at different speeds, in no doubt whatsoever about which of them is in charge — though it should be noted that the children of each town are certain it is theirs.

And on the packing-room porch, in the last light, you may see the Race at its most characteristic ease: a flume-keeper asleep sitting up, somehow, in all that noise; the clerks' apprentices racing each other in hand-talk, silent and hilarious; and a young amber-fingered packer from up-basin trying, again, to teach a Race child to let a pause in conversation stand — the child lasting four seconds, five, going red, bursting — and the two of them laughing under the flumes while the water goes by overhead, on its way down, having left the leaves days ago, about to do more work in its last hundred feet than in its whole life before.
