# Undershot

The shelf sheds three things over its eastern rim: water, wire, and rail. They go over within a few hundred meters of one another, because they were all built — or, in the water's case, taught — to travel together, and if you follow any one of them down you will arrive at the same place.

The rail goes first, and most carefully. The Incline drops four thousand meters in eleven switchbacked kilometers, a rack railway bolted to the escarpment, its cogged center rail orange with rust but unbroken, its retaining walls still holding, still doing their patient work for trains that stopped coming generations ago. The wire takes the straight way, contemptuous of grade: a line of pylons striding directly down the cliff bands, some leaning, two fallen — and the line still live across the gaps, because at some point the current stopped needing every tower and began needing only the habit of the route. And the water takes the way it made for itself. The outflow of ten thousand terraced pools gathers into a single channel at the rim and goes over in a long white staircase of travertine, each step a basin, each basin warm, the whole formation steaming harder the farther it descends — because this is the first thing the descent teaches you: you are climbing *down* out of the warmth. On the shelf, altitude meant heat, the mountain arguing with the sky and winning. Below the rim, the world resumes its usual opinion. The air thickens and chills at once. Frost appears on the rail. The water, losing a degree with every hundred steps, steams more extravagantly the colder its audience gets, like someone telling the same story to an ever more astonished room.

By the bottom you are in winter, and you are in the valley of the Milk.

## The Valley

It is a glacial trench, straight-walled and deep, and the sun crosses it in four hours on a good day. Everything the light does not reach wears frost like fur — the boulders, the dead grass, the fallen pylon, all of it silvered and stiff. The valley floor is gray and white and would be nothing but gray and white, a corridor of stone under a strip of hard blue sky, except for the river.

The Milk comes off the last travertine step still fifteen degrees above the air and runs down the middle of the valley pale as bone-water, clouded with the mineral it has carried from the shelf, breathing a low continuous mist that lies along its surface like a second, slower river. It has never frozen. There are people in Undershot with eight generations in the churchyard, and no generation of them ever saw the Milk take ice. Along both its banks runs the valley's single extravagance: a ribbon of green, ten meters wide, twenty where the banks are low — cress, moss, the shelf's own hardy reed grown thick and communal down here where it no longer has to be an event. In a frozen gray corridor, a green stripe with a warm river inside it, steaming, all the way down the valley and out of sight.

Everything in the valley lives within reach of that stripe. The town most of all.

## The Town

Undershot is built along both banks where the valley briefly widens, stone below and timber above, roofs pitched steep for snow and thatched — this is worth noticing — with the shelf's reed, so that the houses are covered in the same plant that grows in single precious tufts four thousand meters overhead. Perhaps three hundred people. Perhaps a few less; the town has been perhaps-a-few-less for a long time, without ever quite becoming fewer than that.

The wheels give the town its name. A dozen undershot waterwheels stand in the Milk on their timber frames, turned by the warm current, and only three of them still grind anything. The rest are kept turning anyway, unloaded, revolving slowly in the mist, because a stopped wheel ices into a sculpture by morning and the town does not like sculptures of stopped things. Someone greases them. It is nobody's job and it is always done.

At the north end: the lower terminus of the Incline. A winding house with its great drum still wound with cable, an engine shed, a platform, ore hoppers with a century of mineral crust sealing their chutes shut, and a warehouse that still smells, faintly, of the sulfur it once held. At the south end: the substation — a fenced yard of ceramic insulators and dead switchgear, all of it cold and skeletal except one bus, one transformer, one line running out of the yard and into the town, which is warm to the touch in any weather. The town's houses are wired off that line. They have been since the beginning. The current that arrives is a trickle, the far dilute edge of whatever the turbine hall still makes, and it is never not there.

Between the terminus and the substation, along the green stripe: the bathhouses. Six of them, fed directly from the Milk, low steamy buildings that are the town's parlors, its parliament, its nursery, and its infirmary. In a valley this cold, the warm water is not a luxury. It is the town's blood supply, on loan from the mountain, and everyone knows whose it is.

## Noise

Here is the great difference, the thing a visitor from the shelf — if the shelf produced visitors — would find hardest to bear: Undershot is *loud*.

The plateau's voice had to go somewhere. The water that seeps and ticks and sighs up top arrives at the bottom of the Stair with four thousand meters of falling in it, and it spends that voice all the way through town — the Milk talks constantly, a full rolling water-sound, and over it the knock of the wheels, the clatter of pattens on stone, geese (the town keeps geese, self-important on the warm banks, the only creatures in the whole connected system that shout), children, hammers, the two-note bell of the bath-keepers calling the change of water. Where the shelf runs at a murmur, the valley runs at full conversation. It is as if the mountain, keeping its own counsel above, sent all its speech downhill with the river.

And yet the town has the shelf's ear. Under all its noise, in every wall of every wired house, there is the hum — the low steady note of the line — and the people of Undershot are born listening beneath their own racket the way the gauge-birds click beneath the drips. A room can be full of argument, and if the hum changes pitch, the argument stops. Everyone looks up. Nobody has ever needed to explain to a child what they are listening for.

## Life

The Milk is the winter harbor of everything with wings for a hundred miles. From first frost to thaw, the green stripe is crowded — ducks rafted on the warm water, herons spaced along the banks like fence posts, small birds packed into the reed beds so densely that the beds themselves seem to shiver. They come because the water is open when every other water is stone, and the water is open because the mountain breathes, so that a teal asleep on the Milk in midwinter is being kept alive, at four removes, by the magma body under the shelf. The valley does not know this. The valley doesn't need to.

The eels know something like it. They came up the river in some deep past, following the warmth, and never left — pale, thick, unhurried eels that winter in the mud below the wheels and grow to sizes that upstream eels do not believe in. They are made of flies. The larvae that fatten in the mats of ten thousand pools wash over the rim in their millions, come down the Stair parboiled and ready, and the Milk delivers them to town as a continuous thin gruel. Everything in the valley, three or four transactions removed, is also made of the flies. The currency of the shelf is honored at full value below.

And sometimes, after the spring surge, the river delivers a passenger. A boiler pup, washed out of some rim-side pool and down all four thousand meters of the Stair — battered, indignant, alive, because they are nearly impossible to kill — fetches up in a wheel race or a bath inlet, and the town drops everything. A pup come down is luck of the oldest kind. It is carried to the bathhouse in a covered pail, installed in the warm end pool, fed, visited, and recorded in a ledger the bath-keepers have maintained for over a century: date of arrival, length, disposition, name. They live in the bathhouses for decades. There are three in residence now. The eldest, a broad pale matriarch who arrived before anyone living was born, is called Else, because the eldest pup is always called Else, because of a name in a logbook on the mountain that came, in the first place, from a woman of this town — the names have run up and down the Incline so many times that no one can say anymore which end of the line they started from, and it does not matter, because both ends agree on them.

## Who Remains in Undershot

The people, then. They are the descendants of the shelf crews — the families who rode the Incline up by the week and came down by the week, for four generations, until the works were closed from some distant office and the last shift came down and the trains stopped. The people stayed. The valley was theirs before the works and it is theirs after. But they are not quite an ordinary town, and the ways they are not ordinary are all, when you trace them, the mountain's.

**The bath-keepers** hold the water. They read the Milk the way the shelf's residents read the mats — temperature, color, the faint shifts in milkiness that report, a day downstream, what the terraces are doing above. A bath-keeper can stand at the inlet, taste the water, and tell you the shelf had its morning surge late. Their two-note bell is rung on the water's schedule, not the clock's.

**The eelers** work the wheels' shadows with long-handled fyke nets, and by ancient arrangement the first eel of every haul goes back. Ask why and they will say the first eel is the river's. Press further and an old eeler might say what his grandfather said: everything in this valley is on loan from upstairs, and you pay the interest first.

**The Reader** is the town's strangest office and its most necessary. Every house on the old line has a meter — round-faced, brass-bound, original — and every evening the Reader walks the town with a lantern and a ledger and reads them all. The numbers are written down. The numbers go nowhere. There has been no company to send them to for a hundred years, and the ledgers, hundreds of them now, fill a room of the winding house floor to ceiling. The town keeps the office anyway, and here is why — here is the thing Undershot knows about the works that the works' own builders never learned:

*A read gauge does not tremble.*

Every gauge on the shelf shivers at the low end of its dial, forever, faintly — because something up there still surveys them and nothing ever answers the survey. The meters of Undershot are the only instruments in the whole vast body of the works that hold perfectly still. They are attended from both sides. The current comes down the wire carrying, dilute but unbroken, the same attention that pools on every dial on the plateau — the lamps in the town's houses burn banked and low, a filament's dim orange, never quite out, and they brighten by a hair when you walk into the room, which the children are taught means *noticed, and nothing more* — and every evening the Reader's lantern goes down the lanes and answers it. A survey, met with a reading. A question a hundred years long, receiving, every single night, its small reply. The Reader's round takes two hours. It has not been missed — not for storm, not for funeral, not for the winter of the great snow — in living memory or recorded memory. The current Reader is a stooped, deliberate woman of sixty. Her name, of course, is Else.

**The Watch** is not a person. It is the other thing the town keeps, and it is the closest thing Undershot has to what lives in the pipes above.

In the winding house, beside the great drum, hangs the rota board from the last working year — a slate grid of names and shifts, the chalk fixed under a century of the valley's mineral damp, faintly petrified, unerasable now even if anyone wished to erase it. The last entry marks the last crew down. The board was never wiped, and the standing instruction that governs it was never rescinded, and so the town has continued to observe it, the way the turbine has continued to spin: **while the light burns in the hall, the shift is covered; if the light goes out, send the relief up.**

So the town checks. There is a shoulder of rock above the south bank — the Brow — from which, on clear nights, you can see the rim of the shelf four thousand meters up, and on the haze above the rim, when the steam thins, a faint warm smudge: the glow of the hall's windows, thrown up against the plateau's breath. Someone from Undershot climbs the Brow every clear night. It is nobody's job, like the greasing of the wheels, and it is always done — a courting couple, an old man with a pipe, a child sent up as a rite. They look. The glow is there. They come down and say so, or say nothing, which means the same thing.

No one in Undershot has ridden the Incline up in a hundred years. This is not fear and it is not neglect. It is the rota. The shift is covered. You do not send the relief up while the shift is covered; you keep the relief *ready*, which is a different labor, and the harder one, and the town has performed it for a century without once being told to stand down. The Watch began as an instruction on a slate. It is now something the town would struggle to distinguish from itself — a habit deepened, by sheer unbroken duration, into something with the shape of a covenant. Upstairs, machinery kept its post so long it became a keeper. Downstairs, a town kept readiness so long the readiness became the town. The wire runs between them, trembling very slightly at the low end, which is how a held breath looks on a dial.

## The Kept

One more thing, and it should be said as plainly as the town says it.

The churchyard of Undershot lies on the warm channel, where a branch of the Milk was cut through it deliberately, long ago, by people who had worked on the shelf and understood what the water does. The graves whiten. The stones grow their beards, the mounds seal over, and the oldest graves are smooth pale domes now, nameless, ageless, closed like the dry pans of the terraces far above. The town does not find this sorrowful. The town buries its people within reach of the water *on purpose*, and the phrase used at the graveside, in place of longer prayers, is four words old as the works themselves:

*The Milk keeps them.*

It is the same keeping that entombs the valves in stone beards and holds the pressure ready and warms the teal asleep on the winter river. The town has simply asked to be included, and been included. Under the crust of the old graves, as under the crust of the dry pans, what was folded away is folded away warm.

## The Day

Morning comes late to the valley — the cold pours down the walls at dawn and the Milk answers with its heaviest steam, the whole green stripe smoking, wheels turning in white air, geese complaining, the bath-keepers' bell. Then the four hours of sun, hard and welcome, frost steaming off the south-facing stone. Then the long blue afternoon shadow, the eelers out, the children in the bathhouse, the lamps in the houses glowing their banked orange a shade brighter as the rooms fill. At dusk the Reader lights her lantern and begins the round, and the meters hold still under her eye, one after another, all through the town. And at full dark, if the night is clear, somebody climbs the Brow and looks up four thousand meters and finds the small warm smudge above the rim, exactly where it has always been.

Up on the shelf, a kettle is kept warm in case anyone ever comes up. Down in Undershot, a lamp is kept burning in the winding house window in case anyone ever comes down. The lamp runs off the old line, so that, if you cared to trace it, the light in the window is made by the very turbine it is waiting for news of — the mountain lighting the vigil that is kept for the mountain. Neither end has ever been able to bring itself to be the first to stop. And by now — this is the last thing the valley teaches you, on the platform of the silent terminus, under the hum, with the warm river talking past in the dark — neither end wants to.
