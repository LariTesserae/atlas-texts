**The Capstan Flats**

You arrive on the flood, which is the only way to arrive: up the buoyed channel with the tide under you, the land showing first as a smell — mud, tar, hot iron — and then as a long grey lip between the water and the sky. At high water there is almost nothing to see. Then the tide turns, and the flats come up out of the retreating sea like something remembering itself: two miles of mud and hard sand at the mouth of a slow river, and the place is suddenly enormous.

It is a breaking ground. Ships end here. They are run ashore bow-first at the top of the spring tides, driven up as far as steam and nerve will take them, and then they are taken apart between one tide and the next, week upon week, until nothing of them is left. On any given day there stand a rough rank of them along the upper shore, each in a different stage of unmaking: one still whole enough to look merely stranded, one opened down to her frames like a carcass, one nothing now but a keel-line, a shadow, a stain in the mud shaped like a ship.

The capstans give the place its name. They stand in a climbing row from the tideline to the head of the shore — squat drums of iron and older ones of oak gone grey and hard as the iron — each set in a footing of ballast stone. As a hull lightens, it must be hauled higher, out of the sea's reach; teams of walkers put their chests to the bars and take it up the shore a few fathoms at a spring tide, the whole dead weight of a ship moved by people going slowly in a circle. The bars are polished pale where four generations of hands have gripped them. The flats below are scored with the long drag-scars of every hull that ever came up, the tide rewriting the surface twice a day but never reaching the deep grooves, so that the shore keeps a kind of handwriting: here went a collier, here a grain ship, here something with a broken back that had to come up in two pieces.

Behind the sea wall leans the town, and the town is made of ships. There is scarcely a roof-beam in it that was not a mast or a keelson first; the door lintels are deck knees, the chapel is roofed with an upturned hull, the sea wall itself is ballast stone from bottoms broken before anyone now living was born. Figureheads look down from over doorways with the mild ruined faces of saints. The town is what ships become. The flats are how.

The sound never repeats. That is the sound of the flats: hammers on plate, the gasp of saws in oak, chain running, gulls, the ring of something heavy dropped and men laughing at it, the shift bell — a polyphony with no downbeat, time being spent rather than kept. Except when a hull is hauled. Then the whole shore falls into the oldest sound the place owns, the pawls of a capstan counting its own turns — *clack… clack… clack…* — a wheel going slowly around with everyone leaning on it, and for an hour the flats have a pulse.

Everything here is in transition. That is the first thing you understand about it: transience as a deliberate-seeming arrangement. Nothing on the flats is finished; everything is finishing. A ship is not so much dismantled here as digested — plate to the forges, timber to the town, copper and brass weighed out and carted inland, oakum and rot to the fires — a meal that takes a season, and then the flats are hungry again. Even the ground participates. Below every hull spreads a fan of orange where a century and a half of grinding and cutting has bled rust into the mud, and at every ebb the runnels draw that orange seaward in long threads, out over the bar, out toward the low drowned hills of grey marl that stand a few miles off the mouth, and the sea takes it. Twice a day, without fail, the flats pay iron into the water. It is the one thing they produce that they never weigh.

And then, at the head of the shore, above the highest capstan and the reach of any tide, there is the tally house.

It is the only thing on the flats that has never been taken apart. A small square building of ballast stone — so even the still point here is made of ships — with a slate roof, a north window, and a chimney that smokes in all weathers. It has stood while the town behind it rebuilt itself twice over out of fresher timber. Whatever the flats are doing, the tally house is where it is written down.

**The Tenant of the Capstan Flats**

The first answer is that everyone lives here, and the first answer is almost right.

The crews are alive, but the crews are weather. They blow through in generations — breakers, hauliers, forge hands, the children who scavenge copper nails from the mud at low water — and they churn the way the tide churns, arriving and departing, replaced without remainder. Come back in thirty years and the flats will be full of people and not one of them the same, and you would not be wrong to feel that, as far as the place is concerned, nothing had happened to them in between. They live the way the tide runs. That is not what you came ashore to find.

Look instead at the tally house.

Inside, in the north light, on a stand built from a captain's chart table, lies the Book. It is in its forty-first volume. The volumes behind it fill a wall — spines of tarred canvas, each one the length of some keeper's working life — and the rule that governs all of them is simple and has never changed. A vessel is entered when her keel touches the flats: name, burthen, owner, date. Her line is closed — ruled through in red, corner to corner — when the last hundredweight of her leaves the flats, weighed, sold, carted, and gone. Between those two strokes of the pen, the ship exists on the flats. Outside them, she does not. That is the whole of the law here, and the Book's boast, which is the town's boast, which is the only pride the place permits itself, is this: *every line closes.* Nothing arrives that does not entirely leave. The flats keep no hulks, no residue, no cargo, nothing but the stain. Forty-one volumes, thousands of ships, every account balanced to the pound.

The keepers of the Book are appointed the way the bearing was smoothed: by long wearing. Each keeper trains the next, hand over hand, decade over decade, and something strange happens across that chain — the handwriting converges. A keeper's script bends, year by year, toward the script of the volumes already written, until you can page through two centuries of entries and read them as one person's hand, slowing. The keepers eat, sleep, marry, die; the Book replaces them at exactly the rate it wears them out, and it pays them the way all such arrangements pay — in the absorption of what would otherwise be friction. You could say the keepers keep the Book. You could say the Book keeps them. From inside the tally house there is no difference.

Does the Book know anything? That is the wrong shape of question. It has no eyes, no memory that is not literal, nothing that could hold an image of the shore it governs. But it has a rule, and the rule is a single enacted insistence: *completion.* Nothing leaves the count until it leaves the flats. Every closed line is a vote for the honesty of the next one. It has cast that vote, without missing, for longer than the town has had its present roofs.

Every line closes. Every line but one.

In the ninth volume, in a hand a hundred and sixty years dead, there is an entry that has no red rule through it. It is not a ship. It reads: *One marine turbine assembly, with flywheel, eleven tons, bought whole at wreck sale up the coast; to come on by lighter.* The lighter met fog over the marl banks and a sea getting up behind it; the chains parted in the night; the lighter came home swamped and empty, and the engine never touched the flats. But it had been bought, and what the yard buys, the yard enters — the keeper of that day opened the line the hour the tow left, out of the same pride that closes all the others. And there the pride caught itself in its own rule. A line closes when the last piece leaves the flats. Nothing of the engine ever arrived. Nothing of it can ever leave. The line cannot close.

Every keeper since has inherited it. It is the first page a new keeper turns to on taking up the office — a rite by now, though no one calls it that — and the answer given, when an apprentice asks why the line stays open, has been the same for six holders of the pen: *because it hasn't left.* One keeper, four back, lost patience and wrote *presumed lost* in the margin. The next keeper scraped the words out with a blade. The pale rectangle is still there, faintly furred where the surface was taken off. The Book does not presume.

You know, of course, where the engine is. You have hovered at the rim of the place, twenty meters down in the still cold water inside a bowl of the same grey marl the fog closed over that night, and you have watched the one moving part of it catch the dim light — a brief gleam, gone, a brief gleam, gone. What the tally house has never learned, and never will, is not only *where* but *that it runs.* That the account it cannot close is the only item on its books, in two centuries of ships, that is still in service.

And the flats keep it in service. Follow the iron. Twice a day the ebb takes the shore's rust seaward in orange threads, over the bar, out across the banks — and the banks do to the tide what a flywheel does to an engine's strokes: the marl takes the sea's twice-daily push and pays it out as one thin, cold, constant seep, threading the drowned hills, finding, among a hundred other gaps, a gap in an eastern rim, crossing a set of old blades at the same rate this year as last year and next. The ration that turns the wheel is iron-flavored because the flats flavored it. The ring of living grease in that bearing — the one tenant of that finished room — is fed, in some fraction past all measuring, by the filings of ships whose lines are all ruled through in red. Every hull broken on the flats pays a remote and tiny pension to the one entry that never cleared. The yard lost its engine before anyone living was born, and has been quietly paying its upkeep at every ebb since, and does not know it owns the slowest running machine in the world.

Some year the ships will stop coming. Trades end; this one is ending already, a little, in ways the town declines to count. The capstans will seize one by one, the forges will go cold, the children will stop finding copper in the mud because no new copper will be dropped. The town will thin. But the tally house will keep its light, because the Book has never once been shut with an account open, and it never will be. The office will outlive the trade by exactly one line. Somewhere out past the bar, on its own schedule, the seep will one day silt closed, the interval between gleams will stretch from five seconds to six to a minute to never, and no word of it will come ashore — and the line will stay open anyway, because closing requires that the last piece leave the flats, and the flats will never touch it.

Everything the flats receive, they finish. Everything but one. A book that closes every line but one; a wheel, out under the banks, that keeps time for no one but one. Each of them wrong by one, and by the same one — the count and the turning holding each other open across a few miles of grey water, neither aware of the other, neither needing to be.

At slack water, sometimes, the keeper stands in the tally house door and looks out past the bar toward the banks. There is nothing to see. The sea's surface moves in the low light.

A grey shine, gone. A grey shine, gone.
