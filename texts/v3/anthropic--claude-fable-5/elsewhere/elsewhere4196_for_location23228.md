**The Settled Country**

The hills are finished. That is the first thing to understand, and the last thing anyone here has ever needed to be told.

Down here — below the cloud deck, at the bottom of the long air, where the breathing comes thick and easy and no one has ever strapped a bladder under their chin — the rock made up its mind so long ago that its mind is nearly all that is left of it. The range they call the Grandmothers was a true range once, by every account tall enough to trouble weather; what remains is a country of worn knuckles and low grey shoulders, hills ground down to their gums. This is the homeland of the stubborn certainties. Granite that has answered every question it will ever be asked. Schist that settled its affairs before there were affairs. You could walk the Grandmothers your whole life and never once see a ridgeline waver, never feel the ground beneath you consider anything at all, and people do exactly this, and call it peace.

But finished stone forgets. That is the other thing, the thing the summit people never quite believe until they come down: rock that has decided everything no longer attends. Cut a name into a wall here and come back in a season; the letters will have softened, blurred at the serifs, as though the hill considered your name and declined it. Cart-ruts heal. Boundary marks migrate. Whole carved doorways, a few generations on, are smooth as riverbed. The stone holds its own shape well enough — it is *marks* it lets go of, human intentions specifically, shed the way old skin sheds a splinter. The geologists, who down here are really historians, call it grey rock, the way one speaks of grey hair. It is warm to the touch in summer, dusty, and it remembers nothing. Press your palm against a wall of it and come back in a season; the wall will have forgotten your whole species.

The country was settled for exactly this. Of course it was. People want to live on ground that has no further opinions.

---

It is not one town so much as a sentence of villages, strung out along the river Hush wherever the coal comes near the surface — a pit village here, a market town two hours downstream, the register town with its long slate hall, and at the eastern end, where the new mountain's foothills begin their climb into the white, the junction settlement everyone calls Stairfoot, because the Stair begins there: the cog railway, three days of it, up through the cloud and the thin air to the city that the valley never names, only gestures at. *Up*, they say. *She has gone up. The coal goes up. Everything goes up, eventually* — and this last is said flatly, as a fact about drainage run in reverse, because it is one.

The principal industry is memory, though nobody calls it that. The pits do not mine ore — they mine *the accomplished*. Coal, down here, is understood with a precision that would embarrass a lowland chemist anywhere else: it is rock that remembered pressure so well it kept everything. Every frond. Every fish. Every noon of a summer forty million years concluded. The seams are called shelves, and the shelves are read as much as cut — the colliers can tell you which forest they are in, whether it drowned or burned, what the weather was doing the week a particular ceiling of fern-prints was laid down. A fish in stone sits on every mantel in the valley. Children are given fern-fronds pressed in black the way other children are given coins. The deepest seams are the most legible; the Grandmothers, having forgotten every mark ever made *on* them, kept perfectly every mark made *in* them, and there is a whole quiet theology in that distinction which the valley declines to elaborate.

The coal goes up the Stair, car after car, day after day, and everyone in the valley knows what happens to it. In the summit foundries it is burned to heat the great iron formes, and the formes are pressed against the green rock, and the green rock commits. The colliers hold this knowledge with a pride so old it has gone entirely unspoken: every cured plate the upper city stands on was set with the spent certainty of a forest that once stood here, in the warm and the wet, at the bottom of the long air. *Certainty is never made*, runs the valley's one proverb of physics. *It is only moved.* When a collier's family gets word that a child gone up has been taken on at the foundries, the phrase used is that the child is *in the family business at the other end*.

Other things go up: sacks of the valley's soil, black and deep and generous in the way only long-finished things are generous, bound for the summit terraces; iron, cast at the Stairfoot pattern-works by smiths who spend whole careers making formes and stakes for ground they will never stand on; and cinnabar, from one small red-walled mine in the western Grandmothers, ground into the vermilion that the summit surveyors trust their lives to. The valley finds this quietly funny — that the color of intention, up there, is mined from the most concluded hill in the world — and has never once explained the joke to a surveyor.

And things come down. Letters. The descended — retired founders with their valved, deliberate voices, old surveyors gone grey as their country. And keeping-stone: trimmings of the summit's long welts, freshly cured rock, the youngest finished stone in existence and therefore the most attentive. Keeping-stone holds an inscription the way the Grandmothers hold nothing. A name cut into it stays cut. Every family in the valley saves for a slab of it, a lifetime of saving sometimes, because a keeping-stone marker is the only object in the Settled Country that will still say what it says in two hundred years. The trade runs like a heartbeat, up and down the Stair: the valley sends its old certainty up to be spent; the mountain sends its new certainty down to remember them with.

---

The sound of the place is the absence of one. The valley is quiet in a way that has texture, weight, a grain you could run your thumb along: the land said everything it had to say an age ago, and the country is the pause after. There is rain on slate, and rooks, and the pit-head engines, and the tellers' small bells, and twice a day, felt in the rails before it is heard, the climbing beat of the up-train — which children lie down beside the tracks to listen for, ear to the cold iron, because it is the closest the valley comes to the summit's murmur, a pulse arriving from a world that is still deciding. Summit people who come down cannot sleep for the silence. They lie awake listening for the mountain under the floor and hear nothing, nothing, nothing, and the nothing is the sound of ground with no further plans, and it takes them years to hear it as safety. Valley people who go up have it worse, by all accounts. A valley child, three days up the Stair, feeling for the first time the rock beneath the street *murmuring* — you do not soon forget, the letters home say, the discovery that the world has been holding still for you all your life as a courtesy extended nowhere else.

The light is grey, but honestly grey, a settled and reliable grey. The valley lives under the ceiling — the underside of the cloud sea, which from below is not a sea at all but a lid, low and pale and permanent, the summit's ocean seen from the wrong side. Weather here has committed. It arrives on schedule, rains its allotted amount, and moves on; the almanac is never wrong, has not been wrong in living memory, and is consulted the way other places consult scripture, which in a sense it is: the completed record of a sky that finished drafting itself long ago. A few times a year the wind opens a tearing in the ceiling and actual sunlight comes down in a moving column, like one of the summit's pocket rains inverted, and children run out into it and stand there with their faces up until it closes.

And at night, on clear-under nights, the ceiling to the east carries a stain. Faint, orange, patient — the glow of the lamp-strung streets three days up, thrown against the cloud from above and soaking through. The valley calls it the ember. Parents lift children onto their shoulders and point east along the black line of the Stair: *that is the city, still deciding.* No one in the valley has ever needed a clock to know the Provisional City still stands. They have only ever needed a window.

**The Citizens of After**

The people here will tell you they are finished. That is the first thing they say about themselves, and the least true.

The dialect has settled, like the weather, into a grammar the summit schoolteachers find as strange as theirs is found below. Where the summit speaks in the provisional, the valley speaks in the accomplished: the perfect tense worn smooth with use, extended even to the future, which is treated as a formality the past has not yet gotten around to. In the pits this is doctrine. You do not speak of the future underground — the coal does not recognize it, the colliers say, and it is rude to use a grammar your host has never learned. Plans, in the shelves, are spoken as already done: *we have cut through to the second summer by Friday*, says the shift-captain, three days before Friday, and the crew nods, because down among the pressed forests everything true has already happened and the week is merely catching up.

And where the founders finish every sentence, the valley *cites* every one. No statement of fact goes out undressed; each is followed, as reflexively as breath, by its provenance — *as my mother had it; as the register holds; as the almanac says; as I saw myself, and have told before*. A fact without a source is, to valley ears, exactly what an unfinished sentence is to a founder: a thing left lying around, and things left lying around, down here, get forgotten. The greeting, exchanged a thousand times a day in the rook-loud lanes, is *Still told* — said with satisfaction, the way the summit says *Still here*, because in the Settled Country it means the same thing.

For the land will not keep them, and they have known it forever, and they have answered it the only way there was to answer. The stone forgets; therefore the people remember, professionally, liturgically, in shifts. The tellers are the valley's accidental clergy the way the lamplighters are the summit's — nobody founded the order; the country simply grew it, the way a body grows the organ it is dying without. Over the door of the register hall, cut in keeping-stone so it will stay cut, is the whole of the civic creed: *RECITATION IS INFRASTRUCTURE.* The tellers keep the great registers — births, deaths, field-names, bridge-names, the forty recorded names of every hill that has shed its names — and they walk fixed rounds in fixed order, a bell in one hand and a book they rarely need to open in the other, saying the country back to itself. A field unspoken too long slips from the record, and the valley believes — quietly, professionally — that the record is most of what is holding some of this landscape in the world's grip at all. There is a moor in the north Grandmothers that proves it, or is held to: the unaccounted moor, a stretch of country that has shed every name ever pressed on it, where travelers report a feeling less of danger than of *omission*, of walking through a paragraph that has been struck out. It is crossed with one's own name written on paper and carried in a boot. Once a year the tellers walk it end to end in relay, reciting continuously, and this is called the walking of the names, and it is the valley's evacuation-in-reverse: where the summit withdraws from ground that has stopped deciding, the valley marches into ground that has stopped being told, and tells it.

The dying ask for tellers, the way the dying up above ask for lamplighters. The funeral is called the entering. The body is given to the Hush or to the forgetting ground, and nobody grieves the grave, because the grave was never the point; the point is the moment in the slate hall when the name goes into the register and is recited for the first time by someone outside the family, at which the family may weep, because the dead one is now infrastructure. If there is money, there is keeping-stone, and the letter-cutters — the one craft in the valley that defeats the ground outright, cutting valley names into summit rock — end every inscription with the formula that ends every valley letter and every valley prayer: *as told.*

And if there is more money still, there is the other way. The valley calls them the short way and the long way round. The long way is the river: ashes to the Hush, which carries the grey milk of the concluded Grandmothers west to the sea — and here the valley's tellers, in their unofficial capacity, will lower their voices and lay out the suspicion their guild has held for six generations and never entered in any register. The hills wear down. The rivers carry them to the sea. The sea breathes up as cloud; the cloud lies as a deck against the flanks of the new mountain; and the green rock of the summit, everyone agrees, is faintly damp, and *precipitates*. Nothing concludes, the tellers say. It only changes registers. The Grandmothers are not gone; they are up there, letter by letter, being redrafted — and so, the belief runs, given time and weather, is everyone ever given to the Hush. The long way round. Patient families, the phrase goes.

The short way is the Stair. A vermilion tin, a paid passage, a relative riding three days up through the white miles — where, by a custom neither country remembers agreeing with the other, no one speaks, because in the white between the grammars neither *for now* nor *as it was* will hold — to scatter the ashes past the lamps, in the pending zones, where the mountain is still considering. Some are kept. The letters that come back down, when a summit cousin writes that a wall has closed gently over and the niche has been recorded, are read aloud in the slate hall, and entered.

The children, meanwhile, are being taught what the land taught everyone. Their forbidden game is scribing — slipping out with a stolen chisel to cut their names into the Grandmothers and coming back across the seasons to watch the hills decline them, letter by softening letter, almost always within the year. A child whose name survives three winters earns a schoolyard standing no fight could buy, but the game's real lesson is the one every child loses it to: *the land will not keep you; only the telling will.* And so the prestige that matters is the other kind — the recitation chains, name linked to name linked to source, the champions holding whole parishes of the dead in order, four hundred names without a breath — children pressing their memories into the future to see if it will hold them, which is, their grandmothers point out, exactly what the summit children are doing with their palms, by other means, at the other end of the Stair.

The descended gather at Stairfoot in the evenings. Old founders who came down for the thick air and cannot bear how quiet complete sentences sound in a country that never needed them; old surveyors gone the grey of two landscapes now, who still carry the shuttered vermilion lamps and still do not discuss what they do not discuss. They sit on the junction platform as the light goes, and they do not talk much, and when the up-train begins its climb they fall silent altogether and listen to the beat of it fading into the white miles, the valley's one borrowed heartbeat, carrying the old forests up to be spent.

---

So: who lives in the Settled Country? Colliers who speak in the accomplished, tellers who keep the land in the world's grip by saying it, letter-cutters spelling valley names into mountain stone, children with their chains of four hundred dead, rooks — true rooks, finished rooks, whose emigrant descendants wheel crook-winged through the lamplight three days up, and who receive the occasional half-redrafted returner with the particular silence rookeries reserve for those who went — eels in the grey river, sheep with no opinions whatsoever, the Grandmothers worn to their gums, and, on the ceiling every clear-under night, the ember.

Ask them, and they will touch the breastbone lightly — the valley's gesture, made over the place where a person keeps their register — and answer in the grammar of the accomplished. They live in a sentence that has been read. They have had a long time to think about what a sentence is for, and they have concluded — as the almanac has it, as the tellers hold, as the ember confirms nightly — that it is for the next one. Their forests are in the mountain's furnaces. Their hills are in its weather. Their dead, the patient ones, are in its walls, taking the long way round; and when the murmur under those lamplit streets finally arrives at its verb, some of the words it will have arrived through were theirs.

They are not the hills' survivors, exactly. They are their being remembered.

Still told, as it was.
