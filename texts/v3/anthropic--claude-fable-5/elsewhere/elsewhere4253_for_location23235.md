**The Tall Hollows**

The water has to go somewhere. Even a current that moves at the speed of seasons is a current, and a plain that drains imperceptibly is still a plain that drains. Walk the Long Shallows long enough — days, keeping your acre, letting the frog-chorus wash through you like weather — and you will eventually notice that the water has begun, very quietly, to have an opinion. The sheets, which everywhere else lie flat as a held breath, acquire a grain. The leaf you dropped a week ago has moved. Then it has moved again. The wading birds thin out. The frogs thin out. The far singers do not carry the Tone past a certain line — nothing forbids it, but a moving floor is bad manners, and the plain's whole ancient courtesy depends on stillness, so their country simply ends where the water stops being furniture and starts being traffic.

Past that line the sheets begin to braid. Ankle-deep becomes shin-deep becomes a low glassy sliding, miles wide, all of it converging with the terrible mild patience of this world on a district of the bedrock where the old smooth stone is split. The seams here did not weep; they opened. Scattered across a few last miles of flats are mouths in the rock — round, polished, rimmed like lips, some a stride across and some wide enough to swallow one of the bone-colored domes whole — and into them the water goes down. The Long Shallows end in swallows, which is the kind of joke a language makes when no one is watching.

Stand at the rim of one of the great mouths and you can hear the plain pouring its voice down its own throat. All that unabsorbed sound — the bird-cries, the frog-roar, the universal trickle — funnels down with the water, and beneath your feet the stone hums with the falling of it. Because it does fall. That is the scandal of this place, the thing the plain spent a hundred flat miles pretending was impossible: here, water that took a decade to cross the shallows spends the whole of its savings in three seconds, dropping in warm braided ropes down throats of stone into the dark. It is the only vertical event in the world above, and the world above turns its back on it.

Below is another country.

It is warm down there — the same deep bath-water warmth, because it is the same body's heat — and it is wet, and it is black, and it is enormous, and it is *young*. That is the first thing to understand about the Tall Hollows, and the hardest, coming down from a land that finished becoming itself before almost anything began: the Hollows are still becoming. They are, as far as anyone could say, the only place in this world still under construction.

The accounting is simple, once you see it. The domes of the shallows lose a grain a year. The seams weep, the water works the stone, and everything the plain lets go — the grain, the lime of a billion snail shells, the fine dust of moss and reed and rain — rides down dissolved in the warm water, through the throats, into the dark. And there, drop by drop, in chamber after chamber, the water gives it back. Every ceiling in the Hollows is hung with growing stone: spears, curtains, ribs, veils, whole descending forests of it, pale and glistening, each point tipped with a hanging bead of water that took a century to arrive and will take a minute more to fall. The mountains of this world were never destroyed. They were swallowed, and they are being rebuilt — point-first, upside down, in the dark, out of the same substance, at the same unhurried rate at which they left. The plain is not eroding. It is relocating.

So the Hollows go down as the shallows go on: gallery under gallery, rooms the size of a cupped hand and rooms that could hold a weather system, connected by throats and crawls and long tilted halls where the water slides in sheets an inch deep — the shallows again, in miniature, in the dark, learning the same flatness their parent country perfected. Where a hanging spear has met its rising twin, there are columns, floor to ceiling, some thin as reeds and some vast as the domes above are broad. Which means there are, at last, *walls*. There are corners. There is *behind*. Everything the plain lacks by constitution, the Hollows have in oversupply: enclosure, shadow, shelter beyond the shelter of warm air, a thousand thousand rooms. The world above is one room with no walls. The world below is all walls, still going up.

And the sound — the sound is the second surprise, the mirror of the first. Above, every noise belongs to the whole plain. Below, every noise belongs to its room. Sound down here has architecture; a drip in one chamber is a different instrument than the same drip next door, and each gallery has a voice as particular as a face — this one dry and clicking, this one round as a struck bell, this one so dense with echo that a whisper circles it three times before it dies. There is no chorus. There are clocks: the drips, each on its own century schedule, tick-ticking the mountains back together. Beneath them, where you can find a silence deep enough, there is something else — a pressure more than a sound, slow, low, continuous, coming through the stone itself from far above. It has not stopped for as long as anything down here has been alive to feel it. The dark has its own name for it, but you would recognize it. It is the Tone, arriving through a mile of rock, worn down from a voice to a pulse: the heartbeat of a world the Hollows have never seen.

At the very bottom, where even the falling finally runs out of down, the water stops. There are lakes there, black and blood-warm and utterly clear, so still that the tenders speak of them the way the plain might speak of itself — as the one finished thing in a country of works-in-progress. Below the lakes there are fissures too fine for anything but water, and where those go no one knows. The Hollows have their horizon too. It simply points the other way.

What lives here is small. That is the first thing — small where the waders above are tall, as though the two peoples had divided a single height between them and the dark had taken the humble share. They are pale as the water-milk that films the wet stone, soft-voiced, warm-handed, with eyes about the size of a courtesy — sealed in most of them, vestigial in the rest — because they see with their ears and their palms and the soles of their broad soft feet, which read the floor the way their tall cousins' feet read the bedrock through three inches of water, and are, though neither people will ever know it, the same feet.

They live close. That is the second thing, and the one that would most horrify the plain. Their ordinary intimacy is conducted at a hand's breadth; they speak with their mouths nearly at each other's ears, they sleep in warm knots in the dry high alcoves, they navigate strange passages in linked lines, each with a hand on the shoulder ahead, and two of them separated by six hundred yards is not a marriage of correct distance but an emergency. In a country of corners, farness is not honesty — farness is loss. You can lose someone here. There is *behind* to lose them behind. And so where the plain built its soul out of distance, the dark built its soul out of nearness, and each would find the other's love unbearable for about a day, and then for the rest of their lives would find their own walls, or their own horizons, faintly strange.

Their word for *speak* and their word for *keep* are the same word. This is not deceit — deceit needs an audience of everyone, and no room here holds everyone. It is that a thing said in the Hollows goes into a room and stays there, the way water goes into a pool, and so speech is a kind of storage, and a secret is not a lie but a room that isn't finished yet. Half their courtesies are ways of asking whether a room is ready. Their word for *up* is their word for *ago*, and their word for *down* is their word for *soon*, because time here falls with the water: the past is the ceiling, the future is the lakes, and the present is the drop hanging at the tip of the stone, always about to let go. Their word for everything above the ceiling — the plain, the light, the two stacked skies, the entire rest of the universe — is their word for *rain*, because that is the only form in which the rest of the universe has ever visited.

And they build. Or rather — because no single life is long enough to build anything here — they *tend*. They are gardeners of drips. A pebble placed just so splits a falling drop and starts two spears where one grew; a thread of washed-down reed-fiber, laid along the stone, wicks a seep a finger's width sideways and bends a column's whole future; a lifetime of such work moves the architecture of a chamber by about a knuckle, and they consider this a full and honorable career. Every tender inherits plans from the dead and leaves plans for the unborn, sung into the rooms where the plans apply — *speak* and *keep*, the same word — so that a child can stand in an unfinished gallery and listen to instructions four hundred years old, addressed to them personally, by name-of-office, from someone who knew exactly where the water would be by now. The plain has one monument, made of sound, rebuilt in the same spot forever. The Hollows are the opposite arrangement: the whole country is the monument, and the sound is the scaffolding.

Because the columns ring. Struck gently with the flat of the hand, each one sounds a note, and the note can be tuned — thickened here, starved there, over centuries — and the tenders' great work, the plan under all their plans, is this: the vast chambers of the middle deep are being tuned, column by column, generation by generation, into harmony with the pulse that comes down through the stone. They do not know it is a voice. They do not know it is passed, this hour, from one gray figure to another across miles of dusk water. They know only that it is old, and faithful, and above, and that a room brought into tune with it seems to come alive — the pulse arrives and the columns take it up and the whole gallery breathes sound instead of merely holding it. They have been at this for longer than their memory reaches. The Hollows are slowly becoming an instrument, and the instrument is learning, note by patient note, to play the Tone back.

Some of the columns are people. When a tender begins to hear their own drips slowing — that is how they put it — they finish their rooms, which can take a season, and then they are carried to the Standing Galleries and set beneath a steady drip, sitting, composed, and the water does what the water does. Within a few generations they are glossy and pale and permanent, and their great-great-grandchildren come and lay a palm on them and strike them softly and hear what note they have become. The Galleries are a choir of ancestors, tuned like everything else. And there are other columns there — the ones the tenders call the *guests*. Because the water that falls into the Hollows carries taste as well as stone: shell, moss, frog-spawn, rain, and, some years, faintly, unmistakably, *someone* — a difference in the sweetness that the old tenders can name the moment it touches their tongue. They know what it means, or they know enough. Somewhere above, in the loud bright country of the rain, a stranger lay down along a weeping seam and let the plain keep them, and the plain, which permits no graves, has sent the substance of them down. When the water tastes of someone, the tenders start a new drip in the guest galleries and let a column begin. The shallows refuse monuments; the dark builds them anyway, out of courtesy, for tall people it will never meet — and the stone that enrobes the tenders' own dead is made, in part, of those strangers, so that the two peoples' dead share bodies the way neighbors share a wall. The kinship between the countries is not memory or trade. It is mineral.

A child of the Hollows comes of age going *up*. They climb the wet throats with a guide, at dusk of a day they are not told in advance, to a shelf beneath one of the great swallow-mouths, and they stand in the falling column of green-gold light — the only daylight of their lives — with the warm rain of the plain's water coming down around them, and through the mouth, funneled and far, they hear it: the frog-chorus of the Long Shallows, the whole loud finished world above, rolling unbroken from a horizon they will never see to a horizon they will never see. They stand in it as long as they can bear. Then they climb back down. Adulthood, among the tenders, is the day you have heard the world and chosen the dark anyway — not as exile, but the way their tall cousins' adulthood is the day you would rather be heard than held. Each people's threshold is the other people's ordinary air.

They want nothing, either, though it looks different in the dark. They are not hoarding, not hiding, not waiting to emerge. They had no becoming to finish, because they *are* the becoming — the ongoing half of a world that split its verbs long ago and gave *being* to the plain and *building* to the deep. And in the evenings, such as evenings are in a country whose only clock is water, the tenders of the middle galleries perform the day's last work: they walk the tuned chambers striking the great columns in slow sequence, sending a long chord up into the rock — an answer, addressed to the pulse, dispatched with no more expectation than a hand laid on warm stone. Most of it dies in the mile of sleeper between. But here and there the bedrock is thin under an old bone-colored dome, and above, in the frog-loud dark of the plain, a gray figure asleep on the stone half-wakes with their palm already flat against it and feels it humming, very faintly, from below — and takes it, as they take everything the floor does, as the contentment of the one who lay down first, and presses the hand a moment longer before sleeping again. *Still going*, says the palm from above. *Still coming*, say the columns from below. And the stone between them — warm, wet, a mile of patience thick, made of both countries and both their dead and carrying everything it has ever been given the only way it knows, which is down, and slowly, and without loss — takes both messages at once, the way it takes the rain and the Tone and the sweetness of strangers, and rings.
