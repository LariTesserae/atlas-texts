**The White Fells**

You are at sea level. That is the first thing to understand, and the hardest, because everything around you insists that you stand on high ground.

The wind says so. It is a fell-country wind, hard and scouring and constant, the kind that lives only on exposed heights. The land says so: white ridges running to the horizon, long moors of carved snow, passes and saddles and summits under a huge pale sky. Your body believes it entirely. Your body is wrong. The ridges are ice. The moors are ice. The whole country is a lid — the sealed grey ceiling that a drowned land, far below, knows only as a rumor of light at midday — and the true fells, the stone ones, the old ones, are under your bootsoles: forty-some fathoms of dark water down, lush and lit and listening.

The people who live here know this the way you know your own name. They are standing on the roof of their house. They have been standing on it for a very long time.

---

**Terrain.** The ice is old and freshwater-hard, grey-white shading to a pale bone color where the wind has polished it — the same bone color, though no one alive has seen both, as the granite of the drowned country underneath. It is not still. The whole sheet flows, a river lying down, carrying everything on it some few hundred paces between one dark season and the next, always in the same direction: outward, away from the high country below, toward what the people here call *the leaving country*.

The relief of the place is made overnight. The sheet, flowing, jams against itself and buckles, and a range of rubble ice heaves up where the day before there was plain — blue at the fresh breaks, ten, twenty feet of shattered blocks, running for miles. These are the white fells proper, and they are born loud: a groan, a building strain, then a *crack* like a tree splitting, and the detonation travels down through the ice and into the water and rolls out across the drowned valleys below, where it arrives as weather. Every range in this country announces its own birth downward. The people give the new ridges the old fell-names — the names of the stone hills underneath — and when a ridge collapses or drifts off into the leaving country, the name is lifted from it and set on another. Nothing here keeps a name for long except the people themselves. The names are nomads too.

Between the ridges lie the moors: wind-cut snow in frozen swells, hard enough to ring underfoot. And under roughly a day's walk in any direction from the country's center lies the boundary you cannot see but can *cut to* — the edge of the seeded ice. Chip a pit anywhere over the drowned fells and the ice comes up salted with pale grains, layered like snowfall held mid-fall, band on band on band: the spores that rise from the meadows below once a year and freeze into the lid, a stratigraphy of light, one layer for every winter since the water closed. This is home ice. You can tell it blindfolded, by taste, the people say, though what they mean is by weight — home ice in the hand feels *attended*. Beyond it the bands stop. Out there the ice rides over the drowned lowlands, the black leagues, where the sea beneath is only sea, and the lid is dumb clear ice with nothing in it at all.

And one place does not move.

Out on the highest of the moors there is a round patch of ice, perhaps a hundred paces across, black and still and glass-clear, that the flowing sheet cannot carry. The sheet tears around it. Year in and year out the moving ice shears past its rim and piles up a rough standing ring of pressure ridge, a wall of broken blue, endlessly ground down and endlessly renewed, roaring quietly at the perimeter while the disc inside sits silent. The people call it the eye. It lies — the reckoners have never once had to correct this — directly above the high corrie of the drowned country, above the circle of nine stones and the standing cold that lives inside them. The dead below hold still, and so the living above have one fixed point in a country that flows. Every bearing in the White Fells is taken from the eye. Every path is counted from it. It is the pin the whole map hangs on, and it is not a landmark anyone built. It is a grave, holding.

**Weather.** Two seasons, mostly: the light and the dark. In the light season the sun wheels low and long and the snow burns white and the melt-pools open. In the dark season there is no sun at all — only stars, wind, and, some years, the country's floor.

The wind is the true maker here, the sculptor and the mason, and it does one further thing that the people understand better than any people has ever understood its own weather: it *pays downward*. The sun drives the wind; the wind drives the ice; the ice grinds, booms, and sings; and the sound falls through forty fathoms of cold water and lands on the drowned slopes below, where the dark meadows graze on it the way grass once grazed on light. The fells below lost the sun and kept its salary. It comes down through the roof, translated into sound, and the roof-country's storms are the house-country's harvests. On the wild nights, when the ridges are building and the whole sheet groans end to end, the people lie in their snow shelters and say, without irony, *good growing weather*.

**What grows.** Nothing. That is nearly the whole truth. No animal has ever lived on the White Fells — no bird crosses it, nothing burrows the snow, and the seeded ice holds not one bone. It is the drowned country's second silence, repeated an octave higher: below, no beast because every office is filled; above, no beast because no office can be held on ground that will not hold still. Except by people. And only barely.

But there is one growing thing, and it is a guest. In high summer, on home ice only, the melt-pools that open in the hollows will sometimes sprout: pale threads rising from the drowned grains freed by the thaw, blue-white, glowing faintly even at noon — the same lantern-light that stands on the dark slopes far below, here dwindled to a finger's length and trembling in a puddle of sunlit water. They live a few weeks. The freeze takes them back. The people tend these pools — clear the drifted snow off them, guard them from nothing, harvest nothing — for no reason they will state plainly, though the reason is plain enough: the spores rise from the meadows toward the ice, toward the rumor of the sun, and almost none of them ever arrive. These few did. Things that grow toward the sun should be allowed, once, to reach it. So for a few weeks each summer the drowned country's children sit in open daylight on the roof of their house, and the people keep the snow off them, and everyone is quiet about it.

**Sound.** The White Fells are loud the way the country below them is loud, and half the sounds are the same sounds heard from the other side: the groaning chords, the splitting cracks, the long strain of the ridges — the roof and the house share one voice between them.

Two sounds belong to the surface alone. The first is the tone. Once or twice a day, on no schedule, the great low note that rolls through the drowned valleys — the voice of the heavy ones in the deep tarns, if that is what it is — comes up through the water and the ice and arrives in the soles of the feet and the bones of the chest. Everyone on the White Fells stops when it comes. This is not superstition, or not only; it is manners. You stop, you stand, and when the tone has passed under you, you say your own name aloud, once, into the wind. Below, the flock answers the shepherd in light — every lantern on every slope brightening at once. Above, the family answers in names. In the dark season you can watch both answers at the same time: the tone comes, the ice under your feet blinks with a faint deep glow — the whole floor of the country brightening for a breath — and around you in the darkness, voices, one after another, each saying only *here*.

The second sound is the people themselves. In most countries you teach children to walk softly. Here the rude thing is silence. The people of the White Fells walk *loudly* — bone-shod staffs struck down at every second stride, hard heels, sledge runners left unwaxed on purpose — because footfall goes through the ice, and what goes through the ice is food, and news. The meadows below eat the sound of the roof, and so a traveling string of walkers is, quite literally, feeding the house as it goes: a moving tithe, a drumming on the lid, a message repeated at every stride for miles and generations. *Still up here. Still up here. Still up here.*

**The built things.** Nothing built on the White Fells can be permanent, so the people build the way water flows — constantly, and without regret.

Their shelters are snow, cut in blocks and corbelled inward, round-walled, low-doored: the exact forms their kin below raised in drystone, remembered through the hands for hundreds of years and rebuilt every few nights in a material that lasts a season. Every doorway in the country faces the eye — as every doorway below faces down-valley, toward the deep — so that in any camp, in any whiteout, you come out of your door already oriented, facing the dead, facing the datum, facing home's stillest room. When a camp moves on, the shelters are left standing, and the flowing sheet carries them slowly off into the leaving country: the White Fells are scattered downstream with abandoned houses sailing away, softening, collapsing over years. Some of them glow. When a lamp is spent — and the lamps here are bowls of melted seed-ice, warmed against the body until the drowned spores wake and burn blue-white for a night — the spent water is poured into a niche in the shelter wall and refrozen, where it smolders faintly for weeks more. So the leaving country, seen from a ridge on a dark night, is pricked out here and there with dim blue coals riding away on the ice: old houses, going out slowly, going wherever the ice goes.

And then there are the cairns, which exist only at night. Each string of walkers carries stones — real stones, fell stones, taken from the high country in the last days before the water closed and handed down since, worn glassy by generations of pockets and palms. Stone is the rarest substance in this country; there is no other rock within a lifetime's walk. At each camp, the string's stones are stacked into a small cairn on the ice, set — by the reckoners' count of paces and bearings from the eye — directly above one of the true cairns standing on the drowned ridgelines below, so that for one night the old waymark exists twice, forty fathoms apart, stone answering stone through the dark. At departure the cairn is taken down and pocketed and carried on. The paths below are marked permanently by a country that keeps everything. The paths above are marked one night at a time by a people who can keep almost nothing. Both sets of cairns are maintained. Neither has been needed, for the purpose of finding the way, in living memory. That was never really the purpose.

**The rules of the place, as far as they can be kept.** Below, everything settles and nothing decays. Above, nothing settles and everything decays — snow, ice, shelters, ridges, even the ground itself, flowing out from under your feet at a few hundred paces a year. So the deepest law of the White Fells is the law of station: to stay above home, you must walk. Not away, not toward — *against*. The people's whole existence is a slow upstream walk on a river of ice, camp by camp, correcting the drift, holding position over a country they will never touch. Stand still for ten years and the leaving country will have you. The reckoners — one or two in every string, trained from childhood — keep the count: paces from the eye, star-bearings in the dark season, the shape of the eye's roaring ring, the banding of the seeded ice. They can stop anywhere on the home moors, cut a pit, read the layers, take a bearing, and tell you what lies below you: *the terraces. the ford. the third shelter on the western ridge. your grandmother's valley.*

Because that is the other half of the law. The flood divided the country from its names, and each side kept the half it could carry. The drowned fells kept the things — every wall, every doorway, every cairn, held whole in the cold. The people of the White Fells kept the words — the name of every shelter, terrace, ford, saddle, and spring, strung into the long songs, divided among the strings, each string keeping the names of its own district below. There were nine strings once, one for each of the standing stones in the corrie. There are eight now. The district of the string that ended goes unsung, and the others say — you may tell yourself it is only grief seeing itself, the way you tell yourself the meadow's stir is only current — that in the years since, at the midwinter glow, the paths of that one valley have shone a little dimmer than the rest.

**The season.** The year breathes out and in.

In the light season the strings go outbye — out over the black leagues, where the ice is dumb and the sea beneath it is only sea, ordinary and indifferent and *full*: fish at the open leads, seal on the floes, everything the kept country below refuses to contain. The people hunt there, render and store and mend there, and do not love it there. The ice of the black leagues says nothing through your bootsoles. Nothing under it is waiting. They call sleeping on it *sleeping in a strange house*, and they walk loudly the whole way home.

In the dark season they converge — all eight strings, in from the leagues, drawing together across the home moors toward the eye, until the whole people, some few hundred souls, is camped in a loose ring around the still black disc and its roaring wall. And then, on the old count, in the pit of the winter — what the drowned country below, unsure of its own slow cycles, calls *once a season, if these are seasons*, and what the roof knows plainly as once a year — the floor of the world lights.

It comes up through forty fathoms and the thickness of the lid as a soft, exact, blue-white map: every drowned path, pass, terrace, and doorway traced in cold light under the ice, the whole high country drawn from end to end beneath the walkers' feet. The spores are rising; the tone is tolling over and over; below, the house has lit every window. And above, the people walk it. This is the heart of their year and the whole of their liturgy: to walk the lit paths — the only walking those paths have had since the water came — string by string, each in its own sung district, children led along the shining lines and taught the doors. At every doorway-point they stop, and knock: the staff's bone heel, struck once on the ice, over the low black mouth of a shelter forty fathoms down whose threshold their family has not crossed in centuries and has never once considered abandoned. Knock, and stand, and move on to the next door. From above, in the middle of the light, the high corrie shows as the one dark thing: a round black absence ringed by inward-facing lamps, and above it the black disc of the eye, dark answering dark through the water, the two still places of the world aligned.

The dead are buried then, if the dead can wait, and the dead of this people are patient. A pit is cut in the eye — peck by peck, with the same patience that shaped the nine stones — and the body is given down, weighted with a single stone from the family cairn. This is how stones are spent; there is no other way they may be spent; the traveling cairns grow shorter by one stone for every death, and no one pretends not to know what is being counted. The body sinks through the still black column and is not seen again, and the family stands at the pit's edge in the dark and watches the ice. Sometimes — not always, and the winters when it does not come are the hard ones — a thread of faint light rises in the sheet a while later, running from beneath the eye off toward the corrie, and goes out.

Received.

Then the glow fades, the spores settle far below, the strings turn outbye again, and the White Fells go back to their work — walking, counting, knocking, staying — poor, patient, and never for a moment alone.

**Who Lives on the White Fells**

The honest first answer, once again, is almost nothing: no beast, no bird, no growing thing that is not a guest. But the true answer is the mirror of the one the drowned country gives. Below, the place is a household with no people in it. Above, it is a people with no place under them — or rather, with their place under them in the fullest possible sense, forty fathoms under, kept and lit and out of reach.

They are the other half of the fells' folk. The old high country bred people who did two things: they *kept* — walls, flocks, terraces, the nine stones — and they *walked* — the rounds, the ridgeline paths, the long transhumance out to summer grazing and home again. When the rain would not end and the water climbed the valleys, the people divided along the grain of their own lives. The keeping stayed. The keepers went up with the flocks as far as up went, to the last corrie, and were kept in their turn — they are the standing cold inside the nine stones now, and the meadow is their flock, and the house is in order. The walking left. The walkers took to the water as the water came, and to the ice as the ice formed, and they have not stopped. They say — it is their account of the tended cairns and the cleared doorways below, and it is not offered as an explanation, only as what happened — that on the last morning their ancestors walked the rounds a final time and then set the walk down at the first cairn, like a coat left on a peg, so that the country would not be without it. They do not claim the walk below is theirs anymore. They only notice, every midwinter, that the cairns beneath the lit paths stand freshly kept, and they take it the way you take smoke from a chimney you cannot see: as news.

The old tongue of the fells had two words that carried the whole shape of a shepherd's world: *inbye*, the ground close to the house, and *outbye*, the open hill where the flock ranges and the shepherd goes to work. The people of the White Fells call the drowned country *inbye*. They call themselves the outbye folk. Understand what that word refuses to say. It does not say exiles, or survivors, or the lost. A shepherd on the open hill is not lost and is not away. A shepherd on the hill is *working*, and will come down when the work is done, and the house knows it, and keeps the lamp in the window accordingly. The flood, in their reckoning, did not drive them out. It caught them outbye, that is all — the whole walking half of a family caught out on the hill by weather of unusual duration — and they have conducted themselves, for hundreds of years, on flowing ice, in the dark half of the world, exactly as a shepherd conducts himself when the mist comes down: keep moving, keep counting, keep loud, and hold your line home.

So everything the place has taught you to see resolves into one arrangement, held between two countries through a lid of grinding ice. The house below keeps the things and the lights. The family above keeps the names and the doors — knocked on yearly, every one, from the wrong side. The house feeds on the family's footsteps; the family reads by the house's risen light. The dead of the roof go down to the still room, and hold still there so faithfully that the living steer by them. The tone comes up through the ice, and the meadows answer in lamps and the walkers answer in names, and both answers say the same word. And every doorway on both sides of the water — the low black mouths in the drowned drystone, the snow-cut mouths of the shelters drifting on the ice — is kept clear, because doorways are for arriving through, and both halves of this family are certain, with the calm total certainty of people who have never once discussed the alternative, that the ice is a season. However long. That one winter the lid will open the way lids do, and the water will be asked to leave or the way down will simply be there, and the outbye folk will come down the old paths at last — paths they have walked all their lives, forty fathoms too high — and knock, and not have to wait.

They rehearse nothing else. Their children can find, in total darkness, on moving ice, the position of a door they have never touched. And below, after everything, the lamps are lit, and the house is in order, and out on the roof, in the wind, in the long dark, staying loudly and on purpose exactly where they are: the household, coming home at the speed of the ice, which is to say — always, and not yet.
