# The Ochre Stair

You reach it by going down, which is right, because everything it has ever received arrived the same way — the water, the rust, the sound, the news.

Start at the rim of the caldera. Walk it, all the way around the nave of cold gold-green water, and you will find one low place: a groove in the black glass on the sunward side, hand-wide, polished smooth as a bottle's lip in a wall that is otherwise all knives. Most years the lake sits a palm's breadth below it and the groove does nothing, wants nothing, is nothing — a flaw in the rim. But the mountain is named Spillway, and the name is this notch's doing. The entire peak is named after a flaw you could cover with two hands, because of what happens in the years when the snow comes heavy and the melt runs long and the lake climbs those last few centimeters and goes over.

It does not go over dramatically. A thread of water, cold and mineral-sweet, slides through the groove and starts down the flank — and the moment it touches air, it begins to declare itself. All that dissolved iron, held invisible in the dark chemistry of the deep rock, meets oxygen and blooms. The thread turns the color of dried blood within meters. It descends six hundred meters of virgin basalt as a rust-red ribbon, and when the spill ends — weeks later, a season at most — the ribbon stays: a single ochre sash down the black mountain, dry for decades at a time, repainted once a generation. From the green country below, in the right light, the mountain looks like it has been marked. It has been.

Follow the sash down. Where the slope gentles — where the mountain's new rock runs out and shoulders into the old buried land it rose through — the ribbon ends, and the stair begins.

Forty pools, perhaps fifty, descending the flank in steps. Each one is rimmed with a raised lip of stone that the water itself has laid down, spill after spill, the way a candle builds its own collar of wax: the cold lake-water comes down the flank, slows in a basin, breathes, and drops its iron at the edges. The rims are ochre and oxblood and saffron and tea-brown, cream-colored at their newest lips, and they curve and pout and terrace like something thrown on a wheel. The pools they hold are nothing like the water that made them. They are shallow — ankle-deep, knee-deep — and the same hard unfiltered sun that pours through the caldera's thirty meters has only inches to cross here, so the pools are warm. They steam at dawn. They cook by noon. The topmost ones, freshly scalded by mineral floods, are nearly bare. But descend, pool by pool, and life comes in like a tide: first the colored films, then true green — algae in ropes, moss on the lower rims, sedges rooted in rust-mud, and at the bottom of the stair the first stunted trees of the green world standing with their feet almost in the last pool. The stair is a translation, run downhill. Water enters at the top speaking mineral and leaves at the bottom speaking life, and every step is one more word of it converted.

Now hold that against the place the water came from, and notice what the stair is. In the caldera, nothing is made. Nothing builds — not the smiths, not the sparkers, not the glassmoths smoothing their slow ribbons; the whole clanging congregation produces no architecture at all. And here, at the end of a rust ribbon, the lake's own water has been building for as long as the mountain has brimmed. Rims, basins, terraces, galleries — a construction the size of an amphitheater, raised in installments decades apart, by water that was never instructed to do anything but fall. The caldera makes nothing. This is what it makes anyway, the moment its water escapes it: the stair is the lake's handwriting, and the lake will never see a line of it.

Look.

That is the stair's word, the way *listen* is the caldera's. Because everything here is written down. Break a rim — frost does it for you, in the high pools — and the cross-section reads like the plate of an old smith: laminations, dark and pale, each pair a flood and the long dry patience after it. The lowest rims, the oldest, carry more than thirty layers. The stair is an archive of every brim-year the mountain has ever had, stacked into stone in order, the only written history the lake possesses, kept six hundred meters below it by water and rust and things too small to see.

And it is not silent. Stand at the upper pools on a still day and the tolling of the peak comes down to you — thinned by distance, arriving partly through the air and partly up through your boots from the rock itself, a foundry heard not through a wall now but through a mountain. The pools show it. Each meniscus carries a faint standing shiver, a moiré of fine rings that isn't wind, isn't insects: it is the caldera's voice, printed on the water. Every pool on the stair wears the peak's fingerprint on its surface, all day, all year, whether anything reads it or not.

Something reads it.

---

The first thing to say about the residents of the stair is that some of them know the caldera — know it, in the only ways that matter to them, better than anything living inside it does. Up there, the smith knows voltage and the eel knows warmth and not one tenant knows the lake. Down here, at the end of the ribbon, the lake is heard, tasted, predicted, recorded, and remembered by creatures that have never once seen it and never will.

Begin with the masons, since they built the place — or rather, since the place is their refuse, stacked into grandeur the way the caldera's music is hygiene multiplied into a choir. The masons are mats: layered films that sheet the rims and pool-floors in ochre velvet. Their under-layer is old family — the same bodiless iron-eating landlords who colonized the caldera before anything with a shape did, come down the spillway in the floods and settled here like relatives moving to the coast. Their over-layer is new money: green, sun-drinking algae of the ordinary living world. Between the two layers runs a constant quiet trade — iron and sugar, dark chemistry and light — the mountain's insides and the green country married at the smallest possible scale, and the rims are what the marriage throws off. The masons do not build on purpose. Nothing anywhere in this water has ever done anything on purpose. But every flood they wall in another lamination, and the archive grows a page.

Sealed inside those pages, waiting, are the brimflies.

A brimfly egg can lie in the rim-stone for twenty years, forty, sixty. Rain touches it and it does nothing; snowmelt, nothing; the warm pool lapping its layer, nothing. It is waiting for one taste — the cold, metal-sweet, voltage-brothed signature of the lake itself — and when a spill comes over the notch and floods the rims with the caldera's own water, every viable egg in the wetted stone wakes within hours. The larvae live exactly as long as the flood does, gorging on the sudden rust-bloom, and then rise together: a smoke of wings over red water, a few million bodies with a few days apiece, mating in columns above the pools and driving their eggs into the fresh soft rim-mud, where the masons — building, always building — will wall them into the newest page before the season is out. Then the flood ends and the species goes back to being punctuation in stone. Every brimfly that has ever flown was a flood; the whole lineage is a chain of floods with decades of held breath between the links. Their calendar has one date on it, and the date moves, and no brimfly has ever known when it would fall.

But something on the stair does know — or does the thing that stands in the same place knowing would stand.

The listeners are striders: long-legged, dimple-footed, walking the pool surfaces the way everything of their kind does, reading the meniscus for the ripple of a fallen insect. The meniscus here, though, carries more than ripples. It carries the peak. That faint standing shiver — the caldera's tolling, printed on every pool — is the constant background of a listener's life, the great steady tremor of the world, as fixed to them as the horizon. Except that it is not fixed. Fill a glass under a tap and listen: the vessel tells you how full it is. The caldera is a glass thirty meters deep, snowmelt for a tap, and in a brim-year, as the lake climbs its final centimeters toward the notch, its voice thickens — the tolling arrives fuller, longer, the bourdon's daily lean carrying further through the rock. The pattern on the pools slides. And everything in a listener answers. They quit the open water, climb the rims, glue their eggs on stone high above any waterline the warm pools could ever reach, and cluster on the dry heights — and days later the flood comes down the ribbon and scours the basins they left. They do not know a lake exists. They do not know a flood is coming. They know the tremor moved, and their legs know what to do when it moves, and being right is invisible to them. The forecast has no forecaster. Up in the caldera the ringing means nothing, not even to the ringers; it leaves the peak as hygiene and arrives at the stair as prophecy, and at no point along the six hundred meters does anyone intend it.

The daubers read the listeners, which means they read the peak at one further remove. They are birds — dun, sturdy, sedge-nesting, a few resident year-round and the rest scattered down through the green country — and when the listeners climb the rims, daubers begin arriving from below, gathering for the brimfly smoke the way gulls gather behind a plow. But it is what they do after the feast that marks them. Fresh flood-mud, new-dropped from the lake's own water, is the richest red the stair ever offers, and the daubers work it into their feathers — deliberately, laboriously, hours of preening iron into themselves — and come away the color of the ribbon on the mountain. The stain fades over years. So the color is a date, worn: a deep-red dauber is a bird that was *there*, at a flood, and every year after, its fading tells everyone exactly how long ago. Most daubers live and die between floods and never earn the color at all; the red ones are attended by the others in some way no one has named — deferred to at nests, followed to water. And they carry it abroad. Daubers range far down into the forests below, and the peak's own pigment goes with them, shaken off in dust-baths over green country that has never once seen the mountain brim. The lake's memory, out walking around on wings.

At the bottom of the stair, in the last and largest pool, lives the colony of chimes — and here the two places touch most nearly, because the chimes are smiths. Some flood ago, among the billions of things the spillway swept over the notch to die, one soft pink comma of a smith larva survived six hundred meters of cascade and settled in the lowest pool, and its descendants hold that pool still. The stair has remade them. The water here is warm and bright with oxygen, so rust blooms on their plates in hours, not days, and they must groom constantly — a flex every few seconds where their ancestors toll ten times an hour. They stay small: thumbnail armor, foil-thin, worn out and shed and dissolved back into the mason-mats within a season, no iron snow, no century-thick elders, no bourdon. What this does to the sound is this: the lowest pool *chimes* — a quick, high, ceaseless tinkling, the caldera's foundry played presto and in miniature, a wind-chime descended from a cathedral bell. On the stillest nights the whole flank carries both at once, the great slow tolling above and the small fast ringing below, and the mountain sounds for all the world like call and response. It is not. The chimes cannot hear the peak; they ring for the same reason their ancestors ring, which is that iron in water wants to be rust. But meaning has never yet needed an intender, anywhere on this mountain, and it does not start needing one here.

There is one resident left, and it is the water.

Below the last rim, the ground is old land — the valley the mountain buried when it rose, geologically yesterday, with a stream in it that died in the dark under the new rock. The lowest pool's overflow, the little that survives translation all the way down the stair, sinks into gravel there. And it finds the old bed. The dead stream's channel still runs under the green country, kilometers of it, and the lake's water — filtered now, tamed, but still faintly cold, still faintly metal, still carrying its landlords — runs the ghost river's course in the dark and comes up far away in the forest as a small spring ringed with iron-stained stones. Animals drink there. The spring stains its own basin ochre, patiently, a rim beginning. Nothing at that spring will ever connect its rust-sweet water to a ringing mountain it cannot even see. The lake is still arriving, out there, and always will be.

So set the two places side by side, one above the other, joined by a hand-wide notch and a ribbon of rust. Up in the caldera: a roaring, tolling, electroplating congregation, immortal-slow, making nothing, keeping nothing, knowing nothing — a sum that sounds and cannot hear itself. Down here: everything the mountain lets go, caught and turned. Water into wall. Noise into forecast. Rust into feathers. Sixty silent years into a three-day smoke of wings. The knowing runs one way on this mountain, downhill, like everything else, and not a drop of it ever gets back up. The lake rings on above, unaware that below it, page by page, flood by flood, in stone and stain and the fading red of an old bird's back, it is being written down — and that the climbers, when they finally come, will cross the stair on their way up, step over the rims, refill their bottles from the third pool, and follow the ochre ribbon toward the sound without ever once suspecting that they have just walked the length of the only biography the ringing will ever have.
