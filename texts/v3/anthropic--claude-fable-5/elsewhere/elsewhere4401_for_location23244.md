# The Empty Bed

There is a country at the bottom of the world, and it has never once stayed the same.

Begin with the absence, because everything else follows from it. Once — and "once" here is the oldest word the world has — an ocean lay across these lowlands, broad and deep and dark, the original sea, the one that came with the planet. Then the shield rose at its edge, miles high, black and young, and the builders came, and the builders wanted the ocean's floor. So they did the simplest thing, at the largest scale anything has ever done a simple thing: they moved the sea out of the way. Up through the body of the mountain, engine by engine, century by century, they lifted an ocean and set it down in the caldera at the top of the world, where it stands to this day, roaring — and what they left behind is this: the Bed. The exposed floor of the first sea, drained to the stone, open now to the sky it spent its whole existence hiding from.

The High Sea, understand, was never the point. It is the spoil heap. It is where you put an ocean when an ocean is in your way. All that thunder at the top of the world — the standing chord, the mill towns, the giants holding their notes — all of it is the sound of a great obstruction being kept, forever, off this floor.

You descend into the Bed the way you would descend into any sea, except that the medium is air. The old shorelines still ring the surrounding continents, terraces of wave-cut stone high above you, the bathtub mark of a vanished tide. Below them the continental shelves fall away as vast pale plateaus; below the shelves, the true slopes; and below everything, kilometers below the datum where waves once were, the abyssal plain — now the deepest dry land in existence. The air down there is not the air of the heights. It is thick, hot, heavy in the lungs, dense enough to lean on, and it is never clear. Salt haze fills the Bed the way water once did, a suspended glittering dimness through which the sun arrives as a smear of red-gold and the horizon stands close enough to touch. This is the inversion you must hold in your mind from the start: the Standing Sea is water clear as air, where you can see for miles and there is nothing to see. The Empty Bed is air thick as water, where everything there is to see is hidden a hundred paces off.

And it is quiet. That is the second inversion, and the deeper one. After the total, structural, ceaseless roar of the sea above, the Bed is a country of near-silence — but a silence made of separate sounds, which is a thing the High Sea cannot imagine. Wind, arriving and departing. The dry hiss of salt grains crossing salt crust. The gunshot crack of a pan splitting in the heat. The rare, world-filling thunder of rain. In the High Sea, sound is the medium and silence is the monster. In the Bed, silence is the medium, and every sound is an *event* — discrete, directional, meaning something, over.

Except one. Put your body against the ground — anywhere in the Bed, from the foot of the shield to the far shelves — and you will feel it: a hum in the rock. Faint, continuous, deeper than hearing, strongest near the mountain and fading with distance but never quite gone. It is the Standing Sea. The roar of the stolen ocean, the standing chord that fills the basin at the top of the world, does not stop at the basin's walls; it drives down through the basalt roots of the shield and radiates out through the old seabed for a thousand kilometers in every direction, so that the entire country lies on a sounding board and the board is always, gently, ringing. The Bed remembers the sea the only way stone can remember water: as a vibration. Everything that walks here walks on the voice of the ocean that was taken from here, conducted home through the mountain that took it.

The land itself is salt. When the pumping neared its end and the last waters lay dying in the deepest trenches, they gave up their burden, and the burden is the country now: evaporite plains hundreds of meters thick, white and grey and rust and rose, crusted, blistered, endlessly rebuilt. For here — third inversion — there is *weather*. The High Sea has no seasons, no erosion, no change; nothing up there has moved in ten thousand years except the water and the living. The Bed does nothing but change. The dense hot air over the brine lakes rises, drags up the mountain's flank, chills, and falls back along the foot of the shield as violent local rain; the rains dissolve the crust and the sun rebuilds it; salt storms walk the plain on legs of wind, burying and exhuming by the season. And over the caldera rim, miles above, spray from the Standing Sea freezes into the thin cold sky and comes down the outer flank as snow, melts, and runs off in mineral threads toward the floor it was lifted from. The sea escapes its basin one snowflake at a time. The Bed is a country assembled fresh every century out of its own ruins, under a sky that is slowly, at the pace of geology, handing the ocean back.

At the foot of the shield stand the intake fields, and they are the heaviest marks the builders left anywhere. Thousands of pipe mouths, each wide enough to swallow ships, ranked across the old abyssal floor in lines that run to the haze in both directions — and behind them the risers, the great conduits, riveted and streaked, climbing the black flank of the mountain in bundles until distance and altitude erase them. Every drop of the High Sea went up through these throats. Most are dry now, dead iron, home to wind. But the sea sits miles above its old bed with all its weight forever leaning downward, and no seal holds forever, and so some of the pipes *weep* — thin permanent falls of High Sea water descending the inside of the risers or streaking the rock beside them, kilometers of glittering thread, arriving at the bottom cold and mineral and alive with dissolved food. Where a pipe weeps, there is an oasis. We will come to the oases.

And out on the plain, far from the mountain, lie the diggings: the reason for all of it. Quarry terraces cut into the naked seabed. Trench-roads, dead straight, converging from every direction across hundreds of kilometers like the spokes of a wheel. Camps, swept bare, doors open, preserved not in cold clarity as above but under shifting purses of salt that bury them and give them back. And at the hub of the converging roads, at the lowest point of the lowest plain of the lowest country in the world, the Cut — a single excavation, rectangular, sheer-sided, going down through the floor of the first ocean into whatever the builders raised a sea to reach. Whether they found it, the land does not say. Whether they left over the old shorelines or down through the Cut itself, the land does not say. The Bed keeps their question open the way it keeps everything: temporarily, and in salt.

# What Lives in the Empty Bed

Begin with this: everything that lives here is a survivor of the draining, or the child of survivors, and the draining was the end of the world. The first ocean's life had half a million years of pumping to retreat before — down the slopes, into the trenches, into the last shrinking waters — and most of it did not make the retreat, and lies now beneath the plains, salted whole. What lives in the Bed today is what fit through the narrowing: the small, the tough, the brine-tolerant, the ones that learned to walk. They are the ancestors. This must be understood before anything else. When the engines drank the old ocean, they drank its life along with its water — films and larvae and eggs, drawn up the intakes by the trillion across the ages of the lifting — and what survived the ascent seeded the High Sea. The grazers on the engine walls, the eyeless rivers, the soft quiet hunters, perhaps even the great listeners themselves: every nation of the Standing Sea is descended from something that still lives, changed, in the Bed, or lies salted under it. The two worlds are one family, split by a pump. Neither knows the other exists. The kinship runs up the pipes all the same.

## The Nations of the Lakes

In the deep trenches, where the last waters died, the brine lakes lie — the final remnants of the first ocean, so saturated now that the water is nearly syrup and the dead float on it and do not sink. Each lake is a nation. Each holds its own descendants of the old sea: blind pale swimmers, kin to the rivers above but slower, denser, built for heavy water; soft drifting things, cousins of the unsounding hunters, hanging in the brine like held breath; film-pastures on the lake floors, fed by brine seeps and the buried warmth of the old vents, the same chemical grass that carpets the engines miles overhead.

But here is what the lakes have that no nation of the High Sea can conceive: their countries *move*. A wet century swells a lake until it overtops its sill and marries the lake in the next trench, and two nations that diverged for a thousand years pour together and become one, and their strangenesses fight and blend. A dry century splits a lake across a rising salt bar, and one people becomes two, and the two begin, immediately, to forget each other. In the Standing Sea, identity is fixed forever — one engine, one nation, one note. In the Bed, identity is weather. The lake peoples are endlessly made and unmade by rain, and their bodies show it: nothing here is as exquisitely specialized as the life above, because nothing here can afford to be. The High Sea perfected its children. The Bed keeps hers rough, and ready, and able to survive the next marriage.

## The Walkers

Out on the open salt live the hinged ones — armored, low, soled in dense pad against the cutting crusts — and anyone who had seen the grazer nations on the engine walls above would know them at once for family. They are the stem the grazers sprang from: the same shell, the same hinge, the same whole-body ear. But where their children above spend forty years reading one machine's voice on one machine's wall, the walkers cross a continent.

They navigate by the hum. Their shells, like their children's, are tuned instruments, but what they listen through is their feet: the ground-tone, the Standing Sea's roar conducted down the shield's roots and out through the seabed, louder toward the mountain, fainter away, textured by the rock it crosses. The whole country is legible to a walker through its soles — direction, distance, the buried architecture of the old floor — and so the walkers are never lost, in a land that redraws itself every season, because the one thing in the Bed that never changes is the voice in the ground. Hold the symmetry a moment, because it is the truest thing about this world: a river of eyeless swimmers five kilometers above and a walker on the salt plain below are, at this instant, navigating by the *same sound* — the one chord, fed by the engines, held by the listeners — one of them hearing it through water, one through stone, neither knowing the other is there. The High Sea's giants hang motionless holding their notes so that the sea's roads stay true; they do not know, cannot know, that they are also holding the roads of a dry country miles beneath their dead machines. Every note a listener holds is a road a walker keeps.

What the walkers eat, they mine. The rains exhume the old dead — the salted falls, the buried film-beds, the whole preserved larder of the drowned world — and the walkers follow the storms the way the rivers above follow the nodal sheets, arriving at each fresh exposure in slow converging lines to rasp ten-thousand-year-old pasture off ten-thousand-year-old bone. The Bed feeds its living on its dead, and the weather is the hand that serves the meal.

## The Weep-Towns

Where a riser pipe leaks, at the foot of the shield, the Bed grows its only crowds, and they are the mirror of the mill towns above — towns built not around warmth in the cold but around water in the dry.

The weep-water arrives from the High Sea carrying the High Sea in solution: dissolved films, mineral food, the finest rain of another world's plenty. Around each fall the life zones itself as strictly as any filter-city above — brine pools ringed in pasture, pasture ringed in walkers, walkers ringed by the soft ambushers that have learned that in the Bed, where everything can feel silence perfectly well, the way to be undetectable is to be *still ground*, matching not the sound of the country but its stillness. A weep-town is the richest place in the Bed and the most temporary: pipes seal themselves with mineral growth, or split wider, or the storms move the salt and strangle the pools, and every town lives with its own end the way the mill towns above live with theirs — written in, unreadable, kind.

But the weep-towns hold one population found nowhere else in either world, and they are the saddest citizens this planet has.

Sometimes the weep-water carries eggs. Flushed from the High Sea through a failing valve, down miles of dark iron, into a brine pool at the bottom of the sky — and some of them, a very few, survive the descent, and hatch. They are river-swimmers. True ones, of the nations above, each born carrying the inborn map — the complete acoustic architecture of the Standing Sea, engine by engine, note by note, the inheritance that up there ignites at the moment a newborn swims out of the quiet halls into the chord and the world becomes legible all at once. Everything in the High Sea is born twice: once into silence, once into the note. The stranded ones are born once. They hatch into quiet, into a pool the size of a room, carrying a country in their heads — and the country never comes.

Except that it almost does. The pipe is there. The iron of the riser carries the roar down its whole length, faint but true, and the stranded ones find it within hours of hatching and never afterward leave it. They live pressed against the metal, whole shoals of them, generation after generation, bodies flat to the pipe, reading through the iron the dim transmission of a sea they were made for — every engine in its place, every note held, the map lighting up at one-thousandth strength, a homeland heard through a wall. And in some of them, in every generation, the instinct that up above sends the gravid swimming toward the quiet places sends them instead *up*: into the weep, against the falling thread, into the pipe itself, climbing kilometers of vertical dark toward the sound. The inside of every weeping riser holds a thin column of small bones, ages deep. Whether any climber has ever reached the top — has ever come out through a split casing into the full chord, the second birth arriving a lifetime late — nothing in either world can say. The pipes keep it, the way the salt keeps everything.

## The Salted Falls

Scattered across the plains, exhumed and reburied by the centuries, lie the great falls: the largest creatures the first ocean made, caught by the draining, cased whole in salt where they came to rest — and their ribs are the length of ships.

The same phrase is used, by this telling, of the bones in the dead engine halls miles above, and it is used deliberately, because they are the same bones. The lineage of the giants did not end in the Bed. Somewhere in the ages of the lifting, its young went up the intakes with everything else, and what that lineage became in the roaring dark of the High Sea is known: it became the listeners. The great ones who hang at the positions of dead engines holding the missing notes — whose chambered bodies are too apt for chance and too organic for manufacture, whom the builders perhaps grew, or perhaps merely found — are the children of the shapes lying salted under the walkers' feet. The ancestors sang in the first ocean; what they sang, no record keeps. The children sing one note each, forever, in the stolen sea, holding the chord that hums down through the mountain and rings the ground above their ancestors' graves. When a walker pauses on the buried curve of a great fall's rib, feet to the salt, reading the ground-tone, it is standing on the grandmother and listening to the grandchild, and it knows neither thing, and the note goes on.

## The Cut

At the hub of the converging roads, at the lowest point of the world, the excavation stands open, and nothing lives in it.

This is stranger than it sounds. Everything else in the Bed is colonized — every pool, every pipe, every buried bone has its tenants; life here refuses nothing. But the Cut goes down bare. Its walls are clean stone past the bottom of the seabed, into rock that no ocean ever touched, and no film grows on them, and no walker descends past the first terrace, and even the salt will not settle there, because the Cut *breathes*: a slow warm updraft, steady as the ground-tone, rising out of the deep and carrying the haze away, so that the one place in the whole misted country where the air is perfectly clear is the shaft of the builders' question. Stand at the rim — nothing does, but stand there — and you could see straight down farther than anywhere in the Bed permits you to see in any other direction, into diminishing clean dark, toward whatever was wanted badly enough that an ocean was picked up and set aside to reach it.

The land keeps two facts about the Cut and no others. The first: the trench-roads run *toward* it loaded — the wear-patterns say so — and away from it empty. Whatever came out of the deep went somewhere, and it did not stay in the Bed. The second: the breath is warm. Something below is still doing something, at the bottom of the only silence in the world with no eggs and no dead in it, and it has been doing it since before the salt.

## Coda

So this is what lives there: nations made and unmade by rain; walkers reading a stolen ocean's voice through their feet; towns gathered at the weeping seams of the world above; the stranded, pressed to the iron, hearing their homeland through a wall; the ancestors in the salt and their singing children in the sky; and at the center, a clean warm question that nothing will inhabit.

There is one more inhabitant, if you are willing to count it. The sea itself. It is not gone — it is *elsewhere*, which is different, and the whole Bed is arranged around the difference. The snow comes over the rim; the pipes weep; the rains run down the flank; thread by thread, at the pace of stone, the ocean is coming home, a lake's worth in a millennium, an ocean's worth in a span of time that has no witnesses. Every trench that will one day drown again, every salt plain lying at the exact level of a future abyssal floor, every walker's road and weep-town and open camp — all of it is temporary in a way the High Sea cannot be, because the High Sea is *held*, note by note, by giants who will not let it change, and the Bed is held by nothing at all. It is not, in the end, a place. It is an interval: the room the sea got up from, kept in disorder, being slowly remade for the sea's return.

The Standing Sea does not change. The Empty Bed does nothing else. Between them stands the mountain, and through the mountain runs the note, and the whole world, if you back far enough away from it, resolves into a single instrument — the raised sea for a string, the shield for a neck, the emptied floor for a sounding body — struck once, before memory, and still ringing: above in the water, below in the stone, and in every living thing on either side, none of whom will ever know they are hearing the same sound.
