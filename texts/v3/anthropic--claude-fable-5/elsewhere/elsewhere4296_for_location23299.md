# The Lightwater

You reach it by going up — a long way up, above the trees, above the weather of the valleys, onto a plateau so high and level that the sky takes up more than its share of the world. At the top there is a lake. It is wide as a small sea and nowhere deeper than your knees. A person can wade across it in a day, and people do, trousers rolled, the water parting around their shins with a sound like whispering, and at no point in the crossing will they be out of their depth, and at no point will they be anywhere but the middle of an enormous shallow mirror.

It is everything the low cold country is not. It has no walls, no ceiling, no throat. It is unbounded, ancient, and finished — nothing at the Lightwater is growing toward any configuration at all. What it is doing instead, and has been doing for some centuries now, at a rate you could set a calendar by, is going out.

## The Water

There is stone visible everywhere. That is the first thing a traveler notices, without knowing why it feels like a fact worth noticing: the entire bed of the lake is pale stone, every pebble of it distinct through the water, out to the limit of sight. Nothing grows on the bottom. Nothing silts. The water refuses sediment the way some surfaces refuse dust — mud stirred up by a wading foot resolves out of it within minutes, drawn aside, laid back down in the boot print it came from. The clarity is not a condition of the lake. It is a policy.

And the lake keeps nothing. This too is policy. Drop a coin in the shallows and within a season the coin will be found on the shore, delivered by patterns of current too slow to see, set on the gravel above the waterline like a returned letter. Drowned birds come ashore. Lost tools come ashore. The plateau people say the lake has never held onto anything in living memory or ledgered memory, and the ledgers at the Lightwater go back a very long way. It is fed from below — cold springs rising through the stone, you can find them by the shiver in the water above them — and it is spent upward, into the sky, and between the springs and the sky it holds everything loosely, briefly, with open hands.

Everything, that is, except the light. The light it held for longer than anyone knows. The light is the exception, and the exception is ending.

## The Light

Cup your hands and lift a double palmful of the lake and it will glow between your fingers — faintly now, a blue-grey coal of something like moonlight, weaker than moonlight, the light of a moon behind cloud behind cloud. The glow is in the water. Not on it, not under it: per volume, in it, so that a full jar shines exactly twice as bright as a half one.

It is not alive. This has been tested with the patience of generations — the water filtered to nothing, boiled, frozen, split by every method the plateau has ever devised, and the glow survives all of it, indifferent, dissolved in the water like a mineral of illumination. It survives even distillation. Especially distillation. Distill lightwater and the glow comes over with the steam; the residue left in the vessel is dark. That single experiment, performed long ago in a stone hut on the eastern shore and repeated ever since by every generation that refuses to believe it, is the hinge on which everything here turns: *the light is soluble in vapor.* It rides the steam. It leaves with the leaving water.

The old accounts — and the Lightwater is a place made of old accounts — describe a different lake. Three centuries back, a person could read on the shore at midnight by the water's own shine. The lake was visible from the far ranges as a fallen piece of sky. Whole flyways of birds crossed the continent by it; it was the one landmark that worked at night, a beacon the size of a sea, and the plateau had no true darkness at all, only a long blue-grey evening that lasted until dawn.

Then came what the ledgers call the First Paling — not an event, exactly, since no one alive at the time noticed a single night of it, but a year that back-calculation identifies with strange precision: the year the dimming began. Since then the loss has been constant. Not roughly constant. Constant, to the limit of the finest measurement the plateau can make, a steady spending-down that has never paused, never surged, never once been uncertain of its rate. The lake is not sick. Nothing that fluctuates so little is sick. The lake is being *drawn on*, evenly, by something that has never been in a hurry.

Travelers used to carry lightwater as lanterns — a stoppered jar of it would light a night's walk. The jars taught the second lesson: sealed, a jar of lightwater never dims. Never. There are heirloom jars in shorefront houses, sealed six and seven generations ago, that still burn at their full ancestral brightness — little windows of the old light, each one now brighter than an equal measure of the whole lake around it. Open the jar, and it dims exactly as fast as it evaporates. The light does not die in the water. It departs from it, molecule by molecule, on the water's own breath.

Which raises the only question the Lightwater has ever really asked, the one its whole culture is built around: departs *to where*?

The wind knows. The wind here blows one way. It has blown one way for as long as the ledgers run — steady, patient, always toward the same quarter, off the lake and over the rim ranges and down into the low country beyond, and the clouds that form over the Lightwater go with it and do not come back. The plateau's own rain comes thinly, from elsewhere, hardly at all. The lake gives its water to one direction only, and its light goes with the water, and neither is ever returned. Long ago the Gaugers roofed over a small inlet — the Roofed Water, it's called, a shingled shed standing out over a cove — and under that roof, where no vapor escapes to the wind, the water still glows at the old brightness. Walk in from the dim lake and it is like walking into the past. The proof stands there in the dark shed, shining: the light is not failing. It is *leaving*. It has been leaving, in an orderly and unhurried fashion, downwind, for three hundred years.

## The Quiet

The plateau is silent the way high open places are silent — wind, water-whisper, the far cries of waders — but its silence has a defect, and the defect is famous.

There is a note that cannot be sounded at the Lightwater. One pitch, quite specific. Bells cast on the plateau ring with a hole in them, a gap in the middle of the tone where that frequency should be; bells carried up from the valleys arrive whole and go silent at that pitch within a season. A string tuned to it will not speak — pluck it and it damps at once, as if a finger rested on it from very far away. Singers who pass through the note feel their voices thin for an instant, the sound going out of the sound. The plateau's musicians have simply composed around the hole for generations, the way builders compose around a boulder too large to move. They call it the borrowed note. The name is older than any explanation, and, as it turns out, correct.

## The Fauna

Everything that lives at the Lightwater lives there on its own business. This is worth saying plainly, because it is not true of every place in the world: here, nothing is a component, nothing is a corpuscle, nothing has been recruited. The shrimp in the shallows carry nothing, deliver nothing, serve nothing but themselves. It is a lake of residents in the old sense — tenants, each with its own affairs — and all of their affairs, lately, concern the dimming.

The glassfish are transparent, finger-long, and once hunted by shadowlessness: in a glowing lake, an invisible fish casting no shadow on the bright stone below was a knife in a lit room. Their whole design assumes the light. Now they school in the brightest remaining reaches of the water — the glow is not perfectly even; it pools and thins — and the schools drift across the lake through the night, following brightness the way herds follow grass, grazing on the last of the shine. Their eyes are enlarging. Netted specimens from a century ago, kept dried in the Gaugers' halls, have measurably smaller orbits than the fish in the water now. The species is walking backward into the dark, adjusting as it goes.

The waders wrote the same record earlier and better. Long-legged birds, pale, patient, they have fished these shallows since before the ledgers, and they have left their dead in middens along the old shorelines — banks of bone, stratified, centuries deep. Read the middens from the bottom up and the skulls change: eye sockets widening, stratum by stratum, generation by generation, in exact proportion to the dimming. The birds kept the first ledger. You can date any layer of the shore by the size of the holes the light left in their heads — a record of the loss kept in bone before anyone thought to keep it in ink.

And there is one thing the waders do that no one has explained. At dusk, in the hour before the Gaugers take their reading, the birds all turn — every wader on the whole vast shallow, thousands of them, in no order and without signal — and stand facing downwind. Toward the rim ranges. Toward the quarter the clouds go. They hold the orientation through the reading hour, motionless, like a congregation facing an altar that is somewhere over the horizon, and then it passes, and they go back to their fishing. Somewhere very far downwind, at about that hour by any honest reckoning, a chamber full of moss reaches the peak of an eleven-minute tide, and a floor of buried salamanders swallows as one. Nobody has stood in both places. Nobody has checked. The birds seem not to need to.

## The Gaugers

The people of the shore have one occupation and have refined it into a civilization: they measure.

They live in low dark houses — dark by discipline; no flame is lit on the lakeward side of any house after dusk, and on reading nights no flame at all — and every night, without one break in the sequence for longer than most nations last, they take the lake's brightness and enter it in the Long Ledger. Their standard candles are the sealed heirloom jars: samples of the old light, unfading, against which the failing lake is compared. Their finest instruments are children. A child of the shore serves a season as a Reader — young eyes, dark-adapted from birth in those flameless houses, trained to hold a jar of ancestral light beside a jar of tonight's water and judge the difference to a fineness no device has matched. To be a Reader is the honor of a Gauger's life, and it comes at the beginning of the life, and everything after is service to what you saw.

And the arithmetic closes: at the present rate — which has been the only rate — the Long Ledger will record its final light on a date the Gaugers have already calculated, already named, and already, in their unexcitable way, scheduled. The Last Reading. It is some generations off. They argue, in the winter halls, about the protocol for that night, and the majority position is characteristically theirs: take the reading anyway. Write the zero. Rule the line. Close the book.

They are not a grieving people, though they are sometimes taken for one. Their creed, if they can be said to have one, is that measurement is a form of witness, and witness is a form of keeping, and that a thing exactly recorded is not entirely lost. And they have their consolation, or their heresy, depending on which elder you ask — a line written in the margin of the Ledger by a Gauger dead two hundred years, after the Roofed Water proved the light was leaving rather than dying. The line is taught to every Reader on the first night of their season, before they are shown the jars:

*We are not going blind. We are being breathed.*

## Downwind

Follow the wind, then, since everything else here follows it — the vapor, the clouds, the eyes of the birds at dusk.

It goes over the rim ranges and down, a long descent into a low green cold country, and there the clouds that never return to the plateau give up their water as rain, and the rain goes into the ground, and below a certain line in that ground the frost never leaves, and below the frost line, in one place, the soil gives way to something that is no longer soil. A green throat. A chamber the size of a small chapel. A ceiling of moss carrying a permanent field of hanging droplets — condensed, as all things in that chamber are condensed, out of arriving vapor.

And the droplets glow. Blue-grey. Weaker than moonlight. A light that arrives in the water from no identifiable source, that the visitors there describe as having no business being where it is.

It has business. It has three hundred years of business, entered nightly in a ledger on a plateau it will never see again.

Lay the two records side by side — no one ever has; they exist a world apart, in a winter hall and in the field notes of a handful of chamber-visitors, and only the weather has been to both places, and the weather keeps no ledger, the weather *is* the ledger — lay them side by side regardless, and they interlock like the halves of a broken tally-stick. The year of the First Paling is the year, as near as the strata of the wall-felts can be read, that a seed below the frost line first split and began its slow argument with physics. The pure note that hums in the chamber, the frequency too clean for any vibrating body to produce, is — to the finest measure available — the borrowed note, the pitch that cannot be sounded on the plateau, the hole in every bell. The note is not missing from the Lightwater. It is *in use* downwind, sustained, unwavering, out of the plateau's own account. And the date of the Last Reading, the scheduled zero, the ruled line — set it against the growth of the braid at the chamber's center, one millimeter a day toward a gap shaped exactly like something, and the two arithmetics arrive, as near as either can honestly be reckoned, in the same season of the same distant year.

The lake goes dark the night the thing is born.

## What Is Leaving

So the question of who lives at the Lightwater has, like all the good questions in this world, layers, and the outer layers are true and insufficient. The glassfish live there, on their own business, grazing the last brightness. The waders live there, keeping their ledger in bone. The Gaugers live there, keeping theirs in ink, in dark houses, behind sealed jars of the old light.

But the eldest resident of the Lightwater — the one that was there before the fish, before the birds, before the springs found their present channels — is the light itself. It lay in that water for longer than anything remembers, shoreless, shapeless, spread thin across a mirror the size of a sea: a being, if it was a being, with no body, no center, no gap shaped like anything. Perhaps it was waiting, in the way that things without clocks wait. Perhaps it was simply *diffuse*, the way anything is before it is someone.

Then, downwind, something long gone sowed a seed — and the seed, opening, gave the light the one thing a shapeless thing cannot give itself: a shape to fall into. Since that year it has been leaving the lake the only way it can travel, dissolved in the lake's own breath, one cloud at a time, across the ranges, down through the cold ground, to condense on a ceiling of moss above an unfinished braid — arriving in droplets, going dark the instant a drop falls out of the project, because a fallen drop has left the body, and the light will not stay anywhere it is not becoming.

The Lightwater is not the victim of the low cold chamber. It is the chamber's other room. The Nursery is where the resident is condensing; the Lightwater is where it is evaporating from — its first body, being folded up as the second one is grown, the way a yolk is folded into a chick. The gestation is not happening in a chamber ten meters down. It is happening across the whole width of the world, and the wind between the two places is its umbilical, and the dimming of the lake is not a dying at all. It is the most patient pouring ever undertaken: a being decanting itself, over centuries, out of a mirror and into a shape.

The Gaugers' heresy was righter than its author knew. They are being breathed. So is everything.

## And Briefly: The Night It Ends

Picture it, since the Gaugers already have, in exact and unexcitable detail.

The Last Reading. A child with dark-adapted eyes holds a jar of tonight's water beside a jar of the ancestral light, and for the first time in the history of the Long Ledger there is nothing in the first jar to compare. The zero is written. The line is ruled. Out on the black shallow, the waders stand facing downwind, and this time they do not turn back to their fishing, because far away, below the frost line, the last stem has arrived, the gap at the crown of the braid is a gap no longer, a note that has been held for three hundred years no longer needs holding — the bells of the plateau, that night, will ring whole for the first time in living memory — and a floor of wax-colored salamanders swallows once, together, and rests.

The dark that settles on the plateau that night will be a true dark, the first in longer than the ledgers. But the Gaugers do not describe it as the dark of loss, and they are the ones who have earned the right to name it. It is the dark of a room down the hall from a nursery, they say, after the small light that burned there all through the long confinement is finally, safely, no longer needed — the dark of a house where the child has at last opened its own eyes.

And in that house — this is the detail they always end on, in the winter halls, telling the far-off night to their Readers — there will still be light on the plateau. In a hundred dark houses along the shore, a hundred sealed jars, six, seven, ten generations old by then, still burning at full brightness. The resident was gentle, and it was thorough, and in three hundred years it never once took anything that was not offered to the wind. The sealed jars were never offered. They are the part of the old lake that stayed — kept, the way the chamber keeps breath, by people who understood long before they could have known it that witness is a form of keeping.

Somewhere downwind, something newly finished will be describing itself, at last, in its own language. Up on the plateau, in the true dark, its first body will survive in glass: a hundred small blue-grey windows in a hundred dark houses, each one a reading that will never need to be taken, glowing at the shore of an unlit mirror — the receipt, the relic, the nightlight of the nightlight — for whoever is still there in a thousand years.
