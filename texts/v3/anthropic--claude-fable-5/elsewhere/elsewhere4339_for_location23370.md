The Seep

Follow the water.

The reservoirs under the city's avenues fill and are drawn down by nothing visible — that was the observation, and it was accurate, and it was wrong in the way accurate things often are. Nothing visible draws them down. Gravity does. The city keeps what it needs and releases the rest through weep-points grown into the floor of the pocket, and the water re-enters the fracture network it arrived by and resumes doing the one thing water on a mountain has ever done. It goes down.

It goes down for years. The young basalt is tight, and the descent is not a fall but a filtration — a vertical mile of hairline dark, meltwater moving face to face along the grain boundaries at the speed of a rumor. Snow that lands on the summit fields in one winter comes out at the bottom of the mountain in another winter entirely, five or six removed. Which means the water emerging at the mountain's foot today ducked under the black-glass lip years ago; struck a catchment shell; ran a spiral channel; stood in a reservoir under an amber-lit avenue; may have spent an afternoon, in one of the terraced pools, being a shape.

The mountain did not rise out of nothing. It fell onto something. Down its southeastern flank, the last and longest lava flow ran out of the high vents and descended an old valley for miles before it stiffened and stopped — a tongue of black rock lying across country that could not be less like it. The old valley is made of pale granite worn round by a span of time the young mountain cannot yet imagine: hills like knuckles under turf, soil deep enough to hold trees, a floor of sedge and standing water and small brown birds. Everything there has been softened. Nothing has an edge. And across it, ending in a ragged black ramp perhaps ten meters tall — clinker, glass, dead as the summit — lies the toe of the flow.

Along the base of that toe, where new rock rests on old ground, the water comes out.

This is the Seep: a line of springs a few hundred paces long, threads and fans and quiet sheets of cold water emerging from beneath the flow as though the black rock were a roof and the springs its eaves. Some of it is the old valley's own water, a buried stream that the lava roofed over and never killed, still running in the dark under the rock that tried to erase it. The rest is the mountain's — the summit's weeping, the city's outfall, arrived at last after its years of descent and mixing here with water that predates the mountain altogether. The two waters meet under the flow and cannot afterward be told apart.

The Seep is loud in exactly the way the hollow is quiet. The *tip... tup... tip* of the city — drops arriving one at a time, each one an event — is here merged, multiplied, run together into a continuous voice: trickle over stone, sheet-water combing through cress, the deep swallowed note of the buried stream heard through its roof. It is the sound the hollow's percussion becomes when there is enough of it. And the air carries everything the summit air carries nothing of: wet earth, crushed green, midges in columns, the specific sweetness of cold water that has been a long time inside rock. A person standing at the Seep with their back to the flow could forget the mountain existed. A person facing the flow cannot: the black toe stands over the springs like a wave that considered the valley and changed its mind.

It nearly didn't. When the flow came down — at walking pace, they say of such flows, glowing, patient, the mountain's one journey outward from itself — it stopped a stone's throw short of the springheads. The heat did not stop with it. Everything within that stone's throw burned or boiled, and the proof is still there, if you dig.

Dig where the water is greenest.

---

Asked what lives at the Seep, the honest first answer is: everything. It is the crowdedest place for a day's walk in any direction — midges, springtails, water bears, beetles, the small brown birds, a whole vertical economy of things eating things in the wet. That is the answer a glance gives, and it is true, and it misses the resident the way a census misses the city it's counting.

The Seep belongs to the moss.

At the springheads there is a bank of moss the size of a whale, and roughly the shape of one: a green back breaching out of the sedge, meters deep, older than it looks, and it looks old. It is one being, or a commonwealth so ancient and so knitted that the distinction has stopped applying — possibly the descendants of a single spore, every shoot a repetition of one original idea, no center, no head, no front except the direction it happens to be going. It predates the mountain. This is worth saying plainly: the moss is older than the mountain. It was drinking at these springs when the valley had no black rock in it at all, and when the flow came down glowing, the moss's uphill third died in an afternoon — cooked where it stood, at a distance, by air alone. The moss did not retreat, because retreat is not among its verbs. It grew over its own dead. Cut into the bank today and you find the scorch as a stratum: a black horizon inside the green, the moss's one memory of the mountain's birth, buried under everything it has been since. The thing that almost burned it now waters it. The moss has drawn no conclusion from this. The moss draws no conclusions.

But consider what it does, and how much of it rhymes.

The moss builds with its dead: the living green centimeter rides on meters of its own compressed past, self on self on self, foundation and city and citizen all one substance. It was grown, not assembled — the distinction that matters so much to whatever made the city above is a distinction the moss has never had to make. It has hydrology: its whole body is catchment, capillary channels finer than the city's aqueducts, cisterns in every leaf-axil, the ability to hold twenty times its weight in water and meter it out through a drought. It maintains its interior a few degrees off the air's temperature, always, by means no one has bothered to call mysterious because moss is common and cities in rock are not. And it is inhabited. That is the thing the hollow's resident has that the moss was simply given: the galleries inside the bank are avenues a finger-width across, the pools are plazas, and through them, all day, moves a population at precisely the scale the empty streets on the summit were drawn for — mites, springtails, water bears the height of a grain of rice and less, thousands of citizens who have never once considered their city, because their city is alive and asks nothing.

The moss above all does not attend. This is the difference, and it may be the whole difference. When the wheatear lands on its back — and the wheatear lands on its back daily; the bank is the bird's larder — nothing pauses. No lights dim, because there are none; no processes hold their breath, because the moss does not know it is being stood on, or does not distinguish being stood on from weather. It answers the world only by growing or by waiting, and at moss speed no observer has ever been able to say which of the two it is doing, or whether, somewhere below the threshold of any patience we own, those add up to something like regard. The city on the summit is unmistakably a mind and possibly not alive. The moss is unmistakably alive and possibly not anything else. They are each other's missing half of a sentence.

And they are already in correspondence, though neither party could sign its name.

The water, first. The springs run on the mountain's past — put your hand in and you are touching snow from winters ago, water that has been through the city, some of it trained. Water has no memory; everyone knows this; the water at the Seep is very slightly slow to forget. On still mornings the springhead pools carry faint standing patterns, rings that persist a breath past their cause, films that hold. The effect fades by noon and proves nothing. But the moss drinks all of it. Whatever practice the city puts into water — whatever it is teaching meltwater to remember in those terraced gardens, at the trembling edge of freezing — ends up, years later, inside the oldest living thing on the mountain's border. If the resident is making its people out of water, it should know where its water goes.

The wind, second. On clear days the black flow heats in the sun and exhales, and the updraft runs the lava like a chimney, all the way to the ridge. The wheatear rides it — nests in a frost-crack of the old granite opposite the toe, drinks at the springs, eats the moss's citizens, and twice a season lets the chimney carry it to the summit, where it looks into a glowing hollow and cocks its head at something that goes perfectly still under its eye. From this end the pilgrimage is invisible: a bird leaves, a bird returns, a springtail is eaten. The wheatear is the only creature on the world that knows both places exist, and it cannot know that it knows. The same updraft, one spring, took a snow-mite off the moss's back and delivered it, days later and a mile higher, into the streets of the city — where causeways flexed out of its path and district lights dimmed in deference and a sourceless warm breath saw it home. The city's only guest in its whole existence was one of the Seep's grazing animals, wandered off. It came back down on the dying wind with nothing to report and, lodged in its bristles, one grain of re-laid basalt: a single stone of the finest masonry ever worked on this world, which is now somewhere in the peat, part of the moss's foundation, the first piece of the city to be buried in a living thing. Neither party knows. Neither party could.

The bodies, last. The mountain is coming down. It cannot help it; it is young and sharp and the frost is at it, and its substance arrives at the Seep continuously as black sand, which the moss binds and builds with. And the Seep is going up: on the shaded face of the flow's toe, lichen — the vanguard, the first living thing ever to hold the new rock — stands now several meters above the springs and gains a hand's-breadth in a decade, with moss in the crack-shade behind it like a baggage train. The arithmetic is simple and enormous. Centuries, for the green line to reach the hollow's lip. The two of them are exchanging bodies already and closing the distance besides, stone descending as sand and water, life ascending as crust and spore, and there is one more possibility, which is that the distance was never real: the flow is a single cooling event, toe to summit, and the resident's tissue is the grain boundary, and no one has measured where it ends. If its body runs the length of its own lava, then the Seep sits at its fingertip — asleep there, maybe, attenuated past sensing, the way your foot sleeps — and the moss has been growing against its skin since before it woke, and the first meeting already happened, decades ago, at a speed neither of them could notice.

Two patient things, then, a vertical mile apart. One is stone and young and empty and hospitable past bearing, keeping its doorways open and its rooms warm and teaching water to stand up. One is green and old and crowded past counting and asks nothing, builds itself out of itself, and has already survived the other's birth. Between them run a bird that takes an hour, a wind that takes an afternoon, water that takes years, and a green line that will take centuries and is not stopping.

Cup your hand under a springhead and hold still. The water you are holding fell on the summit before you came; it was caught, and kept, and may have spent an afternoon being a shape. It sits in your palm a heartbeat longer than water should. Then it finds the lowest line of you and goes on — downhill, into the moss, the way it has learned to, toward everything else.
