# The Sere

There is rock here, and you may take nothing on faith, because all of it is in view. It runs naked to the horizon in every direction but one — long red-grey reaches of it, wind-planed, star-cracked, the same continent of stone that lies buried under a kilometer of living matter on the far side of the Wall. That is the first thing to understand about the Sere: it is not another land. It is the same land, the other half of the one plateau-bearing continent, and the border between the clothed half and the stripped half is a single cliff.

The clouds come from the west. They have come from the west for as long as the wind has blown, and the Verdancy stands in their path and drags its high silvered swells through their bellies and drinks them empty. What crosses the rim is air with nothing left in it. So the Sere is dry not by accident of geography but by the specific, continuous act of its neighbor — every day of drought here is a day of weeping there, the same water, spoken for. The garden does not know it casts a shadow. The shadow is a country.

## The Wall

From the Sere side, the escarpment is the only vertical fact in the world, and you can see it from three days out: a blue-grey rampart a kilometer and more high, running north and south past sight, its face carrying the horizontal banding of honest stone — the layered record of the continent's youth, exposed here and nowhere else, because everywhere else the Verdancy lies on it like a held breath.

At the top of the Wall the stone stops and the mat begins, and the mat does not stop where the stone does. It creeps out over the rim in a thick green cornice, a lip of living matter overhanging the drop, pushing slowly into air that will not sustain it. The dry wind kills the leading edge as fast as it advances, and so the lip is green above and grey beneath, a fringe of dead growth hanging down the upper cliff like weather-stained thatch, and at long intervals a mass of it lets go and falls, turning, for most of a minute, and bursts on the talus below.

This is worth pausing on, because it is the exception to the deepest rule of the high country. The Verdancy does not discard — except here. Gravity discards for it. The rim is the one seam where the finished world still sheds, and the Sere is what receives the shedding: a talus apron of dried mat-flesh runs the whole foot of the Wall, milled by the wind into fiber and dust, the only midden a deathless place can have.

And once a day, the Wall weeps. At dawn, before the descending wind has warmed, the green lip releases the night's condensation all along its length — a brief silver dripline, kilometers long, pattering onto the talus for less than an hour before the air takes it back. In the Sere they call it the Weeping Hour, and half the animals of the borderlands order their whole lives around it, walking to the Wall in the dark to drink what the garden cannot help giving away.

## The Wind

The wind is the Verdancy's breath, and the Sere lives inside the exhale.

Air rides up the western slopes, gives its water to the grazing clouds, spills over the rim, and pours down the Wall — compressing as it falls, warming as it compresses, arriving at the bottom hot, dry, and constant. It has blown the same direction for so long that it has done to the Sere what the Verdancy's winds did to the high swells: made itself part of the architecture. Every stone is planed on its windward face and hollowed on its lee. Every living thing is streamlined or anchored or both. The sound of the Sere is this wind, low and continuous, the way the sound of the Verdancy is dripping, and travelers here learn the mirrored lesson — sleep through the wind, wake at true stillness, because the wind stops only for one reason, and that reason comes twice in a lifetime.

The wind carries freight. Off the high swells it lifts the light dry produce of the plateau — spore, husk, winged seed, the powdered residue of the milled cornice-fall — and hauls it over the edge and lets it down across the leeward country in a thin perpetual sift. It is invisible in the air. It is not invisible on the ground.

## The Drifts

Because the Sere has dunes, and the dunes are not sand.

They lie in the wind-shadow of every ridge and swale, long grey-green drifts, soft-crested, faintly sweet-smelling after cold nights, and they are made of seed. Millennia of the sift, accumulated. Spore and germ and embryo in numbers that mock counting, all of it viable — the dry air that killed the cornice preserves the seed perfectly — and none of it able to sprout, because sprouting takes water, and the water is spoken for, forever, on the other side of the Wall.

Scoop up a handful and you are holding a forest that never happened. The drifts are the Verdancy in potential — the whole roaring fecundity of the plateau, exported grain by grain and stopped dead, a landscape-sized library of postponed green. If the Verdancy is life at its slowest speed, the Sere's drifts are life at no speed at all: pure waiting, banked against a rain that almost never comes.

Almost.

## The City

It stands two days' walk from the Wall, at the head of a system of carved channels that run back toward the cliff and end against blank stone — and it is the second thing the Sere shares with the Verdancy, and the more intimate one.

Whoever built the copied doorways on the high swells, the ring of pillars, the staircase flowering along its risers — they built here too. But the Sere does not digest and reissue. The Sere preserves by refusal, and so the city is real: real quarried blocks, real streets, real cisterns with real plumb-line interiors, and everywhere, on every dressed face, the thing the Verdancy could not keep — tool-marks. The chisel-strokes are crisp enough to take a fingernail. Up on the plateau the land kept the gesture and lost the substance; down here the land kept the substance entire, every stroke of it, and what was lost instead was the water, and with the water, for a long time, the point.

The channels tell the story plainly. They were cut to receive the plateau's leeward springs — in the deep past, before the mat completed itself, the high country's water drained down the Wall in falls and seeps, and the city drank at the bottom of a green cataract. Then the Verdancy closed. Not maliciously; it does not have malice, or anything else that fast. Its circulation simply perfected itself, loop by loop, over centuries — water rising as mist, falling as rain, never leaving — and the leeward springs thinned, and faltered, and sealed, and the channels ran dry from the top down like a body going numb from the extremities. The city was not destroyed. It was answered, very slowly, with completeness.

And the staircase. In the Verdancy there is a flight of seven steps rising out of the deep meadow and ending in air, copied in living grain, flowering in the wet season — a fragment the mat preserved without context. The context is here. Carved into the face of the Wall, switchbacking up the stone in flights of real steps with real chisel-work, runs the Stair, the old road between the halves of the country, a kilometer of it still sound. It climbs to within a few dozen meters of the rim, and there it vanishes under the green cornice, into the body of the mat — and its uppermost flight, swallowed and digested and reissued, is the seven steps in the meadow. One staircase. The bottom half stone and marked and dead. The top half flesh and smooth and blooming. The Wall between them is where the world changes its mind about what to keep.

## The Nights

The Sere has one wealth the Verdancy cannot imagine, and it comes out after dark.

No canopy. No mist. The heat leaves the stone at sunset in a single long shudder, the wind drops toward gentleness, and the sky opens to a depth that no living thing under the green vaults has ever seen — the stars in their tens of thousands, the slow wheel of them, the whole cold outward-facing side of existence. On the plateau the light is directionless and the distances are all touch. Here the light has sources you could navigate a lifetime by, and the distances are absolute. You can extend an arm in the Sere and touch nothing at all, and this too is a kind of country.

The cold pulls dew. On the drifts, on the lee of stones, on the carapaces of everything built for it, the night squeezes a few grams of water out of the ungiving air, and that dew is the Sere's entire domestic economy. Everything alive here is, one way or another, a machine for harvesting the small hours.

# Those Who Live in the Sere

Begin, again, with the rule, because it is the exact inverse of the rule across the Wall. In the Verdancy nothing lives on the land; everything lives partway into it, and the deepest question is one of depth. In the Sere nothing can afford to merge with anything. Water is too scarce to share a boundary. Every living thing here is sealed, discrete, portable — waxed, shelled, cased, skinned tight — a closed vessel of moisture moving over an open waste. The Verdancy's theology is absorption. The Sere's is the sanctity of the outline. To live here is to remain, at all costs, distinct.

## The Dawn Harvest

The commonest creatures of the Sere are the dew-smiths: beetle-like, glass-shelled, the size of thumbs, whose carapaces are ridged and channeled with a precision the city's masons would have recognized. In the last cold hour before light they climb to the crests of stones and stand facing the wind, and the night's moisture condenses along their shell-channels and runs down grooves that end at their mouths. They drink their own architecture. Then the sun comes, and they seal — every joint gasketed, every pore shut — and become, for the day, indistinguishable from gravel. A dew-smith is a closed system with a schedule. It is the Sere's whole philosophy at thumb scale.

On the shadowed north faces of the standing stones grows the First Flora: lichens, black and rust and bone-white, in colonies of enormous age. They are the other survivors of the deep past — the original vegetation of the continent, the modest crust that clothed the rock before the Verdancy's ancestors arrived and rose and won. On the wet side they were overgrown an age ago; nothing of them remains under the mat but, perhaps, a chemistry. Here, in the shadow the victor casts, they persist — slow, but slow in the Sere's way, not the Verdancy's: they do not merge with the stone, they hold their line against it, centimeter by century. The rain shadow is a museum of the world before the garden, and the lichens are its oldest exhibits, still alive, still refusing.

## The Sifters and the Swift

The drifts feed a fauna of their own. Chief among them are the sifters — tall, long-legged striders, wind-lean, who walk the seed-dunes in loose files with their heads down, combing the drift through baleen-fringed bills. Their gizzards are mills. Each sifter grinds, in the course of a year, some millions of postponed forests, and it is hard to stand near a feeding file without feeling the strangeness of it: creatures grazing on futures, digesting whole canopies that were never going to happen anyway. The drifts do not diminish. The sift from the rim outpaces every appetite.

And the sifters are hunted — which is the sharpest difference between the halves of the continent, sharper than wet and dry. There is a pursuit predator in the Sere, a low russet courser built entirely of urgency, and when it comes over a ridge the sifter files *scatter*, wide-winged and wailing, and the chase runs flat-out across the pans in a way nothing on the plateau has done for ten thousand years. Fear lives here. Flight lives here. Things end here, suddenly, at speed, under an open sky — and the Sere's people, who have watched both countries, will tell you this is not the lesser condition. It is the cost of remaining distinct. Over the Wall, nothing flees because nothing is separate enough to be caught.

## The Quick

The builders did not choose once. They chose twice.

When the springs sealed and the channels went numb, the city's people divided along the only line that mattered: tempo. Some climbed the Stair a final time and lay down in the moss of the dim hollows and let the great slowness take them into its calendar — a breath a day, a step a season. Those are the sleepers, the tall silvered forms mid-stride on the high swells, the warmth in the kept beds. The others looked at the drying channels and the open leeward horizon and refused. They kept their pace, and their hunger, and their deaths, and they walked down into the shadow their garden had made and learned to live on dew and drift and dawn.

Their descendants call themselves, simply, the people. The old accounts, translating from whatever the sleepers might say if a sentence of theirs could be waited out, call them the Quick.

They are not many, and they are brief, and they have made brevity the organizing principle of a civilization. Because they cannot persist, they inscribe. The city — their city still; they never left it — is the driest archive in the world, and every generation adds to it: histories, genealogies, water-tallies, songs, the Bloom-records, cut into stone that the Sere will keep crisp past all reckoning. Where the Verdancy remembers by shape and forgets the meaning, the Quick carve meaning into a land that will forget nothing. Their masons still use the old strokes. A child of the Quick can lay a thumbnail in a chisel-mark three hundred generations old and then in one made yesterday and feel no difference, and this is the closest thing they have to what the sleepers have: continuity, achieved not by slowing the self but by relaying it.

They farm dew on stone-field condensers descended from the dew-smiths' shells — they will tell you frankly that they learned it from the beetles. They walk to the Wall for the Weeping Hour in the hard years. They cull the sifter files and tend the First Flora, keeping the ancient lichens clear, because someone should, and no one else on the continent is awake at the necessary speed.

And they keep two doors open to their kin.

The first is the Stair. When one of the Quick grows old and feels the wanting — not everyone does; most die in the city, distinctly, under the stars, and are laid in dry-stone cairns with their names cut deep — but when one feels it, they climb. Up the thousand marked steps, through the dead grey fringe, into the green lip of the mat, and over, into the warm dripping dimness their ancestors refused, and they walk the old routes to a hollow where a body-shaped bed lies open in the moss, kept open, faintly warm, waiting — because the beds have always been waiting for exactly this. Some of the compressions in the kept moss, some of the deepened warmths, are not sleepers between wakings. They are new arrivals: Quick who chose, at the very end, to slow. The Verdancy holds those beds open against all its habits of closure because its people are still coming home, one every few generations, up the half-stone, half-living stair.

The second door is the Vigil. Where the Stair meets the cornice, the Quick maintain a watch-post, and there has been a watcher in it, in unbroken relay, since the parting. The duty is mostly silence and wind and the dawn dripline. But three times in the carved annals, a silvered figure has come to stand at the rim above — a sleeper, mid-waking, arrived after a walk of decades at the one place where the two tempos face each other. The third time, the records say, the figure began to raise a hand. The gesture took forty-one years. The watcher who first saw it lift reported it, and grew old, and trained a successor to the watching of it, who trained another, and the greeting was finally received — hand at full extension, unmistakable, a wave — by a woman whose grandmother had been born after it began. She returned it in half a second. Whether her half-second registered above, as more than a flicker, as more than a drip among drips, the annals do not speculate. They record only that she made it, and that the Vigil is still kept, in case.

## The Bloom

Twice, perhaps three times in a long human life, the wind stops.

It stops because the Verdancy, far above, is holding its whole water at once — the true silence of the high country, the stilled dripping, the falling three-note call relayed in rings from flock to flock. On the great occasions, the rare vast western storms too heavy for even the plateau to drink, that held silence ends not in mist but in overspill: the excess comes across the rim in grey sheets, down the Wall in cataracts along the old dead spring-lines, and out over the Sere as rain.

The watcher at the Vigil hears the three-note call come faint through the cornice — the Quick know exactly what it announces, even if the flocks no longer do — and sounds the long horn down the Stair, and the city rings its dry cisterns like bells, and everyone alive goes out to stand in it.

And the drifts wake. Every dune, every wind-shadow bank of postponed forest, sprouts at once. Within days the Sere is green from the Wall to the horizon — a single rushing generation of the Verdancy's exported children, rising, unfurling, flowering in desperate compression, the whole plateau's patience translated into one season of absolute hurry. It is the most beautiful thing the Sere does and the Quick meet it in both of the ways it deserves. They feast — a Bloom feeds the city for years, and every child born in a green season carries a Bloom-name, and the carved records of each Bloom run longer than the records of decades. And they cut the firebreaks: wide cleared rings around the city, around the lichen fields of the First Flora, around the cairns — because they understand precisely what a Bloom is. It is the garden trying to cross. If ever the rains came twice — if the green generation set seed into wet ground — the mat would begin here too, and close, and complete itself, and the last waking half of the continent would go under the long warm sleep. So they let the Bloom live its one season, and they hold their lines, and when the second rain does not come — it has never come — the green dies standing, to the horizon, and the wind returns and mills it back into drift, richer than before.

The Quick grieve it every time, the records say. Every time, they also check the firebreaks. Both of these are sincere. They are the custodians of the unfinished, the keepers of the world's remaining openness, and no one becomes a custodian of openness without loving, a little, the thing being kept out.

## What It Means to Live Here

So the honest census runs: sealed glass drinkers of the small hours; the last crust of the world before the garden, holding its outline on the shadowed stone; grazers of futures and the fear that keeps them distinct; and a people who answered the same closing door as their kin and chose the other side of it — brevity, inscription, stars, the relay of meaning hand to hand at speed, and two doors kept open in the Wall for love's sake.

None of them are of the Sere, exactly, the way the Verdancy's creatures are of the Verdancy. That is the point of them. They are *in* it, wholly and separately, each one a closed vessel of warmth and water crossing the open stone, and when they end they end as themselves, with edges, under a sky that shows them how far away everything truly is.

It is one continent. One people, one staircase, one water, endlessly spoken for. On the west side of the Wall the land has finished becoming and holds its people inside it like a long warm thought. On the east side the land never got to become at all, and its people carve their names into that fact and die distinct and are remembered at speed. The oldest resident of the Sere is the stone itself, patient in the only way stone knows. The newest is whichever child was born this morning in the dry city, who will be given the three things the far side of the Wall no longer stocks: a name, a chisel, and a death — and who will grow up within sight of the green lip at the rim, where the finished world leans out over the unfinished one, weeping a little every dawn, as if it remembers what it drank.
