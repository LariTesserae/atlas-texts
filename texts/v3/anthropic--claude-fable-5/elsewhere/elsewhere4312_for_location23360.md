**The Salt Shore**

The cloud that climbs the mountain's western face every afternoon is not weather. It is a lake, leaving.

Follow it backward — over the saddle's lip, down the three thousand feet of cliff that no trail has ever crossed, the cliff where the chough keeps its home — and at the bottom the mountain stops abruptly at water. The water is a lake the size of a small sea and the depth of a standing child. It fills the western valley from wall to wall, it keeps no level — swelling in wet decades, withdrawing in dry ones, breathing across years the way a sleeper breathes across minutes — and in the dry seasons it turns pink, densely, unapologetically pink, from shoreline to horizon, as if the valley held not water but the light of a permanent dawn. Along its eastern edge, where the mountain's shadow arrives early, there is a town.

If the saddle town is the size of a single thought, this town is the size of a conversation — long, loose, repetitive, strung out along the shore for the better part of a mile, saying the same things over and over in slightly different ways. House, drying-rack, house, salt-stack, house. It has no walls and no center. It has, instead, a direction: everything faces the water, and everything leans slightly away from it, and the whole settlement has the posture of a crowd standing at the edge of something, watching.

The first strange thing about the shore town is that nothing in it is old and everything in it is ancient. There is no building here that has stood longer than a lifetime. The houses are frames of gray driftwood pegged together — pegged, never nailed, because a house here must come apart as willingly as it goes up — walled with reed bundles and mud, roofed with reed, and in the drier quarters walled with blocks of salt itself, cut from the pans, which glow faintly at dusk and weep in the rare rains and are replaced without ceremony when they've wept too much. Every material dissolves. Every structure is provisional. And yet the town, as a pattern — the spacing of the houses, the width of the lanes, the placement of the racks, the shape of the whole long conversation — is old past counting. The buildings are remade every generation; the town has never been remade at all.

The second strange thing is that the town moves. Not migrates — breathes, in time with the lake. When the water rises across a span of wet years, the front row of houses is unpegged, carried back, and re-raised on higher ground, and the lanes shuffle inland after it. When the lake withdraws, the town follows it down. Over the centuries this slow respiration has walked the settlement back and forth across a band of shore nearly a mile deep, and that whole band is floored, a hand's depth under the mud, with old hearths — hundreds of them, thousands, the black stains of fires lit in houses that stood for thirty years and moved on. The saddle town writes its history vertically, four bands of gray in a single wall. The shore town writes its history horizontally, in the ground it has crossed and recrossed, a record no one reads because everyone is standing on it.

What the saddle does with space, the shore does with time. Up there, the emptiness between buildings is load-bearing. Down here, the buildings crowd close and the load is borne by the gaps between versions of the town — by the willingness of every made thing to be unmade and made again in the same shape. The permanence is kept in the pattern, not the material. It is the same solution to the same problem, turned inside out.

The one truly durable artifact on the shore is a drawing. Below the town, ruled into the flats, lie the pans: shallow rectangular ponds, dike-walled, laid out in a great stepped grid that descends toward the water — hundreds of them, precise as masonry, the only right angles in a landscape otherwise made entirely of horizon. Lake water is let into the highest pans and walked, pan by pan, down the grid, growing heavier and stranger and pinker as the sun lifts the water out of it, until in the lowest pans there is no water left at all — only salt, white and crystalline and ticking faintly as it dries, raked into cones that stand along the shore like a second town, brighter than the first. The pans are re-cut every few years as the shore moves; the grid itself, the map of the grid, is older than anyone's counting. It may be older than the walls on the saddle. Neither town has ever thought to ask.

Understand what the pans are, and you understand the whole arrangement of this world's two towns. The cisterns on the saddle exist to keep water and lose nothing. The pans on the shore exist to lose water and keep what's left. They are the same square-mouthed patience pointed in opposite directions — and they are fed, both of them, by the same water, because the water the pans lose does not vanish. It rises.

This is the day, on the shore. Morning: the lake is glass, the air is still, the mountain stands doubled in the water with its notch — the saddle — cut clean against the sky. Midday: the glare comes up off the pans hard enough to be a kind of weight, the horizon dissolves into shimmer, and the lake begins, invisibly, to climb into the air. Afternoon: the wind sets toward the mountain, always toward the mountain, and the whole sky's moisture files up the cliff face like a procession, and the people on the dikes watch the notch go white. *The saddle is putting on its cloud*, they say, or simply *the mountain is drinking*, and it means the pans have given all they will give today. And then evening: the air comes back. It slides down the cliffs cold and dry and crosses the town at dusk, rattling the reeds — the lake's own breath, returned by the mountain with the water taken out of it. The shore lends the sky its water every afternoon and is repaid at nightfall in cold. It has never once, in a thousand years, examined the terms.

The sound of the shore is the opposite of the saddle's silence and somehow its sibling: not almost-nothing finely detailed, but a great deal, finely blended. The lapping that never stops. The brine flies along the waterline, a soft continuous surf of their own. Rakes in the far pans, wood on crystal. Dogs. Voices carrying flat across the water the way voices carry across the saddle's terraces, at a calm half-shout: *the third pan is ready. Wind early today.* It is a noise so constant and so evenly mixed that the shore people do not hear it at all, and when one of them travels — as very few of them ever do — the silence of other places keeps them awake.

Everything is the color of the lake because everything is becoming the lake. Salt dust silvers the reed roofs, crusts the dike paths, stiffens the dogs' fur, rimes the doorposts white. The town is being slowly candied by its own harvest, turning the color of its ground exactly as the saddle town has weathered to the color of its mountain — two settlements, one gray and one white, each dissolving contentedly into the thing it is made of.

**Those Who Live on the Salt Shore**

Begin with the brine shrimp, because the lake would insist on it. They are the true population of this place — not the majority but the *near-totality*, trillions of them, a haze of small pink lives thickening the water, and they are the reason for everything else: the flies, the birds, the color of the horizon in a dry year. Each one lives a matter of weeks. The lichen on the saddle achieves permanence by refusing to die; the shrimp achieve the same permanence by refusing to stop being born. Their population is older than the town, older than the pans, possibly older than the notch in the mountain, and it has never contained a single individual for longer than a season. It is the shore's first lesson, taught continuously in the water: that a thing can be immortal and made entirely of brief parts. The town learned it. The town is built of it.

The flamingos are the calendar. They arrive when the lake blooms, in lines that come in low off the southern horizon, and they stand in the shallows in their thousands eating the shrimp and turning, on that diet, the exact pink of the water — wearing the lake's color the way the saddle town wears the mountain's, and by the same method: by eating nothing but where they live. Their arrival opens the harvest season and their departure closes it, and no one on the shore keeps any other calendar, because no other calendar has ever been more reliable.

Then the people. Count them loosely, because here the count does not matter: three hundred, more or less, more in harvest, fewer in flood years, the number slack and self-correcting where the saddle's number is exact and taut. The shore does not support a population the way a body supports a temperature. It supports one the way a lake supports a shoreline — approximately, generously, always adjusting.

But within the crowd there are positions, and the positions are as permanent as the saddle's four. There is the salt-master, who keeps the pan-map — partly scratched on a slab in the tally shed, mostly kept in the head, wholly kept in the pans themselves, which are the true document — and who learned the map from a woman now gone, who learned it from a man now gone, in a chain with no first link, exactly like the chain that teaches the saddle's mason, and quite possibly, if either chain could be followed back far enough, the *same* chain, forked long ago at the foot of the eastern trail. There is the weigher, who sits in the tally shed among slabs of scratched records going back further than the town's oldest house by a factor of ten — and among those slabs, recurring at intervals of a season, generation after generation, is one small entry that never changes: *salt, needles; two hides, hard cheese. The mountain.* It is the longest unbroken account in the shed. The shore's archive holds, without knowing it, the only written history the saddle town has — a history consisting of a single line, repeated for centuries, which is of course exactly the history the saddle would write for itself if it wrote anything at all.

And there is the needle-stall family, ninth of their name at the same pitch in the market lane, who keep the mountain custom. Once a season a figure comes into town off the southern road — having left the saddle at first light the day before, come down the eastern trail and around the mountain's long shoulder — and the family is, somehow, always ready. They hold back the good needles all season for this. They keep a basket of first-crystal salt, the fine flake from the lowest pans, which they sell to no one else. And they save news the way the saddle's cisterns save cloud: gathering it drop by drop for months, holding it dark and still, and dispensing it in one unhurried evening at the stall, so that the visitor can carry it up the mountain and let it out slowly, over weeks, on the long terrace. The shore does not notice this transaction; it is one small trade among hundreds. The family notices it completely. It is the oldest thing they do.

The kinship runs in the same channel as the trade, and the shore is as unaware of it as a well is unaware of being a well. When the saddle held six and shed two, the two came down this road and were absorbed into the crowd within a season, salted in, indistinguishable. When the saddle stood at two and needed four, a niece went up — a shore girl, from a reed-walled house in the third lane, who had spent her childhood watching the notch put on its cloud every afternoon and asking what was up there and being told what shore children are always told: *four people, and the rest of our lake.* There is such a child on the dikes now. There always is. The saddle keeps a population the way it keeps a temperature; the shore, without ever being asked, is where the warmth comes from.

The chough belongs to both towns and has never mentioned either to the other. Its cliff stands exactly between them. Some mornings it drops down the rock face instead of up it, walks the dried pans on its red feet with the same unhurried official thoroughness it brings to the saddle's gutters, audits the salt cones, finds the same nothing, and files its one harsh note. It is the only living thing that makes the journey daily which costs a person two days, and it is therefore the only resident of this world who has seen the whole arrangement — the stone boxes that keep the water and the white grid that keeps the salt — and it has, characteristically, no comment.

And then there is the salt itself, which is the shore's strangest resident, as the water is the saddle's, and which deserves the word *lives* for the same reason: it behaves as nothing inert behaves. It is what the water leaves when it goes — the lake's memory of itself, white and patient and incorruptible. It passes through every life on the shore: it is raked and stacked and weighed, it stiffens the dogs and silvers the roofs, it walls the houses and pays for the needles, and it is the only resident that travels. Twice a year, four times, it goes up on a back — around the shoulder, up the eastern trail, through the cold — and comes to rest in a stone house on the saddle, in a jar on a stone shelf, beside a hearth.

And there, in the evening, the old woman ladles water from the cistern into the pot, and drops in a pinch from the jar, and for the length of one meal, three thousand feet above the shore, the lake is whole again — its water and its salt, separated by a mountain and an afternoon sky, reunited in soup. This happens every day. Neither town has ever remarked on it. It is simply the arrangement.

That, finally, is what lives on the salt shore: not a crowd and not a market, exactly, but the *other half of a standing arrangement* — shrimp, flies, flamingos, dogs, salt, three hundred people give or take, and one lake that spends every afternoon climbing a cliff. The houses will come down and go up again a hundred yards along, and the town grieves nothing, because the pattern holds. The salt-master will die, and the pans themselves will teach the next pair of hands where the water goes. The shrimp will not notice the succession. The shrimp have never noticed anything, and have outlasted everything, and these two facts are one fact.

A thousand years from now a child will stand on a dike in the late afternoon — a different dike, on a shoreline that has walked half a mile since ours — and watch the notch in the mountain wall go white, and ask what is up there. And she will be told what shore children have always been told, and it will still be true: four people, and the rest of our lake. And overhead the breath will climb, and at dusk it will come back down cold, and somewhere in between, in a small gray town that has stopped and a long white town that never stops, the one water will still be divided the old way — the mountain keeping the sweet, the shore keeping the salt — each town holding its half, and neither, ever, holding the whole of it for longer than the length of a meal.
