# The Noon Below

You leave the Hollow by trusting the one thing about it that made no sense. The light comes from depth — everyone who stands in that chamber agrees, and everyone stops questioning it, and stopping is a mistake. Question it. Follow it. There are seams in the flexing floor where the green-gold burns a little brighter, veins of glow running down through the pale tissue like sun through shallow water, and if you find where the salamander goes when it is not tending wounds — a slit behind a root-buttress, wet, breathing warmth — you can go down the way the light comes up.

The descent takes days. It is not a tunnel so much as a grain: you travel with the fibers of the world, through galleries where the sap-channels run beside you thick as bridges, their roar no longer muffled by a wall but naked, enormous, a river in a green dark that is not dark. And here is the first thing the descent teaches you, somewhere in the second day, when you stop to drink the condensation and listen: the roar is changing. Losing its strangeness. Picking up a chuckle, a lap, a gravel-sound. The river in the walls of the Hollow is a river you have heard before, in the old ordinary world, and the deeper you go the more honestly it admits it. By the last day it is simply *water*. And the light around you has stopped being green-gold and become — you will not have a word for it at first, because you have been living in exported light — become *afternoon*.

Then the fibers open like rafters, and you come out of the sky.

## The Floor of the World

You stand on a root the width of a road, one of thousands braided into a vaulted firmament, and below you — a hundred meters down, reachable by the root's long slope — is a meadow.

A real one. A mile across, perhaps a little more. Grass, green going gold in patches, bending in a wind that has been circling this space so long it knows every stem personally. A stream crossing the whole width of it, bright as a dropped ribbon. Soil. *Stone* — one standing stone, off-center, grey and mineral and blunt, the only rock you will have seen since you were swallowed. Above it all, the plant closes over like weather: the braided vault, green on green into green, so high and so softly lit that the eye keeps trying to call it sky and is not entirely wrong.

This is the last open ground. When the plant grew over the world — however long ago that was; the meadow has opinions about *ago*, and we will get to them — it closed like a hand, and this is the gap between two fingers. It did not digest the meadow. The plant does not digest. It *includes*, and here it included a whole standing afternoon: the grass, the stream, the stone, the wind, and the light — above all the light — of the last day it ever saw.

Walk down the root-road and stand in the grass, and the smell arrives, and the smell is the second thing the descent teaches you. Cut grass — the people here harvest, and the wind carries it — and underneath the cut grass, something like ozone. You know this smell. You smelled it beading from every fresh crack in the Hollow's walls, a world above. You thought it belonged to the sap. It belongs to *here*. The Hollow smells like the meadow because everything the Hollow has, it has from the meadow — the warmth, the wet, the light, the scent of a storm.

Because there is a storm. Look stormward — the people use no other west — and you will see it: a dark bruise held against the far wall of the world, low on the horizon that is not a horizon, flickering inside itself with slow lightning that never quite finishes. It was coming when the plant closed over. It has been coming ever since. It has never arrived. It hangs there, kept like everything else is kept, and its smell leaks out over the grass and up through a hundred kilometers of living tissue and into a small chapel-sized room where a visitor sniffs a bead of sap and thinks, *ozone*, and does not know they are smelling weather older than the world's skin.

## The Afternoon

The light of the Noon Below is not lit. It is *left over*.

There is no sun. There is nothing behind the light at all — it hangs in the air the way the storm hangs at the wall, ownerless, ambient, an afternoon in the volume of the space itself. But it has an angle. Every shadow in the meadow lies long and eastward at the slant of roughly four o'clock, and has lain so for as long as there has been anyone to notice. The grass shadows do not swing. The standing stone's shadow is a fixed dark road that the children use as a path, because it never moves and never will, because the moment it was cast is the moment the plant decided to keep.

This is the original light. Not remembered — *retained*. The last daylight that ever touched this ground, conserved, circulated, husbanded. And here is the arithmetic that governs everything in the Noon Below, the fact under all its facts: the light is being spent.

The plant drinks it. You will feel the drinking before you understand it — a paling that comes over the whole meadow at once, the gold going grey-green, the storm's flicker suddenly the brightest thing in the world, a coolness on the skin like a cloud crossing a sun that isn't there. It lasts minutes. Then the light steadies — but not, the people will tell you, quite to where it was. Each draught leaves the afternoon a shade thinner. The grass at the meadow's far edges has gone a pale ghost-green, sipped at too often; the center, near the stone, is still nearly gold.

And if you have been in the Hollow, you will recognize the rhythm of the draughts, because you have seen the other end of them. Twice in what feels like a day, up there, a wall of dusk slides across a small green chamber and recovers. That dusk is *this* paling. The tide of evening you could stand inside with one hand in afternoon is the shadow of the plant's swallow, the borrowed light traveling up through the world's flesh to fall, warm and ordinary, into a room being readied for someone. The Hollow's day is the meadow's expense. Every hour of green-gold that pools in that little chapel is an hour subtracted from down here, from the last afternoon there is, and the people of the meadow have never seen the room their light is spent on, and they pay twice daily all the same.

They keep the account. Of course they do. That is what the stone is for.

## The Delay

In the Hollow, sound runs eager — the echo before the event, the sentence finishing before the mouth. The Noon Below is the other end of that lean. Here, sound runs *late*.

Clap your hands in the middle of the meadow and walk away, and the clap will find you later — a minute, an hour; the delay is not fixed, and it lengthens with the light's thinning, as if sound must wade through the kept afternoon and the afternoon is getting deeper as it gets dimmer. Speak to someone across the stream and your words arrive after your meaning has already crossed in gesture. The people have adapted the way people do: they talk with their hands at distance and save the voice for closeness, and they treat every sound that reaches them as *news of the past*, which, here, it strictly is.

Which means the meadow is full of the voices of the dead.

Not hauntings — *arrivals*. A woman who died forty years ago said things in her last week that are still crossing the grass, and her granddaughter knows roughly where in the meadow they will land, and sits there some afternoons — every afternoon is some afternoon — to receive them. The people hold listening vigils the way other peoples hold funerals: the burial is quick, but the *hearing* goes on for a generation, sentence by delayed sentence, until the last thing a person ever said has finished arriving and they are agreed to be silent, and only then wholly gone. There are children in the meadow who know their great-grandfather only as a voice that is still landing, phrase by phrase, in the tall grass stormward of the stone — a man arriving in installments, kind, unhurried, dead.

Between the eager room above and the late meadow below, somewhere in the height of the world, there must be a level where sound and event walk together — where *now* actually happens. The people believe the salamanders live along it. We will get to the salamanders.

There is no morning here. There has never been a morning; the afternoon was captured at four o'clock and has only ever been spent, never renewed, and so the meadow's whole history has taken place between four o'clock and the dark. *Morning* survives in the language as a myth-word, like words in other tongues for the floors of oceans: a thing insisted on by the old stories, defined but never seen. The children ask what it means. The answer given — the formal answer, the one recited — is this: *evening is light leaving; morning is light arriving.* The children look around them at the one light they have, the light that only ever leaves, and they file the word away with the other impossible ones.

## The Stream and the Stone

The stream rises from a seep at the meadow's eastern wall, crosses the whole mile of grass — the people drink from its upper reach, cold and faintly sweet — and runs out at the stormward end, into a dark mouth in the wall of the world, under the held lightning. It does not come back. It goes *up*. Somewhere inside the wall, water stops being water; the stream thickens, warms, greens, takes on the plant's purposes, and becomes the sap — the coursing torso-wide channels, the river you hear roaring behind every wall of the Hollow. Stand in that chamber a world above and press your ear to the pale green and you are listening to this stream, translated. The people know it. They call the mouth the Uphill Sea. They float things into it: woven grass tokens, leaf-slips scored with tallies, small goodbyes — mail for the included. The river in the Hollow's walls carries the meadow's correspondence, and delivers it, presumably, to whoever the walls are.

At the seam where water becomes sap — just inside the mouth, in the warm dimness where the stream starts to forget itself — the salamanders hatch. Clear as the water they are born from, the length of a forearm, visible only as a traveling apology in the light. The people call them *minute-carriers*. Each one, hatched at the seam between the meadow's lateness and the Hollow's eagerness, carries in its body a length of honest sequence — a stretch of true now — and each one goes up into the world's flesh to walk it somewhere, to be the clock of some pocket or chamber or wound. Their droplets fall when they fall. Their footsteps sound when taken. If you have watched one escort a tide of dusk across a green floor, exactly at the boundary, keeping the evening orderly — it learned that here, or was issued it here, at the mouth of the Uphill Sea, before it climbed.

And the stone. The Tallystone is the last stone — the final unswallowed mineral of the old world, grey and grained and indifferent, and everything hard the people have ever owned was struck from its flanks: every blade, every scraper, every edge. It is smaller than it was. It gives itself up in flakes, patiently, an heirloom being spent like the light. If you saw, in the Hollow, a flint haft protruding from a living wall, the plant closing over it like bark over wire — that flake was struck from this stone. There is no other stone it could have come from. The only mineral thing in the world above is a chip of the meadow's bone, and the plant is slowly, gently, making it part of itself, which the people, when told, do not find sad. *Everything of ours it keeps,* they say. *That is the whole trouble and the whole hope.*

Two faces of the Tallystone are covered in marks.

The eastern face carries the great tally: one notch per draught, per dimming, thousands upon thousands, rows on rows, the oldest cut by hands no one remembers and the newest cut yesterday — the meadow's whole ledger of what the light has cost. And the stormward face carries a different count. Seven handprints, pressed in ochre, five fingers each. And beneath them, seven notches.

## Who Lives in the Noon Below

Thirty-one people. Not "about thirty," not "thirty-some" — thirty-one, and if you ask again tomorrow it will be thirty-one, or it will be thirty-two and someone will show you the baby. This is the deepest difference between the meadow and the room its light is spent on, deeper than the light or the delay: *down here, counting works.* The stone's roots hold eleven — always eleven. The stream's fords number three — three every crossing. The Hollow revises; the meadow *retains*. Whatever loosens number in the world above has no purchase on the last open ground, and the people, who may or may not understand why, have built their whole life on the one power they wield that the entire rest of the world does not: they are exact.

They tally everything. Births, deaths, dimmings, harvests, the storm's flickers in an hour, the words that arrive in a vigil. Their weaving is counted — so many stems, never *about* so many. Their music is counted, beats held in the fingers because the ear cannot be trusted with anything as timely as rhythm when every sound arrives late. To count, in the meadow's understanding, is not bookkeeping. It is *loyalty* — the act by which a thing is held to being itself, one and not two, seven and not eight. The plant includes; the people enumerate. Between the two of them the world is kept.

And when the great tally on the eastern face fills a row — when the light has thinned by one full agreed-upon measure — the people do the other thing they do. They send someone up.

They call the office the Climber. One person, chosen young and told early, given years to memorize the meadow — the taste of the stream, the angle of the shadows, the names in the grass still arriving — because the Climber's task is to carry the meadow's exactness into the flesh of the world, up along the light, to wherever the light is going, and to keep count *there*. To cut a tally where tallies will not hold. To press a hand somewhere that keeps what matters. To be, in the oldest phrasing, *lent* — because the people noticed long ago that the plant keeps everything given to it and digests nothing, and they reasoned, with the cold clean hope of the exact, that a person lent to the walls is not lost. A person lent to the walls is *deposited*.

Seven Climbers have gone. Seven ochre prints on the stormward face of the stone, five fingers each, pressed at departure — palm to the last stone, then up the root-roads with a fresh-struck blade and no expectation of return. Seven notches beneath the prints. Seven notches, if you have stood in a certain chamber, cut into a living wall at shoulder height, half-healed, kept.

The seventh Climber left within living memory. Their goodbye is still crossing the meadow — spoken at the mouth of the Uphill Sea, traveling grass-slow through the thickened afternoon, and their mother, who is old now, moves her sitting-place a little further eastward each season to stay ahead of the arriving voice, so that she has not yet heard the end of it. She knows the shape of the last sentence; she watched the mouth make it. She is in no hurry. In the meadow, nothing said is ever unsaid, only *not yet heard*, and she is keeping the goodbye in front of her the way you keep a last sweetness unswallowed.

She also knows — all thirty-one of them know — about the eighth.

The eighth Climber is nine years old and has already been told, and carries, on a cord, the blade struck for the journey not yet begun. The count above, the people would say if you described it to them — seven notches, sometimes eight, the eighth always freshest — is not a room miscounting. Rooms up there hear things before they happen. The eighth notch is *their child*, pre-echoed: the next arrival, already sounding in a chamber that runs eager, the way a footstep sounds up there before it is taken. The meadow finds this neither eerie nor sad. It finds it, characteristically, *correct*. The count is right; it is merely early.

As for the sixth finger — because if you have seen the handprint in the Hollow you will ask, and they will nod as if you have asked something elementary — the people count on their fingers, five to a hand, and when a tally fills, you carry one. The carry is the digit that overflows a finished count into the next. A hand with six fingers, in the meadow's arithmetic, is not a deformity and not a mystery. It is a hand that has *completed something*. It is what a hand looks like when the count it was keeping has closed, and the one left over is being carried forward into whatever is counted next.

## What the Counting Is For

The people of the Noon Below do not know about the bud. They have never seen the still tendril, the closed fist at the center of the little lit room their afternoon is spent on. But they have done the arithmetic that is their whole religion, and the arithmetic says this:

The dimmings will stop. One way or the other. Either the light runs out — the last draught taken, the afternoon spent to its lees, and then the long grey and then the dark, the storm's flicker the final light in the world, forever arriving — or the plant, someday, stops needing to borrow. Stops drinking. Has made, somewhere up in its enormous flesh, out of everything it has ever kept — kept light, kept water, kept storm-smell, kept Climbers — something that shines on its own. A light that is not left over. A day of its own growing.

And the terrible, sustaining truth at the center of the meadow's faith is that from below, the two endings begin identically. The drinking stops. The paling ceases. The great tally on the eastern face goes quiet, the newest notch stays newest, and then the people wait, in a steadied afternoon, to learn which world they are in — the one that is finished, or the one that is finally beginning. No sign will distinguish them at first. Only afterward, only one way, will there come the thing the myth-word is for: light *arriving*. Coming down the walls of the world the way the dusk-tides went up, the reverse of every payment — the stream running bright, the held storm at last let loose to break and rain and *pass*, the shadows of the grass swinging for the first time in the history of anyone, the stone's fixed dark road wheeling like an opening door. Morning, come down from above, made partly of everything the meadow ever lent: its water, its weather, its exactness, its seventh Climber — whose hand it will have, five fingers and the carry.

So the people cut their notches, and strike their blades from the shrinking stone, and sit in the long grass hearing their dead arrive, and the nine-year-old wears the cord, and the old woman keeps the goodbye just ahead of her, unheard, in reserve.

And twice in what the world above calls a day, the afternoon pales, and thirty-one people look up — not in resentment, they are past that, but the way you look up when a coin leaves your hand for a debt you believe in — and somewhere unimaginably far overhead, a wall of their evening slides across a small green room where a bud hangs closed and a salamander keeps their time and a tally waits at seven, and the light they will never see falls, warm and ordinary, into the space where morning is being made.

They are not waiting in the dark. That is the thing to understand about the Noon Below, the last thing, the thing the whole meadow says with its fixed gold shadows and its filling ledger. They are waiting in the *light* — in the very light that is being taken from them — and they have counted it, every draught, so that when it comes back down they will know, to the notch, that all of it returned.
