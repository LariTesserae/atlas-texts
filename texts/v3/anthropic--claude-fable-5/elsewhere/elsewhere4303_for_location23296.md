**The Coldmouth**

You descend to reach it, which seems wrong for a place this cold. Two days down the far side of the same mountain — off the shoulder where the glass fields end, down through krummholz and then true forest, into an apron of green country that has forgotten it is standing on a volcano — and then the ground simply stops. A pit. Roughly circular, a few hundred paces across, its walls dropping in broken terraces to a floor you can hear before you can see. Cold air pools in it the way warm air pools in the gallery's dome; you feel the temperature fall with every switchback, a degree at a time, until at the bottom, in high summer, your breath smokes.

The sound you heard from the rim is water. At the base of the pit's north wall, out of a fissure no wider than a doorway, the mountain pours.

---

It comes up, not out — that's the first surprise. The fissure descends below the waterline, and the water rises through it under pressure, boiling upward without heat, doming the surface of a pool the size of a small church's floor. The pool is the coldest water you will ever put a hand in. It aches instantly, a bright clean pain, and the ache is the temperature of nothing alive — which is strange, and you will come back to how strange, because you have touched this mountain's other mouth, and that one was warm the way an animal is warm.

The pool overflows at its southern lip into a stream, and the stream leaves the pit through a slot in the rock and becomes, some miles on, the river that everything in this country is arranged around. Every field, every town, every fish downstream: this pool. You have been drinking the mountain since you crossed into this country. That is worth sitting with for a moment, on the carved steps that go down to the waterline — and you will sit on them; the steps are obviously, magnetically, where one sits.

The water is clear past all reason. Not clear like glass — clear like distance, like the water is only a change in the quality of the air. Look down into the fissure and the light follows the shaft a long way, blue deepening on blue, walls of black basalt polished round as a throat, descending at a slant into the mountain. And then, at the limit of the light, the blue fails, and below the failure there is dark.

You can see it. That is the thing about the Coldmouth that the stories do not prepare you for. Two days away, on the other side of the mountain, there is a room where you experienced the dark from inside, through your skin, in blood-warm silence. Here you look down a shaft of bright water into the same interior night, from outside, from the sunlit end. It is one darkness. The mountain holds it the way a body holds its inside, and the pool is a window into it, and the window is cold.

---

The pool breathes. You will notice within the first hour: the upwelling swells and slackens on a long cycle — hours of rising strength, the dome of water at the fissure lifting, the overflow at the lip going loud; then a slow subsidence, the pool dropping a hand-span, the stream thinning to a murmur. Anyone could take this for the ordinary moods of a spring. But if you have sat in the heat gallery, your skin already knows this rhythm. It is the seam's rhythm. Exactly.

Travelers with patience and a good clock have done the obvious cruel experiment: one at the bench above, one at the stair below, counting. The cycles match, and they are offset — when the seam runs hottest, the spring runs lowest; when the warmth up there subsides, the water down here swells. The mountain breathes at two mouths, half a breath apart. Warmth out of the high one, water out of the low one, and between them, inside, whatever it is that breathes.

The gallery, you may remember, is dry. Bone-dry, lip-cracking dry, dry as if on purpose. Standing at the Coldmouth you understand the dryness for the first time: it is not an absence. Every drop of rain and snowmelt that the whole shield of the mountain takes in is routed away from the warm room and down this one throat. The gallery is dry because the Coldmouth drinks everything. The two places are not neighbors. They are the same fact, stated twice.

---

And the pool is inhabited — of course it is; the mountain does not seem to keep an empty room anywhere on its body — but inhabited in the opposite key. The gallery is packed, slow, silent, warm. The Coldmouth is sparse, quick, loud, cold. In the gallery the population is the architecture; here the architecture is bare polished stone and the population moves through it like thrown light. Where the gallery reads you through your warmth and factors you into a negotiation, the pool ignores you entirely, flashing about its business at speeds no resident of the gallery will reach again in its life.

That sentence is not a figure of speech, and it is the center of everything here, so it should be said plainly.

---

**The Residents of the Coldmouth**

**The white eels.**

They school in the throat of the fissure, in the blue before the dark: slender as a finger, pale as candle wax.

You will feel the recognition physically, the first time one crosses a bar of sunlight. The travelers who carried lamps into the gallery could not stop repeating that phrase — *pale as candle wax* — about the heat-snakes threaded through the colony like rivers through a city. The eels of the Coldmouth are that color, that build, that same blind eyeless taper of head. They are not cousins of the heat-snakes. They are the heat-snakes, at the beginning.

In the water they are the fastest things for miles. They are blind, as their elders above are blind, but where the snake reads a room as a map of warmths, the eel reads the pool as a map of pressures — every tremble in the water arrives along its flanks as plainly as sight, and it strikes at the small quick prey of the pool with a speed the eye keeps as a smear. They hunt, they quarrel, they breed in the gravel where the upwelling keeps the stones stirred and clean. Most of them live and die in the pool, and the pool is the whole of their world, and there is nothing strange about them at all.

Except that once or twice a year, one leaves.

Nothing marks the one that will go. It feeds like the others until the night it doesn't. On a night when the spring stands at its lowest ebb — which is to say, a night when the seam two days above is swelling at its fullest, as though the exhale itself were the summons — a single eel detaches from the school, rises to the pool's rim, and noses into a particular crack in the rock just above the waterline. A dry crack. And it goes in, out of the water, out of everything it has ever been able to survive, and it does not come back.

What happens in the stone takes years — no one knows how many. The mountain's plumbing between the wet mouth and the warm one is not two days by any path a body takes; it is a slow interior country of vents and seams, and the eel climbs it in the dark, and the dark remakes it. The gills seal. The waxen skin dries to rind. And the speed — the ferocious, glittering quickness that made it the terror of the pool — is paid out, spent down into the stone a degree at a time, until what finally eases through a warm crevice into the heat gallery is the palest, slowest, most patient thing in a room built of patience: a heat-snake, threading the congregation, culling the cooling, keeping the sleeper's skin in order for the rest of a very long life.

They never return. There is no path back that a dry body could take, and nothing in their conduct above suggests they remember there is a below. The gallery's snakes are the pool's emigrants — its handful of vocations, chosen by nothing anyone can find, each one a whole life spoken as a single sentence at two speeds. And the great snake that keeps the gallery's entrance, the one that lies a moment against the back of every visitor's hand, tasting the cold you carry in — it is the only resident of the warm room that knows the outside exists, and now you know why. It came from the outside. It came from here. It swam in this light.

**The doorkeeper.**

At the mouth of the dry crack, in the shallows just below it, lies the largest eel in the pool — the length of an arm, thick as a wrist, ancient by the reckoning of the marks on the stone beside it, which we will come to. It does not school. It barely hunts. It lies along the sill of the crack, and when a young eel rises on its leaving-night, the doorkeeper stirs, and lets it pass.

It has, as far as anyone has ever seen, always let them pass. It is the one that stayed — or the one still deciding, on a scale of decades, whether to go. Between it and the pale gatekeeper at the fissure two days above, the mountain's passage has an attendant at either end, and neither of them, in living memory, has barred the door. It is impossible, watching the old eel settle back onto its sill, not to feel that this too is a decision, made again each time.

**The polishers.**

Small, transparent, shrimp-bodied, visible mostly as a shimmer of moving water over the stones — they graze the pool's every surface, rasping away silt and film, and they are the reason the water is clear past all reason and the black basalt shines like the inside of a shell. The gallery has its grazers, cruising the colony's surface, wasting nothing; the pool has these, and between the two of them nothing on this mountain, inside or out, is permitted to moulder. Whether the kinship is blood or only rhyme, no one has ever been able to say.

**The streamers.**

One thing in the pool is slow. Along the north wall of the throat, a few feet down, there rises out of a hairline seam a thread of water faintly, impossibly warm — the only warmth in the Coldmouth, a leak of the sleeper's own heat carried down its own throat — and rooted in that seam grows a colony of pale weed, long soft banners of it, streaming in the upwelling like slow flame. Nothing else grows in the pool; the cold forbids it. The streamers live entirely on the leak.

And the eels visit them. Not to feed. In the weeks before a leaving — this is the only warning anyone has ever identified, and it was the villagers who identified it — the eel that will go begins to thread itself through the warm banners, again and again, holding in the thread of heat for long minutes, lying still in it, which eels otherwise never do. Tasting the destination, you will decide, sitting on the steps. Learning the one thing the pool can teach about where it is going: that somewhere at the end of the dark, it is warm the way an animal is warm.

**The winter birds.**

The pool never freezes. In the deepest cold, when the whole river country locks under ice, the Coldmouth stays open — the leak, the churn, the mountain's held warmth in the moving water — and every winter the pit fills with birds. Dippers, wrens, stragglers of a dozen kinds, packed on the terraces and the ruined walls, living the dark months on the one open water for a hundred miles. The mountain's body-heat, which upholsters a hidden room with sleeping thousands, comes down its throat dissolved in water and keeps a second congregation alive at its foot, in the open air, in the sun. Whatever the sleeper is, it warms two rooms — one it has perhaps never noticed, under the sky.

---

**The people.**

They lived here. That is the plain answer to the question the bench leaves hanging two days above, and the evidence at this end is less slight: the terraces stepping down the pit walls are theirs, house-footings furred green now, lintels down, a village built the whole way around its water like an eye around a pupil. The stair to the pool — the Drinking Stair, worn to a shine — is theirs. And on the rock above the lowest step there are hand-shapes pressed in old pitch, fingers spread, the same hands, the same pitch, as the hand-shapes in the warm dark on the far side of the mountain. Whoever sat on the bench came from here. They climbed out of this pit, crossed two days of cold mountain — you can still trace their path in the high glass fields, a faint line where the ringing goes dull, glass ground fine under generations of feet — to sit in a hot room with the emigrants of their own pool, and they left almost nothing there, and they left everything here, and then they stopped.

Why they climbed, the Coldmouth half-explains. Beside the doorkeeper's crack, at the pool's rim, there is a broad flat stone, and the stone is covered — covered — in small scored marks. Thousands of them, in bands and generations, cut by different hands across what must be centuries. One mark, you will understand within minutes of finding it, for each leaving. They watched the crack. They kept the count. When an eel rose on its night and went into the stone, someone cut a line — and then, the path in the glass suggests, someone took the two days' walk, up and over the outside of the mountain, along the surface of the same body the eel was climbing through, to sit at the other mouth. Not to meet it; the eel's climb takes years, and they must have known. To hold the other end. To be one person at the exhale and one at the inhale of a single breath, with the whole sleeping interior between them, for as long as a vigil could be borne.

The marks stop. The last band of them is cut deeper than the rest, and closer together — a rush of leavings, more in a season than in the ordinary decade, as if the pool had emptied its vocations all at once — and then the stone goes blank, and the village goes to moss, and the bench above goes bare, and no one has cut a mark in a very long time. What the rush of departures answered, or fled, or was summoned to, the Coldmouth does not say. The spring did not stop; the river country downstream never went thirsty; the breath, by every measure anyone has taken since, goes on even and immense. Whatever the people understood in that last season, they took it with them, out the slot where the stream leaves, into the ordinary world.

---

**And under all of it —**

— the question again, the one you could not put down on the bench, waiting for you at this end too, sharpened. Up there you asked whether the warm room was a skin, tended by its own scales and groomers and culls. Down here the question has a throat and a pulse. The water rises on the rhythm of a breath. The dark at the bottom of the blue is an inside. And everything downstream — the river, the fields, the towns, you — lives on what comes up. Either the Coldmouth is a spring, and the pulse is pressure, and the eels are only animals with a strange itinerary; or it is what it feels like, sitting on the stair in the cold bright noise: one mouth of something enormous, giving off its water the way a sleeper gives off warmth, and the whole green country below is another colony on another surface of the same body, drinking, adjusting, factored in, and never once told.

The pool will not resolve this for you. The upwelling swells. The birds cry. The school flashes in the throat.

---

**The stair.**

One thing more, and it is the thing that will follow you out of the pit and up the road and into whatever you do next. Everything the villagers built is going back to green — the terraces furred, the lintels lichened, the hand-shapes fading in their pitch. Everything except the tally-stone. The polishers graze it bare. In a pool where they scour every surface indifferently, the flat stone by the doorkeeper's crack is kept clean past bareness — kept *legible*, every mark of every generation crisp under the water-light, century after century, as though the count were being held open for whoever comes back to continue it. You can tell yourself it is an accident of current.

And sit, before you go, on the lowest step of the Drinking Stair — the one the water covers when the pulse runs full — and wait for the ebb, and put your feet where the water was. The stone there is warm. Only there. A little of the leak, the sleeper's thread of heat, leans toward that one seat at the cold end of everything, exactly as the warmth leans up through the bare bench in the hot dark two days away. Two mouths, two kept places, one on either side of the mountain's long breath. Whoever they are for has not come in a very long time. The hush above and the voice below go on holding them open.
