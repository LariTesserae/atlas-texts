**The Loud Shallows**

You could not fail to find it. Failing is not a sense that works here. The shallows come out to meet you — a half-day before you see them you hear them, a white breathing below the treeline, and the air arrives already tasting of cold water and crushed stone. The chamber has to be entered. The shallows enter you first.

At the foot of the young mountain, where the raw slopes finally run out of steepness, the water that falls on all that new rock gathers itself into a river, and the river, meeting the mountain's toe, bends wide around it and spreads. For a kilometer it forgets how to be deep. It braids across a plain of its own making — channels splitting, rejoining, abandoning themselves — over a bed of pebbles that stretches bank to bank, millions upon millions of them, every one a piece of the mountain.

That is the first thing to understand about the shallows: they are the mountain, proceeding. Every stone on this shoal broke off that summit as something sharp — glassy, new-edged, the same raw crystal your fingertips caught on in the dark, forty meters inside — and the river has been arguing with it ever since. The argument takes ten thousand years and the river always wins. Kneel and run your palm across the bed and it reads nothing like the braille of the ceiling. Those were beginnings, packed and waiting. These are middles — every stone caught partway between edge and egg, no two at the same stage, the whole shoal a single word being said over and over until it comes out round. The chamber is a room where everything is begun and nothing proceeds. The shallows are the proceeding. This is where the mountain goes to be worn into sentences.

Light is total here, and violent about it. The water is thin enough that the sun reaches the bed everywhere and comes back up off a million wet stones at once, so that the shoal does not merely sit in light but *manufactures* it, a glare you squint against even looking away. At dawn the braids run pink; at noon they are unbearable; at dusk the water holds the light a full hour after the banks have given it up. Stone that formed in a darkness older than anything, that has never once been seen — this is where it comes to be seen. Every pebble here has been in the sun for centuries. They are the chamber's rock with the one condition reversed, and the reversal has changed everything about them.

And where the chamber holds one note, the shallows hold all of them. The sound is a full spectrum, and you can walk through it like weather: the gravel-hiss at the tails of the bars, a sound like rain falling upward; the low knuckled talk of cobbles in the faster braids; the steady white roar at the head of the shoal where the river first breaks over the fan. Under flood, at night, there is a deeper sound — boulders knocking along the bed, a blunt subaqueous *dok, dok* felt in the ground more than heard — which is the loud cousin of the chamber's mineral tick. In there, that sound is the mountain being born. Out here, it is the mountain being disagreed with.

Nothing persists. That is the shallows' native condition, the way darkness is the chamber's. The braids move every season; a channel that carried the whole river in spring is a dry ribbon of stones by fall, and next year's map is written by a flood no one witnesses. The shoal keeps no tracks, no marks, no evidence. Step in the wet sand at the water's edge and watch: the print softens as you look at it, fills, smooths, is gone before you have finished lifting your other foot. Time does not pass through this place without leaving fingerprints — time is the only thing it is made of, a calendar with every page turning at once, floods and freezes and afternoons, all of it counted, none of it kept. If you stayed an hour or a decade, the shoal would offer you overwhelming evidence of which, and would retain none of it.

At the head of the shoal, though, right against the mountain's toe, there is one seam of exception. A spring enters the river there — several, in a line along the base of the rock — and the water they deliver is warm. Blood-warm. It comes up through the gravel smelling faintly of sulfur, staining the stones around each vent with a pale slick film, white and silken, laid over the cobbles like wet cloth, and where it mixes into the river it raises a standing ghost of mist on cold mornings.

You know this water. You have felt it on your skin, beading on a ceiling in the dark. It is the mountain's sweat, arrived — the same slow seep that weeps through the chamber and a thousand sealed pockets like it, migrating down through hairline pores for lifetimes of stone until it finds daylight here. The drop that fell, *tok*, into the floor-pool did not end there. The pool drains as slowly as it fills; the mountain passes everything downward eventually; and centuries after any given drop performs its one small round sound in the dark, it emerges at the toe of the shoal, warm, mineral, carrying dissolved within it the faintest trace of everything the chamber is — its stone, its film, its glazed-over dead, the salt of any hand that ever pressed its wall. The river receives the mountain's interior in solution, without ceremony, and thins it to nothing, and carries it on. The shallows drink the chamber. They have always been drinking it. They do not know.

---

The census here is beyond taking. The shallows are crowded the way the chamber is empty — fish in the channels, birds working the bars, the water itself furred with small lives. But one resident belongs to the shoal the way the grazer belongs to the chamber, and it is the grazer's cousin, and its story is the same story given the one thing the chamber withholds: a door.

Call it **the dancer**, for the last afternoon of its life, though it spends almost none of its life dancing.

It spends its life underneath. Below the bed of the shallows, between and beneath the pebbles, there is a second river — a buried one, water moving in darkness through the spaces in the gravel, down where light fails within a hand's depth and the roar above arrives only as a soft pressure. It is dark there. It is wet past all distinction between body and medium. Near the warm seam it holds blood-heat through the deepest winter, when the braids above lock white. It does not change. Under the loudest, brightest, most restless place on the river lies a thin stratum of the chamber's exact conditions — warm, black, constant, silvered on every buried stone with the same slick chemical film — and that is where the dancer lives, for years, as a nymph: pale, soft, nearly eyeless, sweeping ahead of itself with antennae finer than roots, grazing the film off the undersides of stones in slow wandering lines.

Hold the two of them in mind at once, the nymph and the grazer, and the kinship is not a figure of speech. The same ancient stock of small soft things faced the same question — *what do you do with the dark?* — and split on the answer. One lineage followed the water down a fissure into a sealed room and stayed, and the room removed every reason to change, and so it never did, and now it lives a century and is glazed into the floor forever. The other lineage stayed in the gravel under the loud water, where the dark is real but rented, and made of it a childhood.

Because the nymph's dark has an exit, and the nymph knows the date. The one calendar signal the chamber receives — the sweeter water of what would be summer — the buried river receives a hundredfold: the warming, the lengthening light pressing down through the shallows, the flood's retreat. And on one afternoon of the year, all at once, by an agreement no individual makes, the entire generation lets go of the stones and rises.

What comes up through the bright water and splits its own skin at the surface is a different animal wearing the same soul. Wings like wet smoke, drying in minutes. A body gone from pale to gleaming. And — this is the whole of it — **no mouth**. The dancer's final form cannot eat. It is born finished, provisioned only with the fat of its years in the dark, and that provision is measured in hours. Everything the grazer's life is, the dance inverts: the grazer has a century and no urgency; the dancer has an afternoon and is made of nothing else. They rise in columns above the braids, millions together, and the low sun fills the columns so that the air above the shoal becomes visible — a standing shimmer, light given texture — and they climb and fall and climb, and pair, and the females return to the water and give their eggs to the current, and by full dark it is over. The surface of every channel is dusted with spent wings. The fish take some. The river takes the rest. By morning the shoal is scoured and blank and counting again, and the eggs are already sifting down into the gravel, and the next generation is beginning its descent into the warm dark — down, the direction both lineages have always gone first.

The grazer is kept forever and never witnessed. The dancer is witnessed utterly, by the sun, by the fish, by the whole loud reach — for one afternoon — and then erased without residue. Neither is remembered. The shallows cannot remember; the chamber cannot forget. Between them they have divided the work.

Which brings us to the pebbles.

Whoever crawled into the chamber with a hammerstone and a cupped flame came through here — had to; the shoal is the mountain's doorstep, and it is the only place within days of walking where a stone can be found that is *finished*. They knelt in this glare, in this noise, perhaps on the afternoon of the dance itself with the air above them shimmering — unknowable, unrecoverable, the shoal retains nothing — and they chose. A small stack of river pebbles, smooth, selected wet for their doneness, and carried up the mountain and in through the dark and set on a shelf, for no purpose that survives.

Understand what was taken. Every stone on this shoal is mid-argument; the river is not through with a single one of them and never will be, because "through" is not a condition the river offers. Those three pebbles are the only stones of this whole reach that will never be worn again. Someone lifted three words out of the river's endless draft and carried them to the one room in the world where nothing proceeds, and there they sit, the river's only finished sentences, glazing over in the dark, kept mid-gesture for ten thousand years. The shallows do not miss them. Missing requires memory. But the exchange stands, and it runs both ways, and neither place knows of it: the river holds, dissolved and moving, everything the mountain's sealed rooms are made of; the chamber holds, still and sealed, the only surviving evidence that this shoal was ever touched by a hand. Each is the other's archive. Each keeps exactly what the other cannot.

And you, if you came — you would be nothing here, the smallest event of the hour. Wade in and the cold takes your breath and the current leans on your legs like a patient animal, and when you climb out, the water closes, your prints dissolve, and within a season there is no version of the shoal that contains you. The chamber would have flavored you into its walls forever. The shallows forget you before your legs are dry. Choose which mercy you prefer.

Then evening comes down the braids, and the roar continues — all the drops at once, indistinguishable, among them the ones that once fell singly in the dark and were passed around the walls and swallowed — and somewhere in that sound, statistically, unremarkably, the chamber's water goes by, hurrying now, loud at last, on its way to the sea.
