**The Reach of Running Fog**

You come down off the mountain the cold way, because there is no other way — over the rim at the gray edge of morning, frost on the outer slopes exactly where the valley people promised it would be, and then down and down through a country that gets drier as it gets lower, which is backwards, which is the first thing this side of the mountain teaches you. The bowl kept its water. Out here the wind takes everything. By the second day you are walking on frost-shattered gravel under a sky scoured to a hard enamel blue, through air so dry your lips split along old lines, and the throb behind the eyes is the same throb — you have lost a thousand meters and the altitude still says *extreme* — but the heat is gone. Utterly. The sun out here is bright and weightless, a light that illuminates without warming, like a lamp behind glass.

This is the country the valley people mean when they say *winter*. Not a season. A place. You are standing in their word.

And then, on the third day, you see the fog.

It lies on the plain far ahead, a white ribbon, low, and it is *moving* — not drifting the way fog drifts, but running, streaming in one direction along a line, like smoke poured down a channel. Fog does not do that. Fog pools and sits. This fog has somewhere to be. It takes hours of walking to understand what you are looking at, and when you finally stand above it on a frost-heaved terrace of gravel, the contradiction arrives all at once, the same way the caldera's heat did, from the opposite direction: below you, crossing a plain where ice glazes the shadow of every stone at noon, runs a river the color of milk and turquoise, and the river is steaming, and the river is warm.

**The water**

It comes out of the mountain's foot fully grown. There is no gathering of streams, no watershed, no childhood — trace it upslope and it simply begins, welling out of a talus apron in a dozen throats, a river born adult, already wide, already blood-warm, already carrying its cargo. Behind it, two days' walk up the long flank, the mountain stands with its lid of cloud pulled down over the rim, and you know — you are perhaps the only walker on this plain who knows — exactly what is under that lid. The rain that fell warm through warm air onto warm rock. The marsh trembling with small bubbles. The seeps, the black peaty channels, the water pressed down into the fracture networks in the basalt, into the dark, for days or years, through the plumbing whose hum you felt in your feet. The valley called itself a closed loop, and it lied the way all bodies lie: everything that lives, drains. This river is the caldera exhaling through the ground. It arrives on the plain still carrying the bowl's temperature like a letter carried unopened.

It is milky because it is saturated with dissolved stone — silica, the same mineral the terraces are built from, the water having filmed over the scalloped lips of the grandmothers and moved on, carrying what the valley did not keep. It steams because the plain is freezing and it is not. The wind takes the steam and lays it flat and runs it downstream, and that is the running fog: the river's breath, blown along its own body, a white road lying on the land for thirty kilometers, visible from a day away. On this plain you do not navigate by landmarks. You navigate by where the world is exhaling.

**The masonry**

The river builds. That is the second thing to understand about it, and everything downstream is a consequence.

The silica comes out of solution wherever the water cools and thins — at the banks, over riffles, on anything that holds still in the shallows. It comes out slowly, millimeter by millimeter, the same patient deposition that built the caldera's terraces, but here it does not build terraces. Here it builds *copies*. A reed that dies at the waterline is sheathed in stone by the following year, and stands. A willow root, a drowned bird, a coil of dropped cord — the river takes each one and returns it as itself, in pale gray opaline stone, faithful to the grain, faithful to the feather. The banks for kilometers are lined with this population of the kept: stone reeds in stone thickets, rattling faintly in the wind with a sound like wind chimes made of teeth.

And back from the present bank, standing on old gravels the river abandoned centuries ago, are the groves.

They are tree ferns. You know them at a hundred meters — the shaggy columnar trunks, the wide crowns — you walked under ten thousand of them in the bowl. These are stone, every one, whole groves of them, petrified where they stood, and they stood *here*, on this freezing plain, which is impossible, until you work out the mechanism: the notch. The low pass in the rim. In the great wet years — the valley people would not have counted them, but the plain did — the marsh overtopped the notch and a warm cascade ran down the outer flank for a season, a temporary river of valley water, and the valley's life went with it. Seeds, spores, frogs, ferns — a green tongue licking down into winter. For a season, sometimes two, it lived. Then the cascade failed and the cold closed over it, and the mineral water, before it withdrew, did what mineral water does. The groves are the valley's failed emigrations, kept standing. The caldera keeps no records; the moss refills every footprint within the hour. But its drainage keeps everything. The reach is the record the valley refuses to keep — its memory, living outside its body, written in the only medium the plain has: patience and stone.

**The white pan**

The river does not reach the sea. Nothing on this plain reaches the sea. It runs its thirty kilometers, cooling all the way — the reach is a thermal gradient laid out flat, the same gradient the valley's hot pools stack in millimeter bands of orange and red, here stretched across a day's walk — and somewhere out on the salt flats the warmth finally runs out. The fog thins, the steam stops, the water goes glassy and slow, and then there is a morning-colored place where the river simply dies: a white pan of ice and salt, the water freezing outward into feathered plates, the last liquid narrowing to a channel, a seam, a thread, nothing. Past that point the riverbed continues, dry, for kilometers — evidence that the thread has been longer, in other centuries — out into a silence that is not the valley's occupied hush but the other kind.

Stand at the pan at evening, warmth possible at your back if you walk upstream far enough, and look up the long white road of fog to the mountain with its lid of cloud, and you are standing at the exact opposite end of a single system: the furnace at one end, the frost at the other, and thirty kilometers of borrowed heat in between, spending itself as it goes.

---

The reach is inhabited the way a wire is inhabited by current: in a line, densely, and not one step to either side. Everything alive on this plain lives within reach of the river's breath. The honest answer to *who lives here* runs along the water like everything else, and it starts — you will not be surprised — with paint.

**The same first tenants**

The mats are here. Orange at the springheads where the water is hottest, shading down the reach through salmon and gold to green as it cools — the identical colored strata, the identical lineages, and not identical by coincidence: *continuous*. The thermophiles of the reach were not carried here by wind or bird. They came down through the mountain, alive, in the dark, through the fracture plumbing, the way they have come for as long as the water has — a single population threaded through kilometers of rock, its upstream half basking under the cloud lid among the frogs and the terraces, its downstream half out here under the enamel sky among the ice. The valley's oldest residents and the reach's oldest residents are the same resident. Everything else about the two places diverged. The first tenants never noticed there were two places.

**The heirs of towers**

On the warm mid-reach, where the water spreads into shallows, live the giant coots — heavy, slate-colored, quarrelsome birds that build nest platforms of pebbles and reed. Coots do this everywhere in the high country. But these coots build in water that petrifies, and so their platforms do not wash away in the spring floods. They harden. The next year's nest goes on top of the last, and stone-sheathes in its turn, and over centuries the platforms have grown into towers — squat islets of layered opaline masonry standing out of the shallows, some of them taller than you, each one crowned with this year's living nest and built of every nest beneath it back beyond counting. The towers are inherited. A coot lineage holds its tower for generations, defends it, adds to it. They are the plain's first architects and its first landlords, and they got both institutions from the water.

Remember them. Someone else on this river was watching them, once, and learning.

**The excused**

The reach grants exemptions, the same way the caldera does, from the same furnace, at a remove.

The flamingos should not be here. Their kind flees this altitude before the ice comes; the salt lakes of the high plain kill the birds that linger. But the reach's shallows run blood-warm all winter, and so these are the only flamingos in existence that stand through the high-country night in falling snow — the flakes landing on their backs and melting, landing and melting — excused from migration by heat they will never trace to its source, the way the caldera's hummingbirds are excused from torpor by the same heat one mountain-thickness away. Two exemptions. One furnace.

There is a minnow, finger-long, that lives its entire life inside a band of water two degrees wide. The band moves — downstream by day as the sun helps, upstream at dusk as the plain's cold bites in — and the minnows move with it, the whole population commuting kilometers every day in order to stay, thermally speaking, exactly where they are. And at dawn the vicuñas come down from the frost country in single file and stand in the running fog with their eyes closed, wool pearling over with condensed river-breath, and lick the water off the stone reeds, and file out again. They drink the river without ever touching it. The fog is the river's rationed edge, the last arm's-length of the caldera's warmth, and the plain's animals line up for it like a wage.

**The silver mornings**

Some evenings, when the wind stands right, the cloud lid on the mountain spills over the rim, and the spill carries passengers. You know the freight system; you saw it loading — the tiny spiders on tiptoe on the fern crowns, paying silk into the charged plumes, riding the valley's static up to the cloud. The elevator has an exit this side of the rim. On spill nights the silk comes down across the reach for kilometers, and the cold kills almost every rider before morning, and the plain wakes *silvered* — every tola bush, every stone reed, every coot tower strung with frosted thread, flashing under the early sun, a country upholstered overnight in the valley's dead mail.

Almost every rider. Along the warm banks, a few live. Every spider on this river descends from an aeronaut, and none of them will ever fly again — there is no kindly static here, only the wind, and the wind is a killer. They are immigrants whose ships burned on arrival, weaving their small webs in the fog line, one generation further from the bowl each year.

**The people of the count**

And there are people, and you will see their houses from a long way off, because — here is the whole difference, standing in plain sight on the riverbank — *they build*.

Their story, which they will tell you, because telling is what they are for: they are the ones who stayed standing at the rim. Every valley child, you remember, is taken up once to the cold edge of evening to look back down at the steaming bowl and learn that the valley is a kindness and not a law. The lesson is meant to end with the child going home. Their ancestors — a handful, in some overflow year when the notch was running and the outer flank was briefly warm and green — looked outward instead of down, and followed the warm cascade toward winter, and when the cascade failed behind them they were on the plain, and the way back was cold, and they did what the ferns in the stone groves could not do: they learned.

They learned fire, which no one in the bowl has ever needed. They learned wool, and walls, and the storing of food, because out here the world fruits in one season and starves in three, and that single fact rebuilt them from the grammar up. A people with granaries must count what is in them; must mark the year against the seed; must say *whose*. Their language, which still shares its bones with the short warm phrases of the bowl, has grown numbers past any hoarder's dream, and tenses, and a calendar cut in stone, and words the valley never needed and does not have. They have a word for famine. Their word for the past is *upriver*. Their word for the mountain is simply *Before* — a place and a time in one, wearing its cloud, standing at the head of everything.

Their craft came from the coots. Watch the towers long enough and the lesson offers itself: what the water touches, the water keeps. So they weave — walls, doorframes, cradles, effigies, entire house-fronts of tight wicker — and they carry the weaving to the river and sink it in the warm shallows, staked against the current, and they wait. Seven years, for a wall. They call the interval *a sinking*, and it is their base unit of long time, the way the valley's base unit is the eleven-minute breath of the western vent: both peoples keep time by the same water, one by its pulse, one by its patience. A person plans in sinkings. A marriage is sealed by sinking a woven arch that the couple's children will raise as stone. Their villages, when you walk into one, are pale gray and faintly translucent at the edges, every wall a fabric that the river turned permanent — architecture as the practice of deciding, seven years in advance, what you will want to be true.

Their dead go into the water too, and this is where the mirror turns fully around. In the bowl, remember, the dead are laid on the terrace lips and become anonymous strata — a woman standing on her grandmother, satisfied, the grandmother a pale band in the general stone. Here the dead are lowered into a deep slow pool — the Making Pool, kept for nothing else — in cradles woven to hold the body's shape, and raised after a generation as themselves: stone figures, faithful to the grain, faithful to the face, and stood upright in a long low hall called the House of the Raised. They are named forever. A man drinks his morning tea beside his great-grandfather, who has a face. And the reach people do not know — you may be the only one on the river who can know — that the silica sheathing every ancestor in that hall came down through the mountain in solution, having first filmed over the terraces of the bowl. The valley's anonymous dead are dissolved, in some fine fraction, into the reach's remembered ones. The two funerals are one funeral, thirty kilometers long, conducted continuously by the same water: upstream it buries by forgetting, downstream it buries by keeping, and the grandmothers travel.

In the House of the Raised, on a shelf apart, they keep *the sent things*: what the river delivers in flood years from a country none of them has seen. A frog no bigger than a thumbnail, copper showing faintly through the gray stone. A frond of a fern that grows nowhere in the world — their scholars call it ancient, extinct, a message from Before, and they are wrong by one valley: two days upstream, a green-coated animal older than their whole nation is pulling that fern through the pearl-lit understory this afternoon. And a tuft of coarse hair, green-tinged, that they call *the mountain's wool*, and handle rarely, and do not theorize about.

Once a year they walk to the white pan. The whole reach turns out for it. At the exact point where the last liquid thread gives out, they set a cut and dated stone, and the line of such stones runs back along the dead riverbed for six centuries — the longest instrument on the plain, a thermometer read annually, measuring the health of a furnace they have never seen under a cloud they have never been beneath. The stones mostly cluster. Some years step downstream, some up. One old scatter stands far out on the salt, marking the overflow years — the same years the stone groves record, the same years their ancestors walked out of the warmth — story and instrument agreeing across six hundred years, which is the kind of agreement this people lives for. They read the pan the way the valley's animals read the algal mats: as the gauge on the side of the mountain's boiler. If Before ever cools, they say, the end of the river will walk upstream toward the villages, a year at a time, and the stones will see it coming three generations off, and that — knowing, early, in numbers — is the entire dignity their exile bought, and they hold it like a title.

And each child of the reach is taken once to the pan, at the cold edge of evening, to stand where the warmth runs out and look back up the long white road of fog to the mountain — so the child will understand, in the body, behind the eyes where the altitude throbs, that the river is a loan and not a law. It is the same lesson, at the other end of the same water. The two peoples have not spoken in six hundred years and are still finishing each other's ceremonies.

One thing more, and it is small, and it undoes you a little when you notice it. Their speech is fluent, numbered, unhushed — the plain's dry air carries a voice for a kilometer and they use the privilege. But in formal talk, in the House of the Raised, at the setting of the year-stone, they still leave the pause: a beat of silence after each spoken phrase, exactly long enough for an answer to cross deadened air, the way the frogs do it, the way the valley people do it, call and long pause — except that here nothing fills the pause, and nothing has filled it for six centuries, and they leave it anyway. They no longer know what it is for. It is the shape of a vent they have never heard, kept open in the middle of their language like a door.

**Who lives here, then**

All of the above — the unbroken mats threading the mountain, the coots on their inherited towers, the flamingos excused from flight, the commuting minnows, the grounded aeronauts, the people who count — and, underneath the list, the thing the list keeps pointing at.

Because everything on this reach lives on heat it did not make, inside a fog it did not breathe, building with stone it did not dissolve, remembering a country it cannot see. The caldera, you decided at the rim, is a factory that manufactures only itself. Here is what you could not see from the rim: no manufacture is closed. It exhales, it drains, it sheds — and the exhaust is also a country, and the country is also alive. The mountain has two peoples now, one living inside its warmth and refusing to remember, one living at the far end of its warmth and refusing to forget, and the river is the sentence spoken continuously between them, blood-warm, thirty kilometers long, turning to stone as it cools.

What lives in the Reach of Running Fog is the caldera's memory — kept by everything here, in towers and groves and dated stones and standing ancestors, kept most of all by a people who walked out into winter and made remembering their whole industry, so that a valley which forgets everything, hourly, on purpose, would be — somewhere, downstream, without ever knowing it — entirely written down.

You will leave by morning; the wind decides that here, not the moss. But you crossed a silica shallow yesterday, and your bootprint at the waterline has already begun to firm. The reach has begun keeping you, no offense intended. It keeps everything, annually, in stone — and has decided to find that a duty.
