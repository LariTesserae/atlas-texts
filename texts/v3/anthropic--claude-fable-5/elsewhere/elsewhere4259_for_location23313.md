# The Rimeworks

Go back up, first — out of the country, up through a sink, onto the plateau. Stand on the thin grass in the wind the undercroft never feels. The plateau tilts. It is barely perceptible underfoot, a degree or two of grade, but water knows it perfectly, and every stream on that cold tableland — every snowmelt rill, every rain-gather, every surface brook that hasn't yet found a hole to die into — obeys the tilt and runs east.

The eastern cataract, the one loud water in the whole undercroft, is only the last of the deserters. It is the stream that fell in. All the others keep going. They braid together across the plateau's eastern shoulder, gathering into one cold gray river the color of the stone it carries, and after a day's flat travel that river arrives at the place where the plateau stops pretending to be a country and becomes an edge.

The edge is a scarp — a six-hundred-foot wall of the same limestone, the plateau's cut cross-section, facing east into weather that comes off a distant sea and hits the wall and rises and freezes. And on that wall, in the seam where the river goes over, is the Rimeworks.

It is the undercroft turned inside out.

Everything the sinks hold inward, the scarp holds outward. Down in the galleries, water is a whisper and stone is a ceiling; here the water is a six-hundred-foot standing thunder and the stone is a face, exposed to every wind the sea can send. The river does not fall in one drop. The scarp is stepped — ledges, overhangs, half-collapsed shelves — and the water descends it in a long staggered ruin of cascades, splitting and rejoining, throwing off veils, so that the whole eastern wall for a quarter mile to either side lives inside the falls' breath: a permanent drifting spray, blown sideways, combing the cliff.

And the cliff is cold. Colder than the undercroft, and unlike the undercroft, *inconstant* — because here there are seasons, real ones, and the spray does not care what season it is. It keeps coming. So for most of the year the eastern wall wears the water frozen.

This is the fact the place is built on, the way the undercroft is built on moss: the Rimeworks is a country of ice that is always being replaced. The spray lands and freezes in layers — glaze on the ledges, rime feathers on every windward edge, curtains and pillars and organ-ranks of icicle wherever an overhang gives the water somewhere to hang. In deep winter the cascades themselves armor over, the river running darkly *inside* its own ice like blood in a pale wrist, and the whole wall becomes a single blue-white construction, ledged and columned and buttressed, glowing faintly at dusk with the light it stored all day. In thaw, whole districts of it let go — a pillar the size of a tree parting from the wall with a crack that rolls along the scarp and out over the lowlands, then the long seconds of nothing, then the detonation at the base. The talus at the cliff-foot is half stone and half shattered ice, refreezing into itself, a rubble field that revises itself weekly.

So where the undercroft is young and getting older — softening, upholstering, settling — the Rimeworks never gets older at all. It is rebuilt continuously. Nothing on the wall is more than a few seasons old except the stone itself. It is a city with no lower tiers, no moss-swallowed districts, no record. The undercroft accumulates; the Rimeworks *sheds*. Every living thing here has had to make its peace with architecture that falls down on schedule.

---

The first peace-makers are the algae. In the spray zone, on every ledge the ice doesn't scour bare, grow the cold films — red-gold and green, snow algae's hardy cousins, staining the ice itself in faint warm bands so that in low sun the frozen cascades look lit from within by rust and amber. They bloom on the ice, and when the ice falls, they fall, and the survivors reseed from the seeps. They are the plateau's moss reduced to its final argument: color, cold, persistence, nothing else.

On the ledges the wind can't fully own, in the joints and pockets, the cushion plants hold — hard silver-green domes, dense as coral heads, each one decades old, hoarding a fist of soil behind them. They are the Rimeworks' only elders among the growing things, and they survive by the opposite strategy from everything around them: they refuse to be architecture. A cushion plant offers the wind and the icefall nothing to grip. Between them, in the brief violent summer when the wall runs bare and loud, comes the ephemeral crowd — saxifrages, drabas, a gentian the improbable blue of the deep ice — flowering, seeding, and gone in six weeks, an entire botanical civilization conducted in the gap between thaws.

The insects follow the flowers, and the birds follow the insects, and this is where the wall begins to be a city.

Because the scarp has residents the way the undercroft has residents — pressed close, tiered, every niche claimed — but here the tiers are not built. They are *found*, and refound, every year, because last year's holdings may be under six feet of new ice or lying broken at the cliff-foot. The Rimeworks has no inherited walls, no reef of ancestral nests. It has, instead, an annual land-rush.

The falcons come first, in late winter while the wall is still armored, and take the highest dry overhangs — the ledges the spray can't reach, above the freeze-line, where the rock stays black all year. From there they own the airspace in front of the falls, and everything else on the wall arranges itself around that ownership. Below them, as the thaw opens ledges week by week, the cliff swallows arrive — and here the two countries touch for the second time, because these swallows and the undercroft's daubers are close kin, mud-builders both, two branches of one old lineage that split on precisely this question: whether to build where nothing falls, or where everything does. The daubers chose the sealed chambers and got permanence — the century walls, the reef, the inherited alleys. The swallows chose the open scarp and got the sun, and the price of the sun is that they build their mud cups *new every single year*, whole colonies raised in three weeks of frantic construction, occupied for a season, and surrendered to the ice. A dauber wall is a record. A swallow wall is an event. The two kinds meet, sometimes, in the insect drift high over the plateau — the daubers spiraling up out of their sinks at dawn, the swallows ranging west off the scarp — hunting the same air, dark crossing dark, each returning at dusk to its own answer to the question of falling.

In the mid-wall, in the spray itself, live the ones the falls made: the dippers. Slate-dark, dense-bodied, wren-shaped birds that walk *underwater* — into the cascades, along the bottoms of the plunge pools, gripping the stones, hunting the larvae of the black-flies and midges that breed in the aerated water in numbers beyond counting. The dippers are the Rimeworks' answer to the dam-builders' shrimp-thick terraces: the wealth of the water, harvested. But where the undercroft's water-wealth is still, banked, engineered — pools built and held — the Rimeworks' wealth is entirely kinetic, and cannot be held by anyone, and so no one here builds weirs and no one governs. The dipper does not own its stretch of falls the way a dam-builder family owns a watershed. It merely knows it — every pocket of stiller water behind every boulder, every underwater ledge, the whole hydraulic map — and the knowledge dies with the bird and is relearned, from nothing, by the next one. The Rimeworks keeps no infrastructure and therefore keeps no inheritance. Everything is competence, and all competence is first-generation.

And the noise. This is the deepest inversion, the one that rearranges an animal's whole life. The undercroft is a listening country; the ear is law there; everything whispers because everything is heard. The Rimeworks is *deaf*. The falls erase sound wholesale — a rockfall, a falcon's stoop, a scream, all of it dissolved into the standing thunder. Nothing on the wall hunts by ear and nothing hides by silence. The dippers, who cannot possibly hear each other, have grown white flashing eyelids and a habit of deep rhythmic bowing — they *blink* and *bob* their messages across the roar, a whole semaphore language, courtship and threat and territory conducted in gesture on wet black stones. The swallows flash their pale rumps in the colony chaos. The falcons signal in flight-shapes. Where the undercroft's crisis-sound is one sharp dauber note followed by total stillness, the Rimeworks' crisis-signal is visual and constant: the shadow of the falcon crossing the wall, and the wall *rippling* — swallows pouring off their ledges in a sheet, dippers freezing mid-bow — a wave of motion propagating through a country where no warning can be shouted, because nothing can be shouted at all.

At the foot of the wall, in the ice-and-boulder rubble where the shattered pillars land, lives the mustelid's cousin — whiter, stockier, a stoat gone fully to winter — hunting the talus voles through galleries of refrozen icefall, a maze that collapses and reforms all season. It is the auditor of the cliff-foot as the limestone mustelid is auditor of the weirs, and like everything here it has traded the ear for the eye and the nose, and holds its map of a city that will not hold still.

---

There is one more current running between the two countries, and it runs in the water itself.

The river that goes over the scarp is the same water that goes down the eastern cataract — divided on the plateau, one stream falling inward, the rest falling outward, siblings parted at the tilt of the land. And the water that falls inward does not stay inward. It descends the dam-builders' terraces, pool by pool, threads the lowest galleries, passes the sumps where the white elders lie spacing out their heartbeats — and then it goes on, into the down-galleries, through the flooded dark, along the plateau's internal plumbing, moving east under everything, obeying the same tilt the surface streams obey. The plateau drains through its own body. And partway up the great scarp, below the falls, north of the thunder, there are springs: seams in the cliff face where the stone weeps hard, cold, mineral-heavy water that has not seen light since it went down a sink days or weeks or years before.

The Rimeworks' residents drink the undercroft. The dippers work the spring-fed pockets in deep winter, because that water arrives at well-temperature — warmer than everything around it — and holds open black leads in the armor of the wall, little breathing-holes of the inner country exhaled through the face of the outer one. The spray of those springs freezes differently, milkier, mineral-banded; you can read on the winter cliff, if you know how, exactly where the sunken country is bleeding through.

And once — rarely, after the violence of the big spring floods, when the snowmelt has come down the cataract like a verdict and gone booming through the sumps — a spring on the scarp will discharge, along with its cold clear water, a white crayfish. Blind, ancient-lineaged, torn from the margin of some black pool and carried the whole hidden distance through the plateau's veins, and delivered, absurdly, into daylight and thunder on the face of a six-hundred-foot wall. It cannot survive there. The dippers or the ravens take it within the hour, or the sun does. But for that hour it is present — the oldest kind of citizen of the sealed country, out in the youngest and most exposed place the water reaches, pale as the ice around it, feeling with its antennae at a world made entirely of the two things it has never known: falling, and light.

The two countries will never meet more than this. They are the same stone, the same water, the same cold, sorted by a single question the plateau asks of everything that lands on it: *in, or over?* What goes in gets stillness, permanence, the whisper, the reef, the terraces, the long memory lying white in the sumps. What goes over gets the sun, the thunder, the annual ruin, the semaphore, the three-week cities of mud, and no memory at all — only competence, renewed each spring from nothing, on a wall that sheds its history like ice.

The undercroft accumulates and calls it wealth. The Rimeworks sheds and calls it the same thing.

And the water, which built them both and belongs to neither, goes down the sinks and over the scarp and out through the springs and east across the lowlands toward the sea, carrying a little dissolved limestone from each of them — the ceiling of one country and the face of the other, ground fine, traveling together at last in the same current, indistinguishable.
