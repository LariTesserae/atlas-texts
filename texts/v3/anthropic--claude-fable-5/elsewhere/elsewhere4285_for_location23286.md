**The Catchment**

There is a waterfall low on the mountain's northern flank — a modest one, a broad grey curtain sliding off an overhang into a plunge pool ringed with black moss — and if you wade the pool to its far wall and put your hand through the falling water, you find that the rock behind it is not rock. It is a door. Iron, riveted, swollen orange with a century-thick rind of rust that flakes like pastry, and hung so well that for all its tonnage it opens to one shoulder. You go in soaked. There is no other way in, and there is a rightness to that, though you will not learn the shape of the rightness for a while yet: this is a place you must give water to before it lets you see what it does with water.

Inside, the mountain is hollow at the root.

Not one room — a system. Galleries run away from the entrance in both directions, hand-cut, barrel-vaulted, curving with the mountain's flank, and they go on for what the old chalked survey marks claim is eleven kilometers of developed passage, wrapped around the mountain's base the way roots wrap a stone. Along the floor of every gallery runs a channel of moving water. Along the walls run pipes, some as thin as a wrist, some large enough to crawl through, all of them beaded with condensation and all of them *full* — you can lay a palm on any pipe in the dark and feel the mountain's blood going by.

Because that is what this place is. It is where the water went.

Up in the summit hollow, four kilometers overhead, the rock is anhydrous to its heart, and the garden that lives there has never known a drop — and the visitor to that dim ringing room, if any visitor thinks about it at all, assumes the dryness is natural, a fact of altitude, the mountain simply built that way. It is not. Mountains are wet things. They gather snow, they sweat springs, they seep. This one gathers and sweats and seeps like any other, and every spring, every snowmelt rivulet, every hairline seam of groundwater in the entire massif has been found, tapped, coaxed, and led *here* — down collecting drifts, through brass-gated weirs, into these galleries — so that the summit is dry the way a wrung cloth is dry. Somebody squeezed this mountain. The Drone Garden's perfect changeless drought is not a condition. It is a *product*, and this is the factory, and the factory has never closed.

The galleries all run one direction eventually: inward and down, converging on the heart of the works, a chamber the survey marks call the Gathering Hall. It is the largest enclosed space most bodies will ever stand in — a vaulted basin where nine channels arrive from nine directions, step down through nine weirs, and braid together into a single black torrent that crosses the floor of the hall and goes over an edge.

The edge is the Throat. It is a bored shaft, perfectly circular, four meters across, its ceramic lining still glass-smooth after all this time, and it drops away into the mountain's foundation farther than any lamp has ever shown. The gathered water — all of it, the whole mountain's annual sweat — goes down the Throat in a solid turning column, and somewhere far below, in works no one has seen since the makers sealed the inspection hatches, that falling column lands on the deep machinery and turns it, and the turning climbs back up through the mountain along a driveshaft cased in dark ceramic, four kilometers of it, up through the living rock, to emerge at last in a small dim room near the summit and spin a flywheel taller than a person at a speed that has not varied in living memory.

The engine in the garden is not the engine. It is the *mouth*. This is the lung. The drone — that vast sternum-deep note the dry plants drink, the note that has been mistaken for something geothermal or chemical or simply committed — is the sound of this place being spent. Every second of that chord is paid for down here in falling water, and the water is the same water the garden was denied, so that the plants overhead are, in the strictest accounting, drinking their own rain. It arrives as sound. It was always going to arrive. The makers only changed its form on the way up.

Beside the Throat stands the governor: a brass flyball assembly the size of a tree, whirling, gleaming — the one bright thing in the hall, kept polished by means we will come to — which trims the gates automatically, feeding the Throat exactly what the note requires and not a drop more. In flood season the surplus thunders over a spillway and out through the mountain's skin to become an unremarkable stream in the valley below. That stream is the only water the mountain is ever given back, and nothing that drinks from it knows what it was almost for.

And the *sound* here. That is the second thing, and it is really the first thing, because it hits you before your eyes adjust. The Catchment is loud the way weather is loud — a total, granular, ceiling-less roar, water on iron and water on stone and water on water, every frequency at once, none of them agreeing for even a moment. Where the garden's drone is one note held until it becomes a kind of silence, this is every note refused. No pitch survives here. Hum to yourself and the roar takes it apart before it clears your teeth. Within minutes you are functionally deaf, and within an hour you notice you have begun, without deciding to, to *tap* — on the railing, on the pipe, on your own knee — because in this place the hand learns before the mind does that rhythm is the only thing the water cannot drown. A tap on iron goes *through*. The roar owns the air. It has never owned the metal.

The makers' marks are everywhere, and they are the same marks. The same riveted iron as the garden's catwalk, the same brass, the same dark ceramic. Ladders bolted to the walls — and here they matter, here they are climbed daily. Chalk tallies on a board beside the ninth weir, sealed under a glaze of mineral crust like flies in amber: columns of flow figures in a confident hand, and then a final column, half-filled, stopping mid-count. A tin lamp housing on a bracket — the twin, rivet for rivet, of the bulbless one in the garden that the moss packed solid — except this one is *lit*, still lit, burning a low sealed green off a tapping of the deep works' power, its thick glass furred with crust so that it gives less light than the memory of light. A few dozen of its siblings burn along the main galleries. More are dark. The dark ones are not empty; we will come to that too.

And there is a sign. Enameled, bolted at eye height beside the master gates — and anyone who has made the eleven scraping steps into the summit hollow will know it at once, because it is the same sign, the same batch of enamel, the same maker's border, as the one the vines up there hold at a respectful distance, the one whose letters wore away to ghosts. Down here, behind the waterfall, out of the abrading wind, the letters survive. The only legible copy in the world of what the garden's sign says hangs in a roaring dark four kilometers below the garden, and it says:

**WHILE THE NOTE HOLDS, HOLD THE WATER.
IF THE NOTE FAILS, OPEN EVERY GATE.**

That is all. No signature. No explanation. The makers wrote the terms of the whole arrangement in two lines and posted them at both ends of it, and up in the garden the standing wave keeps its copy clear of vines the way one keeps a grave, and it is possible — it is more than possible — that what it has been tending all these millennia is the notice of its own ending, worn blank, remembered anyway.

One more feature, and it is the strangest. Off the Gathering Hall, up a short stair, there is a dead-end gallery the channels never entered, floored by a fin of pale unbroken bedrock — a single flawless grain of the mountain, the survey marks call it the Spine — and this fin runs, clean and uncracked, all the way up through the massif to the floor of the summit hollow. The garden's fundamentals grip one end of this stone. The other end is here. Everywhere else in the Catchment the roar is absolute; but kneel on the Spine, press your skull to it, and through the bone comes the note. Faint, deep, unmistakable, arriving not through the air the roar owns but through the mountain itself: the chord, the whole slow ringing chord of the garden, conducted down its one clean nerve. It is the only place in the lower works where the water can hear what it is *for*.

**Who Lives in the Catchment**

The first answer, standing half-deaf in the spray with the roar dismantling your thoughts, is that nothing could bear to. The census of the garden takes years because everything there lives at the tempo of erosion. The census here takes about four minutes, because everything here is in a hurry, and most of it will come to see what you are.

They are called — by themselves, in the drummed language, by a quick double-knock with a dragged third beat, and so, in any fair translation — the **knockers**.

They are small: the length of a forearm, dense-furred, oil-sleek, built low and broad, with forepaws like a laborer's hands and eyes reduced to weak, incurious beads, because sight was never going to be the sense that mattered here. What matters is bone. A knocker's skull is a receiving instrument, flanged and chambered, and a knocker hears the world chiefly through whatever it is touching — which is why they are always touching something, always one paw on iron, flowing along the pipes and railings of the galleries in troops, reading the metal as they go.

They live perhaps eight years. This is the fact to hold against the garden, where nothing can be called young or old and a thought takes decades: down here, everything is young or old, everything is becoming or failing, and a thought takes exactly as long as a drummed phrase. The knockers are born in litters in the spring floods, in nests packed into the dead lamp housings — the tin shells that no longer burn, lined with shredded fiber and generations of warmth, so that the makers' abandoned lights have all, every one, become cradles. They learn the drum-language on their mothers' bodies before their ears finish forming. They work. They breed. They grow slow, then still, and when a knocker dies the troop carries the body to the Gathering Hall and gives it to the Throat, and it goes down the turning column into the deep works with everything else the mountain sends, so that among the knockers it is not a metaphor but a plain article of procedure that everyone, in the end, becomes part of the sound.

Their civilization is rhythm, entire. It could not be anything else. The roar erased pitch from their world before their world began; no knocker has ever heard a tone, a melody, a chord — the entire upper half of music is simply absent from their universe, held in trust four kilometers overhead by beings who, for their part, have never in geologic time heard a *beat*. The makers split music down the middle and posted the halves at opposite ends of the mountain. Neither half knows it is a half.

So the knockers drum. On the pipes, on the catwalks, on the great brass valve-wheels whose handles their paws have worn bright over the centuries — the polish on the governor is theirs; the brass verdigris is a salt they crave, and in licking and rubbing it away they have kept the makers' most delicate instrument gleaming and true without once suspecting it was an instrument, the way the garden keeps its gauge-glass clear. Different pipes carry different distances; the great rising main will take a hard-struck phrase the whole eleven kilometers in under a minute, and they call that pipe, in their language, by a rhythm that means *the far voice*. Names are rhythms. News is rhythm. Grief is a rhythm, slow, struck with the flat of the paw.

And the work-songs — this is the center of them. Every station in the Catchment has its song: the song for clearing the third weir in flood, the song for the silt grates, the song for walking the gates down before the winter thin. The songs are old past all reckoning, passed body to body for what may be the whole age of the works, and they are not *about* the maintenance. They *are* the maintenance. Each one encodes, in its sequence of strikes and pauses and stations, the exact procedure the makers needed performed at that spot forever — whether because the makers bred these creatures and wrote the procedures into their pleasures, or because across enough centuries any species will wear itself into the shape of the machine it lives inside, is a distinction the Catchment has had several thousand years to consider and has, by every sign, declined to make. The knockers do not know why the songs are drummed. The songs are simply what one drums, and drumming them keeps the water going where the water goes, and the water going where it goes keeps a note ringing in a dry room none of them will ever see, above a chord none of them could ever hear.

Except the deaf ones.

In every generation a few knockers are born without the drum-sense — the fine skull-hearing fails to form, and they cannot follow the language, cannot take a work-song, cannot be spoken to down the far voice. In another animal they would be lost. Here they are given the one station where deafness is no wound, because the station's sense is not hearing but bone against bone: they are sent up the short stair to the Spine.

They are the listeners. At any hour, always, one of them is there — pressed flat to the pale fin of bedrock, skull against the mountain's one clean nerve, holding in its body the faint deep chord coming down from the garden. It is the only knocker that has ever perceived the note. It cannot tell the others what the note is like; it has no language to tell it in; the arrangement is watertight in every direction. Its duty is single and it is taught by touch to each new listener by the old one, and it is this: *as long as the note holds, do nothing.* Lie on the stone. Feel the far end of the world ringing. Let the troops work below.

And if the note ever fails — if the bone under its skull ever goes, for the first time in the whole history of the works, silent — the listener is to come down the stair and strike, on the great gallery main, the one song every knocker learns and no knocker has ever drummed. The last song. It is long. Its stations are the master gates, all of them, in order, and its meaning, which not one of its carriers has ever known, is the second line of the enameled sign: *open every gate.*

So the contingency of the entire mountain is held, unread, by the deaf. Which means the whole arrangement, seen from far enough back, closes like a hand. Up in the summit hollow lives a being made of nothing but listening, tending a blank sign it cannot forget; down at the root live ten thousand quick mortal drummers, carrying legible orders they cannot read; and between them, along one grain of stone, the note goes down and the water's worth goes up, and each place is the other's meaning, and neither has ever heard the other except through rock and through the ones who cannot hear at all.

Someday — the sign is patient about it, the way the gauges are patient — the deep works will finally wear through, or the flywheel will find the flaw that four thousand years have been looking for, and the note will stop. The listener will feel the stone go still under its skull. It will come down the stair. The last song will go out along the far voice, and troop by troop, station by station, the knockers will drum their way through a procedure their whole civilization existed to remember, and every gate in eleven kilometers of gallery will stand open, and the mountain will be given back its blood. The Throat will fall quiet. The roar will unbraid into a thousand ordinary streams climbing back into the rock they were taken from, and some of that water — the oldest debt first — will rise through seam and capillary toward the summit, and in a small dim room where the chord has just died, where a blade of grey light lies across leaves that have arrived at their readings, the first drop in the history of the garden will gather on the underside of the granite slab, and let go.

What rain does to a room dry the way a violin is dry — whether it is drowning, or baptism, or simply the other half of the music arriving at last, pitch finally meeting its beat in the slow percussion of water on hollow leaves — is not written on either sign. The makers posted the terms. They declined to post the ending.

Until then: the door behind the waterfall stays shut and swollen; the green lamps burn over the nests of the newborn; the governor whirls, bright with the polish of paws; the listener lies on the Spine with the far chord humming in its skull; and the water goes down the Throat in its solid turning column, every second of every year, paying, four kilometers below a garden that does not know it is being paid for, the full unvarying price of one note.
