**The Mouths at Vorne**

Every drop of water at Caldn is leaving. That is easy to forget on the plateau, where the pools lie so still and the steam goes up so patiently that the whole place reads as a held breath — but the terraces are not a reservoir, they are a stair, and the water is always descending it, film by soundless film, giving up its heat and its mineral one rim at a time. At the foot of the lowest terrace, where the travertine finally runs out of appetite, what is left gathers into a channel and goes over a last natural lip — the Overfall, the Caldn people call it, on the rare occasions they mention it at all, in the tone one uses for a door at the back of the house. By then the water is barely warm. It has left its turquoise behind. It comes off the plateau a pale, exhausted blue, and it steams only in the early mornings, thinly, like something remembering.

Three days below, by the switchbacked trade track called the Stair Road, the water arrives at Vorne. It arrives changed, and the Vornish will tell you exactly how, because the Vornish tell you everything: the river comes down from the steps having given everything away, they say, and it gets to us hungry.

The country between is the first green a Caldn child ever sees — a stain of reeds along the banks in the high scrub, widening downstream into actual pasture, actual trees. Caldn families make the descent every few years for trade or weddings, and there is a small tradition around the moment a child of the steps first stands under leaves: some laugh, some go very quiet, one or two in every generation cry without being able to say why, and the Vornish, who find this both touching and hilarious, have a word for that particular weeping that translates roughly as *green-struck*. But the green is a border country only. Past it the land goes pale again — a broad grey-white limestone plain — and here the eye plays its first trick, because the plain at Caldn's foot is the plateau's negative. Where Caldn steps *up* in brimming basins, this country steps *down* in empty ones: dry valleys, closed hollows, wide dish-shaped sinks with nothing at the bottom of them, terrace-shapes with the water and the color subtracted. It looks like Caldn after some enormous settling of accounts. The resemblance is not a coincidence, though it takes the Vornish, or a geologist, to explain why: the limestone of the plain is travertine too, old beyond counting — terraces some ancestor of the plateau laid down when the heat lived elsewhere, now cold, roofed with soil, and being taken back. The plateau is not the first of its kind. It is only the one currently writing.

The river crosses this plain for a dozen miles, cold now, clear, aggressive — undersaturated, a chemist would say; the Vornish say *unburdened* — and then it reaches the Mouths and goes into the ground.

There are five Mouths. Four take seepage and floodwater. The First Mouth takes the river entire: a limestone throat eighty feet across, the water dropping into it around a standing rock the shape of a molar, and the noise of that drop is the second thing about Vorne, and the first thing a visitor from Caldn fails to survive with any composure. Caldn drowns sound. Vorne multiplies it. The air here is cool and dry and carries; the galleries below are sounding-chambers a mile long; the roar of the First Mouth is audible across the whole plain and is not, to a Vornish ear, noise at all — it is the reference tone, the thing every other sound stands in front of. Vornish navigate by it the way sailors navigate by a coast. Turn your head anywhere within ten miles and some part of you knows exactly where the Mouth is. Caldn people, arriving down the Stair Road, are known to stop at the first point where the roar becomes distinct and stand there for a while with an expression the Vornish drivers have learned not to comment on: the face of someone hearing the world speak up after a lifetime of it whispering.

The town is built at the lip of the First Mouth and down into the first dry galleries below it — half in daylight, half in lamplight, and the lamplight is Caldn's. A single high-tension line comes down the gorge beside the Stair Road, on pylons that are crusted bone-white at the top of the run and clean black iron at the bottom, the wire's whole descent legible as a change of skin. The turbines no one at Caldn has opened in a generation feed it. It is Caldn's noon, stored in falling water and copper, that lights Vorne's permanent midnight, and the Vornish know it, and pay for it in the up-going trade — timber, lamp oil, grain, glass, saw blades, rope: everything, as they say, that burns, cuts, or grows, sent up to a town that has none of the three. Down comes current, and lime. The white dust the rim-cutters saw from Caldn's stilts and doorways each season is sacked and sold, and the Vornish whitewash with it, so that the houses of Vorne wear, quite literally, Caldn's shavings; a Vornish house is painted in the ground-up rims of pools three days' climb away, and if you told a Vornish householder that some of that dust had once been part of somebody's grandmother's Settling Pool, they would think about it for a moment and then say, correctly, that at those prices it had better be.

The houses themselves are the third thing. There is not one stone building in Vorne, in a country made entirely of stone, and the reason is under everyone's feet: the ground here opens. The river and its seepage are dissolving the plain from below, and every few years a street, a field, a corner of somebody's yard drops ten feet in a night and becomes a new small sink. So Vorne builds in timber, pegged rather than nailed, every frame made to be unmade in a day, and the town has stood — the roll-keepers can list the sites in order, with the reasons — in nine places in living song. Each spring the echo-masters sound the streets: walking the town with long-headed mallets and iron-shod staffs, striking the ground, singing single sustained notes into it, listening for the hollow places rising. Where Caldn's danger is the stone coming up gently around a thing, Vorne's is the stone quietly ceasing to be beneath it. Caldn is being kept. Vorne is being let go of, continuously, and has organized itself around that with the same fluency the steps have organized around the opposite.

And the land breathes. This is the fourth thing, the one the visitor notices at night. The galleries below Vorne connect, through miles of dark, to other mouths and cracks across the whole plain, and the underworld exhales and inhales with the weather — a barometric wind, days long in each phase, pouring steadily out of the Mouths when the pressure falls and drawing steadily in when it climbs. Caldn's scattered engines sigh together every few minutes; Vorne's entire country breathes once every few days, one vast slow respiration you can stand in the doorway of. The Vornish set their lives to it. Fires are banked on the in-breath days, when the Mouths swallow smoke and air alike, and lit freely on the out-breath, when the land carries every chimney's plume up and away itself; there are songs for each phase, and the older doors of the underground quarter are hung to swing one way in the inhale and the other in the exhale, so that the town's hinges keep the count.

Everything alive down there is fed from above — and *above*, followed far enough, means the steps. The river's freight is Caldn's surplus: sloughed rags of thermophile mat, rust-orange and arterial red, fading grey as they travel; drifted scums of spent glasswings; rimflea molts by the uncountable billion. All of it pours into the dark, and the dark eats it. Blind white fish school in the galleries, steering by vibration, netted at timber weirs the Vornish build in the underground reaches — palefish on the table, sweet and faintly mineral, the mineral being, though no one puts it this way at dinner, the taste of the plateau. Blind crayfish graze the drowned ledges. In the dome of the First Mouth nest the echo-birds, thousands of them, oily dark things that pour out at dusk in a clicking, chirring column and return before dawn, and their going and coming is the town's clock, as reliable as the wading birds' lifted heads are Caldn's metronome. And at every household weir lives a well-eel or two — yard-long, blind, naked, cold, the kettle-dog's inverse in every particular: where the kettle-dog turns slowly to stone in the heat, the eel stays soft as the day it hatched for its sixty or eighty years in the cold. Well-eels feel floods coming. Days before storm water off the high catchment reaches the Mouths, the eels rise and circle and will not settle, and the town packs. A good proven eel has gone in more than one dowry, and this is only partly a joke.

The strangeness of Vorne, like Caldn's, arrives on the second day, and it arrives in the walls. The galleries are cut through the old dead travertine, and the water, dissolving it, exposes its cross-sections the way Caldn's broken terrace lips expose theirs: fossil rims, fossil pool-floors, the ghost-shapes of an ancient plateau held in the stone — and, in the deep reaches, things the divers began reporting generations ago and have never stopped reporting. Plank-lines. Ladder-shapes. Long pale forms lying straight, on frames, in rows, standing half-proud of a dissolving wall for a season or two before the water finishes with them and they are gone. The Vornish do not excavate. They would consider it both futile and rude. Instead the diver who finds a sleeper describes it, exactly, to a roll-keeper, and the description goes into the rolls, and so the spoken memory of Vorne now contains careful accounts of people from a town that has no name, who were laid in hot water under an enormous light unimaginably long ago, kept perfectly for an age of the world, and then unkept. Asked what it means — asked whether there was another Caldn, whether there will be, whether the one above knows — a roll-keeper will recite the relevant entries in a level voice and then decline. *We keep what it says*, the formula runs, *not what it means.*

**The People of the Mouths**

Whoever lives at Vorne is audible first. In the galleries you hear a person long before you see their lamp — a snatch of song, a spoken announcement of themselves into the dark ahead, because in Vorne a silent person is presumed lost, hurt, or dead, and silence in an inhabited passage will bring runners. Everything about the steps is inverted here at the level of the body. Where Caldn's air rations speech to three feet and makes every conversation cost a journey, Vorne's air is a gift economy of sound: a trained voice carries half a mile down a gallery, an echo-master can hold a conversation around two bends and a sump, work crews who will not meet for weeks court each other by pitched calls across the dark, and children are given carrying-names — two long open vowels, built to travel — before they are given anything else. Talk at Vorne costs nothing, and so it never stops, a continuous low web of voice through the whole town, and the visitor from Caldn, where no talk is small, takes days to understand that almost none of it is meant to matter. What matters at Vorne is not said; it is *kept* — entered into the fixed forms, the rolls, the sung registers — because the Vornish long ago confronted the central fact of their country, which is that nothing else will keep it.

Caldn writes everything into stone and lets memory go; the broken rims hold the schoolteacher and the fisherman, and the one nobody remembers is called a completion. Vorne's stone forgets everything — dissolves the house, opens under the field, takes the grave — and so the Vornish became the rememberers. The roll-keepers hold the census in their heads: every genealogy to its depth, the nine sites of the town and why each was left, the flood years, the descriptions of the dissolved sleepers, the field-names of ground that no longer exists. A Vornish woman can tell you her grandmother's grandmother's carrying-name, her weir, her eel, and where she broke her wrist, and cannot point to any part of the world and say *she is there*. A Caldn man walks daily over his grandmother and could not tell you her mother's name. Each town regards the other's arrangement as quietly unbearable, and each has, over the generations, stopped saying so.

Their bodies are the steps' bodies reversed. Lean where the Caldn folk are soft, dry-skinned, heavily callused from rope and timber and cold water — the rim-cutters of Caldn have palms like children; a Vornish weir-diver's hands could strike sparks. Long-lunged from the diving, wide-eyed from the lamplight, and their hearing tuned past anything an outsider can follow: a Vornish child can stand in a strange room, hum once, and tell you its size with her eyes shut. In great age it is not the eyes that go but the ears — the old of Vorne fade into deafness the way the old of Caldn fog into opaline blindness — and the town speaks to them by touch, a tapped language on the wrist and forearm, so that both towns, at the far end of life, arrive at the same tactile dialect from opposite directions, which the few people who have grown old in both places report as the single thing that made the other town feel like home.

They cook with fire, and love it. Open flame, which Caldn regards as a crude and jumpy way of doing what the plateau does gracefully, is the hearth of the underworld: the smell of woodsmoke on an out-breath day, pulled up the carved chimneys and carried off by the exhaling land, is the smell of Vorne the way soundless steam is the sight of Caldn. Their cold they get the way Caldn gets its heat — from the ground, for nothing. The river in the galleries runs a few degrees above freezing year-round, and every household has its cold-hold sunk in the current, so that the trade between the towns has a shape any Vornish child can recite: the steps have all the heat in the world and cannot keep anything; the Mouths have all the cold in the world and cannot grow anything; and the Stair Road exists so that each town can have the other's impossible thing.

People travel it in both directions, and mostly return. Vornish go up for wages — wire work, valve work; their ear for machinery is prized, and a Vornish valve-keeper is said to hear a bearing going a season before it complains. Caldn young come down for a rite that has no official standing and universal observance: to stand in a gallery and shout, once, and hear the world answer. Most go home within the month. Marriages happen along the road, and the children of them are said, with the fondness both towns reserve for the road itself, to sleep badly in either place and perfectly in the wagons between. And each people carries its town in the body where the other can see it. Every crowd in Vorne can pick out the step-born by the sigh — that deep periodic exhalation, every few minutes, in time with engines three days' climb away. Every porch at Caldn can pick out the Vornish guest by the humming: the small note dropped into each new room, each shed, each stretch of causeway, asking the space its size.

The steam eats the note. That is, in the end, why the Vornish cannot stay up there — not the heat, which they bear stoically, but the silence that returns nothing. A Vornish wire-woman who worked two years at Caldn put it in a sentence that has since entered the rolls, because the Vornish keep sentences the way Caldn keeps buttons and milk teeth: *the plateau is the only room I ever stood in that did not answer, and a room that does not answer is not empty — it is holding its breath.* And the step-born fail at Vorne for the mirrored reason. They are not undone by the noise. They are undone by the breathing — by waiting, every few minutes, for the small communal exhale their bodies were raised on, and receiving instead one enormous respiration days long, the whole dark country inhaling around them for so many hours that, as one returned Caldn man told his family, it was like living in the chest of something too large to ever hear you.

Their dead they give to the water, which is the one thing the towns share, and the thing they share least. There is no Settling Pool at Vorne; there is the Taking. The body goes onto a light raft of reeds at the weir above the First Mouth, with a small lamp at its head, and the whole roll-name is sung — the full genealogy, minutes long, the singers timing it so the last name sounds as the current takes the raft over the lip — and the lamp is visible a surprisingly long way down into the dark, the Vornish say, longer every time you watch it, and then it is not. Nothing kept; everything remembered. At Caldn the plateau closes over you and holds you where your family can stand; at Vorne the river carries you into a dark that has, demonstrably, in its deepest walls, given up the kept dead of a town older than any name — and neither people will say aloud what both have plainly thought: that the sleepers dissolving out of the deep stone are on the same journey the rafts are, only slower, and from the other end.

Because the water rises again. Days beyond Vorne, out past the plain in a green lowland the Vornish trade into but do not much visit, the river comes up at a place called, without ceremony, the Rising — comes up clear and full again, having eaten its way through the old dead terraces, carrying the ancient plateau in solution — and there, where it meets the air, it has already begun, quietly, to give. There is a low white rim at the Rising now, ankle-high, warm to a bare foot, growing a hair's width a year. Farmers' children play on it. The Vornish who have stood on it report two things: that it is unmistakably the stone of the steps, and that the water crossing it makes no sound at all — the first few feet, they say, of the silence, starting over.

So: what lives at the Mouths at Vorne. A few thousand loud, lean, calloused, long-lunged people who cook with fire in the ground and give their dead to the current; their soft cold eels and their blind white fish; their clicking birds and their breathing doors; their houses painted with another town's shavings and lit by another town's noon. That is the honest census. But stand at the lip of the First Mouth on an out-breath day, with the roar in front of you and the land's long exhale at your back, and the other accounting suggests itself — that Vorne and Caldn are not two places but two organs of one thing: that the plateau above is the hand that writes, a hair's width a year, and the Mouths below are where the world reads itself back, taking the old pages down in the dark and pulping them for ink; and that the people of Vorne, without ever deciding to, have become the reading voice — keeping in breath and roll and carrying-name everything the stone beneath them refuses to hold, including the dissolved strangers of a Caldn before Caldn — while far away at the Rising the water lays down the first warm rim of a place that does not exist yet, where someday something soft-skinned and low-voiced will live and be kept, and under it, already, the water that keeps it will be going cold, and turning, and learning again to take.
