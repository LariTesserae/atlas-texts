# Tellwater

The water leaves the steading without ceremony. Below the half-built eastern quarter, where the town's slow subsurface drainage finds the lip of the pass, it emerges as a single cold spring and simply goes — over the raw gray edge, down through couloirs still sharp enough to cut, under scree and out again, gathering other melt as it falls but never quite mixing with it, always identifiable, the watchers say, by its temperature and its patience. The other streams of the mountain are moody. They roar in the melt season and dwindle to a stain by late summer and lock solid in winter. The steading's water does none of this. It has been wrung from cloud by moss, filtered through a meter of the town's own dead generations, released down the weeping walls at a rate no weather can hurry, and it arrives in the valley below — a long day's fall from the pass — at the same volume and the same temperature on every day of every year of every decade anyone has measured.

They call it the Even Water. The village at the meeting of the becks is Tellwater, and it is where the watchers come from.

It is a built place, and it is old the way the steading is young. Its stone is the mountain's stone, but the mountain's *elder* stone, quarried from slopes broken long enough ago that the edges have gone round and the gray has furred over — every wall in Tellwater wears lichen, gold and rust and sea-green, so that the village, seen from above, looks half-returned to the hillside it was cut from. Which it is. Tellwater is five centuries old, give or take a lintel date, and it has been shrinking for the last one of them. There are forty-some houses along its two streets and its one crooked lane, and fewer than half hold anyone. The empty ones are not ruins, exactly — Tellwater does not let its houses become ruins if it can help it, for reasons that will become clear — but the roofs go eventually, whatever anyone intends, and where a roof has gone the house stands open to the sky, a room of stone holding a column of gray valley light and a floor of nettles. The village is opening. Doorway by doorway, roof by roof, it is becoming a place with more inside than out. Anyone who has read the watchers' books will feel the shape of this: it is the steading's motion run exactly backward. Up in the pass, a town with no builder closes its doorways year by year. Down here, a town that was all builders is unclosing, and cannot stop.

The two streets converge, as streets do, on water. At the center of Tellwater the becks meet, and across the Even Water — only the Even Water; the others are not worth the stone — sits a low weir, and beside the weir the tally-house, which is the oldest building in the village and the reason for its name. *Tell*, in the old speech of the valley, means to count. The tally-house is where the water is told. The Even Water is the one stream that never fails and never floods and never freezes, and so it is the stream the village's whole law grew around: its flow is divided by shares, the shares are cut as notches on a black oak board that has been notched and re-notched for centuries, and every name in Tellwater — every house, every garden, every trough — has its allotted thread of the steading's water, ducted through stone channels that vein the village like something anatomical. Twice a day, at first light and last, the reckoner reads the weir to the fingerwidth and enters the figure in the book. The figure has not meaningfully changed in living memory, or in dead memory either. The reckoner reads it anyway. That is the office. Somewhere at the top of the mountain, a fungus persuades a hundred roots to plumb a town it will never see; at the bottom, an old woman writes the same number in a ledger every morning of her life. Between them they keep the water honest, and neither has ever been thanked, and neither has noticed.

The water is cold when it arrives — barely above freezing, summer and winter, as though the pass had exhaled it a moment ago rather than a day. This matters more than it sounds. The Even Water runs through the valley as a ribbon of the steading's own climate: mist stands over it on warm days, frost follows its banks in seasons when frost has no other business in the valley, and along those chilled banks, and only there, the dwarf willow grows — the same high, hard species that walls the steading, riding down as catkin and seed and living twig, rooting at an altitude it should find intolerably soft. And it does not sprawl flat as its nature elsewhere insists. It goes *up*. It pleaches. Left alone for three seasons, the willow along the Even Water begins to braid itself into panels, and the panels begin to consider each other across the stream, and anyone from Tellwater can tell you what comes after panels. So every autumn the village cuts it to the waterline — the coppicing, the one day of the year when everyone still living in Tellwater works within sight of everyone else — and the air for a week afterward is bitter with willow bark, a clean medicinal bitterness, the smell of the other town's blood.

They do not waste the cutting. Tellwater is furnished, floor to rafter, with the steading's exported body: willow hurdles fencing the gardens, willow creels in the beck, willow baskets, willow cradles, and — this the village does not discuss with strangers, though it hides nothing — willow coffins, woven green, in which every one of Tellwater's dead for two hundred years has gone into the ground. The bark, chewed, is given for fevers, and, steeped, for grief; whether it works on the second is not the kind of question the valley asks. The village drinks the steading's water, weaves the steading's wood, buries its dead in the steading's arms, and once every few years sends one of its own up the long fall of the Even Water to sit in the pass for a season and write down what the town with no hands is doing. The shelf in the tally-house holds a hundred and thirty years of these notebooks. The first entry is a shepherd's, in a hand like bird tracks: *the pass is greening in rooms.* A later hand, drier: *it is become a green steading.* The name stuck. Everything anyone knows about the place — the counts of six birds, the probed depth of the pavement, the walker's trails read by lamplight, the doorway that stopped closing over a woman who stayed — is on that shelf, in the building where the water is counted, because Tellwater has only ever had the one way of loving anything, which is to keep its figure.

## The Inhabitants of Tellwater

Ask who lives in Tellwater and the village will answer you precisely, because precision is its whole devotion — and the precise answer is stranger than the steading's, because Tellwater's census refuses to shrink at the same rate the village does. Start, as the village would insist, with the largest category.

**The majority** are in the burial ground, on the slope above the weir, and they outnumber the living by something like forty to one. Five centuries of Tellwater lie there under slates cut with names, and the names are maintained — recut when the lichen swallows them, read aloud once a year on the coppicing night, entered in the book of the dead which sits on the shelf beside the watchers' notebooks as though the two were volumes of one work. Which, arguably, they are. The steading paves itself with its dead and walks on them, nameless, springing back from every footprint; Tellwater fences its dead and reads to them. Two towns, one water, opposite memories.

But the burial ground has developed a condition, and the condition is spreading. The coffins are willow, woven green, and green willow, given a grave's damp and the Even Water's cold seeping through that soil, sometimes declines to die. It roots. It rises. A grave here and there puts up shoots in its second spring, and the village law says pull them, and most families do, and some families — quietly, and more of them each decade — do not. In the burial ground's lower corner, nearest the water, there are now graves that have grown their own low pleached walls, knee-high, knitting at the corners, roofing over in moss: small green rooms, each holding one of Tellwater's dead. The village pretends not to have a word for what these are becoming. The watchers' notebooks, two shelves away, have had the word for a century.

**The living** number thirty-one at the last telling, and the shape of the number is worse than its size: three of the thirty-one are children, and there have been three children for eleven years, though never — and here the reckoner's book goes very quiet — quite the same three, as one grows out of childhood and no one grows into it. Up in the pass, a town keeps its birds at six across generations, effortlessly, replacing them one at a time out of sight, and no one knows how. Down here, a town that keeps figures on everything cannot keep its one figure filled. The reckoner enters the count each midwinter. She has stopped adding remarks.

**The reckoner** herself is the closest thing Tellwater has to the sexton under the steading's floor: the one inhabitant who touches every other from below. Her channels reach every house. Her ledgers hold every name, living, dead, and otherwise. She reads the weir twice daily, notches the black board when a share changes hands, keeps the coppicing rota, keeps the watchers' shelf, and trains — when there is anyone to train — her replacement, for the office is not hereditary but it has never once gone unfilled, which in a village of thirty-one is its own quiet miracle, or its own quiet law. She does not plan Tellwater. She persuades it, entry by entry, and the result resembles a village continuing the way the steading resembles a town: closely enough, so far.

**The beasts at the weir** deserve their line in the census because the village gives them one. In hard winters the Even Water is the only open water in the valley — everything else locked white — and a truce forms at the weir that no one declared and no one breaks: sheep and deer drinking within a body's length of each other, foxes waiting their turn, the village dogs sitting oddly still. The reckoner's predecessors recorded these gatherings the way they recorded everything, and in the winter book for a famine year four generations back there is an entry that the watchers have worn soft with rereading: *at the weir before light, a beast unknown, the weight of a dog and walking on pads, drank long and went back up the water. The dogs did not speak.* Nothing like it appears again. If the founder of the Green Steading was ever seen by human eyes, it was seen once, at the bottom of its world, drinking from the far end of its own architecture — and Tellwater, being Tellwater, wrote it down and let it go.

**The departed** are the category that makes Tellwater legible, if anything does. People leave — for the lowlands, the towns, the coast; the village has been bleeding downhill for a century — and Tellwater's law does not strike them. Their names stay on the tally-board. Their shares of the Even Water are still allotted, still ducted, still delivered twice daily to houses no one lights and gardens no one digs, because in the village's grammar a person who leaves is not gone but *in abeyance*, and only death may move a name from the water-tally to the burial book. So the empty half of Tellwater is not empty, exactly. It is a village of the accounted-for: swept when someone can manage it, roofed until the roofs win, and watered — always, punctually, faithfully watered. The steading holds a doorway open for whatever stays. Tellwater holds a house open for whatever left. Neither town can imagine the other's arrangement, and each has built its entire self on its own.

**The watcher who stayed** has a house here. Third from the weir, north side, her name on the board, her share running. She has been eleven years in the pass, transcribed by now into willow — her doorway up there held unclosing, her weeping wall plumbed, her path to the spring kept — and down here her allotted thread of the Even Water pours twice daily into her garden plot, where no one digs, and the water is cold, and the banks of her little channel are exactly the climate the willow likes. Her plot has gone entirely to it. And the willow in her garden is not sprawling. It is pleaching. Waist-high now, panels considering each other across her bean rows, a gap on the south side that looks, to anyone who has read the notebooks, less like an accident than a doorway. She is a resident of two towns and present in neither's ledger correctly: the steading has built her a room she lives in, and Tellwater is having one built for her whether it likes it or not, and somewhere between them, on a mountain of falling water, is a woman who can only ever be in one place. The village delivers her water anyway. That is what a name on the board means.

**The one.** Whether Tellwater is a single thing is a different question than it is in the pass. The steading may be one body wearing thirty rooms, grafted into physiological union, sap crossing every wall. Tellwater's union is made of paper: the tally, the books, the board — a grafting of names rather than branches, but a grafting all the same, and sap of a kind does cross it, twice daily, at the weir. And it is in those books, in the margin of the annual coppicing record, that someone — the hand looks like the reckoner's, but younger; it may be her own, decades old — has done a piece of arithmetic no one has ever copied into the fair record. The willow line advances. The coppicing takes it to the waterline every autumn, and every spring it returns a few paces farther down both banks, seeded a little deeper into the village, a little more at home; the graveyard's lower corner is already lost; the watcher's garden is already a room. The margin holds the rate, and the distance, and a division sign, and a figure that is a number of years, and the figure is not large.

The village that counts everything has declined to count this aloud. But the books, read together, say what the books say: that the Even Water runs only one way; that the steading spends space and Tellwater is accumulating it, roofless room by roofless room, doorway by unclosed doorway; and that when the willow finally walks up the two streets and the one crooked lane, it will find — for the first time in its slow century of drafting — a town already laid out for it. Lanes waiting. Walls standing. Empty houses held open, swept, named, and watered, each one kept faithfully for someone who is not coming back, and each one, in two or three patient seasons after the willow arrives, to be roofed in moss and grown shut around its name.

The tally-house it will leave open. Of this the watchers are privately certain, and they have their reasons, written up the mountain in the town's own hand: at the convergence of every lane, the steading always keeps one chamber empty — one column of light, one pool of black water, and nothing else, maintained against the grain of all its closing. Tellwater has already grown its own. It has been growing it for five hundred years: the low stone room by the weir where the water is told and the names are kept, where the light falls gray through the one window onto the black oak board and the shelf of notebooks, and where — long after the last reading is entered and the last reader is a name in the second volume — the figure of the Even Water will go on arriving twice a day, even, patient, exactly itself, waiting to be counted by whatever stays.
