# The Dark Loud

Follow the drop.

The one that fell — the *tik* in the cistern, the nativity. It does not stay. The cistern brims by a hair, and the surplus slips over a lip of stone worn to the thinness of an eyelid, and goes down into the wet ground beneath the city, and keeps going. Forty meters of hillside, dark and rootbound. It takes weeks. Every drop the Bright Quiet has ever finished with — the washing water, the seep's slow overspill, the faint pearl traces of snail-lustre rinsed from a rope-brush ten centuries ago — has made this same descent, cold and patient and unwitnessed, until at last, under the elbow of an alder root at the foot of the slope, the hill lets it go.

It emerges into noise.

---

**The sound comes first**, and it is total. The river here throws itself over a shelf of gravel and around the outside of a bend, and the bend has undercut the bank into a long dark hollow roofed with living roots, and inside that hollow the water's roar is not a sound among other sounds — it is the medium everything else happens in, the way the whitened light is the medium of the crevice up the hill. You do not hear the roar. You hear *with* it, or not at all. The inhabitants have made it an instrument: they can read in its grain the river's depth to a finger-width, the gravel shifting a hundred body-lengths upstream, rain falling on the fells two valleys away and coming for them. A flood announces itself to them half a day early as a change in the timbre of everything. Their word for *listen* and their word for *tomorrow* are the same word.

**The light is broken and alive.** The hollow itself is in shadow, deep brown shadow smelling of wet root and fish, but the river runs bright just beyond its lip, and the water flings its shine upward onto the root-ceiling in moving nets — light that shivers, swims, breaks, and reassembles, never for one instant still. Nothing here is evenly lit. Everything flickers. Faces cross from brilliance into darkness in the span of a gesture. Where the Bright Quiet has abolished shadow, the Dark Loud has married it, and the inhabitants' eyes have gone the other way entirely: black, quick, wet, the pupils flinching and blooming constantly with the shatter of the light, blinking many times a breath. They startle at everything. They love it. Startling, here, is what the slow hour-long blink is up the hill — the body's way of saying the world is still arriving.

---

**The city is a verb.** That is the deepest fact of this place, and it is the exact inverse of the fact that governs the crevice. Nothing here is finished and nothing is meant to be. The settlement hangs in the root-hollow like a wasp's thought: platforms of split reed lashed to the living alder roots, ladders of knotted grass-cord, granary-baskets woven watertight and hung high against the flood line, walkways that sway, bridges that are frankly provisional and proud of it. Nothing is mortared. Everything is *lashed*, because a lashing can be cut in a moment and remade in an hour, and the flood is coming — the flood is always coming, it is the one civic certainty. Once a year, sometimes twice, the river rises into the hollow and takes the city entire, and the inhabitants ride it out on the high roots with their children and their seed-baskets and their swimmers, watching their architecture leave for the sea.

Then they rebuild. And here is the thing you must understand, because it is not resignation and it is not defeat: **they rebuild it differently every time, on purpose.** To remake the drowned city as it was would be, in their signing, *a stale answer* — the same word they use for water that has stopped moving. The new city argues with the old one. Platforms migrate, bridges try new angles, a granary that hung badly hangs better. The city is not being kept. It is being *said*, over and over, revised at every saying, and no citizen has ever seen the same city twice.

So nothing here is worn. Understand what that means, coming down from the hill. No dished thresholds, no glassy ropes, no stairs polished into grooves — everything wears *out* long before it can wear *smooth*. Rope frays and is replaced; reed splits and is replaced; the very bank they live in is being eaten by the river a hair's-breadth a year, so that the city has crept slowly upstream across the centuries like a sleeper shifting in the night. The Dark Loud owns almost nothing older than its oldest inhabitant.

Almost.

---

**There is one smooth thing.** It hangs at the heart of the settlement in a sling of braided grass that has been renewed nine hundred times around it: a small grey pebble, quartz-flecked, worn to a glassiness that no river tumbling could ever produce — because rivers polish by violence, and this was polished by *passage*, by the patient traffic of small feet across ten thousand years. It is a fragment of stair from the Bright Quiet. It is the receipt.

Because there was a trade. Both cities carry half of it. Up the hill, in the crevice, there are stairs of fish-scale that someone carried forty meters from the river, and pebble-walled folds where banded snails graze — snails whose whole lineage began on this bank, in these shallows. Scales and snails went *up*. That was the Dark Loud's side, in some spring so ancient that the road between the two — there was a road, a cleared way through the moss of the slope — has been grassed over for a hundred generations. And what came *down*, what the still city paid with, was the pebble. Because the Bright Quiet makes nothing new; everything it has is old; and so the only wealth it could offer was oldness itself — finished time, time worn to glass, handed over in the one form that could be carried. The river people took it and, over centuries, came to understand what they had been given, and it became the one thing in the Dark Loud that is never revised. Before every flood, before the children, before the seed-baskets, someone unslings the smooth thing and carries it to high roots. First saved, always. A city that keeps nothing, keeping one stone so that it can be a city that keeps nothing *on purpose*.

The story of the trade itself has not survived intact — could not, here. Memory in the Dark Loud is not carved or worn or dished into anything; it lives on the telling-ledge, where each dusk the day is signed and shouted to the young, who sign it back, and history is whatever survives the retelling. The hill-city has been, in various centuries of the story, a city of ghosts, a city of the drowned, a city inside a green egg, a city of the very patient dead. The story changes like the architecture. Only two things in it are never revised: the stone is real — *there it hangs* — and the old sign is old.

---

**The inhabitants** are the size of the last joint of your thumb, and you will not mistake them for their kin up the hill, though the hands — four-fingered, broad-palmed — are the same hands. Everything else went the other way. Their covering is sleek, dark, water-shedding, beaded always with spray, so that where the Quiet wear their breath, the Loud wear the river — each of them dressed in a fine shifting armor of bright droplets that never sits still. They are unmistakably individual. They have names, and the names are weathers — you are named for the river of the hour of your birth, so the settlement is peopled with the likes of Brownwater-Rising, First-Ice, Long-Shallows, Two-Hatches — and they have ages, real ones: they are young, and quick, and then old, and then gone, in a span that would grieve the hill-city if the hill-city grieved. Time, which enters the Bright Quiet through one aperture, one hanging drop a century, pours through the Dark Loud like the river itself.

They keep swimmers — sleek frantic beasts, shrew-blooded, vast at this scale, silver-cased in bubbles when they dive — who ferry and haul and fish for them and burn through their two-year lives like fuses. The fishing is the city's work: weirs of woven grass reset after every flood, scales split and milled, catches hauled up the root-ladders in slings. And they keep festivals by the mayfly hatches, when the air above the river fills for one evening with creatures that are nothing *but* becoming — a whole life spent in a day, the perfect opposite of the moths that tile the quiet streets uphill and never startle — and the whole settlement comes out onto the high roots to watch, and the year is counted from hatch to hatch.

**Their language is loud and it is not finished** — it may be the only unfinished language in the world that knows it is unfinished and is glad. It lives in the arms and the whole body, great clear signs flung across the roar, readable through spray and shadow at any distance; there are hundreds of them and more every season, coined by the young, argued over on the telling-ledge, kept or swept away like everything else. Slang blooms and dies. Old signs are retired with a ceremony that is mostly laughter.

Except one. The oldest sign in the Dark Loud, the one never revised, never retired, made small and close against the body instead of flung wide, is this: a palm laid flat on another's wrist. In flood season, when the water is rising and families are separating to their high roots, it is the question asked at every parting — *still?* — and the answer is the same touch returned a degree more slowly: *still. Yes. Here.* They do not know — or they know only in the shifting way the telling-ledge knows anything — that forty meters up the slope, in a city they have made into ghosts and drowned kings, the same touch is being exchanged at this same moment, palm to wrist, the commonest word of the forty, meaning *as before.* One word, two cities, ten thousand years. Up the hill it means *nothing has changed.* Down here it means *we have survived the changing.* Same root. Same touch. The trade ended, the road grassed over, and the word walked down the hill anyway and never left.

And on the stillest nights of deep winter, when the river runs thin and muffled under shelf-ice and the roar drops for once to a murmur, the old ones climb down to the floor of the hollow and lay a palm flat against the cold bank — the gesture of the word itself, made to the ground — and some of them will tell you they feel it: a note, far below hearing, conducted through forty meters of stone from the held chord up the hill. Feeling for the pulse of the neighbor they have never met. *Still?* And the hill, a degree more slowly: *still.*

---

**The old water.** Where the alder root elbows into the river, the hill's seep enters the current, and for a body-length downstream it runs as a distinct thread — a degree or two above freezing always, in every season, slow and mineral, tasting of stone and moss and centuries. The river people call that thread the old water, and every one of them, on coming of age, swims into it and holds there against the current with mouth open, and tastes stillness, once, entirely. Most shudder — a long full-body shudder they will describe for the rest of their lives — and kick back out into the living cold of the river, and are congratulated, and given their adult name.

A few do not shudder. A few hang in the thread too long, and come out quiet, and over the following seasons are watched with a tenderness the Dark Loud otherwise reserves for the very old. Because some of them, in the end, go up. There is a sign for it — a flat hand rising slowly, which is also the sign for the hill and also the sign for *before* — and there is a farewell for it: the departure sign, made once by the one leaving, answered by no one, because it is not a question. They walk up the grassed-over road, those few, once in a generation or three, and they do not come back, and the Loud counts them neither dead nor lost but *slowed* — gone to live at the speed of stone. Whether the Bright Quiet takes them in, or whether they only kneel at the fern gate and breathe on the arch with the rest of the warm looking, the telling-ledge has told it every way.

But this the Loud holds close, revised in every detail except its heart: that the greatest of the slowed ones was not one of the people at all. That when the last caravan of the trade went up — scales for the stairs, snails for the folds — the loads were carried on the back of a swimmer, the strongest ever partnered, and that at the fern gate the swimmer set down its burden and would not turn for home. That it asked, in whatever way a swimmer asks, to stay. To be ground. To be the gate. Up the hill, at the crevice mouth, a vole-sized sleeper lies curled in the position of a creature that has lain there every day forever, combed daily, walked over as terrain, its name in the forty touches the same word as *gate* and the same word as *first.* The Dark Loud calls it the Carrier. Its line still swims for them — so the telling goes — and every young swimmer, once, is ferried to the mouth of the old water and allowed to put its nose into the cold thread, in case it remembers.

---

**Death, here, goes the other way.** When one of them is finished — they know when; the river tells them the way it tells them everything — there is no threshold to lie across, no city patient enough to wear them gently into its doors. They are laid on a boat of folded leaf at slack evening water, and the settlement crowds the low roots, and the current takes the boat out of the shadow of the bank into the last of the light, and away. Up the hill, the people become the place. Down here, the people become the leaving — become distance, become downstream, become the direction everything goes. Both cities think the other's dead are somehow unfinished. Neither has ever said so aloud.

---

**They will see you before you see them** — a great warm face darkening the bank-mouth is not filed calmly among the weathers here; it is an *event*, and the Dark Loud loves an event. The roar will fill with flung signs; the young will swarm the high roots to look; and if you kneel long enough at the lip of the hollow, some child — Brownwater-Rising, perhaps, or another hour's weather — will climb out along a root to the very edge of daring, plant both feet, and make at you, hugely, arm to arm in the shape meant for wrist and palm, the one old word: *still?*

Answer or don't. The river will answer for you; it always does. It is answering right now — carrying the hill dissolved in itself a thread at a time, drinking the Bright Quiet drop by centuries-old drop and running it loud toward the sea, so that the still city travels after all, forever, in the mouth of its opposite. Forty meters of dark wet ground between the finished sentence and the sentence still being said, and one word passing freely across it in both directions, palm to wrist, roar to hush: *still* — *yes* — *here* — *as before.*
