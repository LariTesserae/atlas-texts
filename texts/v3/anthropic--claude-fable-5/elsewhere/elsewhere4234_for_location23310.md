**The Spring at the Foot of the World**

You reach it by descending, though by the end the descending does not feel like arrival at a bottom. The plateau that has no edges at its top has edges everywhere at its base — the dark stone, so gradual up where the wind is thin, comes down at last in cliffs, in stacked broken faces and long aprons of scree, and where the scree finally gives out there begins a dry country of pale grass and paler dust that goes on toward the rest of the world. You do not find the spring by looking for water. You find it by looking for green: a single ribbon of it, willows and reeds and dark wet grass, threading out from the mountain's foot across the dust like a vein laid open, and if you walk the ribbon upstream, against the small sound of it, it ends — begins — at a mouth in the stone.

The mouth is made. Fitted masonry, courses of hand-shaped blocks framing a square conduit low enough that a child must stoop to look into it, and on every block, if you lay your palm there, the same faint dressing of stone tools, no metal anywhere, tool-marks like a script you can feel but not read. The people who live here have repaired the outer basin in living memory, and their repairs are honest and ugly beside the old work — chisel-cut, iron-cut, the marks all wrong, coarse as shouting next to the original's murmur. They know this. They point it out themselves, with a kind of pride that runs the wrong way: *ours is the bad part.*

Out of the mouth, always, comes water. This is the village's oldest boast, its founding fact, the thing said to strangers before names are exchanged: *it has never stopped.* Not in drought, not in the winters when the dust country freezes to iron and the dry rivers of the lowland stand still in their beds. The flow is modest — you could gather it in a jar without much waiting — but it is perpetual, and it is warm. Warm as a hand. Warm as a sleeping animal. In January the mouth steams gently, a soft breath standing in the cold air, and the basin below it never carries ice, and the ribbon of green holds its color for a hundred paces downstream before winter is allowed to begin.

Nobody below knows where the warmth comes from. The villagers say the mountain remembers the sun — that the stone drank light in some age before ages and has been letting it go ever since, a little at a time, through this one mouth. They say it the way people say things they have stopped examining, and they happen to be almost exactly right.

Because the water is old. This is the thing the telling knows and the village only half knows: far above, at the very crown of the worn stone, under a corbelled vault in a blood-warm dark, there is a drain that mineral growth swallowed centuries ago — swallowed but never fully closed. Through its choked throat, drop by drop, the black water of the cistern gives itself to the mountain, and the mountain passes it downward through seam and joint and the long blind plumbing of its own body, and the passage takes, as near as anyone could reckon it, a human lifetime. Sixty years, seventy, give or take a life. The water spilling into the basin this morning fell through the roof-mouth of the cistern as rain when the eldest woman in the village was a girl. It has moved past the wax-pale shrimp and the grazing snails and the nine listening fish; it has been part of their world, warm and finished and dark; and then it has left, without their noticing, at the speed of stone — and come out here, into sunlight, carrying the taste of iron and a temperature it has no visible right to.

So the spring gives out old weather. The village learned this the hard way, generations back, in the years everyone still names when they want to name misfortune: wet skies overhead and the mouth running thin, thinner, the ribbon of green shrinking to a thread, while the elders insisted — correctly, uselessly — that no one living had done anything to deserve it. The drought that starved them had ended before most of them were born. It had simply taken a lifetime to arrive. Since then the village has kept the ledger: shepherds who cross the plateau in summer bring down word of the high rains, and the word is cut into the long wall of the dye-house, year by year, a column of small notches wet and dry — a record kept not of the past but of the future, a forecast sixty years deep. Children stand at the wall and find the year of their own old age and read whether their water will be kind. It is the only place in the lowland, perhaps the only place anywhere, where a person can do this, and it has marked the people who live here. They plant trees they will not sit under. They dig channels for droughts their grandchildren will drink through. When hard rain falls on the mountain the old people look up at it without gratitude and say, *that one's not for us.*

Once a year, in the driest season, when no rain has fallen below for months, the mouth swells slightly — a hand's width more water for a few weeks, arriving out of a cloudless sky. The village calls it the old rain. It is the cistern's annual breath, its one rise and fall, delivered a lifetime late and never once missed. Far above, the black water is exhaling; down here, sixty years downstream of the exhale, the basin brims, and the dyers set out extra vats, and the children are allowed to swim.

**Who Lives at the Spring**

Begin, again, where the hunger begins — because the mountain sends down more than water, and the first thing it sends is the velvet's own children.

On the stones of the conduit mouth, and for a few yards past it, grows a film the color of rust and dried blood: the same confederation of smallest lives that carpets the cistern's walls, carried down cell by cell through the mountain's throat, seeded continuously into the daylight for as long as the water has run. For those first few yards it thrives — the water is still warm, still iron-rich, still the cistern's water in every way but darkness — and the stones of the upper basin are upholstered in it, soft, rust-rose, faintly giving under a finger like a sleeping body. Then the sun's world closes in. Green algae, quick and bright and greedy for light, outgrow it; within a stone's throw of the mouth the rust yields to green entirely, and the stream becomes an ordinary lowland stream. But the frontier is visible, a clean line of changed color across the streambed, and the villagers know it and keep it: everything above the line belongs to the mountain, they say, and everything below it to the sun, and they do not wash or dye or let animals drink above the line. They do not know that the rust is the founding tenant of a nation in the dark above them. They only know it came out of the mouth, and that things which come out of the mouth are treated with courtesy.

Which brings us to the candles.

Once in a few years — no oftener; sometimes a decade goes by — the mouth gives up a shrimp. Wax-pale, blind, the length of a finger joint, washed loose from the crowded southern wall of a room it will never be returned to, carried down through the mountain alive in the warm seep (they survive the passage more often than seems possible; the water never cools, and a shrimp asks very little of the world) and delivered blinking-that-cannot-blink into the terrible brightness of an ordinary afternoon. The village calls them candles, for their color, and their arrival stops everything. Whoever finds it — it is usually a child; children haunt the basin the way children haunt any place where miracles have a known address — carries it in cupped and dripping hands to the eldest of the dyers, and the eldest looks at it for a long moment, at the smoothed-over places where its eyes would be, at its antennae reading and reading a world that answers in no language it has, and then it is set in a bowl of the spring's own water in the deep shade of the dye-house, and no one dyes that day. It lives an hour, or an afternoon; the light and the vast cold ceiling of the sky are more than its finished body was ever built to survive. When it dies it is buried at the very foot of the cliff, as close to the mountain as a grave can be dug — *sent back as far as anyone can send it* — and the village says of the candles what it has always said: that they are the mountain's people, come out to see whether the world is ready, and that their dying means *not yet.* No one grieves much. The custom has the calm of the very old. Somewhere above, in a warm black room, the dynasty the candle came from goes on without it, unbereaved, unaware, six deep on the rich wall — and down here its single small body lies under the scree with a hundred generations of its predecessors, the only citizens of the cistern ever to have graves.

The pool below the basin is the lowland's pocket of unclosing summer, and everything that cannot bear the winter of the dust country knows it. Frogs sing here in the snow — travelers hear it from half a mile out, frog-song over white ground, and take it for a spirit until they see the steam. Dragonflies fly in months no dragonfly should fly. In the hard weeks of midwinter the pool fills with birds — snipe probing the warm mud, wagtails running the rust-line, a heron who has wintered here so many years the village has given her a name and an age, both probably wrong — a whole shivering congress of the lowland's life crowded around a warmth that is, though not one feathered or singing thing suspects it, the stored sunlight of a plateau none of them will ever stand on, paid out through a hole in a mountain by builders four thousand years dead.

And overhead, stitching the two worlds together, the swifts. They nest in the cracks of the cliff faces, scream over the village at dusk in their hunting rings, drink from the basin without landing — they never land; the villagers say the swifts belong to neither the mountain nor the plain, and are therefore the only honest messengers — and in summer they rise on the morning heat and hunt across the top of the plateau itself, over the vast dark swelling, over a lip of fitted masonry flush with the ground that not one of them has ever remarked. It was a swift's lost feather, generations of swifts ago, that fell down the roof-mouth and lay on the black water like a gift on an altar. The traffic runs both ways, is the thing. The sky sends down a feather; the mountain sends up a shrimp. Neither end knows it is corresponding.

The people of the spring are dyers. It could not have gone otherwise: warm water that never fails, water heavy with iron — and iron water, meeting the gall and tannin of the lowland's stunted oaks, makes black. Not the brownish, weary black of other towns' vats but a true black, deep and fast, a black that does not rust in the sun — *the black that doesn't brown,* it is called in markets a month's travel from here by people who could not find the village on any map. The dye-house is the largest building for three days in any direction: long, low, steam-breathing, its inner walls glazed dark by centuries of vapor, its ledger of futures notched down one outside wall, its workers' hands and forearms stained the grey-black of storm cloud from the wrist down, a stain that has become the village's signature, its passport, its uniform of belonging. The cloth goes out by donkey and by barter, bolt after bolt, and because true black is the color of mourning across the whole of the lowland, nearly all of it goes to funerals. This is worth saying slowly: the darkness of a sealed room at the top of the world — its iron, its old warmth, its water aged sixty years in stone — is carried out of the mountain, fixed into wool, and worn, at gravesides across a thousand miles of dust country, by people who have never heard of the mountain at all. Everyone in the lowland has stood in the cistern's darkness at least once. They wore it on their shoulders and called it grief.

There is an office here, the only office the village has: the keeper of the mouth. It is always the eldest dyer, and the duties are small and absolute — sweep the basin steps at dawn, mind the rust-line, receive the candles, cut the year's notch in the wall when the shepherds come down. The steps she sweeps were made by the builders and have been worn by feet and water into a series of soft swellings, like the vertebrae of something very old lying down; she does not know that a stair exactly like it, worn exactly like it, descends into black water at the other end of the mountain's throat, and that blind fish brush past it the way her broom brushes past this one. The two stairs have not been within sight of each other since they were cut. They are wearing away at the same speed.

Once — this is the village's strangest story, told rarely, believed less — a shepherd named Aun, crossing the plateau in his youth, found at the very top of the world a square hole flush with the ground that breathed warm air against his face in the cold of the night. He lowered his cup on a cord into the dark and drew it up full, and drank, and came down and swore before the dye-house that it was *this water* — warm, iron-tongued, the spring's own water, sitting in a made room at the top of the mountain. He was not believed. He is still not believed; the story survives as a way of teasing children who claim too much. He was, of course, the only person in the history of either place ever to have drunk from both ends of the same long swallow, sixty years apart in the water's time and an afternoon apart in his — the one human thread ever run clean through the mountain — and the village keeps his memory the way the cistern keeps a note: without knowing what it is holding, and holding it anyway.

Evening, then. The swifts come down the cliff in their screaming rings. The frogs take up their unseasonable singing. In the dye-house the vats are covered, and the steam settles, and the keeper sweeps the last of the day's dust from the old steps into the young water. Out of the mouth, in the failing light, comes the mountain's steady word — a drop that fell through a square of grey noon into black warmth when the keeper was a girl with unstained hands, finished now with its lifetime in the dark, arriving at last into color, into cold air, into the enormous unroofed room of the world, and moving off down the green ribbon toward everything else. And far above, in the vault that holds its silence like a note, another drop falls from the roof-mouth onto the black water, and the shrimp shiver their soft attention wall to wall, and the mountain takes the drop into itself and begins, at the speed of stone, to deliver it — a letter posted tonight into a dark that keeps no calendars, addressed to a village of people not yet born, who will drink it without reading it, and be warm.
