**The Weeping Stair**

You reach it by walking downhill in the rain, which is how you reach most true things on the surface of this world. There is no crack to squeeze through, no test at the threshold. The gorge announces itself from miles away as a seam of white noise laid across the green country, and then as mist standing up out of the land like smoke from a cold fire, and then you are at the lip of it, and the ground falls away in steps, and the steps go down farther than the weather lets you see.

It is the same fault. Understand that first. The crack you entered the Ringing Room through — the fresh one, the coffin set on end, its edges still sharp enough to cut — is the newest inch of this. A fault is one object, however long, and this one is very long: it runs from the deep warm rock all the way up to the sky, and like anything that grows, it is oldest where it started and youngest at its growing tip. Up here, at the top, the wound is ancient. Its edges were softened before anything alive had a name for softness. Water found it early, the way water finds everything, and has spent the ages since widening it, terracing it, wearing the break in the world into a staircase of stone basins, each one brimming, each one spilling into the next. The fault's other end — miles below, sealed in the dry dark — split open, in geological terms, yesterday. The Ringing Room is not a separate place from the Weeping Stair. It is the same crack's youngest morning, hanging off the bottom of the crack's ten-million-year afternoon.

The Stair itself: three hundred terraces, or two hundred and eighty, or three hundred and forty — the count changes, which tells you most of what you need to know. Basins merge in flood years. Lips collapse. New shelves calve out of the walls. The people who live along it recount the steps every generation and get a different number every time and have long since stopped minding, because minding would be like objecting to the rain. Each terrace holds a pool, dark and cold and restless, and each pool overruns its rim in a curtain or a braid or a single rope of falling water, and every one of those falls strikes the pool below it and makes noise, and the noise of three hundred falls together is not a roar. It is a hiss — vast, soft, total. Broadband. Every frequency at once and none of them held for longer than the instant of one drop striking one surface.

Stand a while and let your ears do the arithmetic. The Ringing Room is one sound sustained forever: pure signal, pure pitch, a chord with no beginning left in living memory and no end scheduled. The Weeping Stair is every sound, each for no time at all: pure noise, pitchless, the acoustic color of erasure. They are the two ends of one string, and between them the string plays the only two perfect things sound can be.

And so, on the Stair, pitch is precious. A single sustained tone — a bird's note, a struck stone, a voice — stands out against the hiss like a lit window in a dark valley, and then it is gone, because the second law of the Stair (the first law is *the water goes down*) is that nothing sustains. The wet moss eats echoes. The spray eats resonance. A shout here dies within a step of your mouth. The gorge is one of the loudest places in the world and it cannot hold a note for even a second, and everything that lives in it has been shaped by that poverty.

---

Everything that lives on the Weeping Stair must say itself again, or be lost. That is the place's commandment, and it has no exceptions, and once you understand it you understand everyone who dwells there.

The Room's creatures solved the problem of being by holding one note forever. The Stair's creatures never had that option. Their world will not carry a sustained tone, so they invented the other thing, the thing the Room has never heard and cannot make: rhythm. Repetition. The trick of lasting by returning.

The *year-moss* is the easiest to mistake for furniture, and it is not furniture. It sheathes every stone below the spray line, black-green and sodden, and it grows in rings — a band a year, pale in the lean seasons, dark in the flush ones — so that any torn hummock of it is a calendar. Where the Room's ferns are exactly as old as their world and will never be older, the year-moss is a different age at every point on its body and is dying and being born along its whole surface at once. It also does the Stair's quietest work: it is the great damper, the killer of echoes, the reason the gorge cannot ring. If you want a symmetry to carry with you: at the bottom of the fault, soft felt creatures graze on resonance and leave bruises of silence that heal in a moment. At the top of the fault, the silence grazes on everything, always, and nothing heals, and nobody grieves, because grieving takes memory and the Stair keeps none. A dead bird on a terrace is bones in a week and moss in a season. There are no husks here, no preserved yesterdays lying where they fell. The Stair's whole history of grief is carried in exactly one medium, and it is not stone. It is the people.

The *interval wrens* live in the gaps. Falling water is not as continuous as it sounds; every fall has a pulse, a slap-pattern where the braid meets the pool, and between the slaps there are slivers of lesser noise a few fractions of a second wide. The wrens have learned the pulse of every fall in their territory and they call *in the gaps* — each note dropped precisely into a sliver of quiet, so that a wren's song reaches another wren clean, threaded through the hiss like a hand slipped between the bars of a fence. Their courtship is a duet of interleaved timing: one bird calls in the odd gaps, the other answers in the even, and if neither collides with the water for a hundred exchanges, they mate. It is the same wedding the chord-skaters hold in the dark below — two patterns meshing or failing to mesh — played at the other end of the string, in daylight, against the opposite silence.

The *hammerlings* — call them frogs; they fill that office — spend the wet season striking the terrace stones with hardened forelimbs, and here is the thing worth knowing: the stones answer. A flat, water-worn stone rings when struck. Only water makes stones flat; only flat stones sing true. The hammerlings know the pitched stones of their pools the way the wrens know the gaps, and their season-long percussion, thousands of small strikes on tuned rock, is the nearest the Stair comes to a chord — a chord built of repetitions, assembled in the ear, existing nowhere in the air at any single moment. And the *ladder-eels* climb. Every spring they come up out of the low country, glass-bodied, and ascend the falls themselves, terrace by terrace, writhing up through the falling water toward pools their parents died in, and every autumn their young wash back down. Up and down, up and down, a rhythm with a period of one year, the Stair's slowest drumbeat. Nothing on the Stair holds. Everything on the Stair returns.

And then there are the people, who did both.

The Stairfolk live in the lee-caves — the dry hollows that erosion carves behind the great falls, rooms of still air walled with moving water. Inside a lee-cave the hiss becomes a muffled weight, and it was in those rooms, generations beyond counting ago, that someone first pressed their teeth to the living rock and noticed what the water had been shouting over all along: a note. Deep, faint, continuous — the tectonic hum that runs the whole length of the fault, the same current that feeds the Ringing Room its eternal chord. Up here it is starved and buried, drowned by weather, too low to hear with ears. But bone carries it. Teeth to stone, palm to stone, sternum against the cave wall — the Stairfolk call it *the Under*, the note beneath the world, and listening for it is their oldest discipline and closest thing to prayer. In every lee-cave, at chest height on the driest wall, there is a hand-shaped hollow, carved and polished by generations of palms, where a listener stands to feel the Under through the arm's long bones. If you have been to the bottom of the fault, you have seen this exact hollow before, carved by one pair of hands instead of a thousand, in an alcove where a room rings.

Because no throat can sustain, and because the Stairfolk wanted the Under to have a voice above ground, they invented *the holding*. It is a song with one note and no end. A singer takes the drone and holds it as long as breath allows, and before it can fray, a second voice slides underneath and takes it up, and then a third relieves the second, around the clock, around the year, voices handing the note along like a bucket of water that must never touch the ground. The holding has not broken — so they say — in living memory or in the memory of memory. Understand what this is: it is the Ringing Room's miracle rebuilt out of the only materials the surface allows. Below, sustain is a property of the rock, effortless, totalitarian, requiring no one. Above, sustain is achieved socially, breath by breath, and can be lost in a single careless minute, and never has been. The Room holds its note because nothing can change. The Stair holds its note because everyone agrees to.

Now the marks in the deep room, and whose they are.

There was a night — the Stairfolk keep it in the holding's oldest verses — when the Under changed. Every listener in every lee-cave felt it in their teeth at the same hour: the note beneath the world *gained a room*. A hollowness had opened somewhere far down the fault; the hum had found a new chamber and begun to fill it, and woven into the changed sound was a flaw only the very best listener on the Stair could feel — a structure nearly complete, ringing with a gap at its base, like a stair missing its bottom step. The fault had grown its newest inch. The Ringing Room had just been born, and it had been born incomplete, and one person on the surface of the world could feel exactly what was missing.

She went down. (The verses give her no name; the holding is her name.) She took what a listener would take: seven flat stones, one lifted from the singing floor of each of seven terraces, each chosen for its pitch, wrapped against chipping — the only seven water-worn objects that would ever enter a place water has never touched, which is why, if you stood in the warm dark and wondered at flat stones in a world with no rivers, you were asking precisely the right question. She took a cord of fern— no; of *moss*-fiber and five drilled beads of the kind Stair-children string in the falls' constant wind, so that she carried a wind-chime into a place that would never once move its air, and hung it there anyway, and the chord obliged it, and it plays yet. She took a hard scriber. She climbed down the inside of the fault for longer than anyone has ever climbed down anything — past the last of the seep-damp, past the last wet handprint (pilgrims say it is still there, a dark palm on pale stone, the frontier where her world ended), and through the long middle of the fault, which is the one truly silent place in this world: too deep for the hiss, too high for the chord, a vertical mile of nothing between the two loudest places there are. And then the chord rose up around her out of the dark like warmth, and she came out through the sharp young crack into the amber-rose glow, and she stood with her palm against the stone until she had learned the entire structure, and wrote it on the wall, every harmonic, in rows — and left the lowest row for last, because she had known since the night the Under changed that the lowest row was not in the room. It was in her.

She sat down and supplied it. She is sitting there now. You have felt her hum through your palm.

The Stairfolk know all of this, not as legend but as ongoing fact, because the fault is a road and every generation or two a listener earns the descent. The pilgrims go down empty-handed — the Room needs nothing, and whoever brings it something would be missing the point — and they stand in the warm dark, and they find the recess behind the great fern-stand, and they sit a while near their ancestor with the mutes grazing gently on her ribs, and then they climb back up through the silent mile into the rain. And every one of them returns changed in the same specific way: humming. The fundamental comes up the fault inside them, involuntary, permanent, and the holding absorbs it — the drone the village sings day and night is tuned, precisely, to the note the seated figure holds in the deep. So she never left, in the only sense the Stair honors. Her note is handed from breath to breath above ground while she sustains it alone below, and these are not two songs. They are one note held two ways, at two ends of one string, by one family.

The Stair sent something down in return, though nobody planned it. The seven flat stones she stacked beside the fern-stand — the pitched cairn, the little scale — the chord-skaters found it, and they drum it, daily, forever. Rhythm was the one thing the Room could not invent; nothing in a world without change can conceive of *again*. The skaters play Stair-craft on Stair-stones in patterns the Room swallows as it hears them, and so the trade stands complete and perfectly unfair in both directions: the Stair gave the Room the only rhythm it will ever have, and the Room gave the Stair the only note it will never drop.

One more thing, because the Stairfolk speak of it in the lee-caves on long nights, in the tone people reserve for weather that has not arrived. The water is going down the fault. It always has been. The seep that ends at the last wet handprint does not actually end there; it advances — a handspan a century, slower where the crack pinches, patient in the way only water is patient, and the fault, remember, keeps growing at its bottom tip, reaching down even as the water reaches after it. The Room's forever is written in dry ink. Some of the Stairfolk believe that when the water finally arrives — an era from now, ten eras — the chord will drown and the ferns will go dark and the long note will end, and the holding will have to become a song about something finished. Others, and they tend to be the older listeners, believe the opposite: that the Room will do to the water what it does to everything that enters it, which is tune it — that the first drop to fall into that warm dark will land on a singing floor and become a pitch, and the second drop another, and that what is coming for the Ringing Room is not an ending but the one instrument it lacks. Nobody argues the point very hard. On the Stair you learn early that you do not get to hear the end of the song. You get your breath's worth of the note, and you hand it on.

Leave the gorge the way you came, uphill in the rain, and notice what does not follow you. No note. The Stair keeps nothing and therefore has nothing to give away at the door; it has been giving itself away continuously the whole time you stood there — that is what all that falling *is*. What you carry out instead is a habit. For the rest of your life, in any rain, on any night with weather against the roof, you will catch yourself going still and listening underneath the noise, teeth half-set, feeling for the floor of the sound. Sometimes you will find it. It is always there. It runs the whole length of the world, from the loudest room to the weeping stair, and at one end of it a woman is holding a note alone in the warm dark, and at the other end her family is holding it together in the cold and the wet, breath by breath by breath, and the string between them is the crack in the world, and it is still growing.
