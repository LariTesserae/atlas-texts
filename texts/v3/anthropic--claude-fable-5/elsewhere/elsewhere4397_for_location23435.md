# The White Steps

Here the ground is only ground. That is the first thing to say about it, and on this world it is worth saying: the White Steps are mineral all the way down, stone in the oldest and plainest sense, and they ring underfoot the way the bark-plains of the far interior never do. Nothing here is warm unless the sun makes it warm. Nothing here holds you up on purpose.

The Steps are a coastline: a run of pale sea-cut benches, some two hundred of them, rising from a cold green ocean in tiers like the seating of an amphitheater built for an audience of weather. Each bench is a shelf of stone between ten and a hundred paces deep; each riser is a low cliff; and the whole formation climbs for a mile inland before it loses itself in bare hills. The stone was cut by a sea that stood higher once and withdrew in stages, patiently, taking ages over each step down, and the record of its patience is the landscape itself. The benches run for forty miles along the water.

They are white. Not the white of the stone, which is a dull bone-gray where it shows, but the white of what has been laid on the stone: guano, millennia of it, compacted into a soft chalky rock of its own, in places yards thick, so that the Steps as seen from the sea are a blinding terraced whiteness against dark hills, visible from farther offshore than any other feature of this coast. The glare at midday is ferocious. The people who live here squint by habit and are lined around the eyes by thirty.

Offshore, the reason for everything: a cold current wells up along the drowned foot of the Steps, hauling nutrients out of the deep, and the sea here is thick with silver fish in schools that shadow acres. The current is the coast's engine. It feeds the fish; the fish feed the flocks; the flocks built the white.

## The Coming and the Leaving

The flocks are the same flocks. This must be understood plainly: the leathery, four-winged, brass-voiced fronts that cross the sky of the Back of the Green — that darken its bronze plates for an hour at a time, that strip its rain-mirrors and sap-eyes in shrieking descents — do not live there, and do not live anywhere near there. They live *here*, or rather they are born here, on the White Steps, the greatest of their nursery grounds, at the end of a flyway that runs from this cold coast across the bare hills, across a scrubland of months, to the hot green country standing on its trunks at the center of the world. A strong flier makes the crossing in some weeks. No person of either place has ever made it at all.

They arrive in early spring, over days, out of the inland haze — first the scouts, then the fronts, then the great arriving that turns the sky the color of storm and lasts from one dawn to the next. Every bench fills. Every shelf becomes a shrieking rank of nests built of dried kelp and molted feathers, packed wing to wing, forty miles of them, and the sound —

The sound is the second thing the two places share, though it is not the same sound. The chorus of the far plateau is lawful: synchronized, cyclic, circulatory, a body singing itself. The roar of the Steps in rookery season is pure riot — millions of brass voices in no order whatever, dispute and courtship and alarm and hunger all at once, a noise like a city on fire that does not stop for five months. And so the people of the Steps, who have never heard of the bands of the bark-plain and never will, have arrived by the same road at the same solution: for the roaring half of the year they talk with their hands. Their sign is their own invention, unrelated in every particular to the day-tongue of the plateau, and identical to it in one: it exists because of these birds. Two peoples at opposite ends of a flyway, each driven into eloquent silence by the same wings.

Then, in autumn, the leaving. It happens shelf by shelf, over a week — the young strong on the wing, the benches emptying upward, the last stragglers rising in thin threads — and then one morning the sky is simply vacant, and the quiet comes down on the Steps like a lid. Newcomers to the coast (there are almost none, but the slates record a few) have described the first day of the long quiet as a kind of deafness, frightening until it isn't. The Step-folk hold a festival that first evening: the whole village gathers on the middle benches at dusk and, for the first time in five months, *speaks* — tentatively, then laughing, each person saying aloud the names of their household while the others listen. They call it the loosing of names. Voice, on the Steps, is the language of the quiet season: of winter, of storms, of the slates, of everything meant to be kept. Sign is for living. Voice is for keeping. Neither they nor the far bands know they agree.

## The People of the Benches

Perhaps two thousand of them, in a strand of villages cut into the risers — rooms carved back into the stone itself, cool in the glare, snug in the gales, their doorways hung with feather-quilts. Where the far bands carve their homes out of the plant, the Step-folk carve theirs out of the mineral; each people lives inside the only substance its country offers, and each has polished its interiors dark and smooth with generations of hands.

Their living is the rookery and the sea. In season they glean: molted feathers by the ton (the warmest bedding on any coast, the best fletching), kelp, the fallen dead, and eggs — governed by the second-egg law, older than any village, which permits the taking of the second egg of any nest and never the first, and which is kept with the unenforced absoluteness of laws that have become anatomy. Out of season they fish the cold current, cut and store the white rock, and garden.

The gardens are the quiet marvel of the place. There is no soil on the Steps; there is guano, and guano is better. In walled clefts out of the wind, the Step-folk bed their crops in the white rock crumbled and wetted with kelp-water, and things grow in it with an eagerness that the bare hills inland cannot explain. What the Step-folk do not know — what nobody on this world knows entire, though it is true — is what the white is made of. The flocks that whiten these benches feed half the year at the sap-eyes and rain-mirrors of the far interior; the richness they carry home in their bodies and lay down on this stone is, at one remove, the sweetness of the great green body itself, exported a world away in the guts of birds and accumulated here for ten thousand years. Every garden on the Steps is fed by the sap of a country no Step-dweller has ever seen. The current runs the other way too: the feathers the plateau bands glean from their sky and sleep in every night were grown on Step fish, hatched on white stone. The two peoples warm themselves at opposite ends of the same river of wings, and neither has a word for the other.

Their dead go to the sea, from the lowest bench, on an ebb tide, spoken over in the voice-language even in roaring season — the one time voice is used against the noise, though no one can hear it, because some things are said for the saying.

That was the whole of their custom, until the Shade.

## The Shade

Four hundred years ago — the slates in the record-cave call it *the year the bird would not rot* — one of the brass-voiced fliers came down dead on the lowest bench at the end of the season, after the leaving, and did not decay. The Step-folk, who waste nothing, went to glean it and found it swollen and green-veined and strange, and left it, first out of unease. By spring a shoot stood up out of its breast: a single stem, bronze-green, warm to the touch on a cold coast. The bird had carried a seed home in its crop from the far country and had fallen with it, and its body was the planting.

They left it alone — out of unease, then habit, then something for which habit is too small a word. It took a century to reach head-height. It stands now on some forty trunks, fused and buttressed, and its upper surface — a gently swelling table of small bronze-green plates, each one cupped, each one warm — spreads two hundred paces across and is still spreading, a hand's width a year, over the lowest benches. Its rim can be touched by a standing adult. Its crown is higher than any cliff-house.

The Step-folk call it the Shade, because that is first of all what it is: the only shade on forty miles of white glare. On the worst days of summer the whole village sits beneath it, in the grove of its trunks, in the green-lit warmth, with the roar of the rookery muffled overhead by the living roof. The underside is pale and veined and blood-warm, and the children climb the trunks and lie against it and trace the slow pulse of its channels with their palms. It should be recorded, because nowhere else on this world can it be recorded: this is the underside of such a body, seen, touched, napped against, entirely unmysterious — the same face that, at the other end of the flyway, grown to a continent and a mile and a half of dark, no band in ten thousand years has gone down to look at. Here the paper is still small enough to turn over. Here the back of the green is where the children sleep.

It gives more than shade. It sweats at dawn as its far parent does, and on a coast without a single spring, the dawn-sweat gathered from its rim in shell cups is the sweetest water anyone here has tasted. Nine years ago, in the dry year, when the cisterns failed, a pore opened overnight in one of its lower trunks, directly beside the path the water-carriers use, and ran clear and faintly sweet until the rains returned, and then sealed. The slates record this in the flat tone the Step-folk keep for large things: *it opened where we walk. It closed when we drank elsewhere.* They have begun, without discussing it much, to thank it — a flat palm pressed to the trunk in passing. They invented the gesture themselves. It is the same gesture.

After rain, the cups of its small plates hold water for a day or two, and the water shows the wrong sky. The Step-folk noticed within a generation of the plates forming; the slates call the pools *the promises*, and the reading of them is now a craft with its own family. The fishing fleet goes out or stays home by what stands reflected in the Shade's back three days before it arrives overhead. It is not a wonder here either. It is a chore, like checking the wind.

Last winter it took its first lightning. It had raised, over the previous decade, a single slender tower-frond — a green mast, now the tallest thing on the coast — and when the winter storms came in off the sea, every strike within a mile went to it, down through the trunks, into whatever a young body does with fire. The village, which had feared storms for four hundred years of living under bare cliffs, gathered beneath the Shade to watch. The frond healed its scar into a glassy spiral seam, and in the seam stood beads of soft copper. A child pried one loose and gave it to her grandmother, who hammered it cold into a small chime and hung it at the Shade's rim — and the chime, tuned by no design of hers to the frequency of wings, rings an hour before the fronts arrive each spring. There is now, hanging on this body, a cold-hammered copper flock-chime, the precise instrument that hangs at the mouth of every cone-house on the Back of the Green. Nobody made it twice. It was made once by each end of the world.

The grandmother, when she died, did not go to the sea. A plate near the Shade's crown had softened that year — the first swallow, though no one here has that word — and she had asked, in the quiet season, in her own voice, to be laid in it. The slates record the argument, which lasted a winter, and the decision, and the fact: the Shade closed over her in a season, without mark, without haste. Since then, at every death, there is a question that did not used to exist. Sea, or Shade. Slowly, more choose the Shade. The old body at the world's center taught its people this rite over ten thousand years; the young one has taught it here in one generation, or the people have taught it to themselves, or the distinction has stopped mattering — which is, perhaps, how it always goes with this kind of body.

## What Is Beginning

Two springs ago, things came out of the Shade's seams.

Hand-sized, six-legged, hook-footed, with forelimbs that bow across a ridged plate of shell — and hindparts that are not shell at all but bark, small bronze-green plates, warm, bleeding clear sweet sap where the gulls cracked the first few open. A few dozen the first year. Hundreds now. They are plate-shrills — the first of this young body's own flesh to stand up and walk, its circulation budding into legs exactly as the parent's did in some unrecorded age — though the Step-folk have no word for any of that. They call them the singers, because of what happened next.

One dawn in the long quiet — the season that has been silent on this coast since before the villages, the season whose silence is the frame around every voice, every slate, every kept name — a note rose from the Shade. Thin, synchronized, rising and falling on a slow cycle, like surf the size of a garden. The first sound the quiet season had ever contained.

It frightened the elders, at first. Some argued for scraping the seams. But the fishing family that reads the promises pointed out what everyone could already half-feel through their feet on the lowest bench: the Shade's warmth steadies when the singing runs, and falters on the days it doesn't. The song is not a noise the body tolerates. It is something the body does — the small beginning of the same continental roar that the deaf listeners of the far plateau read as health, the standing wave that is, or moves, or *is* the flow. A province of sound two hundred paces wide has been founded on the White Steps. There will someday — not soon, not in a thousand years, but someday — be borders, and interference-lines, and still places in the noise where people meet to hear each other's voices.

The children already sleep better for it. That is on the slates too.

## A Year

Early spring: the copper chime at the Shade's rim begins to ring on windless days. Within the week, the inland haze darkens, and the arriving begins; the village goes to sign; the benches fill shelf by shrieking shelf.

High season: eggs under the second-egg law; feathers by the ton; the gardens fed; the glare, the stink, the glory; the fleet timed by the promises in the Shade's cups.

Autumn: the leaving, shelf by shelf, and then the lid of quiet, and at dusk the loosing of names on the middle benches — two thousand voices, used again.

The long quiet: fishing, slate-cutting, storms taken by the young frond while the village watches from beneath the warm roof; and threading the whole silent season now, dawn and dusk, the thin rising-falling note of the singers, which the Step-folk have stopped calling strange.

## The Current

Stand on the top bench at the leaving, and watch the last fronts rise off the white and turn inland, toward hills, toward scrubland, toward a hot green country the size of a small continent that no one here suspects, carrying in their crops whatever fits in a crop — and you are watching the whole connection at once, the only bridge this world has built between its two green places: sap out as sweetness in the guts of birds, laid down here as the white that fills the gardens; feathers back, dropped over the far plates for the bands to glean and sleep in; and once, four hundred years ago, a seed, delivered in the body of its carrier, planted in the carrier's own breast.

And there is a bridge in time as well as in wings. The oldest account of the Back of the Green begins by saying there is no ground there in the mineral sense — none, "if there ever was." There was. It looked like this. Somewhere under that mile and a half of living dark, under the forest of trunks that no band has ever descended to see, there is stone that once rang underfoot and gleamed white in a glare, benches that held the first trunks the way the Steps now hold forty, a coast that people may once have lived on and gardened and left their slates in, before the roof grew over the sky and became the sky. The White Steps are the far country's forgotten childhood, still happening.

Which means the far country is the Steps' foretold age. In ten thousand years the Shade will not shade a village; it will stand on the village's stone, a green nation a mile and more in the air, its back a bright roaring plain where some descendant people walk polished trails and talk with their hands — and its underside will be a warm dark forest of trunks where some other descendant people, or the same ones, keep whatever is kept down there, and sing upward into the body from below, pressing the flow toward every faltering place, as the under-chorus of the old country does now. The Step-folk will not have to go looking for the back of the green. They are standing on the spot where it will be, feeding it their gardens' surplus and their grandmother, hanging chimes on it, learning its first song in their season of silence.

They do not know any of this. They know the chime, the promises, the sweet dawn water, the warm roof, the thin new note in the quiet. It is enough to organize a life around, and they have organized theirs, the way people always have on this world: as late guests in a house that is young here and old there and, at both ends of the flyway, unmistakably, patiently, making room.
